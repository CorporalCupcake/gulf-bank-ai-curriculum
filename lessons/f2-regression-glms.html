    F2.5 Day one About 50 minutes

    Regression and Generalised Linear Models

    The model family behind credit scorecards, IFRS 9 default probabilities and most regulatory work: what a coefficient really means, why it can flip sign, and how one recipe handles amounts, yes-or-no outcomes, counts and losses.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAdd up weighted features into one score, push that score through a function that puts it on the right scale (AED, probability, count), and choose the weights that make the observed data most likely. That is every model in this lesson, from a least-squares line to a bank's credit scorecard.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The cards team wants a rule for setting limits: how much more do customers spend each month per extra AED 1,000 of salary? Plot spend against salary for 18 pilot customers and draw a straight line through the cloud.

    Which line? The vertical gap between a customer and the line is the residual: what the line got wrong for that person. Ordinary least squares picks the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals. A miss of AED 2,000 costs four times a miss of AED 1,000, so the line works hard to avoid big misses.

    Now add a private-banking client: AED 140,000 salary, only AED 2,200 of spend on this card (the rest goes through another one). One customer in nineteen. Watch the line.

    • Squaring makes big misses expensive. The line tilts towards the private-banking client to shrink its square. The slope collapses from about AED 120 to about AED 2 per AED 1,000 of salary, and the cards team would conclude salary doesn't matter.
    • Distance along x is power. A point far from the average salary has high leverage, like the far end of a seesaw: a small move swings the whole line. An odd spend at an ordinary salary barely matters.
    • The fit can't tell you it was hijacked. The line is still "optimal". Only a plot, or leverage and Cook's distance, shows one customer writing the rule for everyone.
    Hold onto thisA regression is a weighted sum of features, with weights chosen to make the observed data most likely. Every model here, the credit scorecard included, adds one step: a link function that puts the sum on the right scale (AED, probability or count).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Least squares is maximum likelihood in disguise

    Squares fall out of one assumption: each customer's spend is the line plus Gaussian noise with constant spread. Choose the line that makes the data most likely (F1.6) and you get least squares:

    −lnL(β)=12σ2∑i(yi−y^i)2+constant

    The negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise is the scaled sum of squared residuals, so minimising one minimises the other.

    This shows where least squares breaks. The Gaussian has thin tails: a residual five standard deviations out is treated as nearly impossible, so the fit bends hard to avoid it (the private-banking client). Constant spread is also an assumption, and spend varies more among high earners. Change the noise model instead of forcing squares. Absolute error is maximum likelihood for Laplace noise and fits a median line outliers barely move. Huber loss is squared for small residuals and absolute for large ones.

    Linear regression has a closed form. Stack features into a matrix X (one row per customer) and outcomes into y:

    β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y

    XᵀX records how features vary and co-vary; inverting it untangles their effects. If two features move in lockstep it is nearly impossible to invert, and coefficients become unstable (Simulation 3).

    A point's leverage is the weight its own value gets in its own prediction: 1/n plus a term that grows with its squared distance from the average x. Leverages sum to the number of coefficients (average 2/n for a line). Cook's distance combines leverage and residual size to show how far all fitted values move if that point is deleted. Rule of thumb: above 4/n take a look, above 1 it dominates the fit.

    How good is the fit? R², adjusted R², RMSE, MAE and residuals

    There are two kinds of answer to "how good is the fit?": relative (how much better than guessing the average?) and in AED (how far off is a typical prediction?). Report both.

    R² is the relative one. If you forecast the average every time, you carry some total squared error. R² is the share of that error the model removes:

    R2=1−∑i(yi−y^i)2∑i(yi−y¯)2

    Top: squared error left after the model. Bottom: squared error from predicting the average for everyone. In a one-feature regression R² equals the squared correlation.

    R²: what it means, how to read it, what a change means.

    • Means: the share of the ups and downs in spend that the model accounts for. 0 is no better than the average; 1 is perfect.
    • Read: R² = 0.6 means the model removes 60% of the squared error that guessing the average would leave. Typical values depend on the field: 0.2 can still rank customers well enough to target offers. A high R² on behavioural data is more often leakage (F3.1) than brilliance.
    • Change: 0.6 to 0.7 cuts the leftover squared error from 40% to 30% of the baseline, a quarter smaller.
    • Misreadings: it says nothing about whether the line is right (a straight line through a curve can score 0.9 and be wrong at both ends) or how big errors are in AED. Training R² never falls when you add a feature, even random noise. Check it on held-out customers, where it can go negative: worse than the average.

    Adjusted R² is R² with a charge per extra coefficient, like a gym that bills per machine: R²adj = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p − 1), with n customers and p features. Read it like R², but it is always a bit lower, and the gap grows with more features and fewer customers. If a new feature raises R² but lowers adjusted R², it only soaked up noise.

    RMSE (root mean squared error) is the typical size of a miss, in AED. It squares first, so big misses dominate. RMSE of AED 900 means predictions are typically off by roughly AED 900. Judge it against the natural spread of spend: useful only if well below it. Going from 900 to 800 means the typical miss shrank by AED 100.

    MAE (mean absolute error) is the plain average miss, easier to say aloud: "off by about AED 700 on average". If RMSE is much bigger than MAE, a handful of large errors is driving it, so go and look at those customers. MAPE (percentage version) blows up when actuals are near zero, such as dormant accounts.

    Residual = actual minus predicted. A residual of +AED 500 means the customer spent AED 500 more than predicted; −2,000 hurts the fit four times as much as −1,000. Always plot residuals against fitted values. Good looks like a shapeless band around zero.

    • A curve means a missed non-linearity.
    • A funnel widening to the right (heteroscedasticity) means the spread grows with the level, so usual standard errors are too narrow for big customers. Use robust standard errors or model log spend.
    • A few points far from the band are the high-influence customers of Simulation 1.
    Hold onto thisQuote out-of-sample error in AED (RMSE or MAE), with R² beside it. Training R² rewards complexity whether it helps or not; adjusted and held-out R² are the honest versions.

    What a coefficient means: everything else held fixed

    A coefficient is how much the prediction changes for a 1-unit rise in that feature, with every other feature held fixed. Now spend depends on salary and credit limit:

    spend=β0+βsal·salary+βlim·limit+ε

    βsal = 60 means: two customers with the same limit whose salaries differ by AED 1,000 differ by about AED 60 in average spend. Positive: spend rises with the feature. Negative: it falls. Near zero relative to its standard error: no clear link. Size depends on the unit (salary in AED rather than thousands gives a coefficient 1,000 times smaller). It is not "the effect of salary" in any wider sense.

    "Holding the limit fixed" changes the answer. In Simulation 3 the true coefficients are AED 60 per AED 1,000 of salary and AED 25 per AED 1,000 of limit, and salary and limit correlate at 0.9 (limits are partly set from salary). Regress spend on salary alone and the slope is about AED 116, nearly double. Salary carries the limit's effect with its own. This is omitted-variable bias: the missing coefficient times how strongly the missing feature tracks the included one (25 × 0.9 × 20 ÷ 8 ≈ 56).

    • Size and sign depend on what else is in the model. Ask "holding what fixed?" whenever someone quotes one.
    • "Holding fixed" happens in the data, not the world. The bank can't raise a salary while freezing the limit. A coefficient describes a pattern among existing customers. Reading it as cause and effect needs confounding and causal methods (F2.6, F9.3).

    Correlated features and coefficients that won't sit still

    Two near-duplicate features are like two people lifting a table: you can measure the total force but not who pushes harder. The data pins down their combined effect but can barely split it. A slightly different sample gives a very different split, sometimes with one coefficient negative to offset the other. Predictions barely move. The story changes completely.

    The diagnostic is the variance inflation factor. Regress each feature on all the others; if they explain a share Rj² of it:

    VIFj=11−Rj2

    How much coefficient j's variance is inflated against uncorrelated features; the square root inflates the standard error. VIF 1 means no problem. At correlation 0.9, VIF is about 5.3 and standard errors are 2.3 times wider. At 0.99, VIF is about 50 and they are 7 times wider.

    The green stat stays tight at any correlation: collinearity damages interpretation far more than prediction, provided the features keep moving together. If they stop (say the bank changes its limit policy), the shaky split suddenly matters.

    For a pure prediction model, often do nothing. For a scorecard or IFRS 9 model a validator will read, drop or combine one of the pair, or check that signs survive bootstrapping. VIF limits of 5 or 10 and pairwise correlation limits of about 0.5 to 0.7 are conventions, not laws.

    Ridge and lasso: regularisation as a prior

    The other fix is a budget rule: make every coefficient justify its size by adding a penalty to the loss.

    Ridge:minβ∑i(yi−y^i)2+λ∑jβj2 Lasso:minβ∑i(yi−y^i)2+λ∑j|βj|

    λ sets how much you care about small coefficients versus fitting the data. Features are standardised first so the penalty treats salary and limit fairly. The intercept is never penalised.

    These penalties are priors (F1.6): ridge is MAP with a Gaussian prior centred on zero, lasso a Laplace prior. So λ says "before seeing data, I expect effects to be small."

    • Ridge shares the credit: in Simulation 3 it pulls correlated coefficients towards each other and zero, trading a little bias for a big cut in variance.
    • Lasso sets coefficients exactly to zero, so it selects features. With a correlated pair it keeps one, and which one can flip between samples.
    • Elastic net mixes both for selection with some of ridge's stability.

    Choose λ by cross-validation, which in a bank should respect time order (F4.5). Caution for regulated work: standard p-values and confidence intervals assume features were chosen before looking at the data, so after lasso they are too optimistic. Refit on a fresh sample or use post-selection inference.

    Interactions: when one effect depends on another

    A plain linear model gives every feature the same effect for everyone. Often it doesn't: high card utilisation may warn more for customers whose salary is not paid into the bank, because the bank can't see income arriving. Add a product term to let the model learn that:

    η=β0+β1·util+β2·noSalary+β3·util×noSalary

    Utilisation's effect is β1 for salary-transfer customers and β1 + β3 for everyone else. β1 alone now means the effect when the other variable is zero.

    Once an interaction is in, main-effect coefficients change meaning. Centre continuous features (subtract the average) so main effects describe a typical customer.

    Interactions are where linear models need the most human effort: 30 features give 435 pairs, too many to test honestly. Credit teams use segmentation (separate scorecards for thin-file and established, or salaried and self-employed). Gradient boosting finds interactions automatically, a large part of its accuracy edge. Explainable boosting machines add a few pairwise ones deliberately.

    The GLM idea: linear predictor, link, distribution

    Linear regression predicts any number. A default probability must sit in 0 to 1, a count can't be negative, a loss is positive and skewed. Nelder and Wedderburn (1972) showed one framework covers all of them if you separate three choices:

    g(E[y∣x])=η=β0+β1x1+⋯+βkxk

    1. The linear predictor η: a weighted sum of features, exactly as before. It can be any number. 2. The link function g: connects the expected outcome to η. Its inverse squeezes η onto the outcome's valid range. 3. The distribution of y around its mean: it decides how the spread behaves and therefore which loss you minimise.

    All GLMs are fitted by maximum likelihood, usually by iteratively reweighted least squares (repeated weighted regressions that converge in a few steps). For the standard pairings the fit is convex, so every package finds the same answer, which validators like.

    Read the fourth stat. What "a 1-unit change" does depends on the link:

    • Identity: adds a fixed amount, wherever you start.
    • Log: multiplies the expected value by a fixed factor.
    • Logit: multiplies the odds by a fixed factor, so the effect on probability depends on where you start.

    Mixing these up is one of the commonest write-up mistakes. The distribution matters as much, though it barely moves the curve: it sets the weights and the standard errors. Poisson assumes variance equals the mean. If the real variance is three times bigger, coefficients may be fine but every standard error is too small by √3 ≈ 1.7, and features look significant when they aren't.

    Logistic regression and odds ratios

    Logistic regression is the GLM with a Bernoulli outcome and the logit link. It models the log-odds as a straight line:

    lnp1−p=η⟺p=11+e−η

    p is the probability of default; p/(1 − p) is the odds. A 20% probability is odds of 1 to 4, or 0.25. The sigmoid on the right turns any η into a valid probability.

    The odds ratio. Raising a feature one unit adds β to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by eβ. That multiplier is the odds ratio. If β = 0.4 per missed payment, the odds ratio is e0.4 ≈ 1.49: each missed payment multiplies the odds of default by about 1.5, whoever the customer.

    How to read it. 1 means no effect. Above 1 raises the odds (β > 0). Below 1 lowers them: 0.59 means odds cut by 41%. Two missed payments multiply twice (1.49² ≈ 2.2).

    What it does to probability depends on where you start. At a 2% PD, odds of 0.0204 become 0.0304: PD about 2.95%, up about 1 point. At 40%, odds of 0.667 become 0.995: PD about 49.9%, up about 10 points. Simulation 5 shows it.

    Misreadings. An odds ratio is not a risk ratio. "Odds ratio 2" means "twice as likely" only when the outcome is rare: close for defaults at a few per cent, far apart for 40% churn. And a coefficient of 0.7 is not "70% more likely".

    The intercept is the log-odds when every feature is zero, so it carries the base rate. If the portfolio default rate moves but relationships hold, only the intercept changes (the prior shift from F1.4). Likewise, keep all defaulters but only a fraction of goods and slopes stay consistent; the intercept is off by the log of the sampling ratio.

    Logistic regression is the credit default because it gives valid probabilities, a convex reproducible fit, effects that add on the log-odds scale (so the model becomes a points table), and good calibration without help.

    The credit scorecard

    A retail scorecard is a logistic regression dressed for credit officers, validators and customers. The build:

    1. Fine classing. Cut each feature into many small bins (say 20) and look at each bad rate.
    2. Coarse classing. Merge neighbouring bins until each has enough defaulters to be stable and the bad rate moves in a sensible direction. Higher utilisation should never look safer.
    3. Weight of evidence. Replace each bin by its WoE, below.
    4. Selection. Rank by information value; drop weak or redundant features.
    5. Fit a logistic regression on the WoE values.
    6. Scale into points and check on a later time period.
    WoEb=ln% of all goods in binb% of all bads in binb IV=∑b(%good−%bad)·WoEb

    Positive WoE: the bin holds more than its share of good customers. It is the bin's log-odds of good minus the portfolio's, so a logistic fit on WoE has coefficients near 1. Information value is the same formula as PSI (F1.7), comparing goods with bads instead of now with then.

    Over 70%3,8003508.4%19.8%43.8%−0.79
    All19,2008004.0%100%100%IV = 0.34

    This simulated sample has 20,000 personal loans. Bad rates rise steadily across the bins: the monotonic pattern a validator looks for. Rule of thumb for IV: below 0.02 useless, 0.02 to 0.1 weak, 0.1 to 0.3 medium, above 0.3 strong. Above about 0.5 usually means the feature leaks the outcome. Investigate, don't celebrate.

    Scaling scores the odds of being good, so higher means safer, using two bank-chosen anchors. A common choice: 600 points at odds of 50 to 1, and 20 points to double the odds (PDO).

    Score=Offset+Factor×ln(odds of good) Factor=PDOln2≈28.85 Offset=600−Factor×ln50≈487.1

    So 620 means odds of 100 to 1, 580 means 25 to 1, and 560 means 12.5 to 1 (PD about 7.4%). Because the model is additive in log-odds, the score splits into points per feature: Factor × (coefficient × WoE + intercept ÷ number of features) + Offset ÷ number of features, rounded.

    The scorecard gives you a lot for free. The whole model fits on one page and an officer can check any decision by hand. Every effect is monotonic by construction. Reason codes fall straight out of the points: rank features by points lost against the best attribute, and the top three are the reasons. US Regulation B requires specific principal reasons for a decline, and CFPB Circular 2022-03 says a complex algorithm is no excuse. The CBUAE's February 2026 guidance note expects meaningful explanations of AI-driven decisions and a route to human review. A scorecard meets both with little extra machinery.

    The price is accuracy. Binning discards detail (31% and 69% utilisation score the same) and interactions need segmentation. On large, rich behavioural data, gradient boosting usually ranks better.

    Counts: Poisson and negative binomial

    Fraud alerts per merchant, complaints per branch, collections calls per customer: counts are everywhere. The Poisson GLM models the log of the expected count as a straight line, so eβ is a rate ratio: the factor by which one extra unit multiplies the expected count (β = 0.1 gives ×1.105, about 10.5% more).

    lnE[y]=ln(exposure)+η

    The offset ln(exposure) has its coefficient fixed at 1. It makes the model one for rates: complaints per 1,000 customers, alerts per 10,000 transactions. Without it the model mostly learns which branches are big.

    Poisson assumes variance equals the mean. Real counts nearly always vary more (overdispersion, F1.5), because customers and branches differ in ways the features miss. Check residual deviance divided by degrees of freedom: near 1 is fine, 2 or more is a warning. The usual fix is the negative binomial, with one extra parameter (Simulation 4's toggle). If there are far more zeros than either allows, zero-inflated or hurdle models split "any at all?" from "how many?".

    If the counts come from a classifier (complaints tagged "fees" by an Arabic–English language model), they inherit its errors. A tagger change looks like a change in complaints.

    Loss amounts: Gamma and Tweedie

    Loss amounts are positive and right-skewed, and spread grows with size: a loss on a AED 500,000 mortgage varies by far more dirhams than one on a AED 5,000 card. The Gamma GLM with a log link builds that in: standard deviation proportional to the mean, coefficients that multiply the expected loss.

    Loss given default is usually modelled as a fraction of exposure, with clusters at 0 (full recovery) and near 1. Fractional logistic or beta regression handles the fraction; two-stage models ("any loss?", then "how much?") handle the clusters.

    For total loss per account per year, most accounts are exactly zero and a few positive. The Tweedie family fits this in one model: with power between 1 and 2, a Poisson number of Gamma-sized losses, so a point mass at zero plus a continuous tail:

    Var(y)=φμp,1<p<2

    p = 1 is Poisson, p = 2 is Gamma, in between blends frequency and severity. It is the workhorse of insurance pricing, takaful included, and LightGBM and XGBoost both offer it as a loss.

    Neither family has a heavy enough tail for the very largest losses (a corporate default, a big authorised-push-payment scam). Handle those separately with caps, extreme-value methods or scenarios (F1.5).

    Are the probabilities right? Calibration

    Calibration means that of customers given a 5% PD, about 5% really default. Logistic regression gets this for free: at the maximum-likelihood fit with an intercept, predicted probabilities sum to the observed defaults exactly:

    ∑i(yi−p^i)=0

    This is the log-likelihood's derivative with respect to the intercept, set to zero. It also holds within every category of any binary feature, including every WoE bin entered as dummies.

    So on training data a logistic model is calibrated on average and roughly across its features. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) found logistic regression well calibrated and boosted trees not. Platt scaling (F7.4) is itself a one-feature logistic regression on the score.

    The guarantee is in-sample and in-time. Three things break it:

    • The default rate moves. The intercept is stale, the scale shifts, and 600 no longer means 50 to 1.
    • Overfitting (too many features, too few defaults) makes it overconfident, with a calibration slope below 1. Regularise.
    • Training on approved applicants only biases both (reject inference, B4.5).

    Regulators use calibration too. IFRS 9 needs point-in-time, forward-looking PDs adjusted with macro scenarios. Basel internal-ratings models need PDs anchored to long-run default rates. One ranking can feed both. Validators test calibration by score band with binomial tests, separately from ranking measures such as Gini.

    In practice the two work together. Validators often build a gradient-boosted challenger on the same data. If it is much better, the scorecard is missing structure (an interaction, a non-linear effect) and developers go looking. If only slightly better, the gap is the measured price of interpretability, and the bank decides whether it is worth paying. Where boosting goes live, a GLM often stays as benchmark and fallback.

    IFRS 9 PD models are where GLMs hold on hardest. They must respond sensibly to macro scenarios (an oil-price fall, a rise in EIBOR), and committees want to see each driver's coefficient and check its sign.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Reading a coefficient as cause and effect

      "Salary transfer cuts default risk by 40%, so push salary transfers." The coefficient compares existing customers who chose differently. It says nothing about persuading someone to switch.

      Spot itAsk "holding what fixed?" and "would this survive an experiment?". If a decision depends on it, use causal methods (F9.3).
    • Signs that flip because of collinearity

      A validator rejects a negative salary coefficient, or a developer silently drops the "wrong" sign.

      Spot itCheck the correlation matrix and VIFs, and bootstrap. If the sign flips across resamples, the split isn't identified.
    • One high-leverage point writing the rule

      A few very large salaries, exposures or transactions tilt the whole fit (Simulation 1).

      Spot itPlot the data. Ask for the highest leverage and Cook's distance, and a refit without them.
    • R² or RMSE quoted alone

      "R² is 0.9" for a curved relationship fitted with a line, or a training R² with no held-out figure, or RMSE with no comparison to the spread of the outcome.

      Spot itAsk for held-out error in AED and the residual plot.
    • The wrong family for the outcome

      Linear regression on default flags gives probabilities below 0 and above 1. On counts or losses it gives negative predictions and misjudges the spread.

      Spot itCheck the range of predictions. Ask why that distribution and link.
    • Poisson on overdispersed counts

      Counts vary far more than Poisson allows, standard errors are too small, and every feature looks significant.

      Spot itResidual deviance over degrees of freedom well above 1. Refit as negative binomial and see what survives.
    • Counts modelled without exposure

      Big branches get more complaints. The model learns size, not service.

      Spot itLook for an offset of ln(customers) or ln(transactions).
    • Odds ratios read as risk ratios

      "Odds ratio 2.5, so 2.5 times as likely to churn." With a 40% base rate that is badly wrong.

      Spot itConvert at a realistic baseline: odds × OR, then back to p.
    • Bins and WoE built with leaked information

      Bins and WoE computed on all data including the test period, or an IV of 0.9 for a feature recorded after default, such as "account in collections".

      Spot itAsk which data set defined the bins. Treat IV above about 0.5 as leakage until shown otherwise (F3.1).
    • A scorecard whose odds have drifted

      Default rates rose after a shock, the scale no longer means what it says, and the cut-off approves riskier applicants than intended.

      Spot itCompare predicted and actual bad rates by score band on recent cohorts. A consistent gap means recalibrate the intercept, not redevelop.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't add up

      "R² of 0.12 for the logistic model" (ordinary R² doesn't apply), p-values after lasso with no caveat, "a coefficient of 0.7 means 70% more likely", or a WoE sign flipped halfway so reason codes come out backwards.

      Spot itRecompute one WoE and one bin's points by hand, and check one reason code against the points table.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A scorecard is scaled at 600 points = odds of 50:1 with PDO 20. What are the odds and PD at 640? At 560?

    640 is two doublings up: odds 200:1, PD = 1/201 ≈ 0.50%. 560 is two halvings down: odds 12.5:1, PD = 1/13.5 ≈ 7.4%.

    2. A logistic coefficient is 0.4. What is the odds ratio? What happens to a 2% PD, and to a 40% PD?

    Odds ratio e0.4 ≈ 1.49. 2%: odds 0.0204 → 0.0304, PD ≈ 2.95%. 40%: odds 0.667 → 0.995, PD ≈ 49.9%. Same multiplier on odds, very different change in probability.

    3. Two features are correlated at 0.95, and there are no others. What is each one's VIF, and how much wider are their standard errors?

    VIF = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.95²) = 1 ÷ 0.0975 ≈ 10.3. Standard errors are √10.3 ≈ 3.2 times wider.

    4. A Poisson model of monthly fraud alerts per merchant has a coefficient of 0.1 on "number of card terminals". Interpret it. What's missing if merchants were observed for different numbers of months?

    Each extra terminal multiplies expected alerts by e0.1 ≈ 1.105, about 10.5% more, other features fixed. With unequal observation periods the model needs an offset of ln(months observed), or it partly learns which merchants were watched longer.

    5. A scorecard was built when the bad rate was 4%. It is now 6% at every score in the same proportion. How far has the scale drifted?

    Odds of bad go from 0.04/0.96 ≈ 0.0417 to 0.06/0.94 ≈ 0.0638, a factor of about 1.53. In log-odds that is ln 1.53 ≈ 0.43, so 0.43 × 28.85 ≈ 12 points. Every score overstates safety by about 12 points. Recalibrate the intercept, or raise the cut-off by about 12; the ranking is unchanged.

    6. In ridge regression, what happens as λ goes to zero, and as it goes to infinity?

    λ → 0: ordinary least squares, with all its variance. λ → ∞: every penalised coefficient shrinks to zero and the model predicts the average. Cross-validation picks the best point in between.

    7. Why is minimising squared error the same as maximum likelihood, and what does that tell you about outliers?

    With Gaussian noise of constant variance, the negative log-likelihood is the sum of squared residuals over 2σ², plus a constant. Gaussian tails are thin, so a big residual is treated as nearly impossible and the fit bends to shrink it. Outliers get far more influence than they deserve.

    8. A model has training R² 0.80, adjusted R² 0.74, held-out R² 0.35 and held-out RMSE AED 900 against a spend sd of AED 1,000. What does each tell you?

    Training R² says the model removes 80% of squared error on the data it saw. The gap to adjusted R² shows features are being charged for. Held-out R² of 0.35 is the honest figure: overfitting. RMSE of AED 900 against an sd of 1,000 means typical misses are barely smaller than guessing the average's, which fits an R² near 0.2 to 0.35 at best.
