    F2.5 Day one About 50 minutes

    Regression and Generalised Linear Models

    The model family behind credit scorecards, IFRS 9 default probabilities and most regulatory work: what a coefficient really means, why it can flip sign, and how one recipe handles amounts, yes-or-no outcomes, counts and losses.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAdd up weighted features into one score, push that score through a function that puts it on the right scale (AED, probability, count), and choose the weights that make the observed data most likely. That is every model in this lesson, from a least-squares line to a bank's credit scorecard.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The cards team wants a rule of thumb for setting limits: how much more do customers spend each month for every extra AED 1,000 of salary? You pull 18 customers from a pilot, plot monthly card spend against monthly salary, and draw a straight line through the cloud.

    Which line? Each customer sits some vertical distance above or below any line you draw. That gap is the residual: what the line got wrong for that person. Ordinary least squares picks the line that makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible. Squared is the important word. A miss of AED 2,000 costs four times as much as a miss of AED 1,000, so the line works hard to avoid big misses.

    Now a private-banking client joins the sample: AED 140,000 a month in salary, but only AED 2,200 of spend on this card because most of it goes through another one. One customer out of nineteen. Watch what happens to the line.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Squaring makes big misses expensive. The private-banking client's square dwarfs everyone else's, so the line tilts towards that one point to shrink it. With the client included, the slope collapses from about AED 120 to about AED 2 per AED 1,000 of salary, and the cards team would conclude that salary doesn't matter at all.
    • Distance along the x-axis is power. A point far from the average salary has high leverage: it sits at the end of a long lever, so moving it up or down swings the whole line. An unusual spend at an ordinary salary has little effect. An unusual salary is what gives a point the power to steer.
    • The fit can't tell you it's been hijacked. The line is still "optimal". Only plotting the data, or checking leverage and Cook's distance, shows that one customer is writing the rule for everyone.
    Hold onto thisA regression is a weighted sum of features, fitted by choosing the weights that make the observed data most likely. Every model in this lesson, including the credit scorecard, is that recipe plus one extra step: a link function that puts the weighted sum on the right scale for the outcome, whether that's AED, a probability or a count.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Least squares is maximum likelihood in disguise

    Why squares, and not absolute distances or something else? Because squared error is what falls out if you assume each customer's spend is the line plus Gaussian noise with a constant spread, and then pick the line that makes the data most likely. This is the maximum likelihood idea from F1.6:

    −lnL(β)=12σ2∑i(yi−y^i)2+constant

    The negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise is the sum of squared residuals, scaled. Minimising one minimises the other, so least squares is maximum likelihood under that assumption.

    Seeing it this way tells you where least squares breaks. The Gaussian has thin tails: a residual of five standard deviations is treated as nearly impossible, so the fit bends hard to avoid one. That's the private-banking client in Simulation 1. The constant spread is also an assumption, and spend usually varies more among high earners than low ones. When either assumption is badly wrong, change the noise model rather than forcing the squares. Absolute error is maximum likelihood for Laplace noise and fits a median line that outliers barely move. The Huber loss is squared for small residuals and absolute for large ones, a compromise between the two.

    For linear regression there's a closed-form answer. Stack the features into a matrix X, one row per customer, and the outcomes into a vector y:

    β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y

    XᵀX records how the features vary and co-vary. Inverting it untangles their effects. If two features move almost in lockstep, that matrix is nearly impossible to invert, and the coefficients become unstable. That's the subject of Simulation 2.

    Leverage has an exact meaning too. Each fitted value is a weighted sum of all the observed values, and a point's leverage is the weight its own value gets in its own prediction. In a simple regression it is 1/n plus a term that grows with the squared distance from the average x. Leverages always add up to the number of coefficients, so the average is 2/n for a straight line. Cook's distance combines leverage with the size of the residual to measure how far all the fitted values would move if you deleted that one point. A common rule of thumb flags anything above 4/n for a look and anything above 1 as dominating the fit.

    What a coefficient means: everything else held fixed

    Add a second feature. Spend now depends on salary and credit limit:

    spend=β0+βsal·salary+βlim·limit+ε

    βsal is the difference in average spend between two customers whose salaries differ by AED 1,000 but who have the same credit limit. It is not "the effect of salary" in any wider sense.

    "Holding the limit fixed" changes the answer. In the simulated world of Simulation 2, the true coefficients are AED 60 per AED 1,000 of salary and AED 25 per AED 1,000 of limit, and salary and limit are correlated at 0.9 (the bank sets limits partly from salary). Regress spend on salary alone and the slope comes out at about AED 116, nearly double. Salary is carrying the credit limit's effect along with its own, because customers with higher salaries also tend to have higher limits. This is omitted-variable bias, and the size of it is exact: the missing coefficient times how strongly the missing feature tracks the included one (here 25 × 0.9 × 20 ÷ 8 ≈ 56).

    Two consequences matter in practice. First, a coefficient's size and even its sign depend on which other features are in the model. Ask "holding what fixed?" every time someone quotes one. Second, "holding fixed" happens in the data, not in the world. The bank can't actually raise a customer's salary while freezing their limit. A coefficient describes a pattern among existing customers. Reading it as cause and effect needs the separate arguments of confounding and causal inference (F2.6, F9.3).

    Correlated features and coefficients that won't sit still

    When two features carry nearly the same information, the data can tell you their combined effect precisely but can barely split it between them. A slightly different sample of customers produces a very different split, and occasionally one coefficient goes negative to compensate for the other going too high. The model's predictions hardly change. Its story changes completely.

    The standard diagnostic is the variance inflation factor. Regress each feature on all the others, and if they explain a share Rj² of it:

    VIFj=11−Rj2

    The factor by which the variance of coefficient j is inflated compared with uncorrelated features. Its square root inflates the standard error. At a correlation of 0.9 the VIF is about 5.3, so standard errors are 2.3 times wider. At 0.99 the VIF is about 50, and standard errors are 7 times wider.

    Notice the green stat. The combined effect of a customer one standard deviation higher on both salary and limit stays tight however high the correlation goes. That's the general rule: collinearity damages interpretation far more than prediction, as long as the features stay correlated in the same way in future data. If they stop moving together, for example after the bank changes its limit policy, the shaky split suddenly matters.

    What practitioners do about it depends on what the coefficients are for. For a pure prediction model, often nothing. For a scorecard or an IFRS 9 model that a validator will read, they drop or combine one of the pair, or check that the signs are stable under bootstrapping. Rules of thumb vary by bank, but VIF limits of 5 or 10 and pairwise correlation limits between transformed features of around 0.5 to 0.7 are common conventions, not laws.

    Ridge and lasso: regularisation as a prior

    The other fix is to stop letting the data push coefficients anywhere it likes. Add a penalty for large coefficients to the loss:

    Ridge:minβ∑i(yi−y^i)2+λ∑jβj2 Lasso:minβ∑i(yi−y^i)2+λ∑j|βj|

    λ sets how much you care about small coefficients versus fitting the data. Features are standardised first, so the penalty treats salary and limit fairly. The intercept is never penalised.

    In F1.6 you saw that these penalties are priors. Ridge is the maximum a posteriori estimate with a Gaussian prior centred on zero; lasso uses a Laplace prior. So λ is a statement of belief: "before seeing the data, I think effects are probably small."

    They behave differently on correlated features, which is why both exist. Ridge shares the credit. In Simulation 2 it pulls the two coefficients towards each other and towards zero, trading a little bias for a large cut in variance. Lasso can set coefficients exactly to zero, so it does feature selection, but with a correlated pair it tends to keep one and drop the other, and which one survives can flip between samples. Elastic net mixes the two penalties to get selection with some of ridge's stability. λ is chosen by cross-validation, which in a bank should respect time order (F4.5).

    One caution for regulated work. The standard p-values and confidence intervals assume the features were chosen before looking at the data. After lasso selection they're too optimistic. If a validator asks for significance tests on a lasso-selected model, the honest answer is to refit on a fresh sample, or use methods built for post-selection inference.

    Interactions: when one effect depends on another

    A plain linear model assumes every feature's effect is the same for everyone. Often it isn't. High card utilisation might be a much stronger warning sign for customers whose salary is not paid into the bank, because the bank can't see their income arriving. You let the model learn that by adding a product term:

    η=β0+β1·util+β2·noSalary+β3·util×noSalary

    The effect of utilisation is now β1 for salary-transfer customers and β1 + β3 for everyone else. β1 on its own no longer means "the effect of utilisation". It means the effect when the other variable is zero.

    That last point catches people out: once an interaction is in, the main-effect coefficients change meaning. Centring continuous features first (subtracting their average) keeps the main effects interpretable as effects for a typical customer.

    Interactions are where linear models need the most human effort. With 30 features there are 435 possible pairs, and you can't test them all honestly. Credit teams handle the biggest interactions by segmentation: separate scorecards for thin-file and established customers, or for salaried and self-employed applicants. Gradient boosting finds interactions automatically, which is a large part of its accuracy advantage. Explainable boosting machines, in Part 4, add a small number of pairwise interactions deliberately and show each one as a picture.

    The GLM idea: linear predictor, link, distribution

    Linear regression predicts an AED amount, which can in principle be any number. Most bank outcomes can't. A default probability must stay between 0 and 1. A count of collections calls can't be negative. A loss amount is positive and skewed. Nelder and Wedderburn's 1972 insight was that one framework covers all of them if you separate three choices:

    g(E[y∣x])=η=β0+β1x1+⋯+βkxk

    1. The linear predictor η: a weighted sum of features, exactly as before. It can be any number. 2. The link function g: connects the expected outcome to η. Its inverse squeezes η onto the outcome's valid range. 3. The distribution of y around its mean: it decides how the spread behaves and therefore which loss you minimise.

    All GLMs are fitted by maximum likelihood, usually with an algorithm called iteratively reweighted least squares: repeated weighted linear regressions that converge in a handful of steps. For the standard pairings the negative log-likelihood is convex, so there's one best answer and every software package finds the same one. That reproducibility is part of why validators like GLMs.

    The fourth stat is the one to dwell on. With the identity link, a coefficient adds a fixed amount wherever you are. With a log link it multiplies the expected value by a fixed factor. With the logit link it multiplies the odds by a fixed factor, so its effect on the probability depends on where you start. That's why "what does a one-unit change do?" has a different shape of answer for each family, and why mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in model write-ups.

    The distribution matters as much as the link, and it's easy to overlook because it doesn't change the fitted curve much. It decides how much weight each customer gets. A Poisson model assumes the variance equals the mean. If the real variance is three times bigger, the coefficients may be roughly right but every standard error is too small by a factor of √3 ≈ 1.7, so features look significant when they aren't.

    Logistic regression and odds ratios

    Logistic regression is the GLM with a Bernoulli outcome and the logit link. It models the log-odds as a straight line:

    lnp1−p=η⟺p=11+e−η

    p is the probability of default. p/(1 − p) is the odds: a 20% default probability is odds of 1 to 4, or 0.25. The sigmoid on the right turns any η into a valid probability.

    Raising a feature by one unit adds its coefficient to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by eβ. That multiplier is the odds ratio. If β = 0.4 for each missed payment in the last year, the odds ratio is e0.4 ≈ 1.49: each missed payment multiplies the odds of default by about 1.5, whoever the customer is.

    The effect on the probability isn't constant. For a customer at a 2% PD, odds of 0.0204 become 0.0304, so the PD rises to about 2.95%. For one at 40%, odds of 0.667 become 0.995, a PD of about 49.9%. The same coefficient adds about 1 percentage point in one case and 10 in the other. Simulation 4 makes this visible.

    Two traps follow. An odds ratio is not a risk ratio: "odds ratio 2" means "twice as likely" only when the outcome is rare. For defaults at a few per cent the two are close; for a churn rate of 40% they're far apart. And the intercept has a special role. It is the log-odds for a customer with every feature at zero, so it carries the base rate. When the portfolio's default rate moves but the relationships hold, only the intercept needs to change. That's the prior shift from F1.4, and a classic result says the same about sampling: if you keep all defaulters but only a fraction of good customers, the slopes stay consistent and only the intercept is off, by the log of the sampling ratio.

    Logistic regression is the default model for credit because every one of its properties is useful there. Outputs are valid probabilities. The fit is convex and reproducible. Effects add up on the log-odds scale, which, as the next section shows, is exactly what lets you turn a model into a table of points. And it tends to be well calibrated without help, which matters later.

    The credit scorecard

    A retail credit scorecard is a logistic regression dressed for an audience of credit officers, validators and customers. Building one follows a well-worn sequence:

    1. Fine classing. Cut each feature into many small bins, say 20, and look at the bad rate in each.
    2. Coarse classing. Merge neighbouring bins until each has enough defaulters to be stable (often a few per cent of all bads at least), and the bad rate moves in a direction that makes business sense. Higher utilisation should never look safer.
    3. Weight of evidence. Replace each bin by its WoE, below.
    4. Selection. Rank features by information value, drop weak or redundant ones.
    5. Fit a logistic regression on the WoE values.
    6. Scale the result into points, and check it on a later time period.
    WoEb=ln% of all goods in binb% of all bads in binb IV=∑b(%good−%bad)·WoEb

    Positive WoE means the bin holds more than its share of good customers. WoE is the bin's log-odds of good minus the portfolio's, which is why a logistic regression on WoE values has coefficients near 1. Information value is the same formula as PSI (F1.7), comparing goods with bads instead of now with then.

    Card utilisationGoodsBadsBad rate% of goods% of badsWoE

    Over 70%3,8003508.4%19.8%43.8%−0.79
    All19,2008004.0%100%100%IV = 0.34

    A simulated development sample of 20,000 personal loans. Bad rates rise steadily across the bins, which is the monotonic pattern a validator will look for. The common rule of thumb reads an IV below 0.02 as useless, 0.02 to 0.1 as weak, 0.1 to 0.3 as medium and above 0.3 as strong. An IV above about 0.5 is usually a sign that the feature leaks information about the outcome, and should be investigated rather than celebrated.

    The last step is scaling. Scorecards score the odds of being good, so a higher score means safer, and they use a log-odds scale with two anchors chosen by the bank. A common choice: 600 points at odds of 50 to 1, and 20 points to double the odds (PDO).

    Score=Offset+Factor×ln(odds of good) Factor=PDOln2≈28.85 Offset=600−Factor×ln50≈487.1

    So 620 means odds of 100 to 1, 580 means 25 to 1, and 560 means 12.5 to 1 (a PD of about 7.4%). Because the model is additive in log-odds, the score splits into points per feature: each attribute gets Factor × (coefficient × WoE + intercept ÷ number of features) + Offset ÷ number of features, rounded.

    Look at what the scorecard gives you for free. The whole model fits on one page. A credit officer can check any decision by hand. Every feature's effect is monotonic by construction, because the bins were merged until it was. And reason codes fall straight out of the points: rank the features by how many points the applicant lost compared with the best attribute, and the top three are the reasons. In the US, Regulation B requires specific principal reasons for a decline, and the CFPB's Circular 2022-03 says that using a complex algorithm is no excuse for not giving them. In the UAE, the CBUAE's February 2026 guidance note expects meaningful explanations of AI-driven decisions that affect a customer's access to finance, and a route to human review (Part 4). A scorecard meets both with almost no extra machinery.

    The price is accuracy. Binning throws away information within each bin: a customer at 31% utilisation scores the same as one at 69%. Interactions are handled only by segmentation. On large data sets with rich behavioural features, gradient boosting usually ranks better. The rest of this part, and Part 4, is about how banks live with that trade.

    Counts: Poisson and negative binomial

    Fraud alerts per merchant per month, complaints per branch per week, collections calls per customer per quarter: counts are everywhere in bank operations. The Poisson GLM models the log of the expected count as a straight line, so each coefficient is a rate ratio: eβ multiplies the expected count.

    lnE[y]=ln(exposure)+η

    The offset ln(exposure) has its coefficient fixed at 1. It turns the model into one for rates: complaints per 1,000 customers, alerts per 10,000 transactions. Forgetting it means the model mostly learns which branches are big.

    The Poisson assumes the variance equals the mean. Real counts nearly always vary more, because customers and branches differ in ways the features don't capture (F1.5 calls this overdispersion). The quick check is the residual deviance divided by its degrees of freedom: near 1 is fine, 2 or more is a warning. The usual fix is the negative binomial, which adds one parameter so the variance becomes the mean plus the mean squared over a size parameter, as in Simulation 3's toggle. If there are far more zeros than either allows, for example most merchants never generate an alert, zero-inflated or hurdle models split "any at all?" from "how many?".

    One honest caveat for text work: if the counts come from a classifier, such as complaints tagged "fees" by an Arabic–English language model, the counts inherit that classifier's errors. A change in the tagger looks like a change in complaints.

    Loss amounts: Gamma and Tweedie

    Once a loan defaults, how much is lost? Loss amounts are positive and right-skewed, and their spread grows with their size: a loss on a AED 500,000 mortgage varies by far more dirhams than one on a AED 5,000 card. The Gamma GLM with a log link builds that in. Its standard deviation is proportional to its mean, so a constant coefficient of variation, and its coefficients multiply the expected loss.

    Credit risk usually models loss given default as a fraction of the exposure instead, between 0 and 1, often with a big cluster at 0 (full recovery) and another near 1. Fractional logistic regression or beta regression handle the fraction. Two-stage models handle the clusters: first "any loss?", then "how much?".

    When you want total loss per account over a year, most accounts have exactly zero and a few have a positive amount. The Tweedie family fits this in one model. With a power parameter between 1 and 2, it's a Poisson number of Gamma-sized losses, so it has a point mass at zero and a continuous tail:

    Var(y)=φμp,1<p<2

    p = 1 is Poisson, p = 2 is Gamma, and values in between blend frequency and severity. It's the workhorse of insurance pricing, including takaful, and LightGBM and XGBoost both offer it as a loss. That's a clue that it's the right likelihood whatever model sits on top.

    Neither Gamma nor Tweedie has a heavy enough tail for the very largest losses, such as a single corporate default or a large authorised-push-payment scam. Those are usually handled separately, with caps, extreme-value methods or scenario analysis (F1.5).

    Are the probabilities right? Calibration

    Logistic regression has a property most models lack. At the maximum-likelihood fit with an intercept, the predicted probabilities add up to the observed number of defaults exactly:

    ∑i(yi−p^i)=0

    This is the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to the intercept, set to zero. The same holds within every category of any binary feature in the model, which includes every WoE bin when bins enter as dummies.

    So on its own training data a logistic model is calibrated on average, and roughly calibrated across the features it uses. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found back in 2005 that logistic regression gives well-calibrated probabilities, while boosted trees and several other strong classifiers don't. Platt scaling, the standard repair from F7.4, is itself a one-feature logistic regression on the model's score.

    That guarantee is in-sample and in-time. Three things break it in practice. The default rate moves after development, so the intercept is out of date: the whole score scale shifts, and a 600 no longer means 50 to 1. Overfitting from too many features on too few defaults makes the model overconfident, with a calibration slope below 1, and regularisation helps there. And training on a selected population, only the approved applicants, can bias both (reject inference, B4.5).

    In credit, calibration has a regulatory meaning as well. IFRS 9 needs point-in-time, forward-looking PDs, so banks adjust them with macroeconomic scenarios; Basel internal-ratings models need PDs anchored to long-run average default rates. The same scorecard ranking can feed both, with different calibrations on top. Validators test calibration by score band with binomial tests or similar, separately from ranking measures such as Gini.

    In practice the two are used together, not in competition. When a scorecard is in production, validators often build a gradient-boosted challenger on the same data. If the challenger is much better, the scorecard is missing structure, perhaps an interaction or a non-linear effect, and the developers go looking for it. If the challenger is only slightly better, that gap is the measured price of interpretability, and the bank can decide whether it's worth paying. Where boosting does go into production, a GLM often remains as the benchmark and fallback.

    IFRS 9 PD models are where GLMs hold on hardest. A PD model there has to respond sensibly to macroeconomic scenarios, such as an oil-price fall or a rise in EIBOR, and committees want to see the coefficient linking default risk to each driver and check it has the right sign. A logistic or survival-style GLM gives them exactly that.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Regression sits in the middle of the curriculum. Nearly every topic either feeds into it or measures it.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodEvery GLM is a likelihood

    Least squares is Gaussian likelihood, logistic regression is Bernoulli, Poisson regression is Poisson. Ridge and lasso are MAP estimates with Gaussian and Laplace priors. Lesson

    F1.4 – F1.5 · Base rates and distributionsThe intercept is the prior

    A base-rate shift moves only the intercept. The outcome's distribution, such as Poisson, overdispersed or heavy-tailed, chooses the GLM family. Lesson

    F2.2 · The bootstrapHow stable are the coefficients?

    Simulation 2 is a bootstrap. Validators use the same idea to test whether signs and sizes survive resampling.

    F2.6 · Confounding"Holding fixed" isn't causal

    A coefficient changes when you add or drop a correlated feature. That's omitted-variable bias, and it's why coefficients aren't effects of interventions.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisTime to default is a GLM too

    A discrete-time hazard model is a logistic regression on customer-month rows. Lifetime PDs for IFRS 9 are often built this way.

    F4.3 · Gradient boostingThe challenger

    Boosting learns interactions and non-linearities automatically. Monotone constraints and Tweedie or logistic losses bring it closer to a GLM.

    F4.4 · RegularisationRidge, lasso, elastic net

    The fix for unstable coefficients and too many features, and the bridge from statistics to machine learning.

    F7.4 · CalibrationLogistic is calibrated by design

    In-sample, predicted PDs sum to observed defaults. Out of time, recalibrating is usually just an intercept shift. Lesson

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringIV and PSI are the same formula

    Information value compares goods with bads; PSI compares this month with development. Both sum a difference times a log-ratio. See F1.7

    F8.4 · Adverse-action reasonsPoints below maximum

    Scorecard reason codes are exact. For boosted models, reasons come from SHAP or counterfactuals, which are approximations that need validating.

    B4.1 – B4.3 · PD, LGD, IFRS 9Three GLMs and a multiplication

    PD from logistic regression, LGD from fractional or beta regression, exposure from a credit-conversion model; expected loss is their product.

    B4.2 · Scorecards, WOE and IVThe full build process

    Binning choices, reject inference, overrides, cut-off strategy and monitoring, all resting on this lesson's mechanics.

    G3.3 · Independent validationBenchmarks and challengers

    A validator's first move is often to refit a simple GLM and a boosted model to see where the champion sits.

    A10.2 · Fair lending with MLProxies hide in coefficients

    A feature can stand in for nationality or gender through correlation. Holding it fixed in a regression doesn't remove the proxy effect.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    GLMs themselves are settled science. Three things are moving: glass-box machine learning that aims for boosting's accuracy with a scorecard's transparency; supervisors writing down what they expect from machine learning in credit; and LLM agents doing parts of the modelling work.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in model documentation, validation findings and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Reading a coefficient as cause and effect

      “Salary transfer reduces default risk by 40%, so let's push salary transfers.” The coefficient compares existing customers who chose differently; it says nothing about what happens if you persuade someone to switch.

      Spot itAsk “holding what fixed?” and “would this coefficient survive an experiment?”. If the decision depends on the answer, it needs causal methods (F9.3).
    • Signs that flip because of collinearity

      A validator rejects a model because the salary coefficient is negative, or a developer silently drops the feature with the “wrong” sign.

      Spot itLook at the correlation matrix and VIFs, and bootstrap the coefficients. If the sign flips across resamples, the split between correlated features isn't identified.
    • One high-leverage point writing the rule

      A handful of very large salaries, exposures or transaction values tilt the whole fit, as in Simulation 1.

      Spot itPlot the data. Ask for the points with the highest leverage and Cook's distance, and for a refit without them.
    • The wrong family for the outcome

      Linear regression on default flags gives probabilities below 0 and above 1. Linear regression on counts or losses gives negative predictions and misjudges the spread.

      Spot itCheck the range of the predictions. Ask why that distribution and link were chosen.
    • Poisson on overdispersed counts

      Complaint or alert counts vary far more than Poisson allows, standard errors come out too small, and every feature looks significant.

      Spot itResidual deviance divided by degrees of freedom well above 1. Refit as negative binomial and see which features survive.
    • Counts modelled without exposure

      Big branches get more complaints. The model learns size, not service quality.

      Spot itLook for an offset of ln(customers) or ln(transactions) in the specification.
    • Odds ratios read as risk ratios

      “An odds ratio of 2.5, so these customers are 2.5 times as likely to churn.” With a 40% base rate, that's badly wrong.

      Spot itConvert to probabilities at a realistic baseline: odds × OR, then back to p.
    • Bins and WoE built with leaked information

      Binning and WoE calculated on the full data set, including the test period. Or an IV of 0.9 for a feature recorded after the default, such as “account in collections”.

      Spot itAsk which data set defined the bins. Treat any IV above about 0.5 as a leakage suspect until shown otherwise (F3.1).
    • A scorecard whose odds have drifted

      Default rates rose after an economic shock, the score scale no longer means what it says, and the cut-off approves riskier applicants than intended.

      Spot itCompare predicted and actual bad rates by score band on recent cohorts. A consistent gap across bands means recalibrating the intercept, not redeveloping.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't add up

      Typical signs: “R² of 0.12 for the logistic model” (ordinary R² doesn't apply), p-values reported after lasso selection with no caveat, “a coefficient of 0.7 means 70% more likely”, or a WoE sign convention flipped halfway so the reason codes come out backwards.

      Spot itRecompute one WoE and one bin's points by hand, and check one reason code against the points table.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a credit committee · 30 seconds
    “We kept the scorecard as the production model and built a gradient-boosted challenger on the same data. The challenger ranks a little better, about three Gini points. We traced most of that to one interaction, utilisation for customers without salary transfer, and added it to the scorecard as a segment, which recovered about half of the gap. The rest is the price of keeping a model every credit officer can read, with exact reasons for every decline and a human-review route, which is what the CBUAE's guidance expects. We'll rerun the challenger every year to check the gap hasn't grown.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “A GLM is a linear predictor, a link and a distribution, fitted by maximum likelihood. Least squares is the Gaussian case, logistic is Bernoulli with the logit link, so coefficients are log odds ratios. For scorecards I bin to monotonic WoE, select on IV and correlation, fit logistic on WoE and scale with PDO. I check coefficient stability by bootstrap and VIF, and use ridge or elastic net if I need to. In-sample, logistic is calibrated in the large by construction; out of time I recalibrate the intercept by score band. I benchmark against a monotone-constrained boosted challenger and report the gap.”
    “Why don't we just use XGBoost everywhere?”

    Where the data is rich and explanation requirements are light, such as fraud scoring or marketing, we often do. For credit decisions we need monotonic behaviour, specific reasons for every decline, and coefficients a committee can sign off under stress scenarios. A scorecard gives all three by construction. The gap is often small and we measure it with a challenger, and recent benchmarks put the cost of monotone constraints on boosting under 0.2% of AUC on large datasets, so constrained boosting or an EBM is a reasonable middle ground when the gap is worth closing.

    “The salary coefficient is negative. Isn't the model wrong?”

    Not necessarily. A coefficient is an effect with everything else in the model held fixed. If salary is highly correlated with credit limit, which is also in the model, the data can't split their effect cleanly, and one of them can go negative to balance the other. I'd check the VIF, bootstrap the coefficients, and if the sign isn't stable, drop or combine one of them. Predictions are often fine either way; the story isn't.

    “What does an odds ratio of 1.8 actually mean for a customer?”

    The odds of default are multiplied by 1.8. What that does to the probability depends on the starting point. At a 2% PD it becomes about 3.5%. At a 30% PD it becomes about 43.5%. It's only roughly “1.8 times as likely” when the outcome is rare.

    “How do you produce reason codes from a scorecard?”

    For each feature, compare the points the applicant got with the maximum available for that feature. Rank features by the shortfall, and report the top few, mapped to plain-language reasons. Some banks compare with the average points instead of the maximum. Either way it's exact, reproducible and auditable, which is the main reason scorecards still make credit decisions.

    “Would a regulator accept an explainable boosting machine?”

    I can't promise that for any specific supervisor, but the arguments work in its favour. An EBM is still an additive model. Each feature's effect is a curve you can print, check for monotonicity, and constrain, and reason codes come from the per-feature contributions exactly, as with a scorecard. The questions to prepare for are the stability of the learned shapes, how pairwise interactions are explained, and documentation. I'd pilot it as a challenger first.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A scorecard is scaled at 600 points = odds of 50:1 with PDO 20. What are the odds and PD at 640? At 560?

    640 is two doublings above 600: odds 200:1, PD = 1/201 ≈ 0.50%. 560 is two halvings below: odds 12.5:1, PD = 1/13.5 ≈ 7.4%.

    2. A logistic coefficient is 0.4. What is the odds ratio? What happens to a 2% PD, and to a 40% PD?

    Odds ratio e0.4 ≈ 1.49. 2%: odds 0.0204 → 0.0304, PD ≈ 2.95%. 40%: odds 0.667 → 0.995, PD ≈ 49.9%. Same multiplier on the odds, very different change in probability.

    3. Two features are correlated at 0.95, and there are no others. What is each one's VIF, and how much wider are their standard errors?

    VIF = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.95²) = 1 ÷ 0.0975 ≈ 10.3. Standard errors are √10.3 ≈ 3.2 times wider than with uncorrelated features.

    4. A Poisson model of monthly fraud alerts per merchant has a coefficient of 0.1 on “number of card terminals”. Interpret it. What's missing if merchants were observed for different numbers of months?

    Each extra terminal multiplies the expected alert count by e0.1 ≈ 1.105, about 10.5% more, holding the other features fixed. With unequal observation periods the model needs an offset of ln(months observed), or it partly learns which merchants were watched longer.

    5. A scorecard was built when the bad rate was 4%. The bad rate is now 6% at every score in the same proportion. How far has the score scale drifted?

    Odds of bad go from 0.04/0.96 ≈ 0.0417 to 0.06/0.94 ≈ 0.0638, a factor of about 1.53. In log-odds that's ln 1.53 ≈ 0.43, which is 0.43 × 28.85 ≈ 12 points. Every score now overstates safety by about 12 points. Recalibrate the intercept, or move the cut-off up by about 12 points; the ranking is unchanged.

    6. In ridge regression, what happens as λ goes to zero, and as it goes to infinity?

    As λ → 0 it becomes ordinary least squares, with all its variance. As λ → ∞ every penalised coefficient shrinks to zero and the model predicts the average. Cross-validation picks the point in between with the best out-of-sample error.

    7. Why is minimising squared error the same as maximum likelihood, and what does that tell you about outliers?

    If the noise is Gaussian with constant variance, the negative log-likelihood is the sum of squared residuals divided by 2σ², plus a constant. So the best-fitting line under that assumption is the least-squares line. Gaussian tails are thin, so a big residual is treated as nearly impossible and the fit bends to shrink it. Outliers get far more influence than they deserve.
