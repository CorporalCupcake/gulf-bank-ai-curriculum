    ML1.1 Day one About 10 minutes

    Regression: Linear, Logistic and GLMs

    Part 1

    What regression is

    In one sentenceRegression predicts an outcome as a weighted sum of features, where each weight says how much one feature moves the answer.

    Our running example is house prices. Bigger houses cost more, so we draw the straight line that best fits past sales and read new prices off it.

    The line says "price = a starting amount + £3,000 for every extra square metre". The starting amount is the intercept. The £3,000 is the coefficient (the weight) on floor area. Add more features, such as bedrooms and distance to the station, and each gets its own coefficient.

    How it differs from other models

    • Linear regression predicts a number: a price, a score, a sales figure.
    • Logistic regression predicts a yes/no probability, such as "will this house sell within a month?". Despite the name, it is used for classification.
    • A GLM (generalised linear model) is the family that holds both, plus models for counts and positive amounts. Same weighted sum, different rule for turning it into an answer.
    • Trees and boosting draw boxes and steps rather than one straight line. They find "only if" patterns on their own; regression needs you to add them.
    Part 2

    How linear regression learns

    1. Start with any line. Any intercept and any £-per-m² will do.
    2. Measure each miss. A miss (or residual) is the real price minus the line's price, shown as the red tick in the picture.
    3. Square the misses and add them up. Squaring makes every miss positive and makes big misses count much more.
    4. Move the line to make that total as small as possible. This is called least squares, and it has a direct answer, so no trial and error is needed.

    How to read it

    • A coefficient is the change in the prediction for one more unit of that feature, with the other features held fixed. "£3,000 per m²" means two houses identical except for 10 m² differ by £30,000.
    • Standard error says how uncertain a coefficient is. £3,000 ± £500 is a clear effect. £3,000 ± £4,000 could be zero.
    • R² is the share of the spread in prices the model explains. 0 means no better than guessing the average price, 1 means perfect. On new data it can even go negative.
    • RMSE is the typical miss in pounds. "RMSE £25,000" means predictions are usually within about £25,000.
    • Residuals plot. Plot the misses against predictions. A curve means the true pattern bends. A funnel (misses widening for dearer houses) means the spread grows with the price.

    One extreme house can drag the line. Because misses are squared, a single mansion priced oddly pulls the whole line towards it. Check for outliers before trusting a fit.

    Correlated features share credit unpredictably. Floor area and number of rooms move together. The model can tell their combined effect, but not who deserves the credit, so individual coefficients can swing between samples while predictions stay fine.

    Part 3

    Logistic regression and GLMs

    In one sentenceLogistic regression takes the same weighted sum and squeezes it through an S-shaped curve, so the answer is always a probability between 0 and 1.

    Say we now predict "sells within a month?" A straight line would predict 130% for some houses and −20% for others. The S-curve cannot.

    Each dot is a past house: sold within a month (1) or not (0). The S-curve gives the chance of a sale.

    How to read it: odds, not points

    • Odds are chance of yes divided by chance of no. A 50% chance is odds of 1 to 1. A 20% chance is odds of 1 to 4.
    • An odds ratio multiplies the odds. An odds ratio of 2 doubles them.
    • The effect on probability depends on where you start. Doubling the odds takes a 5% chance to about 9%, but a 40% chance to about 57%. Never say "adds 10 points" without a starting point.
    • "Twice as likely" is only roughly right when the outcome is rare.

    The GLM idea

    A GLM has three parts: the weighted sum, a link that turns it into a sensible answer, and a distribution describing how real outcomes scatter around that answer.

    OutcomeExampleGLM
    Any numberSale priceLinear (identity link)
    Yes / noSold in a month?Logistic (logit link)
    Count of eventsViewings per weekPoisson (log link)
    Positive, skewed amountRepair costGamma (log link)

    With a log link, a coefficient multiplies the expected value rather than adding to it. For counts, real data usually varies more than Poisson allows. If so, coefficients may be fine but standard errors are too small, so use a negative binomial model instead.

    Part 4

    Which one to use

    ModelPredictsStrong atWeak at
    Linear regressionA numberReadable, quick, few data neededBends and "only if" effects
    Logistic regressionA probabilityHonest probabilities, readable, auditableNeeds interactions added by hand
    Other GLMsCounts, amountsFits the shape of the outcomeSame straight-line limits
    Boosted treesAnythingFinds interactions on its ownHarder to explain and audit

    Regression is strong at:

    • Small and medium tables. Few parameters means it generalises from modest data.
    • Explanation. Each coefficient is one feature's effect, with a standard error. Anyone can recompute a prediction by hand.
    • A baseline. Fit it first; any fancier model must beat it to justify itself.

    It is weak at:

    • Bends and interactions unless you add them: transform a feature (use its log), add a product of two features, or fit separate models for segments.
    • Text, images and sound, which need learned features.
    • Many correlated features with little data, where coefficients turn to noise. A penalty helps (see Bias, Variance and Overfitting).
    Show the formulas

    Linear: price = intercept + (weight × floor area) + (weight × bedrooms) + …, with the weights chosen to minimise the sum of squared misses.

    Logistic: the same sum gives the log-odds. Probability = 1 ÷ (1 + e−sum), which is the S-curve.

    In banking, logistic regression on binned inputs is the standard credit scorecard because regulators can follow every point, and linear and Poisson models are the usual first choice for balances and claim counts.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading coefficients as causes

      A coefficient describes a pattern in past data with the other features held fixed. It does not prove that adding 10 m² raises a price by £30,000.

    • Trusting R² on training data

      Adding any feature, even random noise, never lowers training R². Judge the model on data it has not seen.

    • Saying "twice as likely" for an odds ratio of 2

      That is only true for rare outcomes. State the starting probability.

    • Predicting far outside the data

      A line keeps going, but the world may not. A price model fitted on flats is not safe for castles.

    1. In the house example, what does a coefficient of £3,000 per m² mean?

    Two houses identical in every other feature, with one 1 m² larger, are predicted to differ by £3,000.

    2. Why can a straight line not predict a yes/no probability?

    It can go below 0 or above 1. Logistic regression squeezes the sum through an S-curve to stay between 0 and 1.

    3. A feature has an odds ratio of 2. The base chance is 40%. What is the new chance?

    Odds go from 2 to 3 up to 4 to 3, so about 57%.

    4. Which GLM would you use for the number of viewings a house gets per week?

    Poisson, with a log link. If the counts vary more than Poisson allows, use a negative binomial.

    5. Floor area and rooms are strongly correlated. What happens to their coefficients?

    Predictions stay fine, but each coefficient becomes unstable and can change a lot between samples.
