    Staff Copilots and Reconciliation

    Part 1

    What they are

    In one sentenceA staff copilot and a reconciliation engine both do the easy bulk of a job and hand the hard remainder to a person, with enough context to act fast.

    Two running examples. Noura, a relationship manager, needs a credit exception. She finds three similarly named policies and spends twenty minutes working out which is current. A copilot finds the policy, summarises it and drafts her case note. Hamad, in finance operations, spends his day clicking "match" on ledger items that obviously match. A reconciliation engine clears those and routes the rest.

    How they differ from similar things

    • A copilot is not a search box. It writes an answer and drafts text. So its risk is retrieval (the wrong version, or a document the user must not see), not eloquence.
    • A copilot is not a customer chatbot. Its users are staff, and it reads internal documents and customer data.
    • Reconciliation checks that two records of the same money agree, like checking your bank statement against your receipts: ledger against a correspondent's statement, or card settlement files against internal postings.
    Part 2

    How they work

    The copilot

    It is usually retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): fetch relevant passages, then let a language model answer from them, with a chat front end.

    1. Filter first. Keep only documents the asker may open and that are in force now.
    2. Retrieve. Rank the remaining passages by similarity to the question.
    3. Answer with a citation. Quote the clause so the user can open it.
    4. Decline if the match is weak. "I can't find this in the documents you can access. The owner is Credit Policy."

    Why the filters matter: an old policy often matches the question as well as the new one, because similarity cannot tell which is in force. Add status and effective-date tags. Enforce access rights at query time: text already in the prompt can be coaxed out. Carry the title and version into every chunk, since "the limit is AED 500,000" means nothing without product and year.

    The reconciliation engine

    It matches in tiers, strict to loose. Each tier handles what the one before could not.

    1. Exact. Same reference, amount, currency and date. The large majority. Very low risk.
    2. Tolerance. Amount and date within a small gap. Look-alike items can be paired wrongly.
    3. Many-to-one. One statement line against a group of items that sum to it. Coincidental groupings are the risk.
    4. Learned or fuzzy. A model scores candidate pairs from narratives and history. Match only above a high threshold, otherwise suggest.
    5. Exception. Everything left: classify the cause, assign an owner, start an ageing clock.
    Part 3

    How to read the results

    Count three things, never one: work removed, errors let through, and work pushed to people.

    • Time saved is net of checking. A draft in 30 seconds that takes 10 minutes to check saves almost nothing. Add gathering, review and the rework of unusable drafts.
    • Review time is not waste. It is what catches errors. Shortening it raises savings and escaped errors together.
    • Automation rate is a vanity number. "98% auto-matched" says nothing about how many were wrong. A wrongly cleared item looks fine and hides a real break. An exception sent to a person is merely a cost.
    • Error rate comes from a sample. Audit 300 random auto-matches and find 6 wrong: the sample says 2%, but the true rate is roughly 0.4% to 3.6%. Report the range.

    The trade-off is the same as in the previous lesson. Loosening tolerance auto-matches more but pairs more items with the wrong partner. Tightening gives people more work, but every exception is visible.

    Show the evidence on copilots

    An assistant raised support agents' output by about 14% (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2023). But consultants were more likely to be wrong on a task outside the tool's capability (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023). Value depends on which tasks you point it at.

    Part 4

    Design rules and what to use

    JobBest toolMain riskWho checks
    Policy searchCopilot with citationsStale version; document the user may not seeThe user, by opening the clause
    DraftingCopilotInvented facts; promises the bank would not makeThe author
    Obvious matchesPlain rules (tiers 1 and 2)Look-alike itemsMonthly sample audit
    Messy matchesLearned matcherOverconfident scoresPerson, below the threshold
    • Ambiguity goes to a person, not a tie-break. "Pick the closest" quietly creates hidden breaks, for example two identical recurring payments paired wrongly so that two real differences cancel out. List the candidates in an exception instead.
    • Exceptions need a cause, an owner, an age and evidence. A language model can summarise and suggest a cause. A person with authority approves the journal, write-off or recall. A break that looks like unauthorised activity goes to fraud or anti-money-laundering (AML), not closed in finance.
    • Run it responsibly. Put a gateway in front that logs and redacts customer identifiers, decide hosting with risk and the regulator, and test on 100 to 300 real staff questions labelled with the right source.
    • Prompt injection: a pasted email or PDF can carry hidden instructions. Use read-only tools and require approval for anything outbound.

    In banking, these are the staff-facing tools with the most users and the least glamour. The aim is fewer wasted minutes without more hidden errors.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Access applied to the app, not the user

      A service account that reads everything lets everyone ask about everything. Test as a junior user asking about an HR case: the right reply is "not available to you".

    • Fluent drafts nobody checks

      Busy reviewers approve in seconds. Seed known-wrong drafts into the queue. If they are approved, the control is not working.

    • Reporting automation, not errors

      Ask for the audited error rate by tier, and the age of open exceptions.

    • Answering from a superseded policy

      Ask about a policy you know changed recently and check which version is cited.

    1. Why can an old policy outrank the current one, and what is the fix?

    Similarity cannot tell which version is in force. Add status and effective-date tags and filter before ranking.

    2. Why enforce access at retrieval rather than ask the model to hide content?

    Anything retrieved enters the prompt and can be coaxed out. Never put text there that the user could not open.

    3. The best passage a teller may see scores 0.41 on a credit question. What should the assistant say?

    That it cannot find the answer in documents available to the user, and who owns the topic. A weak match risks a confident wrong rule.

    4. A matching engine reports 98% automation. What do you ask next?

    The audited error rate on a random sample by tier, how ambiguous candidates are handled, and how old the open exceptions are.

    5. What stays with a person in an exception workflow?

    Approving journals, write-offs and recalls, and judging suspected fraud. The model reads narratives, proposes a cause and summarises evidence.
