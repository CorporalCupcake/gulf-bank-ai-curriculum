    Model Risk: What Counts as a Model

    Part 1

    What a model is, and what model risk is

    In one sentenceA model is anything that turns inputs into a number using assumptions, where a decision rests on that number. Model risk is the harm done when that number is wrong or used for the wrong job.

    Take a relationship manager's spreadsheet. It takes salary and debt, multiplies salary by 4.5, and suggests a credit-card limit. Nobody calls it a model. But it has inputs, an assumption (the 4.5) and an output that moves money. It is a model.

    Now picture that sheet spreading from one branch to forty. It has no owner and no documentation, and the person who chose 4.5 has left. Then an auditor asks, "which models drive credit decisions?" and the risk team's list does not include it. You cannot govern what you have not listed.

    This follows the standard definition in US supervisory guidance (SR 11-7), which many banks use as their reference. Model risk comes from two sources:

    • Wrong: bad data, bad assumptions or bad code.
    • Misused: a sound model applied to a purpose it was never built for.
    Part 2

    What is in, and what is out

    Format and size do not matter. A spreadsheet, a line of SQL, a vendor black box and a neural network can all be models. Here are the cases people argue about.

    A salary × 4.5 sheet
    In. It has an assumption and drives a limit.
    A rule that flags payments over a set amount as risky
    In. A rule that estimates risk counts, even with no machine learning.
    A vendor's sanctions matcher
    In. Buying a model moves the code to someone else, but the accountability stays with the bank.
    An FX-rate lookup or a report that sums balances
    Out. No assumptions and no estimate.
    An LLM that tags complaints as "fraud allegation"
    Argued openly, because it produces text, not an estimate. If it drives routing, it behaves like a model. Many banks list such tools as "AI systems" and govern them in a similar way.
    The consequence testIf it were wrong or misused, would a decision go wrong in a way that costs money, customers or compliance? If yes, treat it as a model until someone senior says otherwise in writing.
    Part 3

    How the governance works

    Banks build four things around the definition. Each is a plain control.

    1. Inventory. A register of every model, kept up to date. This is the first control, because every other control attaches to something on the list.
    2. Tier. Each model gets a materiality tier, because effort is finite. A model setting billions in provisions needs far more scrutiny than one forecasting branch queues.
    3. Lifecycle with gates. A fixed route (build, validate, approve, run, monitor, retire). At each gate, someone other than the builder asks, "is this fit for its intended use?"
    4. Change control. Most incidents follow a change: a retrain, a new feature, a vendor update, a new customer segment. Each change is sized as minor or major, and major ones go back through validation.

    What the inventory records

    • Name, ID and version. Controls attach to a version.
    • Owner, developer and users. A named person is accountable. The validator must be someone else.
    • Intended use and limits. For example, "initial limits for salaried residents, AED 3,000 to 80,000, not self-employed". Misuse is half of model risk, so this field matters.
    • Method and inputs. This tells the validator what to test.
    • Tier, status and dates. Live since, last validated, next review. An overdue validation is itself a finding.

    The three lines

    • First line: builds and uses the model.
    • Second line: independent validation challenges it. "Effective challenge" means informed people who can force changes. A validator who cannot say no is decoration.
    • Third line: internal audit checks that the whole process works.
    Part 4

    How to read a tier

    A tier combines two things: exposure (how much money or how many customers the decisions touch) and risk factors (regulatory reporting, automated customer decisions, a vendor black box, a new method, thin data). Every bank writes its own scale. These readings are illustrative.

    Tier 1
    Large exposure or heavy regulatory use, such as provisions or capital. Full independent validation and a yearly review.
    Tier 4
    Small exposure, internal use only. A light check and a longer review cycle.
    A pilot on AED 2m (Tier 4) scaled to AED 800m
    The tier is now wrong. Growth in volume or use should trigger a re-tier.
    A modest-exposure model that is vendor-built, customer-facing and regulatory
    Can still land in a high tier. Risk factors push it up.

    A tier is a human judgement, supported by a score.

    Strong at: the framework is simple, and it works for spreadsheets, rules, ML and vendor tools alike.

    Weak at: it depends on people reporting what they use. Banks run attestations ("list every tool that produces a score, forecast or flag") and cross-check procurement records to find what is missing.

    In banking, the inventory is the first thing an auditor or regulator asks for, and it is the quickest way to find where AI is already making decisions.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Shadow models

      Spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor tools that decide things but were never registered. Ask each business line, "what produces a score, forecast or flag that someone acts on?" and compare with the inventory.

    • "It's just a rule" or "just a vendor tool"

      Arguing the definition narrowly to avoid review. Apply the consequence test, and record any exclusion with a name and a reason.

    • Tier or intended use drifts

      A retail scorecard is quietly used for small-business lending, or a pilot grows with pilot-level controls. Make growth a re-tiering trigger.

    • Zombie models

      A retired model still runs in a nightly batch because something reads it. Trace dependents before retiring anything.

    1. What is the three-part test for a model? Is a sheet that totals daily branch cash one?

    Inputs go in, assumptions transform them, and an estimate comes out that supports a decision. A sheet that only adds balances has no assumption, so it is arithmetic, not a model.

    2. What are the two sources of model risk?

    Errors (data, assumptions, code) and misuse (a sound model used for the wrong purpose). The lifecycle gates guard against the first, and the recorded intended use guards against the second.

    3. Why is the inventory called the first control?

    Every other control attaches to a registered model. An unlisted model has no owner, tier, validation or retirement date.

    4. A vendor ships a new version of its sanctions matcher. What do you do?

    Treat it as a change to an inventoried model. Size it as minor or major, test it against the old version, record the decision and monitor after go-live.

    5. Why must the validator be separate from the developer?

    Independence removes the pressure to approve. Effective challenge adds the skill to find real flaws and the standing to block approval.
