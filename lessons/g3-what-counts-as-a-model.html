    What Counts as a Model: The Inventory and the Governed Lifecycle

    The first question a governance team asks is "what do we have?". Why a spreadsheet can be a model and a chatbot is a grey area, how an inventory and a risk tier decide how much scrutiny each model gets, and the lifecycle every serious model walks through from idea to retirement.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModel risk is the harm from wrong or misused model outputs, so a bank first lists every model it runs (the inventory), ranks each by how much damage it could do (the tier), and then makes each one pass the same gates of build, independent challenge, approval, monitoring and retirement, with scrutiny proportional to the tier.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager in the retail division builds a workbook. It takes a customer's salary, existing debt and years at the employer, and returns a suggested credit-card limit. Over three years it spreads from one branch to forty. Nobody designed it as a system. It has no owner on paper, no documentation, and its multiplier of 4.5 times salary was typed in by someone who has since left.

    Then the numbers go wrong. Salaries in one segment are overstated at onboarding, and the workbook happily lends against them. When an auditor asks "which models do you use for credit decisions?", the model risk team's list does not include it. They never knew it existed.

    This is the story model risk management is built to prevent. The workbook is a model in the sense that matters: it takes inputs, applies assumptions, and produces a number that drives a decision with money attached. How sophisticated it is does not matter. What matters is whether anyone with the job of challenging it knows it exists.

    So the field starts from two ideas, in this order.

    • You cannot govern what you have not listed. The inventory is the first control. A model missing from the inventory has no owner, no validation, no monitoring and no retirement date.
    • Not every model deserves the same scrutiny. A model that sets provisions worth billions of dirhams and a model that forecasts branch queue lengths are both models. Treating them the same wastes effort on one and under-protects the other. So each model gets a tier, and the tier decides the depth of everything that follows.

    After that comes the lifecycle: a fixed route every model travels, with a gate at each step where someone other than the builder asks "is this fit for the use we intend?". The route is old (the US supervisors' SR 11-7 guidance dates from 2011) and every major regulator's version looks similar. What changes is how it is applied to machine learning and, now, to language models.

    Hold onto thisModel risk comes from two places: the model being wrong (bad data, bad assumptions, bad code) and the model being misused (right for one purpose, applied to another). The lifecycle guards against the first. The inventory and its recorded "intended use" guard against the second.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The definition, and why it is wider than you expect

    The most quoted definition comes from the US interagency guidance SR 11-7 (Federal Reserve) and OCC Bulletin 2011-12: a model is a quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates. The same guidance says model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model outputs.

    Three parts, each with a test you can run in a meeting.

    • Input data goes in. If nothing is fed in, it is not a model.
    • A method or assumptions transform it. A coefficient, a threshold, a trained network, a rule. This is what separates a model from arithmetic. Adding up balances applies no assumption; multiplying salary by 4.5 does.
    • A quantitative estimate comes out, and a decision rests on it. A score, a probability, a forecast, a label, a flag, an amount.

    The consequences surprise people. Format is irrelevant: a spreadsheet, a SQL query, a Python notebook and a vendor black box can all be models. Simplicity is irrelevant: a one-line formula that sets credit limits is a model. Rules count: if "flag deposits above a threshold" estimates risk, many banks govern it as a model, which is why transaction-monitoring rule sets show up in inventories (see rules versus ML). And vendor models count: buying a system moves the code to someone else, but the risk and the accountability stay with the bank.

    Where does it stop? On the other side are things that look technical but are not models: a lookup table of FX rates from a market feed (that is data), a report that sums and averages, a workflow engine. The grey zone is large and deliberately left to each bank's policy. The question to ask of any candidate is: if this were wrong or used for the wrong purpose, would a decision go wrong in a way that costs money, customers or compliance? If yes, it belongs in scope until someone senior decides otherwise in writing.

    A note on generative AI. Whether a large language model application is a "model" under the classic definition is argued openly. It produces text, not a quantitative estimate. Yet it can drive decisions: classifying a complaint as "fraud allegation", drafting a credit memo, or answering an agent's policy question. Sources I found disagree on whether the revised US guidance covers it; the practical stance in many banks is to bring such systems into the inventory as AI systems and govern them with a model-risk-like process even where the formal definition is unsettled. Part 4 returns to this.

    The model inventory

    The inventory is a register, not a spreadsheet of names. For each model it records enough that a stranger could find the owner, understand the purpose, and know what controls are due. A usable record has at least these fields.

    Two hard problems keep inventories honest or dishonest. Discovery: models hide in spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor products, so banks run attestations ("list every tool you use that produces a score, forecast or flag") and cross-check against procurement and data-platform logs. Granularity: is a model with five regional variants one entry or five? A common rule, which the CBUAE's Model Management Standards appear to follow, is that a component that is separately validated or approved should be recorded as its own model. Check the exact wording in the standards before quoting it.

    Materiality: how much scrutiny does this model deserve?

    Effort is finite. A bank with 400 models cannot give each a six-month independent validation. So each model is tiered by materiality, which combines two things. Exposure: how much money or how many customers the decisions touch. Purpose and uncertainty: whether it feeds regulatory or financial reporting, makes customer-facing automated decisions, relies on a vendor black box, uses a novel method, or has thin data. The guidance reportedly replacing SR 11-7 in the US, SR 26-2, is described by secondary sources as defining materiality by exactly these two factors, exposure and purpose, and scaling oversight to it.

    The tier drives what happens next: depth of validation, how often it is revisited, who must approve it, how closely it is monitored. High-tier models get full independent validation and annual review. Low-tier models might get a light check and review only when something changes. The mapping below is illustrative: every bank writes its own scoring, and none of it comes from a regulation.

    Two cautions. First, tier is a judgement made by people, with a score as support. Governance committees override scores in both directions. Second, the tier can change. A pilot that touches AED 2m is Tier 4. When it scales to the whole book, the tier must be re-assessed or the controls will lag behind the risk.

    The governed lifecycle

    SR 11-7 does not give a numbered lifecycle. It describes model development, implementation and use; validation; and governance, policies and controls. Banks turn that into a staged route. The exact names vary, but the shape is stable.

    Four ideas hold the lifecycle together.

    • Separation of roles. The people who build a model do not approve it. The usual "three lines" arrangement: first line owns and uses the model and develops it; the second line (model risk, independent validation) challenges it; the third line (internal audit) checks that the whole process works. Developers' pride and deadlines are real pressures, so the structure removes the conflict instead of trusting good intentions.
    • Effective challenge. SR 11-7's term for critical analysis by objective, informed people who can identify limitations and push for changes. Challenge is only effective if the challenger has competence, influence and independence. A validator who cannot say no is decoration. G3.3 covers how validators actually work.
    • Approval comes with conditions. A model is approved for a use, often with restrictions: "not for self-employed customers", "overlay of +10% until the new data arrives", "re-validate in 12 months". These conditions are recorded in the inventory and monitored.
    • Change is the main danger. Most incidents come after approval: a retrain, a new feature, a vendor update, a new customer segment. So the lifecycle is a loop, and every change is triaged by size. The sim below shows which stages a change sends you back through.

    Monitoring cadence: how long does a broken model run unnoticed?

    Once a model is live, its performance decays at different speeds. Customers change, fraud tactics adapt, a data feed drifts (F7.7 covers the statistics). Monitoring exists to catch the moment a model leaves its approved performance range. G3.5 covers it in depth; here is only the lifecycle point: the interval between checks sets how long a broken model can run before anyone knows. If you check once a year, a model that breaks right after the check runs broken for nearly a year.

    The last lifecycle stage gets neglected: retirement. A model nobody uses still sits in a pipeline, still feeds a report, still shows up in the inventory as "active". Retirement needs a decision, a date, a check that nothing downstream still depends on it, and an archive of the documents, because regulators can ask about decisions made years ago. Dependencies in the inventory make this check possible.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit. Scorecards, PD/LGD/EAD, IFRS 9 expected-credit-loss models and their overlays. Tier 1 almost everywhere: they drive provisions and capital.
    • Fraud and AML. Card-fraud scores, mule-account detection, monitoring rules, screening. Customer-facing and regulatory; vendor products are common.
    • Treasury and finance. Liquidity and ALM forecasts, valuation models, stress-testing. Regulatory use, limited data for rare events.
    • Marketing and operations. Churn, next-best-offer, queue forecasting. Often Tier 3 or 4, but they can touch customers, so check for fairness and consent.
    • Generative AI. Staff copilots, complaint triage, credit-memo drafting. Often arrive via a business unit's purchase rather than the data-science team, which is how they miss the inventory.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core logic has been stable for 15 years. What is in motion is the scope (does it cover AI and language models?), proportionality (how much less scrutiny for low-risk models?) and who the rules are written for. 2026 saw the biggest change to the US text since 2011.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most governance failures are not exotic. They are gaps between the process on paper and what people actually do. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Shadow models

      Spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor tools that make decisions but were never registered. The credit-limit workbook in Part 1.

      Spot itAsk each business line "what produces a score, forecast or flag that someone acts on?" and compare the answers with the inventory. Cross-check against shared-drive and data-platform usage.
    • "It's just a rule" or "it's just a vendor tool"

      The definition is argued narrowly to avoid review. Rules that estimate risk and bought systems still drive decisions.

      Spot itApply the consequence test: if wrong, does a decision go wrong? If yes, record the decision to exclude it, with a name and a reason.
    • Tier set once and never revisited

      A pilot at AED 2m becomes the production system for AED 800m. The controls still match the pilot.

      Spot itCompare current volumes and uses with those recorded at tiering. Make growth a trigger for re-tiering.
    • Intended use drifts

      A retail scorecard quietly used for small-business lending because "the process is similar".

      Spot itRead the "intended use and limits" field, then look at who actually calls the model and for which segments.
    • Validation that rubber-stamps

      The validator reports to the same executive as the developer, has no authority to block, or only reads the documents.

      Spot itAsk how many models in the last two years were sent back or restricted. If none, challenge is probably not effective.
    • Approval conditions that nobody tracks

      "Restricted to existing customers" is in a committee minute, not in the system.

      Spot itPick three approved models and ask where each condition is enforced in code or process.
    • Unreviewed change

      A retrain, a new feature or a vendor patch goes live without anyone deciding if it is minor.

      Spot itCompare the model version in production with the version in the inventory. Mismatches are the finding.
    • Governance theatre for LLM tools

      A copilot gets a checklist but no evaluation set, no logging and no owner, so the paper trail hides an ungoverned system.

      Spot itAsk for the test set used before approval, the logs of real use, and the person who can switch it off.
    • Zombie models

      Retired in theory, still running in a nightly batch because something downstream reads the output.

      Spot itBefore retiring, trace dependents in the inventory and the pipeline. After retiring, check that no job still calls it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Any tool that turns data into a number we act on can hurt us if it is wrong, whether it is a machine-learning system, a spreadsheet or a vendor product. So we keep one list of all of them, rank each by how much damage it could do, and give the risky ones independent review before launch and regular checks after. The first step is the list. I'd start by finding the models nobody has registered, because those are the ones with no owner.”
    To a model risk validator or internal auditor
    “I'd register it with a named owner, a written intended use and limits, its data lineage and its dependencies, then tier it by exposure and purpose. Validation depth follows the tier, done by someone independent of the team that built it. Approval is for the stated use with recorded conditions. Retrains inside agreed bounds take a fast path; new features, new segments and vendor upgrades go back through validation. Monitoring thresholds are set at approval so that breaches have a defined escalation.”
    “Is a spreadsheet a model?”

    If it takes input data, applies assumptions or a formula, and produces an estimate that drives a decision, yes. The tool does not matter. What matters is the risk if it is wrong. Spreadsheets are the most common shadow models, because they are quick to build and invisible to the inventory.

    “Do we need to put our ChatGPT-style staff assistant in the inventory?”

    I would, as an AI system, with an owner, a purpose, a risk rating and use restrictions, even if the narrow definition of model is arguable. Regulators' treatment of language-model tools is still moving, and reportedly the CBUAE's AI guidance expects an AI inventory. Having it listed costs little; being asked about it and not knowing it exists costs a lot. I would check the exact requirement with compliance.

    “Why does the churn model get less scrutiny than the fraud model?”

    Proportionality. Fraud scores are customer-facing, tied to regulatory expectations, and can cost millions if they fail. A churn model's errors cost a missed retention call. Tier decides depth. If the churn model starts to decide who gets offers or pricing, the tier is revisited and fairness checks come in.

    “We retrain every month. Do we validate every month?”

    No, and nobody expects that. The first approval should define the retraining process and its bounds: same features, same method, performance checks on a holdout, automated tests. Retrains within those bounds follow a fast path. A change outside them, such as a new feature or algorithm, returns to validation. The validator approves the process, not each run.

    “The vendor won't show us the model internals. How do we govern it?”

    The bank still owns the risk. We can test outcomes on our own data, benchmark against a simple challenger, require documentation and change notices in the contract, monitor the outputs, and set a use restriction. A fully opaque tool in a high-tier use should be a decision the risk committee signs knowingly. G3.6 goes deeper.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Give the three-part test for whether something is a model, and apply it to a sheet that totals daily branch cash.

    Inputs go in, a method or assumptions transform them, and a quantitative estimate comes out that supports a decision. A sheet that only adds up balances applies no assumption, so it is arithmetic, not a model. Multiplying salary by 4.5 to set a limit applies an assumption, so that is.

    2. What are the two sources of model risk, and which part of the framework addresses each?

    Errors in the model itself (data, assumptions, code) and misuse (applying a sound model to the wrong purpose). The lifecycle's development and validation gates mainly address the first. The recorded intended use and limits in the inventory, and monitoring of actual use, address the second.

    3. Why is the inventory called the first control?

    Every other control attaches to a registered model: owner, tier, validation, monitoring, review dates, retirement. A model not on the list has none of them. Completeness is also the first thing an auditor or supervisor tests.

    4. A pilot touches AED 2m and is Tier 4. Next year it covers AED 800m. What should happen?

    Re-tier. Exposure moves from one to three points in the scoring, which with any regulatory or customer-facing factor could mean Tier 2 or 1. Validation depth and review cadence follow the new tier. The control is a trigger: material growth in volume or use forces re-assessment.

    5. In Simulation 4 with no automated monitoring, why does the average delay come to about half the review interval for large intervals?

    Breaches begin at a roughly even spread through time, so on average they occur halfway between two reviews, and wait for the next one. For a 12-month interval that is about 6 months. Automated monthly monitoring caps it near half a month regardless of the interval.

    6. A vendor ships a new version of its sanctions matcher. What do you do?

    Treat it as a change to an inventoried model. Read the release notes, assess whether it is minor or major under policy, test on your own labelled name pairs against the old version, review false-positive and miss rates, record the decision and version in the inventory, and monitor after go-live. Contractual notice of changes should make this possible.

    7. Why must the validator be independent of the developer, and what does "effective challenge" add to independence?

    The developer has pressure and bias toward approval. Independence removes the conflict. Effective challenge also needs competence to find real flaws and the standing to force changes or block approval. Independence alone gives a reviewer who may not understand the model or be listened to.

    8. Is an LLM-based complaint classifier a model? What is the safe position?

    It produces a classification that drives routing and sometimes regulatory reporting, so in substance it behaves like a model even if the narrow definition is arguable. The safe position is to inventory it as an AI system, give it an owner and tier, test it on a labelled Arabic and English set, and monitor it. Confirm the formal status with compliance.
