    What Counts as a Model: The Inventory and the Governed Lifecycle

    The first question a governance team asks is "what do we have?". Why a spreadsheet can be a model and a chatbot is a grey area, how an inventory and a risk tier decide how much scrutiny each model gets, and the lifecycle every serious model walks through from idea to retirement.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModel risk is the harm from wrong or misused model outputs, so a bank first lists every model it runs (the inventory), ranks each by how much damage it could do (the tier), and then makes each one pass the same gates of build, independent challenge, approval, monitoring and retirement, with scrutiny proportional to the tier.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A retail relationship manager builds a workbook: salary, debt and years at employer in, suggested credit-card limit out. In three years it spreads from one branch to forty. It has no owner on paper, no documentation, and a 4.5-times-salary multiplier typed in by someone who has left.

    Then salaries in one segment turn out to be overstated at onboarding, and the workbook lends against them. An auditor asks "which models drive credit decisions?" and the model risk team's list does not include it. They never knew it existed.

    The workbook is a model in the sense that matters: inputs, assumptions, a number that drives a decision with money attached. Sophistication is irrelevant. What matters is whether anyone whose job is to challenge it knows it exists.

    So the field starts from two ideas, in order.

    • You cannot govern what you have not listed. Like a fire register, the inventory is the first control. An unlisted model has no owner, validation, monitoring or retirement date.
    • Not every model deserves the same scrutiny. A model setting billions in provisions and one forecasting branch queues are both models. Equal treatment wastes effort on one and under-protects the other. So each gets a tier, and the tier sets the depth of everything after.

    Then comes the lifecycle: a fixed route with a gate at each step where someone other than the builder asks "is this fit for the intended use?". The route dates from 2011 (US SR 11-7) and every major regulator's version looks similar. What changes is how it applies to machine learning and language models.

    Hold onto thisModel risk comes from a model being wrong (bad data, assumptions, code) or misused (right for one purpose, applied to another). The lifecycle guards against the first. The inventory's recorded intended use guards against the second.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The definition, and why it is wider than you expect

    The most quoted definition (US SR 11-7 and OCC 2011-12): a model is a quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates. Model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused outputs.

    Think of a recipe: ingredients in, a method applied, a dish out. Three tests:

    • Input data goes in. Nothing fed in, not a model.
    • Assumptions transform it (a coefficient, threshold, trained network or rule). This separates a model from arithmetic: adding balances applies no assumption; multiplying salary by 4.5 does.
    • A quantitative estimate comes out and a decision rests on it: a score, probability, forecast, label, flag or amount.

    Consequences: format is irrelevant (spreadsheet, SQL, notebook, vendor black box); simplicity is irrelevant (a one-line limit formula is a model); rules count when they estimate risk, which is why transaction-monitoring rule sets are inventoried (rules versus ML); vendor models count, because buying moves the code but not the accountability.

    Not models: an FX-rate lookup from a market feed (data), a summing report, a workflow engine. The grey zone is left to bank policy. The test for any candidate: if this were wrong or misused, would a decision go wrong in a way that costs money, customers or compliance? If yes, it is in scope until someone senior says otherwise in writing.

    Whether an LLM application is a "model" is argued openly: it produces text, not a quantitative estimate. Yet it can drive decisions, such as tagging a complaint "fraud allegation", drafting a credit memo or answering a policy question. Sources disagree on whether the revised US guidance covers it (SR 26-2's primary text puts generative and agentic AI out of scope). The practical stance in many banks is to inventory such systems as AI systems and govern them with a model-risk-like process.

    The model inventory

    The inventory is a register, not a list of names. A stranger should be able to find the owner, understand the purpose and see which controls are due. Minimum fields:

    Two problems decide whether an inventory is honest. Discovery: models hide in spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor products, so banks run attestations ("list every tool you use that produces a score, forecast or flag") and cross-check procurement and data-platform logs. Granularity: is a model with five regional variants one entry or five? A common rule, which the CBUAE Model Management Standards appear to follow, is that a separately validated or approved component is its own model. Check the exact wording before quoting.

    Materiality: how much scrutiny does this model deserve?

    Like airport security, effort is finite: a bank with 400 models cannot give each a six-month independent validation. Each model is tiered by materiality, which combines two things.

    • Exposure: how much money or how many customers the decisions touch.
    • Purpose and uncertainty: regulatory or financial reporting, customer-facing automated decisions, vendor black box, novel method, thin data.

    Commentators describe SR 26-2 (April 2026, replacing SR 11-7 in the US) as defining materiality by exactly these two factors. The tier then sets validation depth, review frequency, approver and monitoring intensity. High tiers get full independent validation and annual review; low tiers a light check, reviewed when something changes. The mapping below is illustrative: every bank writes its own, and none comes from a regulation.

    Two cautions. Tier is a human judgement with a score as support; committees override in both directions. Tier can change: a pilot on AED 2m is Tier 4, but when it scales to the whole book it must be re-assessed or controls lag the risk.

    The governed lifecycle

    SR 11-7 gives no numbered lifecycle, only development and use, validation, and governance. Banks turn that into a staged route. Names vary; the shape is stable.

    Four ideas hold the lifecycle together.

    • Separation of roles. Like a restaurant where the chef does not grade their own dish. First line builds and uses the model; second line (model risk, independent validation) challenges it; third line (internal audit) checks the whole process works. The structure removes the conflict instead of trusting good intentions.
    • Effective challenge. SR 11-7's term for critical analysis by objective, informed people who can identify limitations and push for changes. It needs competence, influence and independence. A validator who cannot say no is decoration (G3.3).
    • Approval comes with conditions. A model is approved for a use: "not for self-employed customers", "+10% overlay until new data arrives", "re-validate in 12 months". Conditions are recorded in the inventory and monitored.
    • Change is the main danger. Most incidents follow a retrain, new feature, vendor update or new segment. So the lifecycle is a loop and every change is triaged by size. The sim below shows what each change sends you back through.

    Monitoring cadence: how long does a broken model run unnoticed?

    Like a smoke alarm, monitoring only helps if it checks often enough. Performance decays as customers change, fraud adapts or a feed drifts (F7.7 covers the statistics; G3.5 covers monitoring in depth). The interval between checks is how long a broken model can run unnoticed. Check yearly, and a model that breaks just after a check runs broken for nearly a year.

    Retirement is the neglected last stage. An unused model still sits in a pipeline and shows as "active". Retiring needs a decision, a date, a check that nothing downstream depends on it (the inventory's dependencies field), and an archive of documents, because regulators can ask about decisions made years ago.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are gaps between the process on paper and what people do. Each comes with its quickest check.

    • Shadow models

      Spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor tools that decide things but were never registered. The credit-limit workbook in Part 1.

      Spot itAsk each business line "what produces a score, forecast or flag that someone acts on?" and compare with the inventory and shared-drive usage.
    • "It's just a rule" or "just a vendor tool"

      The definition is argued narrowly to avoid review. Risk-estimating rules and bought systems still drive decisions.

      Spot itApply the consequence test. If yes, any exclusion is a recorded decision with a name and a reason.
    • Tier set once, never revisited

      A pilot at AED 2m becomes the production system for AED 800m, with pilot-level controls.

      Spot itCompare current volumes and uses with those at tiering. Make growth a re-tiering trigger.
    • Intended use drifts

      A retail scorecard quietly used for small-business lending because "the process is similar".

      Spot itRead the intended-use field, then check who calls the model and for which segments.
    • Validation that rubber-stamps

      The validator reports to the developer's executive, cannot block, or only reads documents.

      Spot itAsk how many models were sent back or restricted in two years. None means challenge is probably not effective.
    • Approval conditions nobody tracks

      "Restricted to existing customers" sits in a committee minute, not in the system.

      Spot itPick three approved models and ask where each condition is enforced.
    • Unreviewed change

      A retrain, new feature or vendor patch goes live with nobody deciding if it is minor.

      Spot itCompare the production version with the inventory version. A mismatch is the finding.
    • Governance theatre for LLM tools

      A copilot gets a checklist but no evaluation set, logging or owner.

      Spot itAsk for the pre-approval test set, logs of real use, and who can switch it off.
    • Zombie models

      Retired on paper, still running in a nightly batch because something downstream reads it.

      Spot itTrace dependents before retiring; check no job still calls it afterwards.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Give the three-part test for whether something is a model, and apply it to a sheet that totals daily branch cash.

    Inputs go in, a method or assumptions transform them, a quantitative estimate comes out that supports a decision. A sheet that only adds balances applies no assumption: arithmetic, not a model. Multiplying salary by 4.5 to set a limit does apply one: a model.

    2. What are the two sources of model risk, and which part of the framework addresses each?

    Errors in the model (data, assumptions, code) and misuse (a sound model on the wrong purpose). Development and validation gates address the first. Recorded intended use and limits, plus monitoring of actual use, address the second.

    3. Why is the inventory called the first control?

    Every other control (owner, tier, validation, monitoring, review dates, retirement) attaches to a registered model. An unlisted model has none. Completeness is also the first thing auditors test.

    4. A pilot touches AED 2m and is Tier 4. Next year it covers AED 800m. What should happen?

    Re-tier. Exposure moves from one point to three, which with any regulatory or customer-facing factor could mean Tier 2 or 1. Validation depth and review cadence follow. Material growth in volume or use should trigger re-assessment.

    5. In Simulation 4 with no automated monitoring, why does the average delay come to about half the review interval for large intervals?

    Breaches start evenly through time, so on average they land halfway between two reviews and wait for the next. At 12 months that is about 6. Automated monthly monitoring caps it near half a month whatever the interval.

    6. A vendor ships a new version of its sanctions matcher. What do you do?

    Treat it as a change to an inventoried model. Read the release notes, classify it minor or major under policy, test on your own labelled name pairs against the old version, review false-positive and miss rates, record the decision and version, and monitor after go-live. Contractual change notices make this possible.

    7. Why must the validator be independent of the developer, and what does "effective challenge" add?

    Developers are pressured towards approval; independence removes that conflict. Effective challenge adds the competence to find real flaws and the standing to force changes or block approval. Independence alone can give a reviewer who does not understand the model or is not listened to.

    8. Is an LLM-based complaint classifier a model? What is the safe position?

    It produces a label that drives routing and sometimes regulatory reporting, so in substance it behaves like a model. Inventory it as an AI system, give it an owner and tier, test it on a labelled Arabic and English set, and monitor it. Confirm formal status with compliance.
