    What Counts as a Model: The Inventory and the Governed Lifecycle

    The first question a governance team asks is "what do we have?". What counts as a model, how an inventory and a risk tier set the scrutiny each one gets, and the lifecycle from idea to retirement.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModel risk is the harm from wrong or misused model outputs, so a bank lists every model (the inventory), ranks each by potential damage (the tier), and runs each through the same gates, with scrutiny proportional to the tier.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A retail relationship manager builds a workbook: salary, debt and years at employer in, suggested credit-card limit out. In three years it spreads from one branch to forty. It has no owner, no documentation, and a 4.5-times-salary multiplier typed in by someone who has left.

    Then salaries in one segment turn out to be overstated, and the workbook lends against them. An auditor asks "which models drive credit decisions?" and the model risk team's list does not include it. They never knew it existed.

    The workbook is a model in the sense that matters: inputs, assumptions, a number that drives a decision with money attached. So the field rests on two ideas.

    • You cannot govern what you have not listed. Like a fire register, the inventory is the first control.
    • Not every model deserves the same scrutiny. A model setting billions in provisions and one forecasting branch queues are both models. So each gets a tier, and the tier sets the depth of everything after.

    Then comes the lifecycle: a fixed route with a gate at each step where someone other than the builder asks "is this fit for the intended use?".

    Hold onto thisModel risk comes from a model being wrong (bad data, assumptions, code) or misused (right for one purpose, applied to another). The lifecycle guards against the first. The inventory's recorded intended use guards against the second.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The definition, and why it is wider than you expect

    The standard definition (US SR 11-7): a model is a quantitative method that applies assumptions to input data to produce quantitative estimates. Model risk is the harm from decisions based on incorrect or misused outputs. Think of a recipe: ingredients in, method applied, dish out. Three tests:

    • Input data goes in.
    • Assumptions transform it (a coefficient, threshold, trained network or rule). Adding balances applies no assumption; multiplying salary by 4.5 does.
    • A quantitative estimate comes out and a decision rests on it: a score, forecast, label, flag or amount.

    So format and simplicity are irrelevant (spreadsheet, SQL, vendor black box, a one-line formula). Rules count when they estimate risk (rules versus ML), and vendor models count, because buying moves the code but not the accountability. Not models: an FX-rate lookup, a summing report, a workflow engine.

    The test for any candidate: if this were wrong or misused, would a decision go wrong in a way that costs money, customers or compliance? If yes, it is in scope until someone senior says otherwise in writing.

    Whether an LLM application is a "model" is argued openly: it produces text, not an estimate. Yet it can drive decisions, such as tagging a complaint "fraud allegation". Many banks inventory such systems as AI systems and govern them with a model-risk-like process.

    The model inventory

    The inventory is a register, not a list of names. A stranger should be able to find the owner, the purpose and the controls due. Key fields:

    Two problems decide whether an inventory is honest. Discovery: models hide in spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor products, so banks run attestations ("list every tool you use that produces a score, forecast or flag") and cross-check procurement logs. Granularity: is a model with five regional variants one entry or five? Commonly, a separately validated component is its own model.

    Materiality: how much scrutiny does this model deserve?

    Like airport security, effort is finite: a bank with 400 models cannot give each a six-month independent validation. Each model is tiered by materiality, which combines two things.

    • Exposure: how much money or how many customers the decisions touch.
    • Purpose and uncertainty: regulatory reporting, customer-facing automated decisions, vendor black box, novel method, thin data.

    The tier then sets validation depth, review frequency and monitoring. High tiers get full independent validation and annual review; low tiers a light check. The mapping below is illustrative: every bank writes its own.

    Tier is a human judgement with a score as support, and committees override in both directions. It can also change: a pilot on AED 2m is Tier 4, but when it scales to the whole book it must be re-assessed.

    The governed lifecycle

    Banks turn the regulatory guidance into a staged route, with a gate at each step. Names vary; the shape is stable.

    • Separation of roles. Like a restaurant where the chef does not grade their own dish. First line builds and uses the model; second line (independent validation) challenges it; third line (internal audit) checks the whole process works.
    • Effective challenge. Critical analysis by objective, informed people who can push for changes. A validator who cannot say no is decoration (G3.3).
    • Approval comes with conditions. A model is approved for a use: "not for self-employed customers", "re-validate in 12 months". Conditions are recorded in the inventory and monitored.

    Change is the main danger. Most incidents follow a retrain, new feature, vendor update or new segment, so every change is triaged by size. The sim below shows what each change sends you back through.

    Monitoring cadence: how long does a broken model run unnoticed?

    Like a smoke alarm, monitoring only helps if it checks often enough. Performance decays as customers change, fraud adapts or a feed drifts (G3.5 covers monitoring in depth). The interval between checks is how long a broken model can run unnoticed. Check yearly, and a model that breaks just after a check runs broken for nearly a year.

    Retirement is the neglected last stage. Retiring needs a decision, a date, a check that nothing downstream depends on the model, and an archive of documents, because regulators can ask about decisions made years ago.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are gaps between the process on paper and what people do. Each comes with its quickest check.

    • Shadow models

      Spreadsheets, notebooks and vendor tools that decide things but were never registered. The credit-limit workbook in Part 1.

      Spot itAsk each business line "what produces a score, forecast or flag that someone acts on?" and compare with the inventory.
    • "It's just a rule" or "just a vendor tool"

      The definition is argued narrowly to avoid review. Risk-estimating rules and bought systems still drive decisions.

      Spot itApply the consequence test. Any exclusion should be a recorded decision with a name and a reason.
    • Tier or intended use drifts

      A pilot at AED 2m becomes a AED 800m production system with pilot-level controls, or a retail scorecard is quietly used for small-business lending.

      Spot itCompare current volumes and users with those at tiering and in the intended-use field. Make growth a re-tiering trigger.
    • Validation that rubber-stamps

      The validator reports to the developer's executive, cannot block, or only reads documents.

      Spot itAsk how many models were sent back or restricted in two years. None means challenge is probably not effective.
    • Unreviewed change or untracked conditions

      A retrain or vendor patch goes live with nobody deciding if it is minor, or "restricted to existing customers" sits in a committee minute, not in the system.

      Spot itCompare the production version with the inventory version, and ask where each approval condition is enforced.
    • Zombie models and theatre for LLM tools

      A retired model still runs in a nightly batch because something reads it. A copilot gets a checklist but no evaluation set, logging or owner.

      Spot itTrace dependents before retiring. For LLM tools, ask for the test set, logs of real use, and who can switch it off.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What is the three-part test for whether something is a model? Apply it to a sheet that totals daily branch cash.

    Inputs go in, assumptions transform them, a quantitative estimate comes out that supports a decision. A sheet that only adds balances applies no assumption, so it is arithmetic. Multiplying salary by 4.5 to set a limit does apply one, so that is a model.

    2. What are the two sources of model risk, and which part of the framework addresses each?

    Errors in the model (data, assumptions, code) and misuse (a sound model on the wrong purpose). Development and validation gates address the first. Recorded intended use, plus monitoring of actual use, addresses the second.

    3. Why is the inventory called the first control?

    Every other control attaches to a registered model. An unlisted model has no owner, tier, validation or retirement date.

    4. A pilot touches AED 2m and is Tier 4. Next year it covers AED 800m. What should happen?

    Re-tier. The exposure is now far higher, so validation depth and review cadence should rise with it. Material growth in volume or use should trigger re-assessment.

    5. In Simulation 4 with no automated monitoring, why is the average delay about half the review interval?

    Breaches start at random points between reviews, so on average they wait half the gap for the next one. Automated monthly monitoring caps the wait near half a month whatever the interval.

    6. A vendor ships a new version of its sanctions matcher. What do you do?

    Treat it as a change to an inventoried model. Classify it minor or major under policy, test it against the old version on your own labelled name pairs, record the decision, and monitor after go-live. Contractual change notices make this possible.

    7. Why must the validator be independent of the developer, and what does "effective challenge" add?

    Independence removes the pressure towards approval. Effective challenge adds the competence to find real flaws and the standing to force changes or block approval.

    8. Is an LLM-based complaint classifier a model? What is the safe position?

    It produces a label that drives routing, so in substance it behaves like one. Inventory it as an AI system, give it an owner and tier, test it on a labelled Arabic and English set, and monitor it. Confirm formal status with compliance.
