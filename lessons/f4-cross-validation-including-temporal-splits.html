    Honest Validation and Honest Segments: Cross-Validation, Time Splits and Clustering

    How to estimate a model's future performance without borrowing from the future, why random folds fail on bank data, and what a customer segment really is once k-means has drawn it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA validation scheme is a simulation of deployment, so it must copy how the model will really meet new data (later in time, new customers, labels still arriving), and a cluster is a description of the features you gave the algorithm, never an explanation of why customers differ.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model graded on data it learned from may simply have memorised it. So hold some data back, train on the rest, and score on what the model has not seen. That is a validation split. Cross-validation (CV) repeats the trick so every row gets a turn as unseen data and the score does not hinge on one lucky split.

    It is honest only if the held-out data resembles the future the model will face. Bank data breaks this in three ways: it is ordered in time (fraud tactics, rates and behaviour drift), it comes in clumps (one customer, card or device makes many rows), and its labels arrive late. A random shuffle ignores all three, so the score looks good in the notebook and disappoints in production.

    Picture an exam that repeats the textbook's questions: the mark shows memory, not understanding. A random split is that exam. Row 9,041 (a purchase at 14:02) is in training, row 9,042 (same card, 14:05, same merchant) is in test, and the model scores brilliantly by recognising a neighbour. Tomorrow's transactions have no such neighbours.

    Two ideas to carry through.

    • The split is part of the model. A good scheme answers "how well will this do on new data of the kind we will meet?" A bad one answers "how well does it recall data like what it saw?"
    • Segments are a different question. Clustering has no label, so nothing to score against. An algorithm always hands you segments whether or not customers fall into groups, and even real segments describe without explaining.
    Hold onto thisBefore any CV, finish this sentence: "In production the model will be scored on ___ that arrives ___ after the training data, for customers who ___." Build the folds to match it.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    K-fold cross-validation: what it estimates

    Split rows into K equal folds. For each fold k, train on the other K−1, score on fold k, record the loss. The CV estimate is the average:

    CVK=1K∑k=1KLk

    Lk is the loss (error, log-loss, 1 − AUC) of the model trained without fold k, measured on fold k. With 100,000 rows and K = 5, each fit trains on 80,000 and scores 20,000. Every row is scored once, by a model that never saw it. A lower CV means better expected performance on data like the folds. A drop from 0.30 to 0.25 means 0.05 less loss on average, not a guaranteed gain, since fold noise can be that large.

    Four properties come up in review.

    • It estimates a procedure, not the final model. The K fitted models are discarded; you ship a refit on all data. A CV score says how well "this algorithm, these settings, about this much data" tends to do. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani (2023): it estimates the average error over training sets better than the error of your one model, so naive fold-based confidence intervals are too narrow.
    • K trades bias against variance and cost. K = 2 trains on half the data, so the estimate is pessimistic. Leave-one-out (K = n) is nearly unbiased but costs n fits whose errors are highly correlated. Five or ten is the usual compromise (ESL ch. 7). On huge tables one large hold-out is often enough.
    • Fold scores are not independent, so their spread understates uncertainty. Training sets overlap by (K−2)/(K−1) of rows, and Bengio and Grandvalet (2004) proved no unbiased variance estimator exists. Read "mean ± sd across folds" as a rough guide; use repetition or a bootstrap (F2.2) when it matters.
    • Everything learned from data happens inside the fold. Scaling, imputing, feature selection, target encoding, SMOTE (F3.5): fit on the training part only, then apply to the test part, using a pipeline. Fitting a scaler on all rows leaks the test mean into training (F3.1).
    from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, TimeSeriesSplit

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), HistGradientBoostingClassifier())
cv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, gap=14)          # 14-day gap for label delay
scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")

    Three variants matter for banking data. Stratified K-fold keeps the positive rate equal in each fold, so a fold with three frauds does not produce nonsense (F3.5). Repeated K-fold re-runs with different shuffles and averages, shrinking partition noise. Group K-fold keeps all rows of one entity together (next section).

    Groups: the same customer on both sides

    If a customer has 40 transactions and folds are random, the other 39 are likely in training when row 40 is scored. A flexible model can learn "this card, this device, this salary pattern" instead of "what fraud looks like". The score then measures recognising known customers, but production also meets new ones, and a new fraudster is by definition not in training. It is like a teacher testing pupils on the exact worksheets they practised.

    The rule: split at the level at which you will generalise. Scoring new customers: hold out whole customers. Scoring tomorrow's transactions of existing customers: hold out time. Often both, which means a grouped temporal split (Roberts et al., 2017). Simulation 1 shows the "customers on both sides" counter dropping to zero under Group 5-fold. Typical clumps: card, account, customer, household, device, branch, relationship manager, merchant, and the same document scanned twice.

    Temporal splits: train on the past, test on the future

    The deployed model predicts forwards, so validate forwards, like a weather forecaster scored only on tomorrow. Expanding-window (walk-forward) validation trains on everything up to a date, tests on the next block, then moves the date on. A rolling window keeps a fixed length of history, which suits data where old behaviour stops mattering. The final fold shows what the model would have done last month.

    • Add a gap (embargo) for label delay and overlapping windows. If a fraud label is trusted only after 45 days, then on the day you would have trained, the last 45 days had no reliable labels, so training must stop 45 days before the test block. Likewise, a 30-day rolling-average feature shares information across the boundary. Dropping that buffer is embargoing; removing training rows whose label windows overlap the test period is purging (López de Prado, 2018). Both match scikit-learn's gap argument. Delayed labels are covered in B1.
    • Avoid "blocked" K-fold (contiguous blocks, training on both sides). It stops neighbour leakage but still trains on the future: a model trained on November and tested on October knows a pattern that did not yet exist. Acceptable for stationary sensor logs, poor for fraud.

    The simulation below shows the cost of getting this wrong on a series where today's level depends on yesterday's. The "model" is deliberately crude: predict a point as the average of the three nearest training points in time. Even that looks excellent when it may interpolate between past and future neighbours.

    Random folds are harmless only when rows are exchangeable, meaning shuffling changes nothing. Transactions, balances, rates, complaints and defaults are not: they have time order, drift and repeating entities. Cerqueira, Torgo and Mozetič (2020) found the same split across many series: ordinary K-fold is fine for stationary series, and forward methods win for non-stationary ones.

    Caveats. Early forward folds have fewer training rows, so the estimate is noisier and a little pessimistic. And no scheme fixes a true regime change: if the regulator introduces instant payments, last year's data does not know. Temporal validation measures how well the past predicts next month, not the next crisis. That is the job of drift monitoring (F7).

    Tuning and selection: nested CV and the final test set

    CV also chooses between models and settings (F4.7, tuning), and here lies a trap. Try 200 settings and report the best CV score, and the number is biased upwards because you picked it for being high. Among 200 coin flips, the best one looks skilled. The bias is small with few settings and lots of data, large with many settings and little data (Cawley and Talbot, 2010; Varma and Simon, 2006).

    The remedy is nested CV: an inner loop picks settings inside each outer training set, and the outer loop scores the whole "tune then fit" procedure on data the inner loop never saw. It is costly (5 outer × 3 inner × 20 candidates = 300 fits). The cheaper production equivalent is a time-ordered three-way split: train (fit), validation (choose settings, compare models), test (the final months, touched once). Peek at the test set to decide anything and it becomes another validation set.

    Two habits. Compare models on the same folds and look at the paired difference per fold; it is steadier than either score. When two settings are close, prefer the simpler one (the "one standard error" rule, ESL ch. 7).

    Clustering: finding groups when nobody told you the answer

    Clustering is unsupervised: no label, just a table of customers and a wish to group similar ones. Think of seating guests at tables by how alike they are. The standard method is k-means (Lloyd, 1957; MacQueen, 1967). Pick k. Place k centres. Assign each customer to the nearest centre. Move each centre to the average of its customers. Repeat until nothing moves. It minimises the within-cluster sum of squares:

    J=∑j=1k∑x∈Cj‖x−μj‖2

    Each customer x pays the squared distance to its group's centre μj. Smaller J means tighter groups, but J always falls as k grows, so it cannot choose k. k-means finds a local minimum, so results depend on the starting centres: use k-means++ seeding (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007) and several restarts. Squared straight-line distance means it finds round blobs of similar size and is sensitive to units.

    Four mechanics decide whether segments mean anything.

    • Scaling is a modelling choice. If one feature is monthly spend (0 to 40,000 AED) and another is tenure (0 to 15 years), raw distance is almost all spend, like ranking runners by a race where one is timed in milliseconds. Standardising gives each feature equal weight, which is also a choice. Heavy-tailed money amounts usually want a log first. Simulation 3 carves the same customers two ways.
    • You choose k, and the data will not object. k-means returns k groups for any k, even on a featureless cloud. Helpers: the elbow in J (often ambiguous), the gap statistic, stability (same groups on a different sample?), and the silhouette. A customer's silhouette compares its average distance to its own group (a) with the nearest other group (b): s = (b − a) / max(a, b), from −1 to 1. Near 1: clearly inside its group. Near 0: on the border. Negative: probably in the wrong group (Rousseeuw, 1987). With a = 2 and b = 6, s = 0.67. Averaged over customers, about 0.7 is strong and about 0.4 is weak. Going from 0.4 to 0.7 means groups are far more separated than they are wide. It does not say the groups are useful.
    • Other algorithms answer other questions. Gaussian mixtures give soft membership and ellipses. Hierarchical clustering gives a tree to cut at any height. DBSCAN and HDBSCAN find dense regions of any shape and label the rest noise (HDBSCAN is in scikit-learn from 1.3). k-medoids and k-prototypes handle mixed categorical and numeric fields. For text, embed first (F1.1), then cluster; this is how complaint themes are found in Arabic and English together.
    • High dimensions blur distance. With hundreds of features everyone is roughly equally far apart, and clusters follow the noisiest or most numerous features. Reduce first (PCA or an embedding) and check what drives the groups.

    Segments describe; they do not explain

    A cluster label such as "young high spenders" summarises how the algorithm grouped the columns you chose. It is not a cause. It is like sorting a wardrobe by colour: the piles are real, but they say nothing about why you bought the clothes. Four consequences:

    • Change the features, change the segments. Cluster on spend and you get spend segments; add product holdings and you get different people. Kleinberg (2002) proved no clustering method can satisfy scale invariance, richness and consistency together. Hennig (2015): "true clusters" depend on what you mean by similar and why you want groups.
    • A segment does not tell you what to do. Customers who look alike need not respond alike to a profit-rate promotion. Run an experiment (F3.6) or estimate the treatment effect (F9.2). Targeting by predicted response often beats targeting by cluster.
    • Segments drift. A January cluster may dissolve by June. Re-fit, match old to new, and fix names and owners for segments, not raw cluster ids.
    • They can proxy protected traits. Postcode, nationality-correlated fields or language can leak in. Check each segment's composition before it drives pricing or access (G2).

    Judge a segment on four tests: actionable (someone can treat it differently), stable (same people next month), distinct (describable in one sentence) and sizeable (worth separate treatment). Silhouette is a secondary check.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Shuffled K-fold on time-ordered data

      Neat folds, tight spread, strong score, then a weaker first quarter live.

      Spot itAsk for the score on the most recent months, trained only on earlier ones. A large drop means the shuffle was borrowing from the future.
    • Same entity on both sides

      One customer, card or device in training and test. Scores look superb.

      Spot itCount the IDs that appear in both train and test. It should be zero if you score new entities.
    • Preprocessing fitted on all rows

      A scaler, imputer, encoder, feature selector or SMOTE fitted before the split.

      Spot itAsk whether it is inside a pipeline. A feature selector run on all data before CV can produce high scores on pure noise.
    • Ignoring label delay

      Training includes rows whose labels were not yet known at the time, or whose recent labels are incomplete.

      Spot itAsk "on the day this model would have been trained, which labels existed?" Look for a gap equal to the maturation period.
    • Reporting the best CV score from a big search

      The tuned number is presented as expected performance.

      Spot itAsk how many configurations were tried and where the untouched test score is. If it is missing, expect a lower real number.
    • Reusing the test set

      Looking at the test score, adjusting, looking again.

      Spot itCount how many times the test set was scored. After a few, it has become a validation set.
    • Trusting fold spread as an error bar

      "Mean 0.91 ± 0.01" taken as proof that model A beats model B.

      Spot itFold scores are correlated; ask for paired differences on identical folds and a repeated or bootstrap interval.
    • Choosing k to make a nice story

      "Our customers fall into five segments," with the five picked by taste.

      Spot itAsk for silhouette and stability at several k, and what the segmentation does on a different month. If there is no peak, the groups are conventions.
    • Unscaled or heavy-tailed features

      Segments that are really "big balance" versus "small balance".

      Spot itLook at the cluster centres per feature; if one feature explains the whole split, the others were drowned out.
    • Treating clusters as causes

      "Segment 3 churns because they are digital-first."

      Spot itAsk what was manipulated. Without an experiment or a causal design, say "associated with".
    • Segments that quietly proxy a protected trait

      A cluster is 90% one nationality and gets worse terms.

      Spot itTabulate segment composition by sensitive attributes before use.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. 100,000 rows, 5-fold CV: rows per train and test, and fits with 3 repeats?

    80,000 train, 20,000 test. 15 fits over 3 repeats, plus one final refit on all rows to ship.

    2. A card model uses 14-day rolling counts; chargebacks confirm after 45 days. You test on March. Where does training end?

    At least 45 days before March starts, since those labels were not reliable yet. The gap must cover the larger of the label delay and the 14-day feature window, so no training label or feature window reaches into March.

    3. AUC is 0.97 in shuffled 10-fold CV and 0.83 on the last two months. Name three explanations and a check for each.

    (a) Time leakage or drift: re-run with forward folds. (b) Same customers on both sides: count shared IDs, re-run with group folds. (c) Preprocessing outside the fold: move it into a pipeline. Also possible: immature labels in the last two months, so check by label age.

    4. Nested CV: 5 outer folds, 3 inner folds, 20 settings. How many fits?

    5 × 3 × 20 = 300 inner fits, plus 5 refits on the outer training sets: 305. This is why large-data teams prefer a time-ordered train, validation and test split.

    5. Why can k-means on a featureless cloud still look decent?

    It always cuts space into k regions. The tell is a modest silhouette (about 0.4 in the simulation) with no peak at any k, against about 0.7 for real groups. Stability alone is not enough: a blob split along its long axis can be repeatable (about 0.88 at k = 3).

    6. A customer has a = 2.0 and b = 6.0. What is the silhouette? What if they swap?

    s = (6 − 2) ÷ 6 = 0.67, clearly placed. Swapped: −0.67, closer to another group than its own, so probably misassigned.

    7. You cluster on monthly spend (AED) and age without scaling. Which dominates?

    Spend: it spans tens of thousands while age spans about 60, so squared distance is nearly all spend. Standardise, or log spend then standardise.

    8. Why is "segment 2 churns more because it is young" unsupported by clustering alone?

    Clusters only group by features. Young customers may differ in income, products or channel, and any of those could drive churn. Nothing was varied independently. A cause needs an experiment or causal design (F3.6, F9.2).
