    Honest Validation and Honest Segments: Cross-Validation, Time Splits and Clustering

    How to estimate a model's future performance without borrowing from the future, why random folds fail on bank data, and what a customer segment really is once k-means has drawn it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA validation scheme is a simulation of deployment, so it must copy how the model will really meet new data (later in time, new customers, labels still arriving), and a cluster is a description of the features you gave the algorithm, never an explanation of why customers differ.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    You cannot grade a model on the data it learned from. It may have memorised it. So you hold some data back, train on the rest, and score the model on what it has not seen. That is a validation split. Cross-validation (CV) just repeats the trick several times so that every row gets a turn as the unseen data, and so that the score does not hinge on one lucky or unlucky split.

    The idea is sound. The way it is usually run is not always. A split only gives an honest score if the held-out data resembles the future the model will face. A bank's data has three habits that break this. It is ordered in time, and fraud tactics, rates and customer behaviour drift. It comes in clumps, because one customer, card or device generates many rows. And its labels arrive late. A random shuffle ignores all three and gives a score that looks good in the notebook and disappoints in production.

    A useful picture: a student sits an exam. If the exam repeats questions from the textbook she studied, her mark tells you about her memory, not her understanding. A random split is that exam. Row 9,041 (a purchase at 14:02) sits in training, row 9,042 (the same card, 14:05, same merchant) sits in the test set, and the model scores brilliantly by recognising a neighbour. In deployment, tomorrow's transactions have no such neighbours in the training data.

    Two ideas to carry through the lesson.

    • The split is part of the model. Choosing it is a modelling decision, as important as the algorithm. A good scheme answers the question "how well will this do on new data of the kind we will meet?" A bad one answers "how well does it recall data like the data it saw?"
    • Segments are a different kind of question. The second half of the lesson is clustering, where there is no label and so no score to validate against. That makes honesty harder: an algorithm will always hand you segments, whether or not the customers really fall into groups. We will see how to test that, and why even real segments describe customers without explaining them.
    Hold onto thisBefore running any CV, write one sentence: "In production the model will be scored on ___ that arrives ___ after the training data, for customers who ___." Then build the folds to match that sentence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    K-fold cross-validation: what it estimates

    Split the rows into K equal folds. For each fold k, train on the other K−1 folds, score on fold k, and record the loss. The CV estimate is the average:

    CVK=1K∑k=1KLk

    Lk is the loss (error, log-loss, 1 − AUC, whatever you care about) of the model trained without fold k, measured on fold k. With 100,000 rows and K = 5, each fit trains on 80,000 rows and is scored on 20,000. Every row is scored exactly once, by a model that never saw it.

    Four properties are worth knowing precisely, because they come up in review.

    • It estimates the performance of a procedure, not of the final model. The K fitted models are thrown away. The model you ship is refit on all the data. CV tells you how well "this algorithm with these settings, trained on about this much data" tends to do. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani (2023) showed the sharper point: CV is a good estimate of the average error over training sets, and a poorer estimate of the error of the one model you fitted, which is why naive confidence intervals from fold scores come out too narrow.
    • The choice of K trades bias against variance and cost. Small K (say 2) trains on half the data, so the models are weaker than your final one and the estimate is pessimistic. Large K (leave-one-out, K = n) is nearly unbiased but costs n fits, and the n models are almost identical, so their errors are highly correlated. Five or ten is the standard compromise (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 7). On huge tables a single large hold-out is often enough and much cheaper.
    • The folds are not independent, so the spread of fold scores understates the real uncertainty. Training sets overlap by (K−2)/(K−1) of their rows. Bengio and Grandvalet (2004) proved there is no unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold CV. Treat "mean ± standard deviation across folds" as a rough guide, and use repetition or a bootstrap (F2.2) when it matters.
    • Everything learned from data happens inside the fold. Scaling, imputing, selecting features, target encoding, SMOTE (F3.5): fit each on the training part only, then apply to the test part. The tool for this is a pipeline object. Fitting the scaler on all rows first leaks the test set's mean into training. F3.1 covers this class of error.
    from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, TimeSeriesSplit

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), HistGradientBoostingClassifier())
cv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, gap=14)          # 14-day gap for label delay
scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")

    Three more variants matter for banking data. Stratified K-fold keeps the positive rate equal in each fold, which stops a fold with three frauds producing nonsense on rare-event data (see F3.5). Repeated K-fold re-runs the whole procedure with different shuffles and averages, which shrinks the noise from one particular partition. Group K-fold keeps all rows of one entity together, which comes next.

    Groups: the same customer on both sides

    If a customer has 40 transactions and the folds are random, the other 39 are probably in training when row 40 is scored. A flexible model (a deep tree ensemble, a nearest-neighbour method) can learn "this card, this device, this salary pattern" rather than "what fraud looks like". The test score then measures recognition of known customers, while production meets new customers as well as known ones, and a new fraudster is by definition not in training.

    The rule: split at the level at which you will generalise. If you score new customers, hold out whole customers. If you score tomorrow's transactions of existing customers, hold out time. Often it is both, and the answer is a grouped temporal split. Roberts et al. (2017) give the general argument for any data with temporal, spatial or hierarchical structure. Simulation 1 shows the "customers on both sides" counter dropping to zero under Group 5-fold. Typical clumps in a bank: card, account, customer, household, device, branch, relationship manager, merchant, and the same document scanned twice.

    Temporal splits: train on the past, test on the future

    The deployed model always predicts forwards. So validate forwards. Expanding-window validation (also called walk-forward or forward chaining) trains on everything up to a date and tests on the next block, then moves the date on. A rolling window keeps a fixed length of history, which suits data where old behaviour is no longer relevant. The final fold is your best guide to what the model would have done last month.

    Two refinements make the simulation honest.

    • A gap (embargo) for label delay and overlapping windows. If a fraud label is only trusted 45 days after the transaction, then on the day you would have trained, the last 45 days of rows had no reliable labels. Your training set must stop 45 days before the test block. Likewise if a feature is a 30-day rolling average, rows within 30 days of the boundary share information across the split. Dropping that buffer is called embargoing; removing training rows whose label windows overlap the test period is called purging. Both come from financial machine learning (López de Prado, 2018) and match scikit-learn's gap argument. The full story of delayed labels is in B1.
    • Why not "blocked" K-fold, with contiguous blocks but training on both sides? It stops neighbouring rows leaking, but it still trains on the future. If the world changed in October, a model trained on November and tested on October knows about a pattern that did not exist yet. It is a reasonable compromise for stationary data, such as sensor logs, and a poor one for fraud.

    What the simulation below shows is the cost of getting this wrong, on a series where today's level depends on yesterday's. The "model" is deliberately simple: predict a point as the average of the three nearest training points in time. Even this humble rule looks excellent when it is allowed to interpolate between past and future neighbours.

    The mechanism is general. Random folds are only harmless when rows are exchangeable, meaning that shuffling them changes nothing about the problem. Bank transactions, balances, rates, complaints and defaults are not exchangeable: they have a time order, a drift, and entities that repeat. Cerqueira, Torgo and Mozetič (2020) compared validation methods on many time series and found the same split: in stationary series, ordinary K-fold is fine; in non-stationary ones, forward-in-time methods win.

    One caution on the other side. A forward scheme has fewer training rows in early folds, so the estimate is noisier and a little pessimistic. And no scheme can fix a true regime change. If the regulator introduces instant payments, CV on last year's data does not know. Honest temporal validation measures how well the past predicts the next month, not the next crisis. That is what F7 monitoring is for (drift monitoring).

    Tuning and selection: nested CV and the final test set

    CV is also how you choose between models and settings (F4.7, tuning). Here lies a trap. Try 200 settings, take the best CV score, and report it as the performance of the winner. That number is biased upwards, because you picked it for being high. Among 200 coin flips, the best one looks skilled. The effect is small when there are few settings and a lot of data, and large with many settings and small data (Cawley and Talbot, 2010; Varma and Simon, 2006).

    The remedy is nested cross-validation: an inner loop chooses the settings within each outer training set, and the outer loop scores the whole "tune then fit" procedure on data the inner loop never touched. It costs more (5 outer folds × 3 inner folds × 20 candidates = 300 fits), but it answers the right question. For most production work the cheaper equivalent is a three-way split by time: train (fit), validation (choose settings, compare models), test (the final months, touched once). If you peek at the test set to decide anything, it becomes another validation set, and you need a new one.

    Two more habits. When comparing two models, score them on the same folds and look at the paired difference per fold. The folds share most of their difficulty, so the difference is much steadier than either score. And apply the "one standard error" rule when two settings are close: prefer the simpler one (ESL ch. 7).

    Clustering: finding groups when nobody told you the answer

    Clustering is unsupervised: there is no label to predict, only a table of customers and a wish to group similar ones. The standard algorithm is k-means (Lloyd, 1957; MacQueen, 1967). Pick the number of groups k. Place k centres. Assign every customer to the nearest centre. Move each centre to the average of its customers. Repeat until nothing moves. It minimises the within-cluster sum of squares:

    J=∑j=1k∑x∈Cj‖x−μj‖2

    Each customer x is charged the squared distance to the centre μj of its group. k-means finds a local minimum of this, not the global one, so the answer depends on the starting centres. Smart seeding (k-means++, Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007) and several restarts are standard. Because distance is squared and straight-line, k-means finds round blobs of similar size, and is strongly affected by the units of the features.

    Four mechanics decide whether the segments mean anything.

    • Scaling is a modelling choice, not a chore. If one feature is monthly spend in AED (0 to 40,000) and another is tenure in years (0 to 15), raw distance is almost entirely spend. Tenure may as well be absent. Standardising puts each feature on a common scale, but that is also a choice, since it gives a one-year difference in tenure the same weight as a one-standard-deviation difference in spend. Heavy-tailed money amounts usually want a log first. Simulation 3 shows the same customers carved two ways.
    • You choose k, and the data will not object. k-means returns k groups for any k, including on a featureless cloud. Tools to help: the elbow in J as k grows (usually ambiguous), the silhouette score, the gap statistic, and stability. The silhouette of a customer compares its average distance to its own group (a) with the average distance to the nearest other group (b): s = (b − a) / max(a, b). It runs from −1 to 1; near 1 means clearly inside its group, near 0 means on the border (Rousseeuw, 1987). If a = 2 and b = 6, s = 0.67. Stability asks whether you get the same groups on a different sample of customers.
    • Other algorithms answer other questions. Gaussian mixtures give soft membership and ellipses. Hierarchical clustering gives a tree you can cut at different heights. DBSCAN and HDBSCAN find dense regions of any shape and label the rest as noise (HDBSCAN has been in scikit-learn since version 1.3). k-medoids and k-prototypes handle mixed categorical and numeric fields. For text, documents are first turned into embeddings (F1.1) and then clustered, which is how complaint themes are found in Arabic and English together.
    • High dimensions blur distance. With hundreds of features, all customers become roughly equally far apart, and clusters reflect whichever features are noisiest or most numerous. Reduce first (PCA, or a learned embedding), and check what drives the groups.

    Segments describe; they do not explain

    Even when the structure is real, a cluster label such as "young high spenders" is a summary of how the algorithm grouped the columns you chose. It is not a cause. Four consequences follow, each of which comes up in banking meetings.

    • Change the features, change the segments. Cluster on spend and you get spend segments; add product holdings and you get different people. Kleinberg (2002) proved that no clustering method can satisfy three reasonable-sounding demands (scale invariance, richness, consistency) at once. Hennig (2015) puts it practically: "true clusters" depend on what you mean by similar and why you want groups.
    • A segment does not tell you what to do. Customers in a group look alike on the features. It does not follow that they will respond alike to an offer. To know whether a segment should get a profit-rate promotion, run an experiment (F3.6) or estimate the treatment effect (F9.2). Targeting by predicted response often beats targeting by cluster.
    • Segments are not stable over time. A cluster found in January may dissolve by June as behaviour shifts. Re-fit and compare with a matching measure, and fix naming and ownership of the segments, not the raw cluster ids.
    • They can encode what you must not use. Clusters built on postcode, nationality-correlated fields or language can proxy for protected characteristics. Check the composition of each segment before it drives pricing or access decisions (G2).

    Practical test for a segment: is it actionable (someone can do something different for it), stable (the same people fall in it next month), distinct (a stakeholder can describe how it differs in one sentence) and sizeable (worth a separate treatment)? A good segmentation is judged on these, with the silhouette as a secondary check.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Validation design links the statistics of the foundations to the process of every deployed model. Clustering links to the unsupervised side of the bank.

    F4.1 · Bias, variance and generalisationWhat CV is estimating

    CV is the practical measurement of generalisation error. The bias-variance tradeoff explains why K matters and why training error flatters.

    F4.7 · Hyperparameter tuningTuning on the validation folds

    Every tuning method uses CV as its objective. Nested CV and a locked final test set protect you from your own search.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeLeakage is a split problem

    Fitting preprocessing outside the fold and random splits on time-ordered data are both leakage. Fold design and feature timestamps must agree.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataFew positives per fold

    Stratify, group and use time order together; resample only inside the training fold; remember the effective sample is the number of frauds.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and bootstrapUncertainty on a CV score

    Fold scores are correlated, so a plain standard error is optimistic. Repeat the procedure or bootstrap before declaring a winner.

    F9.1 · Time series and forecastingForecast evaluation is walk-forward

    Liquidity and volume forecasts are validated with rolling origins. The same principles, with horizons built in.

    F7 · Metrics and monitoringThe loss inside the fold

    Pick the metric that matches the decision before building folds. Then drift monitoring tells you when the CV result has stopped applying.

    F4.8 · Anomaly detectionThe other unsupervised family

    Clustering asks "who is alike"; anomaly detection asks "who is unlike everyone". Peer groups from clustering are often the baseline for anomalies.

    B6 · Segmentation and lifetime valueSegments at the bank

    The business use of this lesson's second half: how segments feed value models and campaigns, and why they need an experiment behind them.

    G3 · Independent validationWhat validators will ask

    Model validators look first at the split design, leakage and out-of-time performance. This lesson is the vocabulary for that conversation.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Fraud and AML. Grouped, forward-in-time folds with a label-delay gap. Investigators confirm alerts weeks later, so the freshest rows are the least trustworthy (B1).
    • Credit scoring and IFRS 9. Out-of-time validation on later vintages. A scorecard validated on a shuffled sample of the good years will not reveal its behaviour in a downturn. Regulators expect out-of-sample and out-of-time evidence; the exact requirements differ by regulator, so check CBUAE or SAMA guidance rather than assuming.
    • Customer segmentation. Retail segments by behaviour, affluent versus mass, salary-account versus not, and merchants grouped by transaction profile. Islamic-finance product eligibility is a business rule layered on top of segments, not something to infer by clustering.
    • Peer groups for monitoring. AML models compare a customer's activity with that of similar customers. The peer group is a cluster, so its quality directly sets the false-alert rate.
    • Complaints, KYC text and call-centre transcripts. Clustering embeddings finds themes (card blocked, transfer delayed) in Arabic and English. Review the themes with the people who read the complaints.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated notebooks. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Shuffled K-fold on time-ordered data

      Neat folds, tight spread, strong score, then a weaker first quarter live.

      Spot itAsk for the score on the most recent months, trained only on earlier ones. A large drop means the shuffle was borrowing from the future.
    • Same entity on both sides

      One customer, card or device in training and test. Scores look superb.

      Spot itCount the IDs that appear in both train and test. It should be zero if you score new entities.
    • Preprocessing fitted on all rows

      A scaler, imputer, encoder, feature selector or SMOTE fitted before the split.

      Spot itAsk whether it is inside a pipeline. A feature selector run on all data before CV can produce high scores on pure noise.
    • Ignoring label delay

      Training includes rows whose labels were not yet known at the time, or whose recent labels are incomplete.

      Spot itAsk "on the day this model would have been trained, which labels existed?" Look for a gap equal to the maturation period.
    • Reporting the best CV score from a big search

      The tuned number is presented as expected performance.

      Spot itAsk how many configurations were tried and where the untouched test score is. If it is missing, expect a lower real number.
    • Reusing the test set

      Looking at the test score, adjusting, looking again.

      Spot itCount how many times the test set was scored. After a few, it has become a validation set.
    • Trusting fold spread as an error bar

      "Mean 0.91 ± 0.01" taken as proof that model A beats model B.

      Spot itFold scores are correlated; ask for paired differences on identical folds and a repeated or bootstrap interval.
    • Choosing k to make a nice story

      "Our customers fall into five segments," with the five picked by taste.

      Spot itAsk for silhouette and stability at several k, and what the segmentation does on a different month. If there is no peak, the groups are conventions.
    • Unscaled or heavy-tailed features

      Segments that are really "big balance" versus "small balance".

      Spot itLook at the cluster centres per feature; if one feature explains the whole split, the others were drowned out.
    • Treating clusters as causes

      "Segment 3 churns because they are digital-first."

      Spot itAsk what was manipulated. Without an experiment or a causal design, say "associated with".
    • Segments that quietly proxy a protected trait

      A cluster is 90% one nationality and gets worse terms.

      Spot itTabulate segment composition by sensitive attributes before use.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The 95% figure came from shuffling last year's data, so the model was tested on days it had effectively already seen. We re-ran it the way it will be used: trained up to a date, scored on the following weeks, and we left out the most recent weeks where fraud labels are still arriving. The honest figure is lower, and it's the one I'd plan around. We also keep the final three months untouched, so the number you see at sign-off hasn't been tuned to.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I use expanding-window validation grouped by customer, with a gap equal to the label maturation period, preprocessing inside a pipeline, and a final out-of-time test I touch once. Model comparisons are paired on the same folds with a repeated or bootstrap interval, because fold scores are correlated. For hyperparameter search on small data I'd nest the loop. For segmentation I'd standardise or log-transform, check silhouette and subsample stability across k, and validate that segments are actionable with an experiment, since a cluster describes the features, not the cause.”
    “Why not just use 10-fold CV? That's what the textbook says.”

    Ten-fold assumes rows are exchangeable. Ours aren't: they are ordered in time, clumped by customer, and labelled late. For a model that will score future transactions, I'd validate forward in time. Ten folds is fine for a benchmark on a static snapshot, such as an application scorecard compared across algorithms, and I'd still stratify.

    “Forward validation gives a lower score than random CV. Which is right?”

    The forward one, because it matches the question. The gap is not a flaw in the model; it measures how much the random split was borrowing from the future or from known customers. The size of the gap is itself useful: a big one says the world drifts quickly and the model needs frequent retraining and monitoring.

    “Can we use the test set to pick the better of two models?”

    Once, as the final decision, and not to tune. If the choice affects the model, the test set has become a validation set and the reported score is optimistic. Make the selection on validation folds, then score the single chosen model on the test period.

    “How many customer segments do we have?”

    The data doesn't dictate a number. I'd show how silhouette and stability change with k and propose the smallest k that is stable and where each group is distinct and actionable. If there's no peak, I'd say we have a continuum and choose k for the business use, and I'd say so openly. Then I'd test whether treating segments differently pays off.

    “The clustering says segment 4 is 'digital-first customers'. Can we target them for the new savings product?”

    The label describes how they behave on the features we clustered. It doesn't say they'll respond. I'd run a small randomised campaign with a holdout, which tells us the uplift for that segment, and compare it with targeting on a response model. I'd also check that the segment is stable month to month and doesn't proxy a protected group.

    “Why does the model do better on the validation folds than in production?”

    Usual suspects, in order: a shuffled or non-grouped split, preprocessing fitted outside the fold, labels in recent data not yet matured, many configurations tried on the same folds, and genuine drift since training. I'd check the splits first because they're cheapest to verify, then compare the live feature distributions with training.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You have 100,000 rows and use 5-fold CV. How many rows does each model train on and test on, and how many fits are needed with 3 repeats?

    80,000 train, 20,000 test per fold. Five fits per repeat, so 15 fits over 3 repeats. Plus one final refit on all rows if you are going to ship the model.

    2. A card model uses 14-day rolling counts as features, and fraud chargebacks are confirmed after 45 days. You test on transactions in March. What should the training data cut-off be?

    Training should end at least 45 days before the start of March, because the labels from then were not yet reliable when the model would have been built. If a feature also looks back 14 days, ensure no training row's feature window or label window reaches into the test period; the gap should cover the larger of the two effects.

    3. A model gets AUC 0.97 in shuffled 10-fold CV and 0.83 on the last two months. Name three explanations and a check for each.

    (a) Time leakage or drift: re-run with forward folds. (b) Same customers in train and test: count shared IDs, rerun with group folds. (c) Preprocessing or feature selection outside the fold: move into a pipeline. Also possible: label immaturity in the last two months, so check by label age.

    4. Nested CV with 5 outer folds, 3 inner folds and 20 candidate settings. How many model fits?

    5 × 3 × 20 = 300 inner fits, plus 5 refits on each outer training set using the chosen setting: 305 in total. That is why large-data teams usually prefer a time-ordered train, validation and test split.

    5. Why can k-means on a featureless cloud still show a decent-looking result?

    It always partitions the space into k convex regions, whatever the data. The signal is in the comparison: a modest silhouette (around 0.4 in the simulation) that does not peak sharply at any k, against about 0.7 for real groups. Stability alone is not enough either, since a blob split along its long axis can be repeatable (about 0.88 at k = 3 in the simulation).

    6. For one customer a (average distance to own group) is 2.0 and b (to the nearest other group) is 6.0. What is the silhouette? What if they swap?

    s = (6 − 2) ÷ 6 = 0.67, a clearly placed customer. Swapped: s = (2 − 6) ÷ 6 = −0.67, meaning the customer is closer to another group than its own and is probably misassigned.

    7. You cluster customers on monthly spend (AED) and age (years) without scaling. Which feature dominates and why?

    Spend. Its values range over tens of thousands while age spans about 60, so squared distances are almost all spend. The clusters would be bands of spend. Standardise, or take log spend and then standardise, depending on what difference you consider meaningful.

    8. Why is "segment 2 churns more because it is young" not supported by the clustering alone?

    The clusters only group customers by their features. Young customers may differ in income, product mix or channel, and any of those may drive churn. Nothing was varied independently. To claim a cause you need an experiment or a causal design (F3.6, F9.2).
