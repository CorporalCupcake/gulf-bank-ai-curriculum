    Cross-Validation and Time Splits

    Part 1

    What cross-validation is

    In one sentenceCross-validation tests a model several times, each time holding back a different slice of the data, so that every row gets a turn as "new" data the model has never seen.

    Our running example: a café wants to predict daily sales. A model scored on days it has already learned from just looks clever, because it can memorise them. You need a score from days it has not seen.

    How it differs from a single split

    • A single split (say 80% train, 20% test) gives one score. If that 20% happens to be easy or hard, the score is misleading.
    • Cross-validation gives five scores from five different test slices, plus an average. It uses the data more fully, but costs five model fits instead of one.
    • A final test set is separate again. You touch it once at the end. Cross-validation is for comparing models and settings before that.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Shuffle and cut the rows into K equal slices, called folds. K = 5 or 10 is typical.
    2. Hold out one fold and train on the other K - 1.
    3. Score the model on the held-out fold.
    4. Repeat until every fold has been the test fold once.
    5. Average the K scores, and look at how much they vary.

    Cross-validation judges a recipe (the algorithm plus its settings), not one finished model. Once you have picked the recipe, refit it on all the data.

    How to read the result

    • The average is your best guess at performance on new data. A café model with an average error of 40 sales per day is typically about 40 off.
    • The spread shows stability. Errors of 38, 41, 40, 42, 39 are steady. Errors of 20, 70, 35, 60, 15 mean the score depends heavily on which days were held out, so trust it less.
    • Training score far better than the CV score means overfitting.
    • Small gaps between models (say 40 vs 39.5, when folds wobble by 3) are ties. Take the simpler one.
    • Choice of K: a bigger K trains on more data per round, so the score is a little less pessimistic, but costs more fits. 5 or 10 is the usual answer.

    Two practical rules. Do all data preparation (scaling, filling gaps) inside each round, using only the training slice, or test information leaks in. For rare events like fraud, use stratified folds, which keep the same share of rare cases in every fold.

    Part 3

    When shuffling is cheating: time and groups

    In one sentenceIf the real use is predicting the future, test only on days after the training days.

    Café sales drift: they creep up in summer and down in winter, so today looks like yesterday. A random fold tests a day whose neighbours on both sides were in training. That is like marking an exam with the answer to the next question printed beside it. The score looks great and real use disappoints.

    The fix is a forward split, also called expanding-window validation.

    • Expanding window keeps all history, so each round has more training data. Use it by default.
    • Rolling window keeps a fixed length of recent history. It suits behaviour that goes stale, such as fraud tactics.
    • Add a gap if answers arrive late. If fraud labels are only confirmed after 45 days, stop training 45 days before the test block.
    • Cover different seasons with three to six forward rounds, and report the spread. A model that is strong in four periods and poor in one is telling you something an average would hide.

    The same problem with groups

    If one customer has 40 transactions and rows are shuffled, 39 of them are likely in training when row 40 is tested. The model learns "this customer" instead of "what fraud looks like". Group K-fold keeps all rows of one customer (or account, or device) in the same fold. Split at the level where you will generalise: new customers means hold out whole customers; tomorrow means hold out time. Often you need both.

    Part 4

    Which split to use

    SchemeUse whenWatch out for
    Single train/test splitLots of data, a quick checkOne lucky or unlucky slice
    K-foldRows are independent, such as survey responsesLeaks if rows are linked in time or by customer
    Stratified K-foldRare outcomes, such as fraudStill ignores time and groups
    Group K-foldMany rows per customerOne huge customer makes a lopsided fold
    Forward (expanding window)Anything that predicts the futureEarly rounds have little training data

    The question to ask first: "In production, the model will be scored on data that arrives after the training data, for customers who are new or not." Build the folds to match that sentence.

    Show the formula

    CV score = the average of the K fold scores. The spread across folds (the standard deviation) tells you how steady it is. Fold training sets overlap, so this spread understates the true uncertainty a little.

    In banking, transactions and defaults are ordered in time and clumped by customer, so forward and group splits are usually the honest choice, with a gap for label delay.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Shuffling time-ordered data

      The model sees the future during training, so the score is far too good. Use forward splits.

    • Customers on both sides of the split

      The model recognises the customer instead of learning the pattern. Use group folds.

    • Preparing data before splitting

      Scaling or filling gaps on all rows lets test information into training. Do it inside each round.

    • Reporting the best CV score from many tries

      You picked it for being high, so it is optimistic. Keep a final test set that you touch once.

    1. What does 5-fold cross-validation do?

    It trains five times, each time holding out a different fifth of the data for testing, then averages the five scores.

    2. Why is a random split wrong for predicting next month's sales?

    Days near each test day are in the training data, and the model has seen the future. Test only on days later than the training days.

    3. Fraud labels are confirmed 45 days late. What do you change in the split?

    Add a 45-day gap between the end of training and the start of the test block, so training uses only labels that would have been known.

    4. Fold errors are 20, 70, 35, 60 and 15. What does that tell you?

    The score depends heavily on which data is held out, so the average is unreliable. Investigate the bad folds.

    5. After cross-validation picks the best settings, what do you do?

    Refit the model on all the training data with those settings, and score it once on the untouched test set.
