    Buying Accuracy With Thinking: Verifiers, Process Rewards and Test-Time Scaling

    Why checking an answer is easier than producing one, how scoring each reasoning step differs from scoring the final answer, how sampling many answers and voting or picking can lift accuracy, and the specific ways each trick backfires.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model can often be made more accurate by spending more compute at answer time (longer thinking, many samples, search), but every such method is only as good as the thing that judges the answers, so a hard check (code, arithmetic, a rules engine) scales well, a learned judge scales until the model learns to fool it, and a vote scales only when the right answer is already the most common one.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit analyst at a Gulf bank asks an assistant to compute the debt-service ratio for a retail customer: salary AED 28,000, a car loan instalment of AED 2,150, a personal finance instalment of AED 3,400, and a proposed Murabaha home finance at AED 9,300 a month. Six steps: add the existing instalments, add the new one, divide by income, compare with a limit. The assistant writes a fluent page of reasoning and lands on 45.4%. The right answer is 53.0%. Somewhere in the page it dropped the AED 2,150 car loan.

    Now suppose you could ask for ten attempts. Seven say 53.0%, two say 45.4%, one says 58%. Or suppose you ran each attempt's arithmetic through a calculator. Or suppose a second model read each step and flagged the one where a figure vanished. These are the three families in this lesson. They share one idea: generating a good answer is hard, but recognising or checking one is often much easier, so spend compute on checking.

    The idea has an old shape. Finding a route through a city is hard; seeing that a proposed route is wrong is quick. Writing a proof is hard; checking one is routine. If checking is cheaper than producing, then producing many candidates and keeping the best one converts money into accuracy. This is what is meant by test-time scaling: improving results by using more compute when the question is asked, with no change to the trained weights. The 2024-25 wave of "reasoning models" (see A5, Reasoning models and test-time compute) is built on it.

    Four things to take from it.

    • Long reasoning chains are fragile. Even at 95% per step, an eight-step chain is fully sound only about two times in three. This is why harder problems need checking and not just a bigger model.
    • The grade can be given at two places. An outcome grade scores the final answer. A process grade scores each step. Process grades give far more information per example, at far higher labelling cost.
    • A grader is a model of "good", and it can be wrong. Everything downstream (picking the best of many, searching, training on graded outputs) optimises against the grader. The harder you optimise, the more you find its blind spots.
    • More thinking is not free and not always better. It costs tokens and latency, it helps a lot on some problems and not at all on others, and on some it hurts.
    Hold onto thisBefore using any test-time trick, ask three questions. Who or what judges the candidates, and how hard is it to fool? Is the right answer already the one the model produces most often? And what does each extra sample cost, in AED and in seconds? The answers decide which method, if any, pays.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Outcome rewards, process rewards and what each teaches

    A reward model is a network that reads a prompt and a response and outputs a score for how good it is. Two designs matter here.

    • Outcome reward model (ORM). One score for the whole response, trained from labels on the final answer: right or wrong. Cheap to label, because for many tasks the right answer is known. The cost is credit assignment. If a twelve-step solution ends wrong, the label says nothing about which step failed, and the model must work it out from many examples.
    • Process reward model (PRM). A score after each step, trained from labels on every step: correct, neutral or wrong. The first wrong step is then known. A solution is typically scored by combining step scores, for example the product of step "correct" probabilities, or the minimum.

    The comparison was made carefully on school-level maths by Uesato and colleagues at DeepMind (2022): process supervision reached similar final-answer error to outcome supervision but sharply cut the rate of flawed reasoning behind right answers. OpenAI's "Let's Verify Step by Step" (Lightman et al., 2023) then showed on the harder MATH benchmark that a PRM trained on about 800,000 human step labels (the PRM800K data set) picked better solutions out of many than an ORM. Simulation 1's last statistic is the reason that matters: an outcome grader pays for right answers reached by wrong reasoning, which is exactly what a bank cannot accept in a credit memo or an AML narrative, where the working is the product.

    There is a second reason for step-level scores that has nothing to do with labels. They make search possible. If you can score a half-finished solution, you can stop exploring the bad branches early and spend compute on promising ones, as in beam search or tree search. An outcome score only exists at the end, when all the compute has already been spent.

    Where step labels come from

    Human step labels are expensive: a skilled annotator reading a twelve-step proof takes minutes. Three routes exist.

    Rollout estimation (Math-Shepherd, OmegaPRM and relatives)From a step, finish the solution many times. If most completions reach the right answer, call the step good.     Notice what rollout estimation does: it turns an outcome check into step labels by averaging. That is a standard move in reinforcement learning (estimating a value from sampled returns, see F9, Bandits and reinforcement learning), and it carries the same variance problem: few rollouts give noisy values.

    Verifiers: from hard checks to learned judges

    "Verifier" is the general word for anything that decides whether a candidate answer is good. The useful thing to understand is a spectrum of how trustworthy it is.

    VerifierExample in a bankCan it be fooled?
    Exact checkRecompute the debt-service ratio in code. Check a Murabaha profit figure against cost × margin × tenor. Run generated SQL and compare the row count with a control total.Not on what it checks. It says nothing about what it does not check, so the specification can still be wrong.
    Rule and constraint checkDoes the draft cite a limit that exists in the credit policy? Do the figures in the memo match the source table? Is every required KYC field present?Only by omission. Coverage is the weakness.
    Learned checker (ORM, PRM, LLM judge)A model scores whether a credit memo's reasoning holds up, or whether an AML narrative is supported by the transactions.Yes. It was trained on examples and it has blind spots. Fluent, confident, well-formatted errors score well.
    Human reviewThe analyst signs off.Yes: tired, rushed, anchored by the model's confidence (see B7, Internal copilots for staff).

    The most consequential development of 2025 was the realisation that, where an exact check exists, you can skip learned reward models and train directly on it. This is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: run the model's answer through a checker and give reward 1 or 0. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) used rule-based rewards for answer correctness and format and said it avoided neural reward models for reasoning because they were open to reward hacking and costly to retrain. Maths and code have cheap exact checks. That is why reasoning models improved fastest there, and why progress on messier work such as policy interpretation or credit judgement is slower: there is no calculator for "is this a good decision".

    This asymmetry is the key to deciding where test-time scaling will pay in your bank. List your use cases by the strength of the best available check. The strongest are reconciliation, calculation, structured data extraction against a schema, code, and SQL. The weakest are advice, summaries and anything requiring judgement.

    Best-of-N: sample many, let the verifier pick

    The simplest test-time recipe: draw N independent answers, score each, return the highest. Two ceilings frame what is possible.

    • Coverage, or pass@N. If each sample is right with chance a, the chance that at least one of N is right is 1 − (1 − a)N. With a = 30%, N = 16 gives 99.7%. This is what a perfect verifier would deliver. Brown and colleagues (2024) showed that coverage keeps rising with the number of samples over several orders of magnitude, on maths and on software tasks with unit tests. On those tasks the tests are the verifier.
    • The verifier's discrimination. A learned verifier does not know which sample is right; it gives scores that are higher on average for correct ones. Picking the top score is then right only as often as the scores separate the groups.

    The simulation below models the verifier as a score with some overlap. Correct answers score on average d standard deviations above ordinary wrong ones. The extra knob, fooling share, is the part that matters most in practice: a fraction of wrong answers are fluent, confident and look right to this particular verifier, so they score higher than correct ones. With few samples, the fool is rare in the pool. With many, the maximum score is almost certainly a fool, because you have gone looking for the extreme.

    Three observations carry over to real systems.

    • The ceiling is a model property; the gap to it is a verifier property. If the model almost never produces a right answer (a near zero), no verifier helps. If it produces right answers 5% of the time and you can verify exactly, 64 samples are enough to cover 96% of problems.
    • A learned verifier has an optimal N and then it declines. This is reward-model over-optimisation (Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2023): the proxy score keeps rising with selection pressure while true quality peaks and falls. Best-of-N is the gentlest form of selection pressure. Reinforcement learning against a learned reward is stronger and breaks sooner (the topic of R3.1–R3.2, RLHF, DPO and GRPO).
    • Step-level verifiers raise the bar but do not remove it. A PRM is harder to fool with a lucky guess, since a broken step shows up. It can still be fooled by a fluent step that is subtly wrong, and in 2025 papers on PRM weaknesses documented it rewarding verbose or stylistically "correct-looking" steps. DeepSeek's own report on R1 listed hard-to-define steps, hard-to-judge intermediate correctness and reward hacking as the reasons it did not use a PRM.

    Self-consistency: let the model vote

    When no verifier is available, there is a cheaper judge: agreement. Sample N reasoning paths, extract each final answer, return the most common one. This is self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022). The logic is that there are many ways to reach a right answer and they converge, while errors scatter in different directions. It lifted accuracy on school maths benchmarks by double-digit points with the models of that time.

    The mechanism needs a condition that is easy to miss. Voting returns the plurality answer. It improves accuracy exactly when the right answer is the most common single answer, because more samples then make the vote more reliable. If one wrong answer is more common than the right one, more samples make the vote reliably wrong. Voting amplifies whatever the model's most popular answer is.

    Two refinements are used in practice. Weighted voting gives each answer the sum of its verifier scores instead of one vote each, combining Simulations 2 and 3, and usually beats either alone. Early stopping samples until the lead is clear: on easy questions where the first three agree you stop, on contested ones you keep going. This keeps the average cost down, and agreement is itself a useful uncertainty signal: a question on which the samples split five ways is one to send to a human.

    Thinking longer: budget forcing, search and spending compute well

    Voting and best-of-N are parallel scaling: many independent attempts. The other direction is sequential: let one attempt think for longer. Reasoning models do this by writing a long chain of thought before the answer (see A5). The question is how to control how long.

    The cleanest demonstration is budget forcing from the s1 paper (Muennighoff et al., 2025). At decode time you control the thinking length by hand. To stop early, you cut the thinking off and force the model to start its answer. To think longer, when the model tries to finish you suppress the end-of-thinking marker and append the word "Wait". The model often rereads its work and corrects itself. A model fine-tuned on only 1,000 carefully chosen reasoning examples went from about 50% to about 57% on the AIME 2024 maths-competition set with this control, and accuracy rose smoothly with the thinking budget until it flattened.

    Alongside this, three other families scale test-time compute:

    • Search over steps. Beam search, tree search (including Monte Carlo tree search) and "lookahead" use a step-level verifier to prune. They are worth it when a PRM is reliable, and wasteful when it is not.
    • Iterative revision. The model writes, critiques and rewrites. Works when the model can actually find its own errors; Snell et al. (2024) found it helps more on easier problems than harder ones.
    • Adaptive allocation. Spend more on hard questions and little on easy ones. In the same study, allocating compute by estimated difficulty let a small model with extra test-time compute beat a model about 14 times larger on easy and intermediate questions, but not on the hardest. Test-time compute substitutes for model size only when the question is within reach.

    The word "forced" matters. Longer is not monotonically better. Studies of "overthinking" show models spending thousands of tokens on trivial questions, and Anthropic-led work on inverse scaling in test-time compute (Gema et al., 2025) found tasks where longer reasoning made models worse: distracted by irrelevant detail, or drifting from a good first answer to a worse one.

    Put the pieces together and test-time scaling is a three-way tradeoff among accuracy, cost and latency, with the verifier deciding how much each extra unit of compute is worth. A rough order of reliability for a bank: (1) exact verifier plus parallel samples, which gives dependable gains; (2) adaptive thinking length matched to difficulty; (3) voting, when you have checked that the right answer is the plurality; (4) learned verifiers and PRMs, only after measuring where they top out on your data.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Verification is where post-training, evaluation and bank controls meet.

    R3.1 – R3.2 · RLHF, DPO, GRPOReward models used for training

    The same reward models that rank samples at answer time are used to train the model. A verifier you trust at inference can become the reward in training; one you do not should not be.

    A5 · Reasoning models and test-time computeThe applied view

    This lesson explains why long reasoning and sampling help. A5 covers how to choose and price reasoning models for bank workloads.

    R4 · Chain-of-thought faithfulnessIs the written reasoning the real reasoning?

    A PRM scores the text of the steps. If the text is a rationalisation rather than the cause of the answer, step scores can be high while the real process is not what they show.

    M1 · Pretraining then post-trainingWhere this stage sits

    Pretraining gives the knowledge, post-training shapes the behaviour, and test-time compute adds a third lever that needs no new weights.

    A4 · Statistics for evals · Hallucination detectionMeasure the gain properly

    Does best-of-16 beat a single sample on your cases? That is a paired comparison with an interval, not an impression. Groundedness checks are verifiers for text claims.

    F2.1 · F2.2 · Intervals and the bootstrapHow wrong is this accuracy?

    Accuracy on 60 test questions has an interval near ±12 points. Most claimed test-time gains on small sets are inside that.

    F7.4 · CalibrationScores that mean something

    A verifier score is only useful as a threshold ("send to a human below 0.7") if it is calibrated on cases like yours.

    F1.6 · Likelihood and entropyWhy a vote is an uncertainty signal

    The spread of sampled answers is an empirical entropy. High disagreement is a cheap sign the model does not know.

    M4 · Agents and guardrails · M5 · Agent evaluationA verifier before an action

    An approval gate is a verifier in front of an irreversible step. Trajectory evaluation is the process-reward idea applied to agent steps.

    P3 · Quantisation and distillation · A5 · Cheap inferenceCutting the cost per sample

    Sampling many times is only affordable if each sample is cheap. Smaller or cheaper-to-serve models change the break-even N.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit memos and ratios. Recompute every figure in code and compare to the source table. This is an exact verifier, and it catches the dropped-number error from Part 1. See A10, LLMs for credit memos.
    • AML alert narratives. Generate a narrative, then check that each claim cites a transaction in the case file. A groundedness verifier plus human review is cheaper than a human writing it from nothing.
    • Document extraction for trade finance. Sample the extraction several times; fields where samples disagree go to a person. Agreement is the uncertainty signal (B7).
    • Islamic finance calculations. Profit rate, rebate on early settlement and instalment schedules have exact formulas, so generate-then-recompute is available. Shariah judgements have no calculator and need scholar review.
    • Arabic and English customer answers. Verifiers trained mostly on English reasoning data may be weaker in Arabic; measure per language before trusting a score threshold.
    • Latency-bound channels. A chat assistant cannot wait for 64 samples. Overnight batch jobs such as reconciliation can.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The central result has held since 2024: extra inference compute buys accuracy, with a clear divide between domains that can be verified exactly and domains that cannot. The live arguments concern whether step-level learned verifiers are worth their cost, how to train models that decide for themselves how long to think, and how far any of this transfers beyond maths and code.

    1. 2021
      Trained verifiers on school maths

      Cobbe et al. sampled many solutions per problem and trained a verifier to pick one, finding that this beat simply fine-tuning the generator at larger data sizes. The template for best-of-N with a learned judge.

      Cobbe et al., arXiv 2110.14168, 2021
    2. 2022
      Self-consistency and the first process-versus-outcome study

      Majority voting over sampled reasoning paths gave large gains on maths benchmarks. In the same year, Uesato et al. compared process and outcome supervision directly and found process supervision cut flawed reasoning behind right answers.

      Wang et al., ICLR 2023 (arXiv 2203.11171); Uesato et al., arXiv 2211.14275
    3. 2023
      PRM800K and automatic step labels

      Lightman et al. released about 800,000 human step labels and showed a PRM beat an ORM at selecting solutions on MATH. Math-Shepherd soon showed step labels could be estimated by sampling completions, without humans. Gao et al. quantified how best-of-N and RL against a learned reward model over-optimise.

      Lightman et al., arXiv 2305.20050; Wang et al., arXiv 2312.08935; Gao et al., ICML 2023
    4. 2024
      Test-time compute as a scaling axis

      Brown et al. found coverage rising log-linearly with samples and flagged verification as the bottleneck outside domains with automatic checks. Snell et al. studied compute-optimal allocation by difficulty and showed small models with extra inference compute matching much larger ones on easier questions. OpenAI's o1 made long reasoning a product.

      Brown et al., arXiv 2407.21787; Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314
    5. 2025
      Verifiable rewards beat learned ones for reasoning RL; budget forcing

      DeepSeek-R1 trained long reasoning with rule-based rewards, reporting that it left out neural reward models because of reward hacking. The s1 paper showed that 1,000 examples plus budget forcing give a smooth test-time scaling curve, with AIME 2024 rising from about 50% to about 57%.

      DeepSeek-AI, arXiv 2501.12948; Muennighoff et al., arXiv 2501.19393
    6. 2025
      Verifiers that think, and PRM caveats

      ThinkPRM trains a verifier that writes a long chain-of-thought critique, using orders of magnitude fewer process labels than discriminative PRMs and beating them on several benchmarks under best-of-N and guided search. Other 2025 work (the Qwen team's "Lessons of developing PRMs") reported that rollout-estimated labels underperform and that best-of-N tests can flatter a PRM that is not actually checking steps. Gema et al. documented tasks where longer reasoning lowers accuracy.

      Khalifa et al., arXiv 2504.16828; Zhang et al., arXiv 2501.07301; Gema et al., arXiv 2507.14417
    7. 2025–26
      Surveys and generalisation to code

      Surveys of PRMs and reward models (October 2025) summarise that re-ranking with step scores consistently helps, and a study of PRM transfer from maths to code found best-of-N winning at low compute and tree search overtaking it as budgets grow. I could not confirm specific 2026 benchmark results; the field's direction is generative, reasoning verifiers and RL on verifiable tasks beyond maths.

      arXiv 2510.08049; arXiv 2510.01925; arXiv 2506.00027

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in vendor decks, internal pilots and AI-written analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Picking by a learned score at very large N

      "We sample 256 and keep the best-scored." The top score is increasingly an answer that fits the verifier's taste.

      Spot itPlot true accuracy against N on labelled cases. If it flattens or falls beyond some N, cap N there. Read a sample of top-scored answers by hand.
    • Voting when the common answer is wrong

      A systematic misreading (for example the wrong tax treatment, or a unit confusion) is repeated in most samples.

      Spot itLook at single-sample accuracy and the top wrong answer's share. If a wrong answer is the plurality, voting will hurt; see Simulation 3.
    • Right answers from wrong reasoning

      Outcome-checked accuracy looks fine, but the written working is flawed and will be read by an auditor or customer.

      Spot itSample 30 right-answer cases and have an expert read the working. Report the share with a flaw.
    • Verifying the wrong thing

      The code check confirms the arithmetic but not that the right inputs were chosen, or the policy cited is current.

      Spot itList what the verifier does not check. Test it with a plausible answer that is wrong in exactly those ways.
    • Over-thinking and "Wait" loops

      Longer budgets on easy tasks waste money, add latency, and sometimes turn a right first answer into a wrong final one.

      Spot itCompare accuracy at a short and a long budget by task difficulty. Check the distribution of thinking length, not only the mean.
    • Reporting gains on a handful of questions

      Best-of-8 beats one sample by 6 points on 50 questions.

      Spot itAsk for the number of questions and a paired interval. At 50 questions the interval on one accuracy is about ±14 points.
    • Benchmark leakage and contaminated test sets

      A public maths or finance benchmark was in the model's training data, so sampling "finds" memorised answers.

      Spot itTest on questions written after the model's data cutoff, or on private bank cases.
    • Ignoring cost and latency in the headline

      "Accuracy up 8 points" with a 40x increase in tokens, quoted without the AED figure.

      Spot itAsk for cost per correct case and 95th-percentile latency. Compare against a cheaper model used once.
    • Trusting the shown reasoning as the real reasoning

      A PRM approves clean steps that do not reflect how the model reached the answer.

      Spot itRun a perturbation test: change a fact in the input and see whether the stated steps and the final answer change consistently (see R4).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These models can be made more accurate by letting them think longer or try several times and checking the answers. It works best where we can check automatically, such as recalculating a ratio or reconciling a total. It works less well where the check is another AI's opinion, and each extra attempt costs money and time. I'd start with the workflows that have an automatic check, measure the accuracy gain and the cost per correct case on our own data, and keep a person on anything involving judgement.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd separate the generator from the verifier and evaluate each. Where an exact check exists, I'd use best-of-N or RL against that check. Where only learned verifiers exist, I'd find the N at which true accuracy peaks on labelled cases, because over-optimisation against a proxy is guaranteed past some point. Majority voting is only safe if the right answer is the plurality, which I'd measure per task. Process reward models are attractive for credit or compliance reasoning because they penalise flawed working behind right answers, but step labels need expert annotators, so I'd start with outcome checks plus audits of the reasoning and report cost per correct case with intervals.”
    “What is the difference between an outcome reward model and a process reward model?”

    An outcome reward model scores the final answer; a process reward model scores each reasoning step. The process version tells you where the reasoning went wrong, penalises right answers reached by flawed working, and lets you prune branches during search. The price is step-level labels, which are expensive or noisy.

    “Why can't we just generate 100 answers and pick the best?”

    You can, if 'best' is checkable. With an exact check, such as a recomputed ratio or a unit test, more samples reliably raise accuracy. With a learned judge, selection pressure finds its blind spots and accuracy peaks and then declines. And every sample costs money and latency. So: yes with a calculator, carefully with a model, and measure it.

    “Does a reasoning model make a separate verifier unnecessary?”

    No. It makes errors less frequent but they still occur, and the model's own confidence is a poor guide. The deterministic parts (arithmetic, reconciliation, policy lookups) should still be verified in code. The reasoning model helps most on the parts that cannot be.

    “The vendor says accuracy rises with more thinking time. Should we always set it to maximum?”

    No. The gains depend on difficulty: easy questions saturate almost immediately and can get worse with long reasoning, while hard ones keep improving. I'd set the budget by workflow, measure accuracy and cost per correct case at two or three levels on our own labelled cases, and use routing so that only hard cases get the large budget.

    “Is self-consistency a reliable way to cut hallucination?”

    Partly. Disagreement between samples is a good warning sign, and it can be used to route to a person. Agreement is weaker evidence: a model can be consistently wrong, and voting then reinforces the error. I'd use it as an uncertainty flag and check grounding against the source documents separately.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A twelve-step reasoning chain has 97% reliability per step. How often is the whole chain sound? If a broken chain still gets the final answer right 10% of the time, what share of right answers rest on flawed reasoning?

    Sound: 0.9712 ≈ 0.694. Right final answers: 0.694 + 0.306 × 0.1 ≈ 0.725. Flawed among right: 0.0306 ÷ 0.725 ≈ 4.2%. Small here, but it grows quickly as per-step reliability falls or the lucky-guess rate rises.

    2. A model is right on a task 8% of the time per sample, and you have an exact check. How many samples give at least a 90% chance that one is right?

    Need 1 − 0.92N ≥ 0.9, so 0.92N ≤ 0.1. N ≥ ln 0.1 ÷ ln 0.92 = 2.303 ÷ 0.0834 ≈ 27.6, so 28 samples. With a learned verifier the answer is not available this way; the check's quality limits it.

    3. Why does best-of-N against a learned verifier often get worse at large N?

    The top of a large pool is the extreme of the verifier's scores, and extremes are where the verifier is least reliable. Fluent but wrong answers that happen to look right to it become more likely to be selected than the genuinely correct ones. This is reward over-optimisation: the proxy keeps climbing while true quality peaks and falls.

    4. When does majority voting make accuracy worse as N increases?

    When a wrong answer is more common than the right one. Each sample is more likely to land on that wrong answer than on the right one, so the more you take, the more certain it is that the wrong answer wins the vote. Single-sample accuracy can be identical in the good and bad cases, so measure the top wrong answer's share.

    5. Why are rollout-estimated step labels noisy?

    They label a step by the share of completions from it that reach the right final answer. A wrong step can be followed by completions that happen to recover or guess correctly, giving it a good label, and a right step followed by an unlucky model gets a bad one. The same outcome-versus-process gap from Simulation 1 re-enters through the back door.

    6. Describe budget forcing in two sentences.

    You control reasoning length at decode time: to stop early, you cut the thinking off and force the answer to begin; to think longer, you stop the model from ending its thinking and append "Wait" so it rechecks. Accuracy then follows a smooth rise with the thinking budget until it flattens.

    7. Rank these by how well test-time scaling should work, and say why: recomputing loan instalment schedules; drafting a Shariah-compliance opinion; extracting invoice totals; summarising a complaint.

    Best: instalment schedules (exact formula, so generate-then-recompute). Next: invoice totals (can be reconciled against line items and sampling agreement flags weak cases). Weaker: complaint summaries (groundedness checks help, no exact answer). Weakest: the Shariah opinion, which is a judgement without an automatic check; more samples only produce more fluent text and a scholar must decide.
