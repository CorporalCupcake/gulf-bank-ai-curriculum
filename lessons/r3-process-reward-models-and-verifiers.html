    Buying Accuracy With Thinking: Verifiers, Process Rewards and Test-Time Scaling

    Why checking an answer is easier than producing one, how scoring each reasoning step differs from scoring the final answer, how sampling many answers and voting or picking can lift accuracy, and the specific ways each trick backfires.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model can often be made more accurate by spending more compute at answer time (longer thinking, many samples, search), but every such method is only as good as the thing that judges the answers, so a hard check (code, arithmetic, a rules engine) scales well, a learned judge scales until the model learns to fool it, and a vote scales only when the right answer is already the most common one.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An assistant computes a customer's debt-service ratio (DSR). Salary AED 28,000; car loan AED 2,150; personal finance AED 3,400; proposed Murabaha home finance AED 9,300. It writes a fluent page and answers 45.4%. The right answer is 53.0%: it dropped the car loan somewhere in the page.

    Three fixes: ask for ten attempts and take the most common answer (seven say 53.0%); run each attempt's arithmetic through a calculator; or have a second model flag the step where a figure vanished. All three rest on one idea: producing a good answer is hard, but checking one is often easy. A maze is hard to solve and quick to check. So spend compute on checking.

    Test-time scaling means getting better answers by spending more compute when the question is asked, with no change to the trained weights. Reasoning models are built on it (see A5, Reasoning models and test-time compute).

    • Long chains are fragile. Like a chain of 8 links each 95% strong, the whole is sound only about two times in three (0.958). Harder problems need checking, not just a bigger model.
    • A grade can sit at two places. An outcome grade scores the final answer. A process grade scores each step: far more information per example, far higher labelling cost.
    • A grader is a model of "good", and it can be wrong. Best-of-many, search and training all optimise against it, and the harder you push, the more blind spots you find.
    • More thinking costs tokens and latency. It helps a lot on some problems, not at all on others, and hurts on some.
    Hold onto thisBefore using any test-time trick, ask: what judges the candidates, and how easily is it fooled? Is the right answer already the one the model gives most often? What does each extra sample cost, in AED and seconds?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Outcome rewards, process rewards and what each teaches

    A reward model reads a prompt and response and outputs a quality score. Marking an exam shows the two designs: a mark for the final answer only, or a tick or cross on every line of working.

    • Outcome reward model (ORM). One score for the whole response, trained on right/wrong final answers. Cheap to label. The cost is credit assignment: if a twelve-step solution ends wrong, the label does not say which step failed.
    • Process reward model (PRM). A score after each step (correct, neutral, wrong), so the first wrong step is known. A solution is scored by combining steps, for example the product of step "correct" probabilities, or the minimum.

    Uesato et al. (DeepMind, 2022) found process supervision gave similar final-answer error but far less flawed reasoning behind right answers. Lightman et al. (OpenAI, 2023) trained a PRM on about 800,000 human step labels (PRM800K) and it picked better solutions on the harder MATH benchmark than an ORM. The flawed-but-right share is Simulation 1's last statistic, and it matters where the working is the product, as in a credit memo or AML narrative.

    Step scores also enable search: score a half-finished solution, drop bad branches early and spend compute on promising ones (beam or tree search). An outcome score only exists once the compute is spent.

    Rollout estimation turns an outcome check into step labels by averaging, as in reinforcement learning value estimates (F9), and has the same problem: few rollouts, noisy values.

    Verifiers: from hard checks to learned judges

    A verifier is anything that decides whether a candidate answer is good. Think of a calculator versus a friendly proofreader: what matters is how trustworthy it is.

    VerifierExample in a bankCan it be fooled?
    Exact checkRecompute the DSR in code. Check a Murabaha profit figure against cost × margin × tenor. Run generated SQL and compare the row count with a control total.Not on what it checks. It is silent on what it does not, so the specification can still be wrong.
    Rule and constraint checkDoes the draft cite a limit that exists in the credit policy? Do memo figures match the source table? Is every KYC field present?Only by omission. Coverage is the weakness.
    Learned checker (ORM, PRM, LLM judge)A model scores whether a credit memo's reasoning holds up.Yes. It has blind spots, and fluent, confident errors score well.
    Human reviewThe analyst signs off.Yes: tired, rushed, anchored by the model's confidence (B7).

    Where an exact check exists, you can skip learned reward models and train directly on it: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Run the answer through the checker; reward 1 or 0. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) used rule-based rewards for correctness and format, avoiding neural reward models because they were open to reward hacking and costly to retrain. Maths and code have cheap exact checks, so reasoning models improved fastest there. Policy interpretation or credit judgement has no calculator for "is this a good decision".

    Rank your use cases by the strength of their best check. Strongest: reconciliation, calculation, schema-based extraction, code, SQL. Weakest: advice, summaries, anything needing judgement. Test-time scaling pays most at the top.

    Best-of-N: sample many, let the verifier pick

    Draw N independent answers, score each, return the highest. Like taking many penalty kicks and counting the best one, but only if someone can tell which went in. Two ceilings frame it.

    • Coverage (pass@N). If each sample is right with chance a, the chance at least one of N is right is 1 − (1 − a)N. With a = 30% and N = 16, that is 99.7%. A perfect verifier delivers this. Brown et al. (2024) showed coverage keeps rising over several orders of magnitude of samples on maths and on code with unit tests (the tests are the verifier).
    • Discrimination. A learned verifier gives scores higher on average for correct answers, not certainty. Its pick is right only as often as the scores separate right from wrong.

    The simulation scores right answers d standard deviations above ordinary wrong ones. The key knob is the fooling share: wrong answers that are fluent and confident, and so score higher than correct ones. With few samples a fool is rare. With many, the top score is almost surely a fool, because you went looking for the extreme.

    • The ceiling is a model property; the gap to it is a verifier property. If the model is almost never right (a near zero), no verifier helps. At 5% per sample with an exact check, 64 samples cover 96% of problems.
    • A learned verifier has an optimal N, then declines. This is reward-model over-optimisation (Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2023): the proxy score keeps rising while true quality peaks and falls, like students cramming for one examiner's quirks. Best-of-N is the gentlest selection pressure; RL against a learned reward is stronger and breaks sooner (R3.1–R3.2).
    • Step-level verifiers raise the bar, not remove it. A PRM is harder to fool with a lucky guess, but 2025 papers documented it rewarding verbose or correct-looking steps. DeepSeek listed hard-to-define steps, hard-to-judge intermediate correctness and reward hacking as reasons not to use a PRM.

    Self-consistency: let the model vote

    With no verifier, use agreement. Sample N reasoning paths, extract each final answer, return the most common one (self-consistency, Wang et al., 2022). Right answers converge while errors scatter, like ten people adding a bill: the odd one out is probably wrong. It lifted school-maths accuracy by double-digit points.

    The condition is easy to miss: voting returns the plurality. It helps exactly when the right answer is the most common single answer. If one wrong answer is more common, more samples make the vote reliably wrong. Voting amplifies whatever the model's most popular answer is.

    Weighted voting gives each answer the sum of its verifier scores instead of one vote, combining Simulations 2 and 3. Early stopping samples until the lead is clear: stop when the first three agree, continue on contested ones. Agreement is also an uncertainty signal: a question whose samples split five ways goes to a human.

    Thinking longer: budget forcing, search and spending compute well

    Voting and best-of-N are parallel scaling: many attempts. Sequential scaling lets one attempt think longer, by writing a long chain of thought (A5). The question is how long.

    Budget forcing (s1, Muennighoff et al., 2025) controls length at decode time. To stop early, cut the thinking off and force the answer. To think longer, suppress the end-of-thinking marker and append "Wait"; the model often rereads and corrects itself. A model fine-tuned on only 1,000 reasoning examples went from about 50% to about 57% on AIME 2024 (a maths-competition set), rising smoothly with budget until it flattened.

    Three other families:

    • Search over steps. Beam, tree (including Monte Carlo) and lookahead search use a step verifier to prune. Worth it when the PRM is reliable, wasteful when not.
    • Iterative revision. Write, critique, rewrite. Works when the model can find its own errors; Snell et al. (2024) found it helps more on easier problems.
    • Adaptive allocation. Spend more on hard questions, little on easy ones. Snell et al. found a small model with extra compute beat one about 14 times larger on easy and intermediate questions, not the hardest. Compute substitutes for size only when the question is within reach.

    Longer is not monotonically better. Models "overthink" trivial questions, and Gema et al. (2025, Anthropic-led) found tasks where longer reasoning made models worse: distracted by irrelevant detail, or drifting from a good first answer to a worse one.

    Test-time scaling is a three-way tradeoff of accuracy, cost and latency, with the verifier setting what each extra unit of compute is worth. Order of reliability for a bank: (1) exact verifier plus parallel samples; (2) thinking length matched to difficulty; (3) voting, once checked that the right answer is the plurality; (4) learned verifiers and PRMs, only after measuring where they top out on your data.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Picking by a learned score at very large N

      "We sample 256 and keep the best-scored." The top score increasingly fits the verifier's taste.

      Spot itPlot true accuracy against N on labelled cases. If it flattens or falls past some N, cap N there. Read top-scored answers by hand.
    • Voting when the common answer is wrong

      A systematic misreading (wrong tax treatment, unit confusion) repeats in most samples.

      Spot itCheck single-sample accuracy and the top wrong answer's share. If a wrong answer is the plurality, voting hurts (Simulation 3).
    • Right answers from wrong reasoning

      Outcome accuracy looks fine but the working is flawed, and an auditor or customer will read it.

      Spot itHave an expert read the working in 30 right-answer cases. Report the share with a flaw.
    • Verifying the wrong thing

      The code confirms the arithmetic, not that the right inputs were chosen or the policy cited is current.

      Spot itList what the verifier does not check. Test it with a plausible answer wrong in exactly those ways.
    • Over-thinking and "Wait" loops

      Long budgets on easy tasks waste money and add latency, and sometimes turn a right first answer wrong.

      Spot itCompare accuracy at short and long budgets by difficulty. Check the distribution of thinking length, not only the mean.
    • Reporting gains on a handful of questions

      Best-of-8 beats one sample by 6 points on 50 questions.

      Spot itAsk for the number of questions and a paired interval. At 50 questions one accuracy has an interval of about ±14 points.
    • Benchmark leakage

      A public benchmark was in the training data, so sampling "finds" memorised answers.

      Spot itTest on questions written after the model's cutoff, or on private bank cases.
    • Ignoring cost and latency in the headline

      "Accuracy up 8 points" with 40x the tokens, quoted without the AED figure.

      Spot itAsk for cost per correct case and 95th-percentile latency. Compare with a cheaper model used once.
    • Trusting the shown reasoning as the real reasoning

      A PRM approves clean steps that do not reflect how the model reached the answer.

      Spot itChange a fact in the input and see whether steps and answer change consistently (R4).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A twelve-step chain has 97% reliability per step. How often is the whole chain sound? If a broken chain still gets the final answer right 10% of the time, what share of right answers rest on flawed reasoning?

    Sound: 0.9712 ≈ 0.694. Right answers: 0.694 + 0.306 × 0.1 ≈ 0.725. Flawed among right: 0.0306 ÷ 0.725 ≈ 4.2%. Small here, but it grows fast as step reliability falls or the lucky-guess rate rises.

    2. A model is right 8% of the time per sample, and you have an exact check. How many samples give at least a 90% chance that one is right?

    Need 1 − 0.92N ≥ 0.9, so N ≥ ln 0.1 ÷ ln 0.92 ≈ 27.6: 28 samples. A learned verifier is limited by its own quality, so this does not apply.

    3. Why does best-of-N against a learned verifier often get worse at large N?

    The top of a big pool is the extreme of the verifier's scores, where it is least reliable. Fluent wrong answers that look right to it get picked over correct ones. The proxy keeps climbing while true quality peaks and falls.

    4. When does majority voting make accuracy worse as N increases?

    When a wrong answer is more common than the right one: more samples make that wrong answer win more surely. Single-sample accuracy can be identical in the good and bad cases, so measure the top wrong answer's share.

    5. Why are rollout-estimated step labels noisy?

    They label a step by the share of completions reaching the right answer. A wrong step can be followed by completions that recover or guess right, earning a good label; a right step followed by an unlucky model gets a bad one. The outcome-versus-process gap returns through the back door.

    6. Describe budget forcing in two sentences.

    You control reasoning length at decode time: to stop early, cut the thinking off and force the answer; to think longer, block the end of thinking and append "Wait" so the model rechecks. Accuracy rises smoothly with budget until it flattens.

    7. Rank by how well test-time scaling should work, and say why: recomputing loan instalment schedules; drafting a Shariah-compliance opinion; extracting invoice totals; summarising a complaint.

    Best: instalment schedules (exact formula, so generate-then-recompute). Next: invoice totals (reconcile against line items; disagreement flags weak cases). Weaker: complaint summaries (groundedness checks help, no exact answer). Weakest: the Shariah opinion, a judgement with no automatic check; more samples only give more fluent text, and a scholar must decide.
