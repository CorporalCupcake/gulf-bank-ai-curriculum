    Buying Accuracy With Thinking: Verifiers, Process Rewards and Test-Time Scaling

    Why checking is easier than producing, how step scores differ from answer scores, and when sampling and voting help or backfire.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMore compute at answer time can raise accuracy, but only as far as the judge can be trusted: a hard check scales well, a learned judge scales until it is fooled, and a vote scales only when the right answer is already the most common.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An assistant computes a customer's debt-service ratio. It writes a fluent page and answers 45.4%. The right answer is 53.0%: it dropped the car loan somewhere.

    Fixes: take the most common of ten attempts, run the arithmetic through a calculator, or have a second model flag the step where a figure vanished. All rest on one idea: producing a good answer is hard, but checking one is often easy. Test-time scaling spends extra compute at question time on that checking (see A5).

    • Long chains are fragile. Even at 95% per step, an 8-step chain is sound only about two times in three.
    • A grader can be wrong. The harder you optimise against it, the more blind spots you find.
    Hold onto thisBefore using any test-time trick, ask: what judges the candidates, and how easily is it fooled?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Outcome rewards and process rewards

    A reward model scores a response. An outcome model (ORM) gives one mark for the final answer, which is cheap but does not say which step failed. A process model (PRM) ticks or crosses every line of working, so the first wrong step is known. Step scores also let a search drop bad branches early, and they catch right answers built on flawed working, which matters in a credit memo.

    Where step labels come from

    Humans mark each step: best quality, but slow and costly. Rollout estimation finishes the solution many times and calls a step good if most reach the right answer: cheap but noisy. A model as judge writes a critique per step: few labels needed, but fluent errors can talk it round.

    Verifiers: from hard checks to learned judges

    A verifier decides whether a candidate answer is good. What matters is how trustworthy it is, like a calculator versus a friendly proofreader.

    • Exact check: recompute the DSR in code. It cannot be fooled on what it checks, and is silent on the rest.
    • Rule check: does the draft cite a limit that exists in policy? Weak where coverage is thin.
    • Learned checker (ORM, PRM, LLM judge): has blind spots, and confident errors score well.
    • Human review: can be anchored by the model's confidence (B7).

    Where an exact check exists, you can train directly on it: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. This is why reasoning models improved fastest at maths and code. Rank use cases by their best check: calculation and SQL at the top, advice and summaries at the bottom.

    Best-of-N: sample many, let the verifier pick

    Draw N answers, score each and return the highest. A perfect verifier reaches the ceiling: the chance that at least one sample is right. A learned verifier only scores right answers higher on average. Fluent, confident wrong answers can outscore correct ones, and with many samples the top score is almost surely one of them.

    • The ceiling is the model's; the gap to it is the verifier's. If the model is almost never right, no verifier helps.
    • A learned verifier has an optimal N, then declines. The proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls.

    Self-consistency: let the model vote

    With no verifier, sample many paths and return the most common final answer. Right answers converge while errors scatter. But voting returns the plurality: if a wrong answer leads, more samples make the vote reliably wrong.

    Agreement is also an uncertainty signal: a question whose samples split five ways goes to a human.

    Thinking longer: budget and search

    Voting and best-of-N use many attempts. Alternatively one attempt can think longer (A5). Budget forcing cuts thinking off early, or appends "Wait" to make the model recheck.

    Longer is not always better: models overthink easy questions, sometimes drifting from a right first answer to a wrong one. Match the budget to the difficulty.

    Most reliable first: exact verifier plus samples; thinking matched to difficulty; voting once the right answer is the plurality; learned verifiers only after measuring where they top out.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Learned score at very large N

      The top score increasingly fits the verifier's taste, not the truth.

      Spot itPlot true accuracy against N on labelled cases and cap N where it flattens or falls.
    • Voting when the common answer is wrong

      A systematic misreading repeats in most samples, so voting locks it in.

      Spot itCheck the top wrong answer's share. If it is the plurality, voting hurts.
    • Verifying the wrong thing

      The code checks the arithmetic, not that the right inputs or current policy were used.

      Spot itList what the verifier does not check and test it with answers wrong in exactly those ways.
    • Over-thinking and hidden cost

      Long budgets on easy tasks waste money and sometimes turn a right answer wrong. Gains are often quoted without the cost.

      Spot itAsk for cost per correct case and accuracy by difficulty and budget.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What is the difference between an outcome reward and a process reward?

    An outcome reward scores only the final answer. A process reward scores each step, so it shows where reasoning went wrong.

    2. Why does best-of-N against a learned verifier often get worse at large N?

    The top of a big pool is the extreme of the verifier's scores, where it is least reliable. Fluent wrong answers get picked over correct ones.

    3. When does majority voting make accuracy worse as N grows?

    When a wrong answer is more common than the right one. More samples make that wrong answer win more surely, so measure the top wrong answer's share, not just single-sample accuracy.

    4. Rank by how well test-time scaling should work: recomputing instalment schedules, extracting invoice totals, summarising a complaint, drafting a Shariah-compliance opinion.

    Instalment schedules first, then invoice totals (both can be recomputed), then the summary. The Shariah opinion is last: no automatic check, so more samples only give more fluent text.
