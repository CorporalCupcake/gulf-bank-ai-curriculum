    B3.1 – B3.3 Day one About 40 minutes

    Knowing the Customer: Due Diligence, Digital Identity and Entity Resolution

    How a bank decides who a customer is and how risky the relationship is, how eKYC proves a real person is present, and how five messy records get recognised as one customer. Three problems, one pattern: weigh evidence, choose a threshold, and live with the errors on both sides.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceKnow-your-customer is three linked judgements, who is this (identity and entity resolution), is the person real and present (eKYC), and how risky is the relationship (risk rating), and each is evidence combined into a score, cut at a threshold, with a human on the borderline and an error on each side that costs something different.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer taps through an app in Riyadh and asks to open an account. Before taking a dirham, the bank must answer three questions the law requires.

    • Who is this? Does the document match the person? Are they already a customer under another spelling? Who owns the applying company?
    • Are they really here? A passport photo is easy to steal. Is a live person on the other end, not a photo, replay or deepfake?
    • How risky is the relationship? A salaried teacher and a trading company with a three-layer offshore owner are different risks. Look harder at the second, and keep looking.

    These are customer due diligence and risk rating (B3.1), eKYC and national digital identity (B3.2) and entity resolution (B3.3). Each collects imperfect evidence, converts it to a number and cuts that number somewhere, like a smoke alarm: too sensitive and it goes off over toast, too dull and it misses the fire. Cut loosely and criminals or duplicates get in. Cut tightly and honest customers are turned away and analysts drown.

    Two facts keep this honest. Most customers are fine. With 99.5% legitimate applicants, a check with a small false-alarm rate still produces mostly false alarms (the base-rate problem of F3.5). And KYC is a legal duty. Every rating, rejection and merge must be explainable to a CBUAE or SAMA examiner.

    Hold onto thisEvery KYC step trades two errors: letting a bad actor through, and blocking a good customer. A model moves you along that trade-off; it does not remove it. The business chooses where to stand, knowing what each error costs.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B3.1 Customer due diligence and risk rating

    Customer due diligence (CDD) is what a bank must do before and during a relationship. The standard is FATF Recommendation 10, which Gulf regulators follow:

    • Identify the customer and verify identity from reliable sources.
    • Identify the beneficial owner, the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company.
    • Understand the purpose of the relationship.
    • Monitor it, checking activity fits what you know.

    Related duties: enhanced measures for politically exposed persons (PEPs) (Rec. 12), sanctions screening (next lesson in the module) and ownership transparency (Rec. 24 and 25).

    The risk-based approach scales effort to risk, like airport security lanes. Each customer is rated and gets:

    • Simplified due diligence where risk is demonstrably low and rules allow.
    • Standard CDD for the ordinary case.
    • Enhanced due diligence (EDD) for high risk: source of wealth and funds evidence, senior approval, closer monitoring.

    The rating comes from a customer risk assessment, usually a weighted scorecard. Factor groups: customer (type, occupation, ownership, PEP status), geography, product (private banking, trade finance and cash-heavy remittance are riskier than a salary account), delivery channel (face to face, remote, introduced) and expected activity. Each level earns points, each group has a weight, and the weighted sum maps to Low, Medium or High.

    score=∑kwk·ℓk3

    Each factor k has weight wk (weights sum to 100) and level ℓk from 0 (low) to 3 (very high). The score runs 0 to 100 and is cut into bands. Weights and cut-offs are judgement calls in policy; a validator will ask who chose them and how they were tested.

    Reading it: a score of 40 means the customer earns 40% of the worst possible points. Moving one factor from level 1 to 2 adds w/3 points, so the heaviest factors move the rating most. Two features matter more than the weights:

    • Overrides. Some facts decide the outcome whatever the sum. A PEP is High by policy; a confirmed sanctions match means no relationship; adverse media may force at least Medium.
    • Averaging dilutes. Like a student with one failed exam and four good ones, a customer with one terrible factor can land in Low. Policies add "any single factor at level 3 forces review". The simulation shows both.

    Scorecards are stable and explainable but crude. Banks use ML in three ways, keeping the scorecard as a floor or challenger:

    • Calibrating weights against outcomes (suspicious activity reports, exits, unusual behaviour).
    • Dynamic rating: re-scoring when behaviour changes, such as a salary account suddenly receiving cross-border flows, instead of waiting for the annual review.
    • Triage: ranking High and borderline customers so analysts see the riskiest first.

    Caution from imbalanced data: labels are rare, late and exist only where someone looked. A model trained on past reports learns what the bank already noticed.

    Beneficial ownership is a graph problem. Follow ownership up through holding companies to the natural persons. A common "owns" threshold is 25% (check the current local rule), but control can sit with someone who owns nothing on paper. Layers across jurisdictions are where risk hides, and where graph methods help. Fairness matters too. Nationality and geography are legitimate factors, but leaning on them can lock whole groups out (see fairness trade-offs), so be able to write the reason for each factor in a sentence.

    B3.2 eKYC and national digital identity (in brief)

    eKYC is remote identity verification, like showing your ID to a bouncer over video. It has three layers, each with its own error rate.

    • Document check. Read the Emirates ID, Saudi national ID, iqama or passport (OCR, machine-readable zone, chip), check security features and consistency. Arabic OCR quality matters: Arabic names must be matched to Latin transliterations.
    • Face match. Compare the selfie to the document portrait. A neural network turns each face into an embedding vector and the system measures the distance (the geometry of F1.1). Close enough is a match.
    • Liveness (presentation attack detection). Is a live person there, or a printed photo, screen replay or mask? Passive liveness reads texture, depth and reflections; active liveness asks the user to blink or turn.

    Injection attacks are the newer threat. The attacker feeds synthetic video straight into the app, bypassing the camera, and a liveness model trained on printed photos can score it as perfectly live. Defences look at the device, camera driver and session, not just pixels (see deepfakes against eKYC and device intelligence).

    National digital identity changes the economics. UAE Pass (UAE) and Nafath (Saudi Arabia) confirm identity in an app tied to the state system. The state has already done the document and face work against authoritative records, and the bank receives verified attributes to pre-fill. FATF's 2020 digital identity guidance says a reliable digital ID can be used for CDD under a risk-based approach, but assurance levels matter: how strongly the identity was proofed and the person authenticated. It does not remove the duty to rate, screen and understand the account's purpose. Identity is one input to risk, not the answer.

    A genuine customer who fails is not a statistic: they abandon the app, call the contact centre or go to a competitor. Many banks route borderline cases to a video call or branch visit instead of rejecting, the same two-threshold design as entity resolution below. And measure error by outcome, not vendor claim. Vendors quote rates on their own test sets, in lab lighting. Test across skin tones, ages, lighting, Arabic and non-Arabic names and devices, and see the NIST face-recognition evaluations for demographic variation.

    B3.3 Entity resolution

    The bank holds "Mohammed Al Mansoori", "Muhammad Almansouri", "محمد المنصوري" and "M. A. Mansouri", each with a different phone and address. One person, two or four? Entity resolution (record linkage, deduplication) decides which records are the same real-world customer, company or device. It is like merging duplicate contacts on your phone, at 10 million contacts. Everything else depends on it: a rating on half a customer is wrong, a sanctions hit missed through a spelling variant is a breach, and a fraud ring looks like separate customers.

    Comparing every pair is hopeless: 10 million customers give about 5 × 1013 pairs. The standard pipeline has four stages.

    1. Standardise. Clean names (strip titles, normalise Arabic letter variants such as alef forms, transliteration), dates, phones and addresses.
    2. Block. Generate only plausible pairs that share something cheap: date of birth, ID number, or a phonetic code of the surname. Use several rules together so one typo does not lose a pair. This cuts 5 × 1013 to millions or billions.
    3. Compare and score. Compare each field and compute a match score.
    4. Decide and cluster. Above an upper threshold, link automatically; between the thresholds, send to a human; below the lower, leave apart. Then group links into entities, carefully: if A matches B and B matches C, are A and C the same?

    The classic scorer is the Fellegi-Sunter model (1969), still inside open tools such as Splink (UK Ministry of Justice, used on 100-million-plus record jobs). It is Bayes' rule (F1.4) applied field by field. Each field needs two numbers:

    wagree=log2muwdisagree=log21−m1−u

    m: chance the field agrees when the records are the same person (below 1 because of typos and changes). u: chance it agrees by coincidence for different people. Rare value (tiny u) means strong evidence. Add the field weights, then multiply the prior odds by 2total to get posterior odds.

    Each field multiplies the odds (the likelihood ratio of F1.4). Reading the numbers: a matching Emirates ID number (1 in 100,000 by coincidence) is worth about 16.6 bits, roughly doubling the odds 16 times. A matching first name is worth a few bits. A missing field adds nothing. Two traps:

    • The prior matters. True matches are rare among candidate pairs, so a decent score still gives a modest posterior unless the weight is large.
    • The value matters, not just the field. Agreeing on "Mohammed Ahmed" is weak; agreeing on a rare surname is strong. A birth date of 1 January is weak because systems use it as a placeholder for "unknown". Good systems learn u per value (term-frequency adjustment).

    The threshold is again a base-rate problem. Say blocking leaves a million candidate pairs, 2,000 of them real matches. A small tail of the 998,000 non-matches scores high by accident, and that tail is large in absolute terms.

    Modern entity resolution adds ML on top of this skeleton. Supervised matchers (gradient-boosted trees over edit distance, phonetic match and embedding similarity) learn weights from labelled pairs. Language models can compare records as text (the "Ditto" line of work); LLMs work zero-shot on messy records but are slow and costly per pair, so keep them for the borderline band. Embeddings and approximate nearest-neighbour search can also drive blocking. For companies, hierarchy (subsidiary, branch, group) matters for ownership and exposure and is not "same entity". In Arabic data the hard part is usually names: variant transliterations, patronymic chains ("bin", "ibn", "Al"), name order and diacritics. Everything lives or dies on cleaning.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • An average hides the worst factor

      One very-high factor among benign ones scores Medium or Low.

      Spot itList customers with any top-level factor rated below High. If long, add a single-factor escalation rule.
    • Everyone lands in one band

      85% Medium, so reviews never discriminate.

      Spot itPlot the score distribution. A rating that does not spread customers cannot allocate effort.
    • Stale ratings

      Risk set at onboarding and never updated.

      Spot itCompare current activity with expected activity from onboarding; ask when the rating last changed.
    • Trusting the digital ID for everything

      A UAE Pass login is read as "customer checked, risk fine".

      Spot itCheck that rating, screening and purpose-of-account steps run independently of identity.
    • Error rates from the vendor's lab

      "99.9% accurate" on a clean test set, then many real customers rejected.

      Spot itAsk for rejection and acceptance rates on your own traffic, split by device, light, age, skin tone and nationality.
    • Liveness tuned only against print and replay

      Injected or generated video passes as live.

      Spot itAsk whether injection attacks are tested and which device or session signals are used.
    • A wrong merge of two customers

      An over-eager threshold links namesakes; one inherits the other's sanctions alert, balance or fraud flag.

      Spot itSample auto-links; track the wrong-merge rate; make merges reversible and audited.
    • Transliteration blindness

      Matching only in Latin script misses the Arabic record of the same person.

      Spot itTest known-same pairs in both scripts and variant spellings; check recall by name origin.
    • Placeholder values treated as evidence

      1 January birth dates, "0000000" phones, the branch address.

      Spot itFind values that appear unusually often in a field and give them low or zero weight.
    • Clusters that chain

      A matches B, B matches C, so all merge though A and C differ.

      Spot itCheck cluster sizes; very large ones are almost always chaining.
    • Labels that reflect past scrutiny

      A model trained on suspicious activity reports repeats the bank's blind spots.

      Spot itAsk how labels were produced and whether an unreviewed random sample exists.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. With the Simulation 1 weights (30, 25, 15, 10, 20), a customer has levels 1, 3, 0, 0, 1. What is the score, and what is the problem?

    30×1/3 = 10; 25×3/3 = 25; 0; 0; 20×1/3 = 6.67. Total 41.7: Medium with the default cut-offs. Geography is at its worst level and the average has diluted it. A single-factor rule would force at least High review.

    2. Why is a PEP rated High even if the weighted score is 8?

    PEP handling is a rule set by policy and regulation (FATF Recommendation 12), not a prediction. The corruption exposure and source-of-wealth questions do not depend on how the other factors add up, so an override sets a minimum rating.

    3. An ID-number field has m = 0.98 and u = 0.00001. What are the agree and disagree weights?

    Agree: log2(0.98 ÷ 0.00001) = log2(98,000) ≈ +16.6 bits. Disagree: log2(0.02 ÷ 0.99999) ≈ −5.6 bits. Disagreement is only mildly negative because ID numbers are sometimes mistyped or reissued.

    4. Two records agree on a very common name (u = 0.02, m = 0.95) and on a 1 January birth date (u = 0.03, m = 0.97). The prior of a match is 0.1%. What is the posterior?

    Weights: log2(0.95 ÷ 0.02) ≈ 5.57; log2(0.97 ÷ 0.03) ≈ 5.01. Total 10.58 bits, a likelihood ratio of about 1,535. Prior odds ≈ 0.001, so posterior odds ≈ 1.54 and probability ≈ 61%. This goes to review, not auto-link.

    5. Of 10,000 attempts, 100 are attacks. A check rejects 5% of genuine customers and passes 5% of attacks. How many genuine customers are blocked, and what share of passes are attackers?

    Blocked: 5% of 9,900 = 495. Attackers through: 5% of 100 = 5. Passes are 9,405 genuine and 5 attackers, so about 0.05% of passes are attackers. Tightening further blocks even more good customers, which is why defences are layered.

    6. Why is "just raise the liveness threshold" a poor defence against deepfakes or injected video?

    The attacker's scores move up into the genuine distribution, so no threshold separates them without rejecting many genuine users. You need another signal: device and camera integrity, session behaviour, or national digital ID.

    7. Why is blocking necessary, and what does it risk?

    All pairs among 10 million records is about 5 × 1013 comparisons. Blocking keeps only pairs sharing a cheap key. It can miss true matches whose key differs (a typo or transliteration variant), so several overlapping rules are used.

    8. In Simulation 4, why does fewer true matches make wrong merges a larger share of auto-links at the same thresholds?

    Wrong merges come from the non-match tail times the huge non-match count, which does not change. Correct links fall with the number of true matches. So precision falls. It is the base-rate effect from imbalanced data.
