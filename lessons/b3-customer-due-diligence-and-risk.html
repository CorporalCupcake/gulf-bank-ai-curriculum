    Knowing the Customer: Due Diligence, Digital Identity and Entity Resolution

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer taps through an app in Riyadh to open an account. Before taking a dirham, the bank must answer three questions.

    • Who is this? Does the document match the person? Are they already a customer under another spelling?
    • Are they really here? Is a live person on the other end, not a photo, replay or deepfake?
    • How risky is the relationship? A salaried teacher and an offshore-owned trading company need different attention.

    These are customer due diligence and risk rating (B3.1), eKYC and national digital identity (B3.2) and entity resolution (B3.3). Each turns imperfect evidence into a score and cuts it somewhere, like a smoke alarm: too sensitive and it goes off over toast, too dull and it misses the fire.

    Two facts keep this honest. Most customers are fine, so even a small false-alarm rate produces mostly false alarms (the base-rate problem of F3.5). And KYC is a legal duty: every rating, rejection and merge must be explainable to a CBUAE or SAMA examiner.

    Hold onto thisEvery KYC step trades two errors: letting a bad actor through, and blocking a good customer. A model moves you along that trade-off; it does not remove it. The business chooses where to stand, knowing what each error costs.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B3.1 Customer due diligence and risk rating

    Customer due diligence (CDD) (FATF Recommendation 10) means verifying who the customer is, finding the beneficial owner (the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company), understanding why they bank here, and monitoring that activity fits.

    The risk-based approach scales effort to risk, like airport security lanes. Most customers get standard CDD; high-risk ones, including politically exposed persons (PEPs), get enhanced due diligence (EDD): source-of-wealth evidence, senior approval and closer monitoring.

    The rating usually comes from a weighted scorecard over customer type, geography, product, channel and expected activity. Weights and cut-offs are policy judgements, so a validator will ask who chose them. Two features matter more than the weights:

    • Overrides. Some facts decide the outcome whatever the sum. A PEP is High by policy; a confirmed sanctions match means no relationship.
    • Averaging dilutes. Like a student with one failed exam and four good ones, a customer with one terrible factor can land in Low. Hence rules like "any factor at its worst level forces review".

    Scorecards are explainable but crude. ML can calibrate weights, re-score when behaviour changes (a salary account suddenly receiving cross-border flows) and rank borderline customers. But labels exist only where someone looked, so a model trained on past reports learns what the bank already noticed (see imbalanced data).

    Beneficial ownership is a graph problem. Follow ownership up through holding companies to the natural persons; layers across jurisdictions are where risk hides. Fairness matters too. Leaning on nationality or geography can lock out whole groups (see fairness trade-offs), so be able to justify each factor in a sentence.

    B3.2 eKYC and national digital identity (in brief)

    eKYC is remote identity verification, like showing your ID to a bouncer over video. It has three layers, each with its own error rate.

    • Document check. Read the ID and test its security features. Arabic OCR quality matters, because Arabic names must match Latin transliterations.
    • Face match. A neural network turns each face into a vector, and the selfie and the document portrait must land close together (F1.1).
    • Liveness. Is a live person there, or a photo, replay or mask?

    Injection attacks feed synthetic video straight into the app, bypassing the camera, and a liveness model trained on printed photos can score it as live. Defences look at the device and session, not just pixels (see deepfakes against eKYC).

    National digital identity changes the economics. UAE Pass and Nafath confirm identity through the state system, so the bank receives verified attributes. It still must rate, screen and understand the account's purpose. Identity is one input to risk, not the answer.

    A genuine customer who fails abandons the app, so many banks route borderline cases to a video call instead of rejecting. And measure error on your own traffic, not the vendor's claim: test across skin tones, ages, devices and names.

    B3.3 Entity resolution

    The bank holds "Mohammed Al Mansoori", "Muhammad Almansouri", "محمد المنصوري" and "M. A. Mansouri", each with a different phone and address. One person, two or four? Entity resolution decides which records are the same real-world customer, company or device, like merging duplicate phone contacts at 10 million contacts. A rating on half a customer is wrong, and a sanctions hit missed through a spelling variant is a breach.

    Comparing every pair is hopeless (about 5 × 1013 pairs for 10 million customers), so the standard pipeline has four stages.

    1. Standardise. Clean names, dates, phones and addresses.
    2. Block. Compare only plausible pairs that share something cheap, such as date of birth or a phonetic code of the surname. Use several rules so one typo does not lose a pair.
    3. Score. Compare each field and compute a match score.
    4. Decide and cluster. Link automatically above an upper threshold, send to a human between thresholds, leave apart below.

    The classic scorer is the Fellegi-Sunter model, which is Bayes' rule (F1.4) applied field by field. Each field has m, how often it agrees for the same person, and u, how often it agrees by coincidence. A rare value (small u) is strong evidence. Agreement adds evidence bits, disagreement subtracts, a missing field adds nothing, and each bit doubles the odds. A matching ID number is worth about 16 bits; a matching first name a few. Two traps:

    • The prior matters. True matches are rare, so a decent score still gives a modest probability.
    • The value matters, not just the field. Agreeing on "Mohammed Ahmed" is weak, and 1 January is weak because systems use it as a placeholder.

    The threshold is again a base-rate problem. With a million candidate pairs and 2,000 real matches, even a small high-scoring tail of the 998,000 non-matches is large in absolute terms.

    ML matchers can learn weights from labelled pairs, and LLMs can compare messy records but are slow, so keep them for the borderline band. A subsidiary is not the "same entity" as its parent. In Arabic data the hard part is usually names (transliterations, "bin", "Al", name order), so everything lives or dies on cleaning.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • An average hides the worst factor, or everyone lands in one band

      One very-high factor among benign ones scores Medium, or 85% of customers sit in Medium.

      Spot itList customers with a worst-level factor but a low rating, and plot the score distribution.
    • Stale ratings, or trusting the digital ID for everything

      Risk is set at onboarding and never updated, or a UAE Pass login is read as "risk fine".

      Spot itAsk when the rating last changed, and check rating and screening run independently of identity.
    • Error rates from the vendor's lab

      "99.9% accurate" on a clean test set, then many real customers rejected, or injected video passes as live.

      Spot itAsk for rates on your own traffic, split by device, light, age and nationality, and whether injection attacks are tested.
    • A wrong merge of two customers

      An over-eager threshold links namesakes; one inherits the other's sanctions alert, balance or fraud flag.

      Spot itSample auto-links; track the wrong-merge rate; make merges reversible and audited.
    • Transliteration blindness and placeholder values

      Matching only in Latin script misses the Arabic record, while 1 January birth dates are treated as evidence.

      Spot itTest known-same pairs across scripts, and down-weight values that appear unusually often.
    • Clusters that chain, and labels that reflect past scrutiny

      A matches B and B matches C, so all merge though A and C differ. Or a model trained on past reports repeats the bank's blind spots.

      Spot itCheck cluster sizes (very large ones are almost always chaining), and ask how labels were produced.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A customer has a very high geography factor but low scores on everything else, and the weighted total lands in Medium. What is the problem and the usual fix?

    The average has diluted the one factor that matters. A single-factor rule would force at least High review.

    2. Why is a PEP rated High even if the weighted score is low?

    PEP handling is a rule set by regulation, not a prediction. The corruption exposure does not depend on how the other factors add up, so an override sets a minimum rating.

    3. An ID-number field almost never agrees by coincidence (tiny u) and usually agrees for the same person (high m). What does agreement and disagreement tell you?

    Agreement is very strong evidence. Disagreement is only mildly negative, because ID numbers are sometimes mistyped or reissued.

    4. Two records agree on a very common name and on a 1 January birth date. The prior chance of a match is 0.1%. Auto-link, review or leave apart, and why?

    Review. Both agreements are weak because the values are common or placeholders, and the prior is low. That is too much to ignore and too little to merge.

    5. Of 10,000 attempts, 100 are attacks. A check rejects 5% of genuine customers and passes 5% of attacks. Is it good, and what is the cost?

    It blocks about 495 genuine customers to stop 95 attackers, and 5 still get through. Tightening further blocks even more good customers, which is why defences are layered.

    6. Why is "just raise the liveness threshold" a poor defence against deepfakes or injected video?

    The attacker's scores move into the genuine range, so no threshold separates them. You need another signal: device integrity or national digital ID.

    7. Why is blocking necessary, and what does it risk?

    All pairs among 10 million records is impossible. Blocking keeps pairs sharing a cheap key, but can miss matches whose key differs (a typo), so use several overlapping rules.

    8. In Simulation 4, why does having fewer true matches make wrong merges a larger share of auto-links at the same thresholds?

    Wrong merges come from the huge non-match count, which does not change, while correct links fall with true matches. It is the base-rate effect.
