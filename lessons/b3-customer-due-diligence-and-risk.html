    B3.1 – B3.3 Day one About 40 minutes

    Knowing the Customer: Due Diligence, Digital Identity and Entity Resolution

    How a bank decides who a customer is and how risky the relationship is, how eKYC proves a real person is present, and how five messy records get recognised as one customer. Three problems, one pattern: weigh evidence, choose a threshold, and live with the errors on both sides.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceKnow-your-customer is three linked judgements, who is this (identity and entity resolution), is the person real and present (eKYC), and how risky is the relationship (risk rating), and each is evidence combined into a score, cut at a threshold, with a human on the borderline and an error on each side that costs something different.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer walks into a branch in Dubai, or taps through an app in Riyadh, and asks to open an account. Before the bank takes a dirham, it has to answer three questions it is legally required to answer.

    • Who is this, really? Is the name on the document the person in front of us? Is that person already a customer under a different spelling? Who owns the company that is applying?
    • Are they genuinely here? A passport photo is easy to steal. The bank needs to know a live person matching it is on the other end, not a photo, a replayed video or a deepfake.
    • How dangerous is this relationship? A salaried teacher with one account and a trading company with a three-layer offshore owner are not the same risk. The bank must look harder at the second, and keep looking.

    These map to the lesson's three topics: customer due diligence and risk rating (B3.1), eKYC and national digital identity (B3.2) and entity resolution (B3.3). They look different. Underneath they share one structure. Each collects imperfect evidence, converts it to a number, and has to cut that number somewhere. Cut too loosely and criminals get in or duplicate profiles slip past. Cut too tightly and honest customers are turned away, queues swell and analysts drown.

    Two facts keep this honest. First, most customers are fine. The base-rate problem from F3.5 applies here as everywhere: when 99.5% of applicants are legitimate, a check with a small false-alarm rate still produces mostly false alarms. Second, KYC is a legal duty, not a feature. Models help, but every rating, rejection and merge has to be explainable to an examiner from the Central Bank of the UAE or SAMA, so the decision logic must be inspectable.

    Hold onto thisEvery step in KYC trades two errors against each other: letting a bad actor through, and blocking or annoying a good customer. A model does not remove that trade-off. It moves you along it, and the business must choose where to stand, knowing the cost of each error.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B3.1 Customer due diligence and risk rating

    Customer due diligence (CDD) is the set of things a bank must do before and during a relationship. The international standard is FATF Recommendation 10, which Gulf regulators follow in their own rules: identify the customer and verify identity from reliable sources; identify the beneficial owner, the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company; understand the purpose and intended nature of the relationship; and keep monitoring it, checking that activity fits what you know. Related duties sit around it: enhanced measures for politically exposed persons (PEPs) (Recommendation 12), screening against sanctions lists (the next lesson in the module on name matching), and beneficial-ownership transparency (Recommendations 24 and 25).

    The key idea is the risk-based approach. The bank does not apply the same effort to everyone. It rates each customer and scales the diligence:

    • Simplified due diligence where the risk is demonstrably low and rules allow it.
    • Standard CDD for the ordinary case.
    • Enhanced due diligence (EDD) for high risk: source of wealth and source of funds evidence, senior-management approval, closer and more frequent monitoring.

    The rating is produced by a customer risk assessment, almost always a weighted scorecard. Factors come in the groups regulators expect: customer (type, occupation, ownership structure, PEP status), geography (residence, nationality, where funds come from or go), product and service (private banking, trade finance and cash-intensive remittance carry more risk than a salary account), delivery channel (face to face, remote, introduced by a third party) and expected activity. Each factor level earns points, each group has a weight, and the weighted sum maps to Low, Medium or High.

    score=∑kwk·ℓk3

    Each factor k has a weight wk (the weights sum to 100) and a level ℓk from 0 (low) to 3 (very high). A factor at its worst earns its full weight. The score runs from 0 to 100 and is cut into bands. This is how many bank scorecards are built. The weights and cut-offs are judgement calls written into policy, and a model validator will ask who chose them and how they were tested.

    Two structural features matter more than the weights. Overrides: some facts decide the outcome regardless of the sum. A PEP is rated High by policy. A confirmed sanctions match means no relationship at all. Adverse media may force a minimum of Medium. Averaging dilutes: an additive score lets many low factors hide one terrible one. A customer with a very-high geography factor and everything else benign can land in Low. That is why policies add "any single factor at level 3 forces review" rules. The simulation lets you see both.

    Where does machine learning fit? Rating by scorecard is stable and explainable but crude. Banks now use ML in three ways, usually with the scorecard kept as a floor or a challenger. Calibrating the weights against outcomes: which customers later produced suspicious activity reports, were exited or showed unusual behaviour. Dynamic rating: re-scoring when behaviour changes (a salary account suddenly receiving cross-border flows) instead of waiting for the annual review. Triage: ranking the High and borderline customers so analysts review the riskiest first. The warning from imbalanced data returns: the outcome labels are rare, late, and exist only where someone looked. A model trained on past suspicious activity reports learns what the bank already noticed.

    Two more things to hold. Beneficial ownership is a graph problem. For a company, the bank must follow ownership upward through holding companies to the natural persons who own or control it. A common legal threshold for "owns" is 25% (check the current text of the local rule), but control can sit with a person who owns nothing on paper. Layers across jurisdictions are where the risk hides, and where graph methods help. Fairness matters too. Nationality and geography are legitimate risk factors, yet a rating that quietly leans on them can lock whole groups out of banking. Examiners and customers both ask about it (see fairness definitions and trade-offs), so keep the reason for each factor writeable in a sentence.

    B3.2 eKYC and national digital identity (in brief)

    eKYC is doing identity verification remotely. It splits into three layers, each with its own error rate.

    • Document check. Read the Emirates ID, Saudi national ID, iqama or passport (OCR, plus the machine-readable zone and chip where available), check security features, and test whether the data is consistent. This is where Arabic OCR quality matters: names arrive in Arabic script and must be matched to a Latin transliteration.
    • Face match. Compare the selfie to the portrait on the document, or to a trusted database. A neural network turns each face into an embedding vector and the system measures the distance between them (the geometry of F1.1). Close enough counts as a match.
    • Liveness / presentation attack detection. Is a live person in front of the camera, or a printed photo, a screen replay or a mask? Passive liveness analyses the video for texture, depth cues and light reflections. Active liveness asks the user to blink or turn their head.

    A newer layer sits underneath: injection attacks. Instead of holding a fake up to the camera, the attacker feeds a synthetic video stream directly into the app, bypassing the camera. Liveness models trained to spot printed photos can score such a feed as perfectly live. Defences look at the device, the camera driver and the session, not just the pixels (see deepfakes against eKYC and device intelligence).

    National digital identity changes the economics. In the UAE, UAE Pass is the national digital ID; in Saudi Arabia, Nafath serves a similar role, with the person confirming their login or consent in an app tied to the national identity system. When the bank accepts the national ID provider's assertion, the heavy work of document and face checking has already been done once, by the state, against authoritative records, and the bank receives verified attributes it can pre-fill. The FATF's guidance on digital identity (2020) says a reliable digital ID system can be used for CDD under a risk-based approach, and that assurance levels matter: the bank must understand how strongly the identity was proofed and how strongly the person authenticated. It does not remove the bank's duty to rate the customer, screen them and understand the purpose of the account. Identity is one input to risk, not the answer to it.

    The picture also carries the commercial point. A genuine customer who fails the check is not just a statistic: they abandon the app, call the contact centre or go to a competitor. Many banks route borderline cases to a manual video call or a branch visit instead of rejecting them, which is the same two-threshold design you will see in entity resolution below. And whichever of the two errors is larger, measure it by outcome, not by vendor claim. Vendors quote error rates on their own test sets, in lab lighting, on faces that may not look like your customer base. Insist on testing across skin tones, ages, lighting, Arabic and non-Arabic names and device models, and look at the NIST face-recognition evaluations for how accuracy varies across demographic groups.

    B3.3 Entity resolution

    The bank's systems hold records for "Mohammed Al Mansoori", "Muhammad Almansouri", "محمد المنصوري" and "M. A. Mansouri", with different phone numbers and a different address in each. Are they one person, two or four? Entity resolution (also called record linkage or deduplication) decides which records refer to the same real-world customer, company or device. It underpins everything else in this module. A risk rating on a half-view of the customer is wrong. A sanctions hit that is missed because the name was spelt differently in one record is a breach. A fraud ring is invisible if its members each look like separate customers.

    Comparing every record to every other is hopeless at scale: 10 million customers give 107 × (107 − 1) ÷ 2 ≈ 5 × 1013 pairs. So the standard pipeline has four stages.

    1. Standardise. Clean names (strip titles, normalise Arabic letter variants such as the different forms of alef, transliteration), dates, phone formats and addresses.
    2. Block. Generate only plausible candidate pairs, those sharing something cheap: the same date of birth, the same ID number, the same first three letters of the surname in a phonetic code. Several blocking rules are used together, because a typo in one key must not lose the pair. Blocking cuts the 5 × 1013 to millions or billions.
    3. Compare and score. For each candidate pair, compare each field and compute a match score.
    4. Decide and cluster. Above an upper threshold, link automatically. Between a lower and an upper one, send to a human. Below the lower, leave apart. Then group linked pairs into entities, which needs care: if A matches B and B matches C, are A and C the same?

    The classic way to score is the Fellegi-Sunter model (1969), still the engine inside open tools such as Splink, built by the UK Ministry of Justice and used at scale on 100-million-plus record linkage jobs. It is Bayes' rule (F1.4) applied field by field. For each field you need two numbers:

    wagree=log2muwdisagree=log21−m1−u

    m is the chance the field agrees when the two records really are the same person (typos and changes make it less than 1). u is the chance it agrees by coincidence when they are different people. Agreement on a rare value, with tiny u, is strong evidence; agreement on a common one is weak. Add the field weights to get a total, then multiply the prior odds of a match by 2 raised to that total to get posterior odds.

    The sums are the "likelihood ratio" of F1.4: each field multiplies the odds. A matching Emirates ID number, with a coincidence chance of 1 in 100,000, is worth about 16.6 bits; a matching first name, a few. Missing fields contribute nothing. Two mechanics trip up newcomers. First, the prior matters: among all candidate pairs, true matches are rare, so even a decent score gives a modest posterior unless the weight is large. Second, the value matters, not just the field: agreement on "Mohammed Ahmed" is far weaker evidence than agreement on a rare surname, and an exact match on a date of birth of 1 January is weak where many systems use it as a placeholder for "unknown". Good systems learn u per value (term-frequency adjustment). The simulation lets you feel both.

    The scorer is only half the story. The other half is deciding with a sensible threshold, and that is again a base-rate problem. Suppose blocking leaves one million candidate pairs, of which 2,000 are real matches. Most non-matches score very low and a small tail scores high by accident. Even a tiny tail is large when multiplied by 998,000 non-matches.

    Modern entity resolution adds machine learning on top of this skeleton, not instead of it. Supervised matchers (gradient-boosted trees over similarity features such as edit distance, phonetic match and embedding similarity) learn weights from labelled pairs. Pre-trained language models can compare records as text (the "Ditto" line of work), and large language models can act as zero-shot matchers, strong on messy, free-text records but slow and costly per pair, so best kept for the borderline band. Blocking itself can use embeddings and approximate nearest-neighbour search. For companies, the extra problem is hierarchy: subsidiary, branch and group relationships matter for ownership and exposure and are a different thing from "same entity". In Arabic data the hardest part is usually not the algorithm but names: variant transliterations, patronymic chains ("bin", "ibn", "Al"), the order of given and family names, and diacritics. Everything lives or dies on the cleaning stage.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    KYC is the front door. Almost every other bank AI system takes its customer view, and its risk label, from here.

    B2 · Sanctions screening and name matchingFuzzy matching, with higher stakes

    Screening a name against a list is entity resolution with a one-sided cost: a missed hit is a breach. Same transliteration problems, same thresholds, same review queue.

    B2 · Transaction monitoringRating sets the thresholds

    A customer's risk rating decides how closely their transactions are watched, and "expected activity" captured at onboarding is the baseline monitoring compares against.

    A3 · Deepfakes against eKYCThe attack on Simulation 2

    Synthetic faces and injected video erode the signal liveness checks rely on. The lesson covers the threat and the defences.

    A9 · Synthetic identity fraudIdentities that pass every check

    A real ID number glued to a fake name and face can pass document, face and database checks. Entity resolution on shared phones, devices and addresses is what exposes it.

    A9 · Device intelligenceThe session, not just the face

    Camera-driver and device signals are the defence against injected video, and they help link accounts to one operator.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rulePriors and likelihood ratios

    Fellegi-Sunter weights are likelihood ratios; the prior decides whether a high-looking score is enough. Same arithmetic as precision at low prevalence.

    F3.5 · F7 · Imbalance and thresholdsThe same base-rate trap

    Most applicants are honest and most candidate pairs are non-matches. Threshold choice, review capacity and precision-recall thinking carry over directly.

    F4 · Anomaly detectionSpotting the unusual customer

    Unsupervised methods flag onboarding profiles that look unlike peers, a useful complement when confirmed labels are sparse.

    A14 · Arabic OCR and document AIReading the ID card

    Document checks begin with extraction. Errors in Arabic text become errors in names and, downstream, in matching.

    G2 · Privacy and fairnessBiometrics are sensitive data

    Face templates and linked customer profiles carry privacy duties; fairness trade-offs apply to risk ratings and face-match error rates by group.

    A12 · CBUAE, SAMA and SDAIAWhat the regulators expect

    Local AML and digital-identity rules shape what can be automated and who must approve.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Digital onboarding. UAE Pass or Nafath login, pre-filled data, a selfie only where assurance is not enough, and an automatic first risk rating in seconds.
    • Periodic and event-driven review. Re-rating customers when they change address, add a signatory, or trip a monitoring rule. High-risk customers are reviewed more often.
    • Corporate onboarding. Collecting the ownership chain, resolving shareholders to natural persons, checking each against PEP and sanctions lists.
    • Golden customer record. A single view of the customer across retail, cards, loans and wealth, which cross-sell, credit, AML and complaint-handling all depend on.
    • Fraud and mule detection. Linking accounts that share a device, phone or address.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core ideas, a risk-based approach, scorecards, Fellegi-Sunter, are old and stable. The movement is in three places: government-backed digital identity replacing document-and-selfie checks, generative-AI attacks on the remaining remote checks, and learned matchers (including LLMs) in entity resolution.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in KYC programmes and in AI-written proposals for them. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • An average hides the worst factor

      A customer with one very-high factor and several benign ones scores Medium or Low.

      Spot itList customers with any factor at the top level who are rated below High. If the list is long, add a single-factor escalation rule.
    • Everyone lands in one band

      85% Medium, so reviews and monitoring never discriminate.

      Spot itPlot the distribution of scores. A rating that does not spread customers cannot allocate effort.
    • Stale ratings

      Risk assessed at onboarding and never changed, though the customer's behaviour or ownership has.

      Spot itCompare each customer's current activity with the expected activity captured at onboarding; ask when the rating last changed.
    • Trusting the digital ID for everything

      A UAE Pass login is read as "customer checked, risk fine".

      Spot itCheck that rating, screening and purpose-of-account steps run independently of the identity step.
    • Quoted error rates from the vendor's lab

      "99.9% accurate" on a clean test set, then high rejection of real customers.

      Spot itAsk for rejection and acceptance rates on your own traffic, split by device, light, age, skin tone and nationality.
    • Liveness tuned only against print and replay

      Passes injected or generated video as live.

      Spot itAsk whether the vendor tests for injection attacks and what device or session signals are used.
    • A wrong merge of two customers

      An over-eager threshold links namesakes. One person inherits another's sanctions alert, balance or fraud flag.

      Spot itSample auto-linked pairs for review; track the wrong-merge rate, and make sure merges can be undone and audited.
    • Transliteration blindness

      Matching only in Latin script, missing the Arabic record of the same person.

      Spot itTest with known-same pairs written in both scripts and with variant spellings, and look at recall by name origin.
    • Placeholder values treated as evidence

      1 January birth dates, "0000000" phone numbers, the branch address.

      Spot itLook for values that appear unusually often in a field. Those need low or zero weight.
    • Clusters that chain

      A matches B, B matches C, so all become one entity though A and C clearly differ.

      Spot itCheck cluster sizes; very large ones are almost always chaining errors.
    • Learning from labels that reflect past scrutiny

      A risk model trained on suspicious activity reports repeats the bank's earlier blind spots.

      Spot itAsk how the labels were produced and whether any unreviewed random sample exists.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Know-your-customer is three questions: who is this, are they really here, and how risky are they. National digital ID answers the first two cheaply and quickly for most customers, so we should lean on it. The third is ours to own: a risk rating decides how closely we watch each customer. Our biggest exposure is not the average customer but the few with a hidden red flag, so I'd measure how well we find those, and how many good customers we slow down to do it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd treat each stage as score, threshold and error trade-off. For risk rating I'd keep a transparent scorecard with policy overrides as the floor and test ML as a challenger calibrated on outcomes, mindful that our labels come from past alerts. For eKYC I'd evaluate on our own traffic, by segment, including injection attacks. For entity resolution I'd use blocking plus Fellegi-Sunter or a learned matcher, with a three-way decision and a review band, tracking wrong-merge and missed-link rates separately, with term-frequency adjustments and cleaning for Arabic transliteration.”
    “Can an ML model replace our risk-rating scorecard?”

    Not on its own, at least not yet. Regulators expect a rating they can follow and a policy they can see, and overrides for PEPs and sanctions are legal rules, not predictions. A model can calibrate the weights, re-rate dynamically and triage reviews. I'd run it as a challenger against the scorecard, compare who each flags, and keep the labels' bias in mind: they only exist where someone looked.

    “If the customer logs in with UAE Pass, do we still need risk checks?”

    Yes. The national ID proves who the person is with high assurance. It says nothing about why they want the account, where their money comes from, who owns their company, or whether they are a PEP. Rating, screening, purpose of account and monitoring still apply. What it does remove is much of the document and selfie work, which is a large saving.

    “Our face-match vendor says 99.9% accuracy. Is that good?”

    It depends what the number means and where it was measured. I'd ask for false-reject and false-accept rates at our threshold, on our customer mix, split by skin tone, age, lighting and device, and for results against injection and deepfake attacks, not just print and replay. A single accuracy figure on a 1% attack base rate can hide a lot.

    “Why not just link records that match on name and date of birth?”

    Because agreement on common values is weak evidence. Many people share common names, and many systems put 1 January as a placeholder birth date. A weighted approach scores agreement by how rare the value is, adds the other fields, and sends the borderline to review. A hard rule on those two fields would wrongly merge namesakes and would miss genuine matches whose spelling differs.

    “Could an LLM do our entity resolution?”

    For borderline pairs, probably usefully. It handles messy text and transliteration well, and can explain itself. But it is slow and costly across billions of pairs, outputs vary run to run, and a wrong merge has real consequences. I'd use cheap blocking and a scored matcher for volume, an LLM or learned model on the review band, and a human sign-off for merges that carry risk flags, with an evaluation on labelled local examples before trusting it.

    “Is it a problem to use nationality as a risk factor?”

    It's a standard geography input, which regulators and the FATF expect banks to consider, but it needs care. The factor should reflect actual country risk, be weighed with others, and not become a blanket penalty on a nationality. I'd check outcomes by group, keep a documented reason for each factor, and make sure adverse decisions can be explained and challenged.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones use the weights and formulas in Part 2.

    1. Using the Simulation 1 weights (30, 25, 15, 10, 20), a customer has levels 1, 3, 0, 0, 1. What is the score, and what is the problem?

    30×1/3 = 10; 25×3/3 = 25; 0; 0; 20×1/3 = 6.67. Total 41.7, so Medium with the default cut-offs. The problem is that the geography factor is at its worst level, which the average has diluted. A single-factor escalation rule would force at least High review.

    2. Why is a PEP rated High even if the weighted score is 8?

    Because PEP handling is a rule set by policy and regulation (FATF Recommendation 12), not a prediction. The exposure to corruption and the possible source-of-wealth questions do not depend on how the other factors add up, so an override sets a minimum rating.

    3. An ID-number field has m = 0.98 and u = 0.00001. What are the weights for agree and disagree?

    Agree: log2(0.98 ÷ 0.00001) = log2(98,000) ≈ +16.6 bits. Disagree: log2(0.02 ÷ 0.99999) ≈ −5.6 bits. Disagreement is only mildly negative, because ID numbers are sometimes mistyped or reissued.

    4. Two records agree on a very common name (u = 0.02, m = 0.95) and on a 1 January birth date (u = 0.03, m = 0.97). The prior of a match is 0.1%. What is the posterior?

    Weights: log2(0.95 ÷ 0.02) = log2(47.5) ≈ 5.57; log2(0.97 ÷ 0.03) = log2(32.3) ≈ 5.01. Total 10.58 bits, a likelihood ratio of about 1,535. Prior odds ≈ 0.001. Posterior odds ≈ 1.54, so probability ≈ 61%. This goes to review; it is not safe to auto-link.

    5. Out of 10,000 attempts, 100 are attacks. A check rejects 5% of genuine customers and lets through 5% of attacks. How many genuine customers are blocked, and what share of passes are attackers?

    Genuine blocked: 5% of 9,900 = 495. Attackers through: 5% of 100 = 5. Passes: 9,405 genuine and 5 attackers, so about 0.05% of passes are attackers. Raising the threshold to cut that further blocks even more good customers, which is why layered defences matter.

    6. Why does a deepfake or injected-video attack make "just raise the liveness threshold" a poor defence?

    The attacker's liveness scores move up towards the genuine distribution, so no threshold separates them without also rejecting many genuine users. The control needs another signal: device and camera integrity, session behaviour, or the national digital ID, which doesn't depend on a camera feed.

    7. Why is blocking necessary, and what does it risk?

    Comparing all pairs among 10 million records means about 5 × 1013 comparisons. Blocking keeps only pairs that share a cheap key. The risk is missing true matches whose blocking key differs (a typo or a transliteration variant), so several overlapping blocking rules are used.

    8. In Simulation 4, why does lowering the number of true matches make wrong merges a larger share of auto-links, though the thresholds are unchanged?

    The number of wrong merges is set by the tail of the non-match distribution times the huge non-match count, which doesn't change. The number of correct links falls with the number of true matches. So the share of auto-links that are correct, the precision, falls. It is the base-rate effect from imbalanced data.
