    Know Your Customer: Due Diligence and Entity Resolution

    Part 1

    What Know Your Customer means

    In one sentenceKnow Your Customer (KYC) is the checks a bank must make before and after taking someone on: who they are, whether they are real, how risky they are, and whether you already know them under another record.

    Imagine a customer opens an account in a banking app. Before taking any money, the bank asks three questions.

    Each check turns imperfect evidence into a score and draws a line. Too strict blocks honest customers. Too loose lets bad actors in.

    Most customers are fine, so even a small false-alarm rate gives mostly false alarms. KYC is a legal duty, so every rating, rejection and merge must be explainable to the regulator (in the UAE the CBUAE, in Saudi Arabia SAMA).

    How it differs from screening and fraud models

    • Not sanctions screening. Screening checks names against banned lists. KYC decides who the customer is and how risky they are. Screening is one input.
    • Not fraud detection. Fraud models watch transactions over time. KYC works mainly at onboarding, then reviews.
    Part 2

    Due diligence and the risk rating

    In one sentenceCustomer due diligence (CDD) is verifying who the customer is, who really owns them, and why they bank here, then matching the effort to the risk.
    1. Verify identity. Check the ID document against the person.
    2. Find the beneficial owner. This is the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company. Follow ownership up through holding companies until you reach people.
    3. Understand the purpose. What is the account for, and what is normal?
    4. Rate the risk. A scorecard adds points for customer type, country, product, channel and expected activity, then turns the total into Low, Medium or High.
    5. Monitor. Check later activity still fits the profile.

    This is the risk-based approach, like airport security lanes. Most customers get standard checks. High-risk customers, including politically exposed persons (PEPs: people in senior public roles, and their close family and associates), get enhanced due diligence (EDD): proof of where their wealth came from, senior approval and closer monitoring.

    How to read a risk rating

    Salaried teacher, local
    Low score. Standard checks.
    Trading company with layered owners abroad
    High score. Owners must be traced to real people, and EDD applies.
    Gold dealer: four factors fine, one very high
    The average can land in Medium and hide the red flag. So a rule such as "any factor at its worst level forces a review" is added on top.
    Relative of a foreign PEP, otherwise ordinary
    The score may say Low, but policy overrides it to High. A confirmed sanctions match overrides everything: no relationship.
    • Weights are judgements. A validator will ask who chose them and why. Be able to justify each factor in a sentence.
    • Averaging dilutes. One failed exam among four good ones still averages to a pass. Overrides fix this.
    • ML can help by re-scoring when behaviour changes, but it only learns from cases someone already looked at.

    Is this person real? (eKYC)

    eKYC is identity checking done remotely. It has three layers, each with its own error rate.

    • Document check: read the ID and test its security features. Arabic text reading quality matters, because Arabic names must match their Latin spellings.
    • Face match: a neural network turns each face into a list of numbers, and the selfie and the ID portrait must land close together.
    • Liveness: is a live person there, not a photo, a replay or a mask? Injected fake video can bypass the camera entirely, so defences must also look at the device and session.

    National digital identity (UAE Pass in the UAE, Nafath in Saudi Arabia) lets the bank receive attributes the state has already verified. The bank still has to rate and understand the customer. Identity is one input to risk, not the answer.

    Part 3

    Entity resolution: is this the same customer?

    In one sentenceEntity resolution decides which records belong to the same real person or company, like merging duplicate contacts on your phone.

    The bank holds "Mohammed Al Mansoori", "Muhammad Almansouri", "محمد المنصوري" and "M. A. Mansouri", each with a different phone number and address. One person or four? Rating half a customer gives the wrong rating, and a sanctions hit missed through a spelling variant is a breach.

    Comparing every pair is hopeless: 10 million customers make about 50 trillion pairs. So the pipeline narrows down first.

    1. Standardise. Clean names, dates, phone numbers and addresses into one format.
    2. Block. Compare only plausible pairs that share something cheap, such as date of birth or a sound code of the surname. Use several rules so one typo does not lose a pair.
    3. Score. Compare field by field and compute a match score.
    4. Decide. Link automatically above an upper threshold, send to a human between the thresholds, leave apart below.

    How to read a match score

    • Rare agreement is strong. A matching ID number is almost proof. Matching "Mohammed Ahmed" is weak, because many people share it.
    • Placeholder values are weak. Many systems use 1 January as a default birth date, so agreeing on it tells you little.
    • Matches are rare. Even a good score can mean only a modest chance of the same person, because most candidate pairs are different people.
    Show the classic scoring idea

    The Fellegi-Sunter model works field by field. For each field it asks how often the field agrees for the same person (m) and how often it agrees by coincidence (u). A rare value has small u, so agreement counts for a lot. Agreement adds evidence, disagreement subtracts, and the evidence is combined with how rare true matches are to give a probability.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting the average score

      One very high factor among harmless ones can still land in Medium. Add worst-factor and PEP overrides.

    • Rating once and never updating

      Risk set at onboarding goes stale. Ask when each rating last changed.

    • Treating a digital ID login as "risk fine"

      It confirms identity only. Purpose, ownership and activity still need rating.

    • Merging on weak agreement

      Agreeing on a common name and a default birth date is close to a coin flip. Send these to review.

    1. What is a beneficial owner?

    The real person who ultimately owns or controls a company, found by following ownership through any holding companies.

    2. Why add override rules on top of a scorecard?

    Averaging dilutes a single serious red flag, and some facts, like a PEP link, must decide the outcome regardless of the score.

    3. Does a national digital ID make a customer low risk?

    No. It confirms who they are. The bank still rates the relationship and screens the name.

    4. Why block before scoring in entity resolution?

    Comparing every pair is impossibly many. Blocking keeps only plausible pairs for the scorer.

    5. Why is a wrong merge worse than a missed one?

    It can attach one person's sanctions alert or balance to another. A missed link can be found and fixed later by review.
