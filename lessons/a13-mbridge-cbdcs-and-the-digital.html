    Digital Money: mBridge, the Digital Dirham, Stablecoins and Tokenised Deposits

    Four kinds of digital money, who issues each, why the Gulf is early in central bank digital currency, and what each one does to a bank's funding, liquidity and data.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDigital money comes in forms that differ by who owes you the claim (the central bank, a regulated token issuer, or your own bank), and every question that matters, from deposit flight to run risk to what data you can see, follows from that one fact.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    When you see a balance of AED 1,000 in your banking app, it is not cash. It is a claim on your bank: the bank owes you AED 1,000. Your bank in turn holds a claim on the central bank, called reserves. Money has always been a stack of claims, and "digital money" is a family of ways to rearrange who sits where in that stack.

    Ask one question of any new form of money: who owes me?

    • The central bank. Physical cash is a claim on it. So is a central bank digital currency (CBDC), which is digital cash. The Digital Dirham is the UAE's. No credit risk, because the issuer can create the money.
    • A commercial bank. Ordinary deposits, and their upgraded form, tokenised deposits: the same bank deposit, recorded on a shared ledger so it can move and settle in new ways. Backed by the bank's balance sheet and deposit insurance rules where they exist.
    • A regulated token issuer. A stablecoin is a token that promises to be worth one unit of currency, backed by a reserve of assets. A dirham stablecoin is a claim on that issuer and its reserve, not on a bank deposit, and not on the central bank.

    There are two audiences too. Wholesale money is for banks and institutions settling with each other. Retail money is for the public. The Gulf work so far is mostly wholesale and cross-border, which is where today's pain is: sending AED 10 million to a counterparty in another country can pass through several banks, take a day or two, and tie up cash on the way.

    Hold onto thisThe technology is not the story. The story is who carries the claim, who can hold it, and what happens to bank balance sheets and to the data trail when money changes form.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    mBridge: a shared ledger for central bank money

    mBridge began as a joint project of the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, the Bank of Thailand, the Digital Currency Institute of the People's Bank of China and the CBUAE, coordinated at first by the BIS Innovation Hub. Saudi Arabia's central bank (SAMA) joined later. Its aim is cross-border payments in central bank money: participants' banks hold tokens issued by their own central bank on a shared platform, and a payment between two countries becomes a transfer between those tokens, with the foreign exchange leg settled at the same time (payment-versus-payment, so neither side can be left holding only half the trade).

    The contrast with the status quo is the chain in Simulation 1. Today, a bank without an account at the destination country's central bank asks a correspondent that has one. Each link is a credit risk, a fee, a compliance check and a cut-off time. A shared ledger removes the links, at the price of a new problem: governance. Who runs the platform, whose law applies, and who is admitted.

    That last point became public in 2024, when the BIS handed the project to its participating central banks and stepped back, in part because of geopolitical sensitivity. Since then mBridge has been run by the member central banks. For the Gulf, the pitch is trade settlement with large partners, including in non-dollar currencies. It is also why compliance teams ask hard questions about sanctions exposure and screening on such rails. Treat the geopolitics as a risk factor, not as a reason for a technical team to take a side.

    Related earlier work: Project Aber, a joint SAMA and CBUAE experiment around 2019-20, tested a shared digital currency between the two countries and showed that the two central banks can work together on a ledger. Details of current volumes on mBridge are thin in public sources; be careful about quoting figures.

    The Digital Dirham and CBDCs in general

    A CBDC is a digital liability of the central bank. Design choices decide everything else:

    Design choiceOptionsWhy it matters to a bank
    Who can hold itBanks only (wholesale), or everyone (retail)Retail CBDC competes with deposits. Wholesale mostly improves settlement.
    Account or tokenBalances at the central bank, or bearer-style tokens in walletsAccount-based gives full traceability. Token-based can offer limited offline use.
    Who deals with the publicCentral bank directly, or banks and licensed wallet providers as intermediariesAlmost all projects choose intermediaries, so KYC, AML, support and fraud stay with banks.
    Holding limits and interestCaps per person, no interestThese are the brakes on deposit flight (Simulation 2).
    PrivacyAnonymity tiers for small amounts versus full identificationDecides what data analytics and AML teams can see.

    The CBUAE published a Digital Dirham strategy in 2023-24 that sets out phases. As reported, the first phase is a limited launch of the Digital Dirham within mBridge for cross-border payments, followed by a proof of concept of a bilateral bridge with India and a proof of concept for domestic wholesale and retail use. Public reports describe a first government-to-government payment made using the Digital Dirham via mBridge. Timelines for broad domestic availability have slipped in some press accounts and are inconsistently reported; check the CBUAE's own publications before stating a date in a meeting.

    What a bank should expect to do is a design question, not a technology one: onboard customers to wallets, apply KYC, monitor transactions, and handle conversion between deposits and the CBDC. A model team's work here is the same as before (fraud, AML, customer analytics) on a new rail with new fields and probably a new event stream.

    Notice what this does not say. Customers moving from deposits to CBDC does not move money out of the banking system; the bank's reserve account at the central bank pays for it. The bank's problem is that a cheap, sticky funding source has become a volatile one. Central banks usually respond with holding limits and no interest on the CBDC, which is what the cap slider models. Do not claim the effect is large in the Gulf. Which depends on design, and no retail design has yet been run at scale here.

    Dirham stablecoins

    A stablecoin works because of the reserve. For every token outstanding, the issuer holds assets worth at least one dirham. A holder redeems by handing back tokens and receiving dirhams. The peg holds only as long as people believe that redemption works on demand, at par, and in full. That is exactly the structure of a bank run, with one difference: there is no deposit insurance or lender of last resort unless the law builds one in.

    The UAE regime, as reported, requires a Payment Token Issuer licence from the CBUAE for dirham-pegged tokens, full backing in segregated reserve accounts, and limits use for everyday payments to licensed dirham tokens. Several issuers have been reported as approved or approved in principle, including bank-linked ones (press names include AE Coin, a Zand Bank token and RAKBANK). Sources disagree on who was "first" and on whether approvals are final or in principle, so name no order in a meeting. Check the CBUAE register.

    What is in the reserve matters more than almost anything else. Cash and central bank balances pay out the same day. Short government paper pays out in a day or two, at a small haircut in stress. Deposits at one bank carry that bank's risk. Simulation 3 shows the run arithmetic.

    Tokenised deposits

    A tokenised deposit is a normal commercial bank deposit that lives as a token on a programmable ledger. It is still a liability of the bank, covered by the usual prudential and deposit-protection rules. Its appeal is programmability and atomic settlement: a securities purchase and its cash payment can settle in the same instant (delivery-versus-payment), a trade-finance payment can release when a shipment condition is met, and bank-to-bank transfers can settle on a shared ledger without a chain of intermediaries.

    Here is the central argument made by banks and many central banks: because tokenised deposits sit on bank balance sheets, they keep the lending engine intact, which a fully reserved stablecoin cannot do (reserve assets are not lent out). The BIS has argued for a "unified ledger" in which central bank money, tokenised deposits and tokenised assets interoperate, and ran Project Agora with several central banks and banks to test it. Large international banks have launched tokenised deposit services for corporate clients, and the Gulf banks are watching and piloting.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Mixing up the forms

      Calling a stablecoin a CBDC, or a tokenised deposit a stablecoin.

      Spot itAsk who owes the holder and what backs the claim. Different answers mean different risks.
    • Quoting dates and volumes from press

      Rollout dates and "firsts" vary between articles.

      Spot itLook for the central bank's own publication. Say "as reported" if you only have press.
    • Fully backed treated as run-proof

      A reserve of AED 1 for each token does not mean AED 1 is available today.

      Spot itAsk for the reserve's liquidity profile and redemption terms, not only its size.
    • Ignoring deposit behaviour

      Assuming existing deposit models will hold once a substitute exists.

      Spot itCheck whether the model's history includes any period with a close substitute. Usually it does not.
    • Assuming faster means safer, or riskier

      Instant, final settlement removes recall but also removes settlement risk.

      Spot itAsk which risk moved and who now bears it.
    • Forgetting AML on the new rail

      A wallet is an account for sanctions and AML purposes.

      Spot itAsk who does KYC, who monitors, and whether those teams get the data.
    • Tying a model to a geopolitical stance

      Platform membership brings sanctions and political exposure.

      Spot itRoute platform participation questions to legal and compliance, and keep analysis neutral.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Think of three kinds of digital dirham: central bank digital cash, our own deposits on a new ledger, and a licensed stablecoin backed by a reserve. They differ in who owes the customer. For us the questions are funding, because a retail central bank currency could pull in deposits unless it is capped, and financial crime, because new rails need screening and monitoring from day one. I'd treat wholesale and tokenised deposits as the near-term work, and watch the retail timetable.”
    To a technical colleague
    “Cross-border settlement in central bank money via mBridge cuts the correspondent chain and settles both currency legs together. For modelling, I'd add token and wallet fields to our event data, build graph features for wallet-to-wallet flows, and re-examine deposit-stability assumptions in liquidity models with a scenario where a substitute exists. For stablecoins the reserve's same-day liquidity is the risk to check, not just its size.”
    “Is the Digital Dirham live?”

    As reported, it has had a limited launch in cross-border and government use via mBridge, with domestic wholesale and retail planned in later phases. Public dates have varied, so I'd check the CBUAE's latest statement before giving a date.

    “Will a retail CBDC drain our deposits?”

    It could if it were unlimited and competitive. Designs normally add holding caps and no interest to prevent that. Even then it is worth stress-testing: what if 5% moved, funded from liquid assets versus wholesale? That is a quick scenario for treasury.

    “What's the difference between a stablecoin and a tokenised deposit?”

    A tokenised deposit is a claim on a bank, so the bank can lend against it. A stablecoin is a claim on an issuer holding a reserve that is not lent. Different risk, different regulation, different economics.

    “Why not just use a stablecoin for all cross-border?”

    It can work for some corridors, but you take reserve and issuer risk, need on and off ramps, and face varying regulation per country. Central bank money on a shared ledger has no credit risk, but needs the countries' central banks to agree on governance.

    “What does this mean for the data team?”

    New event types and identifiers, new graph structure, possibly richer payment data, and new fraud and AML typologies. I'd start with data readiness: where would wallet and token events land, and who owns screening for them?

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. For each of CBDC, stablecoin and tokenised deposit, who owes the holder?

    The central bank; the token issuer through its reserve; and the commercial bank.

    2. Why can a fully backed stablecoin still fail to pay everyone on the day?

    Backing measures value, not timing. If part of the reserve takes days to sell, a large redemption exceeds same-day cash. In Simulation 3, 40% same-day cash cannot cover 60% redemptions.

    3. A bank with AED 100bn of deposits sees 8% move to a retail CBDC. Replacement funding costs 1.2 points more. What is the annual cost?

    AED 8bn × 1.2% = AED 96m per year.

    4. Why do designs cap CBDC holdings and pay no interest?

    To stop it competing with deposits and triggering fast moves of funding out of banks in a stress.

    5. What does payment-versus-payment settlement guarantee?

    Both currency legs settle together or not at all, so neither party ends up having paid without being paid.

    6. Why can tokenised deposits support lending when a stablecoin cannot?

    They remain bank liabilities, so the bank keeps the funds on its balance sheet and lends them. A stablecoin reserve is held in full against tokens.
