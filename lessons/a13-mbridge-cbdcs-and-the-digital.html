    Digital Money: CBDCs, Stablecoins and Tokenised Deposits

    Part 1

    What digital money is

    In one sentenceMoney is a claim on someone, so ask one question of any form of digital money: who owes me? The answer decides the risk.

    An AED 1,000 balance in your banking app is not cash. It is an IOU: your bank owes you. Like a cloakroom ticket, it is a claim on whoever holds your coat. Digital money rearranges who issues the ticket.

    The four forms

    • CBDC (central bank digital currency): digital cash issued by the central bank. The Digital Dirham is the UAE's.
    • Tokenised deposit: an ordinary bank deposit recorded as a token on a shared, programmable ledger. Still a bank liability.
    • Stablecoin: a token promising to be worth one unit of currency, backed by a reserve held by its issuer.
    • Ordinary deposit: what you have today.

    Wholesale money is for banks settling with each other. Retail money is for the public. Gulf work is mostly wholesale and cross-border. Our running example is AED 10 million sent abroad, which can pass through several banks and take a day or two.

    Part 2

    How each one works

    mBridge: a shared counter for central bank money

    mBridge is a shared platform where banks hold tokens issued by their own central bank. A cross-border payment becomes a swap of tokens, like two shops exchanging goods at the same moment on one counter instead of posting parcels through middlemen. The CBUAE and SAMA are among the participants.

    1. Today, each bank in the chain adds credit risk, a fee, a compliance check and a cut-off time.
    2. On a shared ledger, the sender's bank pays in its central bank's token.
    3. The currency swap settles at the same time (payment-versus-payment), so neither side is left holding half the trade.
    4. The money arrives directly, with no chain.

    The catch is governance: who runs the platform, whose law applies, who is admitted. Compliance teams also ask hard questions about sanctions screening on such rails. Treat geopolitics as a risk factor, not a side to pick.

    CBDCs and the Digital Dirham

    Design choices decide how a CBDC affects a bank.

    • Who can hold it: banks only (wholesale), or everyone (retail). Retail competes with deposits. Wholesale mostly improves settlement.
    • Who deals with the public: almost all projects use banks as intermediaries, so KYC (know your customer), AML and fraud checks stay with banks.
    • Holding limits and no interest: brakes on customers moving their deposits to the central bank.
    • Privacy: anonymity tiers or full identification, which decides what AML teams can see.

    The Digital Dirham rollout is phased, starting with cross-border payments within mBridge. Timelines are reported inconsistently, so check CBUAE publications before quoting a date. For a model team, it means the same fraud, AML and customer analytics on a new rail with new fields.

    Stablecoins and tokenised deposits

    A stablecoin is like a casino chip: worth a dirham only if the cashier always pays out. The peg holds while people believe redemption works on demand, at par, in full. That is the structure of a bank run, without deposit insurance unless the law builds one in. As reported, the UAE requires a Payment Token Issuer licence from the CBUAE and full backing in segregated reserves. Check the CBUAE register for who is approved.

    A tokenised deposit keeps the bank's liability and the usual rules. Its appeal is atomic settlement: all or nothing, like paying cash and getting the keys at one handover. A securities purchase and its payment settle together, or a trade-finance payment releases when a shipment condition is met.

    Part 3

    How to think about the risks

    • Fully backed is not run-proof. AED 1 of reserve per token does not mean AED 1 is available today. Cash and central bank balances pay out the same day. Short government paper takes a day or two, at a small discount in stress. Deposits at one bank carry that bank's risk. If a reserve holds 40% in same-day cash and 60% of holders redeem at once, not everyone is paid on the day, even though it is fully backed.
    • Deposits that move to a CBDC leave the bank. The bank pays from its reserve account at the central bank. Cheap, sticky funding (a regular customer) becomes volatile (a passer-by), and replacing it with wholesale borrowing costs more every year the gap lasts. That is why retail designs add holding caps.
    • Liquidity buffer. The liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) compares liquid assets with 30 days of stressed outflows. Above 100% is enough. Below is a shortfall.
    Part 4

    How the forms compare

    FormWho owes youCredit riskCan fund lending?
    CBDC (Digital Dirham)The central bankNoneNo, it replaces deposits
    Tokenised depositYour bankBank risk, regulatedYes
    Regulated stablecoinThe issuer, via its reserveReserve and issuer riskNo, the reserve is held, not lent
    Ordinary depositYour bankBank risk, regulatedYes

    Shared-ledger settlement is strong at cutting cost, delay and cash tied up in transit. It is weak at governance, sanctions questions and political exposure.

    Tokenised deposits are strong at new settlement while keeping lending. That is the banks' argument against fully reserved stablecoins. They are weak at being new: Gulf banks are watching and piloting.

    In banking, a wallet is an account for sanctions and AML purposes, and platform membership brings political exposure. Ask who does KYC and monitoring, and route membership questions to legal and compliance.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Mixing up the forms

      Calling a stablecoin a CBDC, or a tokenised deposit a stablecoin. Ask who owes the holder: different answers mean different risks.

    • Treating fully backed as run-proof

      Ask for the reserve's liquidity profile and redemption terms, not only its size.

    • Trusting old deposit models

      Models trained before a close substitute existed may not hold once it does. Check whether the history includes one. Usually it does not.

    • Assuming faster means safer

      Instant, final settlement removes recall but also settlement risk. Ask which risk moved and who now bears it.

    1. For a CBDC, a stablecoin and a tokenised deposit, who owes the holder?

    The central bank; the token issuer through its reserve; and the commercial bank.

    2. Why can a fully backed stablecoin still fail to pay everyone on the day?

    Backing measures value, not timing. If only 40% is same-day cash, it cannot cover 60% redemptions that day.

    3. Why do CBDC designs cap holdings and pay no interest?

    To stop it competing with deposits and draining bank funding fast in a stress.

    4. What does payment-versus-payment settlement guarantee?

    Both currency legs settle together or not at all.

    5. Why can tokenised deposits support lending when a stablecoin cannot?

    They stay bank liabilities, so the bank keeps and lends the funds. A stablecoin reserve is held in full against tokens.
