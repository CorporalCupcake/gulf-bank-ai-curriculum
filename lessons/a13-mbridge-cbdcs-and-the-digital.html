    Digital Money: mBridge, the Digital Dirham, Stablecoins and Tokenised Deposits

    Four kinds of digital money, who issues each, and what each does to a bank's funding, liquidity and data.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDigital money comes in forms that differ by who owes you the claim (the central bank, a regulated token issuer, or your own bank), and every question that matters, from deposit flight to run risk to what data you can see, follows from that one fact.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An AED 1,000 balance in your banking app is not cash. It is an IOU: your bank owes you. Like a cloakroom ticket, money is a claim on whoever holds your coat. "Digital money" rearranges who issues the ticket. Ask one question of any form of money: who owes me?

    • The central bank. Cash, and a central bank digital currency (CBDC), which is digital cash. The Digital Dirham is the UAE's. No credit risk.
    • A commercial bank. Ordinary deposits, and tokenised deposits: the same deposit recorded on a shared ledger so it settles in new ways. Carries bank risk.
    • A regulated token issuer. A stablecoin is a token promising to be worth one unit of currency, backed by a reserve. A dirham stablecoin is a claim on the issuer and its reserve.

    Wholesale money is for banks settling with each other; retail is for the public. Gulf work is mostly wholesale and cross-border, where today's pain is: AED 10 million sent abroad can pass through several banks, take a day or two, and tie up cash on the way.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    mBridge: a shared ledger for central bank money

    mBridge is a shared platform where banks hold tokens issued by their own central bank, so a cross-border payment is a swap between tokens. Think of two shops exchanging goods at the same moment on one counter, instead of posting parcels through middlemen. The CBUAE and SAMA are among the participants. The foreign exchange leg settles at the same time (payment-versus-payment), so neither side is left holding half the trade.

    Today, each correspondent link in the chain (Simulation 1) adds credit risk, a fee, a compliance check and a cut-off time. A shared ledger removes the links but adds governance questions: who runs it, whose law applies, who is admitted.

    The Gulf pitch is trade settlement with large partners, including in non-dollar currencies. Compliance teams ask hard questions about sanctions and screening on such rails. Treat geopolitics as a risk factor, not a side to pick.

    The Digital Dirham and CBDCs in general

    A CBDC is a digital liability of the central bank. Design choices decide the rest:

    Design choiceOptionsWhy it matters to a bank
    Who can hold itBanks only (wholesale), or everyone (retail)Retail CBDC competes with deposits. Wholesale mostly improves settlement.
    Who deals with the publicCentral bank directly, or banks as intermediariesAlmost all projects choose intermediaries, so KYC, AML and fraud stay with banks.
    Holding limits and interestCaps per person, no interestThese are the brakes on deposit flight (Simulation 2).
    PrivacyAnonymity tiers versus full identificationDecides what data AML teams can see.

    The Digital Dirham rollout is phased, starting with cross-border payments within mBridge. Timelines are inconsistently reported, so check CBUAE publications before quoting a date.

    For a model team, it means the same fraud, AML and customer analytics on a new rail with new fields.

    Deposits moving to CBDC do not leave the banking system: the bank pays from its reserve account at the central bank. The problem is that cheap, sticky funding (a regular customer) becomes volatile (a passer-by). Central banks respond with holding limits and no interest, which the cap slider models. Read the LCR as a buffer of liquid assets against 30-day stressed outflows: above 100% is enough, below is a shortfall.

    Dirham stablecoins

    A stablecoin is like a casino chip: worth a dirham only if the cashier always pays out. The issuer holds assets worth at least one dirham per token, and holders redeem for dirhams. The peg holds while people believe redemption works on demand, at par, in full. That is the structure of a bank run, without deposit insurance or a lender of last resort unless the law builds one in.

    As reported, the UAE requires a Payment Token Issuer licence from the CBUAE and full backing in segregated reserves. Check the CBUAE register for who is approved.

    The reserve's contents matter most. Cash and central bank balances pay out the same day. Short government paper takes a day or two, at a small haircut in stress. Deposits at one bank carry that bank's risk.

    Tokenised deposits

    A tokenised deposit is an ordinary bank deposit living as a token on a programmable ledger. It stays a bank liability under the usual rules. Its appeal is atomic settlement (all-or-nothing, like paying cash and getting the keys at one handover): a securities purchase and its payment settle together, or a trade-finance payment releases when a shipment condition is met.

    The banks' argument: tokenised deposits stay on bank balance sheets, so lending continues, which a fully reserved stablecoin cannot do. Gulf banks are watching and piloting.

    FormWho owes youCredit riskCan fund lending?
    CBDC (Digital Dirham)The central bankNoneNo (it replaces deposits)
    Tokenised depositYour bankBank risk, regulatedYes
    Regulated stablecoinThe token issuer, via its reserveReserve and issuer riskNo (reserve is held, not lent)
    Ordinary depositYour bankBank risk, regulatedYes
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Mixing up the forms

      Calling a stablecoin a CBDC, or a tokenised deposit a stablecoin.

      Spot itAsk who owes the holder. Different answers mean different risks.
    • Fully backed treated as run-proof

      AED 1 of reserve per token does not mean AED 1 is available today.

      Spot itAsk for the reserve's liquidity profile and redemption terms, not only its size.
    • Ignoring deposit behaviour

      Assuming existing deposit models hold once a substitute exists.

      Spot itCheck whether the model's history includes a close substitute. Usually it does not.
    • Assuming faster means safer, or riskier

      Instant, final settlement removes recall but also settlement risk.

      Spot itAsk which risk moved and who now bears it.
    • Forgetting AML and politics on the new rail

      A wallet is an account for sanctions and AML purposes, and platform membership brings political exposure.

      Spot itAsk who does KYC and monitoring, and route membership questions to legal and compliance.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. For each of CBDC, stablecoin and tokenised deposit, who owes the holder?

    The central bank; the token issuer through its reserve; and the commercial bank.

    2. Why can a fully backed stablecoin still fail to pay everyone on the day?

    Backing measures value, not timing. In Simulation 3, 40% same-day cash cannot cover 60% redemptions.

    3. If customers move deposits to a retail CBDC and the bank has to replace the funding with wholesale borrowing, what kind of cost is that, and why does it recur?

    It is an ongoing extra interest cost: wholesale money is dearer than sticky deposits, and it is paid every year the gap persists, not once.

    4. Why do designs cap CBDC holdings and pay no interest?

    To stop it competing with deposits and draining bank funding fast in a stress.

    5. What does payment-versus-payment settlement guarantee?

    Both currency legs settle together or not at all.

    6. Why can tokenised deposits support lending when a stablecoin cannot?

    They stay bank liabilities, so the bank keeps and lends the funds. A stablecoin reserve is held in full against tokens.
