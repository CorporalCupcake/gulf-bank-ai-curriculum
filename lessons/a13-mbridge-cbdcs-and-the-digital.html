    Digital Money: mBridge, the Digital Dirham, Stablecoins and Tokenised Deposits

    Four kinds of digital money, who issues each, why the Gulf is early in central bank digital currency, and what each one does to a bank's funding, liquidity and data.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDigital money comes in forms that differ by who owes you the claim (the central bank, a regulated token issuer, or your own bank), and every question that matters, from deposit flight to run risk to what data you can see, follows from that one fact.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An AED 1,000 balance in your banking app is not cash. It is an IOU: your bank owes you. Your bank holds its own IOU from the central bank (reserves). Like a cloakroom ticket, money is a claim on whoever holds your coat. "Digital money" rearranges who issues the ticket.

    Ask one question of any new form of money: who owes me?

    • The central bank. Cash, and a central bank digital currency (CBDC), which is digital cash. The Digital Dirham is the UAE's. No credit risk, because the issuer can create the money.
    • A commercial bank. Ordinary deposits, and tokenised deposits: the same deposit recorded on a shared ledger so it can move and settle in new ways. Carries bank risk, under deposit insurance rules where they exist.
    • A regulated token issuer. A stablecoin is a token promising to be worth one unit of currency, backed by a reserve of assets. A dirham stablecoin is a claim on the issuer and its reserve, not on a bank or the central bank.

    Wholesale money is for banks settling with each other; retail money is for the public. Gulf work is mostly wholesale and cross-border, where today's pain is: AED 10 million sent abroad can pass through several banks, take a day or two, and tie up cash on the way.

    Hold onto thisThe technology is not the story. Who carries the claim, who can hold it, and what happens to bank balance sheets and data trails are.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    mBridge: a shared ledger for central bank money

    mBridge is a shared platform where banks hold tokens issued by their own central bank, so a cross-border payment is a swap between tokens. Think of two shops exchanging goods at the same moment on one counter, instead of posting parcels through middlemen. It began with the HKMA, Bank of Thailand, PBoC's Digital Currency Institute and the CBUAE, coordinated by the BIS Innovation Hub. SAMA joined later. The foreign exchange leg settles at the same time (payment-versus-payment), so neither side is left holding half the trade.

    Today, a bank with no account at the destination central bank asks a correspondent that has one (the chain in Simulation 1). Each link adds credit risk, a fee, a compliance check and a cut-off time. A shared ledger removes the links but adds a governance problem: who runs it, whose law applies, who is admitted.

    In 2024 the BIS handed the project to the member central banks and stepped back, in part for geopolitical reasons. The Gulf pitch is trade settlement with large partners, including in non-dollar currencies. Compliance teams ask hard questions about sanctions exposure and screening on such rails. Treat geopolitics as a risk factor, not a side to pick.

    Project Aber (SAMA and CBUAE, around 2019-20) earlier tested a shared digital currency between the two countries. Public mBridge volumes are thin; be careful quoting figures.

    The Digital Dirham and CBDCs in general

    A CBDC is a digital liability of the central bank. Design choices decide the rest:

    Design choiceOptionsWhy it matters to a bank
    Who can hold itBanks only (wholesale), or everyone (retail)Retail CBDC competes with deposits. Wholesale mostly improves settlement.
    Account or tokenBalances at the central bank, or bearer-style tokens in walletsAccount-based gives full traceability. Token-based can offer limited offline use.
    Who deals with the publicCentral bank directly, or banks and licensed wallet providers as intermediariesAlmost all projects choose intermediaries, so KYC, AML, support and fraud stay with banks.
    Holding limits and interestCaps per person, no interestThese are the brakes on deposit flight (Simulation 2).
    PrivacyAnonymity tiers for small amounts versus full identificationDecides what data analytics and AML teams can see.

    The CBUAE's 2023-24 Digital Dirham strategy is phased. As reported: a limited launch within mBridge for cross-border payments; a proof of concept for a bilateral bridge with India; then a proof of concept for domestic wholesale and retail use. A first government-to-government payment is reported. Domestic timelines are inconsistently reported, so check CBUAE publications before quoting a date.

    For a bank, the work is onboarding wallets, KYC, transaction monitoring and converting between deposits and CBDC. For a model team it is the same fraud, AML and customer analytics on a new rail with new fields and a new event stream.

    Deposits moving to CBDC do not leave the banking system: the bank pays from its reserve account at the central bank. The problem is that cheap, sticky funding (like a regular customer) becomes volatile (like a passer-by). Central banks respond with holding limits and no interest, which is what the cap slider models. Read the LCR as a buffer ratio: liquid assets divided by 30-day stressed outflows. 100% means just enough, and every point below it is a shortfall. Do not claim the effect is large in the Gulf: it depends on design, and no retail design has run at scale here.

    Dirham stablecoins

    A stablecoin is like a casino chip: worth a dirham only if the cashier always pays out. For each token, the issuer holds assets worth at least one dirham, and holders redeem tokens for dirhams. The peg holds while people believe redemption works on demand, at par, in full. That is the structure of a bank run, but with no deposit insurance or lender of last resort unless the law builds one in.

    As reported, the UAE regime requires a Payment Token Issuer licence from the CBUAE for dirham-pegged tokens, full backing in segregated reserve accounts, and licensed dirham tokens for everyday payments. Approvals, some in principle, are reported for AE Coin, a Zand Bank token and RAKBANK. Sources disagree on who was first and what is final, so name no order; check the CBUAE register.

    The reserve's contents matter most. Cash and central bank balances pay out the same day. Short government paper takes a day or two, at a small haircut in stress. Deposits at one bank carry that bank's risk.

    Tokenised deposits

    A tokenised deposit is an ordinary bank deposit living as a token on a programmable ledger. It stays a bank liability under the usual prudential and deposit-protection rules. Its appeal is programmability and atomic settlement (all-or-nothing, like paying cash and getting the keys at one handover): a securities purchase and its payment settle together (delivery-versus-payment), a trade-finance payment releases when a shipment condition is met, and bank transfers settle without a chain of intermediaries.

    The banks' argument: tokenised deposits sit on bank balance sheets, so lending continues, which a fully reserved stablecoin cannot do (reserves are not lent). The BIS argues for a "unified ledger" where central bank money, tokenised deposits and tokenised assets interoperate, and ran Project Agora to test it. Large international banks offer tokenised deposits to corporates; Gulf banks are watching and piloting.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Mixing up the forms

      Calling a stablecoin a CBDC, or a tokenised deposit a stablecoin.

      Spot itAsk who owes the holder. Different answers mean different risks.
    • Quoting dates and volumes from press

      Rollout dates and "firsts" vary between articles.

      Spot itFind the central bank's own publication, or say "as reported".
    • Fully backed treated as run-proof

      AED 1 of reserve per token does not mean AED 1 is available today.

      Spot itAsk for the reserve's liquidity profile and redemption terms, not only its size.
    • Ignoring deposit behaviour

      Assuming existing deposit models hold once a substitute exists.

      Spot itCheck whether the model's history includes a close substitute. Usually it does not.
    • Assuming faster means safer, or riskier

      Instant, final settlement removes recall but also settlement risk.

      Spot itAsk which risk moved and who now bears it.
    • Forgetting AML on the new rail

      A wallet is an account for sanctions and AML purposes.

      Spot itAsk who does KYC, who monitors, and whether they get the data.
    • Tying a model to a geopolitical stance

      Platform membership brings sanctions and political exposure.

      Spot itRoute membership questions to legal and compliance; keep analysis neutral.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. For each of CBDC, stablecoin and tokenised deposit, who owes the holder?

    The central bank; the token issuer through its reserve; and the commercial bank.

    2. Why can a fully backed stablecoin still fail to pay everyone on the day?

    Backing measures value, not timing. In Simulation 3, 40% same-day cash cannot cover 60% redemptions.

    3. A bank with AED 100bn of deposits sees 8% move to a retail CBDC. Replacement funding costs 1.2 points more. What is the annual cost?

    AED 8bn × 1.2% = AED 96m per year.

    4. Why do designs cap CBDC holdings and pay no interest?

    To stop it competing with deposits and draining bank funding fast in a stress.

    5. What does payment-versus-payment settlement guarantee?

    Both currency legs settle together or not at all, so neither party ends up having paid without being paid.

    6. Why can tokenised deposits support lending when a stablecoin cannot?

    They stay bank liabilities, so the bank keeps and lends the funds. A stablecoin reserve is held in full against tokens.
