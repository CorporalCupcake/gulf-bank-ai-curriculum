    Anti-Money Laundering: Rules, ML and Transaction Monitoring

    Part 1

    What transaction monitoring is

    In one sentenceAnti-money-laundering (AML) transaction monitoring scans customers' payments for suspicious patterns and raises alerts, so that human investigators know whom to look at next.

    Money laundering is making criminal money look clean. It has three stages: placement (cash enters the system), layering (moving it around to break the trail) and integration (it returns as clean-looking wealth).

    How it differs from fraud detection

    • Fraud has a clear label: a customer disputes a payment or a chargeback arrives. Models learn from "this was fraud".
    • AML has no such label. Nobody tells the bank who the launderers were. A "positive" usually means "an investigator escalated it", which reflects what old rules caught.
    • The goal also differs. The bank has a legal duty to report suspicion to the national financial intelligence unit (FIU; in the UAE, via goAML), without tipping off the customer.
    Part 2

    How it works, and why rules still run

    The system does not decide who is a launderer. It decides whom a human looks at next.

    1. Monitor. Transactions run through scenarios (rules) and sometimes models. The output is alerts.
    2. Triage. A first-line analyst clears obvious false alarms in minutes. Most alerts end here.
    3. Investigate. Survivors become cases. The investigator gathers the customer file, statements and screening hits, and judges whether the activity fits this customer.
    4. Report. If suspicion stands, the bank files a suspicious transaction report.
    5. Tune. Outcomes should feed back into the rules and models, though the loop is slow and biased.

    A rule (a "scenario") is a condition on transactions and time windows, like a smoke alarm: simple, loud, explainable, sometimes set off by toast. Common kinds are a threshold (one cash deposit over a limit), velocity (total value or count over a rolling window) and profile deviation (activity far above what the customer declared).

    Banks still run rules, even though ML is routine in fraud, because rules are explainable (an auditor can read them), label-free (they encode patterns from regulators and the FATF, the international standard-setter, so they catch things never seen in your data) and controlled (a threshold change is easy to test and supervisors are comfortable with it).

    This is structuring: cash is broken into pieces below a limit, like paying a large bill in many small instalments to avoid a form. Add a 7-day total rule and the criminal deposits every 8 days. Widen the window and the queue fills with shopkeepers and taxi owners whose normal business is cash. Rules are transparent and static, so anyone who learns the threshold learns how much they can move under it.

    Part 3

    Where machine learning helps

    ML does not replace monitoring. It does four useful jobs.

    • Alert prioritisation. Like an emergency-room triage nurse, a model trained on past alert outcomes scores new alerts so analysts work the likely ones first. Rules decide what alerts; the model decides the order. This is the most common and least controversial use.
    • Anomaly detection. Flags customers unlike their peers. It finds what no rule describes, but unusual is not suspicious: a wedding or a property sale is unusual too.
    • Network analytics. Laundering lives in relationships: shared devices, shared beneficiaries, circular flows. Graph methods expose rings that look ordinary account by account.
    • Entity resolution and investigator help. Linking the same person across Arabic and English spellings, and drafting case summaries.

    The label problem

    • Selection bias. Outcomes exist only for customers the rules alerted. A model trained on them imitates the rules and is blind to what they miss.
    • Circular benchmark. "Beating the rules on reported cases" may only mean predicting investigators better.
    • Few, late positives. Reports are rare and confirmed outcomes arrive months later.
    Part 4

    How to judge a system

    Accuracy and AUC mean little when almost nothing is truly suspicious. Measure what the business can act on.

    • Escalation rate. The share of reviewed alerts that become cases. Say 20,000 alerts yield 300 reports: 1.5%. Doubling that at the same capacity is a real gain.
    • Cases found at fixed capacity. With 45 minutes per review, 20,000 alerts need about 107 analysts. Halving false alerts saves over 50 salaries.
    • Efficiency versus effectiveness. Raising thresholds means fewer wasted reviews (efficiency) but can quietly miss real cases (effectiveness). Always ask which one a claim is about.
    • Below-the-line sampling. Check transactions just under each threshold. It is the only direct measure of what the system does not see.
    Show the arithmetic

    20,000 alerts × 45 minutes = 900,000 minutes = 15,000 hours a month. At about 140 productive hours per analyst, that is about 107 analysts. 300 reports out of 20,000 alerts is 1.5%, so 98.5% do not become reports. All figures are illustrative.

    A model trained on past cases is weakest where criminals changed method, while rules written from fresh guidance cover a new pattern fastest. They fail in different places, which is the honest case for keeping both.

    • Treat it as a model. Monitoring systems, including vendor ones, need documentation, independent validation and drift monitoring. Check what the local regulator expects.

    Another pattern: layering

    • Like passing a parcel through many hands: money moves through pass-through accounts (money in from many places, out within hours) and money mules (ordinary holders recruited or tricked into lending an account). Each hop can look unremarkable. The signal is the structure across many accounts, often at several banks.

    In banking, this is the heart of the financial crime team's workload: the real product is the ranking of what gets looked at, not the alert count.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Training on investigator outcomes and calling it truth

      Labels only exist where rules alerted, so the model inherits their blind spots.

    • Cutting alerts without checking misses

      Fewer alerts looks efficient. Sample below the threshold to see what you now miss.

    • Quoting accuracy

      With 98.5% false alarms, a system that flags nothing looks 98.5% accurate. Use escalation rate and cases found at fixed capacity.

    • Assuming unusual means suspicious

      Weddings and property sales are unusual. Anomaly scores need a human judgement.

    1. A rule alerts on cash deposits of AED 50,000 or more. How can a criminal beat it?

    By structuring: AED 49,000 on five mornings moves AED 245,000 with no alert.

    2. Why do banks keep rules when ML is available?

    Rules are explainable, need no labels and are easy to control. They cover new patterns quickly, where models trained on the past are weakest.

    3. Why is AML labelling harder than fraud labelling?

    Fraud has chargebacks. In AML a positive means an investigator escalated an alert, so labels exist only where old rules looked.

    4. What is the most common, safest use of ML here?

    Alert prioritisation: rules decide what alerts, the model decides the order.

    5. A vendor says its model halves false alerts. What do you ask?

    Whether real cases found at the same capacity stayed the same, checked with below-the-line samples.
