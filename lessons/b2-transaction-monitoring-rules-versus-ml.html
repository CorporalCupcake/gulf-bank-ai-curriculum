    Transaction Monitoring: Why Rules Still Run, Where ML Helps, and the Patterns Behind the Alerts

    How an anti-money-laundering alert is born and what it costs, why rules are useful but easy to dodge, what ML adds, and the three patterns investigators look for.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTransaction monitoring is a triage problem under a fixed analyst budget, against opponents who adapt: rules give coverage you can explain and defend, ML gives better ranking inside that budget, and neither can be judged without knowing what each one cannot see.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take a rule: "Alert on any cash deposit of AED 50,000 or more." (Illustrative figure.) It is cheap and easy to explain, and it has a hole. Someone with AED 250,000 of unexplained cash deposits AED 49,000 on five mornings and trips nothing. This is structuring, and it exists because the rule does.

    Add a rule on 7-day totals and the criminal deposits every 8 days. Widen the window and the queue fills with shopkeepers and taxi owners whose normal business is cash. Every fix moves the problem. This is a contest with a budget: fixed investigators, a duty to report, and opponents who adapt.

    Hold onto thisAlmost every alert is a false alarm, so the real product is the ranking of what gets looked at. Labels come from past investigations, so they inherit past blind spots. A rule that is known is a rule that can be worked around.

    Laundering has three stages: placement (cash enters the system), layering (moving it to break the trail) and integration (back as clean-looking wealth). This lesson covers three patterns: structuring, layering and trade-based laundering.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    From transaction to report: the pipeline and its economics

    A monitoring system does not decide who is a launderer. It decides whom a human looks at next.

    1
    Monitor.

    Transactions run through scenarios (and sometimes models). Output: alerts.

    2
    Triage.

    A first-line analyst clears obvious false alarms in minutes. Most alerts die here.

    3
    Investigate.

    Survivors become cases that investigators explain or escalate.

    4
    Report.

    If suspicion stands, the bank files a suspicious transaction report with the national FIU (UAE: goAML), without tipping off the customer. Outcomes should feed back into tuning, though the loop is slow and biased.

    Economics drive every design choice. Say 20,000 alerts a month yield 300 reports, so 98.5% do not. At 45 minutes per review that is about 107 analysts, and halving false alerts saves over 50 salaries. Two goals hide here: efficiency (fewer wasted reviews) and effectiveness (more real cases found). Raising thresholds improves the first and can quietly harm the second. Ask which one a claim is about.

    What rules are, and why they persist

    A scenario is a rule over transactions and time windows. Think of a smoke alarm: simple, loud, explainable, sometimes set off by toast. Common kinds:

    • Threshold: one cash deposit or transfer above a limit.
    • Velocity and aggregation: total value or count over a rolling window.
    • Profile deviation: activity far above what the customer declared (see customer risk rating).

    Banks still run rules, even though ML is routine in fraud, because they are:

    • Explainable. An analyst, auditor and supervisor can all read "7-day cash total above AED 50,000".
    • Label-free. Rules encode typologies from regulators and the FATF, so they catch patterns never seen in your data.
    • Controlled. Changing a threshold is testable, and supervisors are comfortable with it.

    The weakness is the same property: rules are transparent and static. Anyone who learns the threshold learns how much they can move under it.

    The takeaway: a longer window shrinks what a rule-aware criminal can move, but interrupts more innocent customers. With a 7-day window and a AED 50,000 limit, they can deposit just under the limit once a week and move about AED 250,000 a month unseen. No threshold makes both costs small, which is why you need behaviour that is hard to fake.

    Where machine learning actually helps

    ML does not replace monitoring. Its useful jobs are:

    1. Alert prioritisation. Like an ER triage nurse, a model trained on past alert outcomes scores new alerts so analysts work the likely ones first. Most common and least controversial: rules decide what alerts, the model decides order.
    2. Behavioural and anomaly detection. Flag customers unlike their peers (isolation forests and autoencoders). Finds what no rule describes, but unusual is not suspicious: a wedding or a property sale is unusual too.
    3. Network analytics. Laundering lives in relationships: shared devices, beneficiaries, circular flows. Graph methods (F9.2) expose rings that look ordinary account by account (Simulation 3).
    4. Entity resolution and investigator help. Linking the same person across Arabic and English spellings (name matching), and LLM summaries (internal copilots).

    The central difficulty is the label. In fraud, a chargeback says "this was fraud". In AML nobody says who the launderers were; your positive is "an investigator escalated it", which reflects what old rules surfaced. Three consequences:

    • Selection bias. Outcomes exist only for customers the rules alerted (selection bias). A model trained on them imitates the rules and is blind to their misses.
    • Circular benchmark. Beating the rules on "reported cases" may only mean predicting investigators better. Positives are also few and late (imbalanced data, delayed labels).

    Rules, ML, or both: a capacity-limited comparison

    Three designs are common. Rules only: alerts are what scenarios flag. ML ranks everyone: score the whole population, review the top. Hybrid: rules cast a wide net, a model re-ranks it. The test is how many real cases land inside the analyst budget.

    A model trained on past cases is weakest where criminals changed method, while rules written from fresh guidance can cover a new pattern fastest. They fail in different places, which is the honest case for keeping both.

    Tuning, validation and governance

    • Test above and below the line. Check alerts just above a threshold (is it noise?) and sample transactions just below it (are we missing real activity?). Below-the-line sampling is the only direct measure of what the system does not see.
    • Segment thresholds. Retail, SME and corporate customers behave differently, so tune each with a change log.
    • Treat it as a model. Monitoring systems, vendor ones included, need documentation, independent validation (G3) and drift monitoring (G3). Check what CBUAE or SAMA expects; I have not verified the local text.

    Measure what the business can act on: share of reviewed alerts escalated, cases found at fixed capacity, and the miss rate from below-the-line samples. Accuracy and AUC mean little at this prevalence (see F7).

    Typology 1: structuring (smurfing)

    Like paying a large bill in many small instalments to avoid a form: cash is broken into pieces below a limit, across days, branches or people's accounts. The signs are similar-sized deposits just under a round limit, several branches in a short period, and cash the business does not explain.

    Detection means aggregating across windows and linked accounts, and looking for a hump in deposit sizes just below the limit (an honest business has no reason to avoid exactly AED 50,000).

    Typology 2: layering

    Like passing a parcel through many hands so nobody can trace where it started. Money already in the system moves through accounts, companies and countries. Patterns:

    • Pass-through accounts: money arrives from many places and leaves within hours.
    • Money mules: ordinary holders recruited or tricked into lending an account.

    The key point: each hop can look unremarkable. The signal is the structure across many accounts, often at several banks. The simulation shows what each detector observes.

    What each detector sees:

    • A per-transfer rule is beaten by splitting. It is structuring again, in a network.
    • A pass-through feature (share of inflow leaving within hours) survives splitting but not patience, and flags innocents such as payroll processors.
    • A graph view sees one source reaching one destination through fan-out and fan-in, but only on edges you can observe. That is why regulators explore shared, privacy-preserving analytics.

    Typology 3: trade-based money laundering

    Think of a fake receipt: pay AED 160 for goods worth AED 100 and AED 60 per unit has moved quietly. Trade-based money laundering (TBML) does this with trade paperwork. Main techniques: over- and under-invoicing, invoicing a shipment several times, and false description of goods.

    It is hard to catch: trade finance is document-heavy, real prices vary so "wrong price" is noisy, and the two banks involved each see half. Gulf banks sit on busy trade corridors, so supervisors focus here (see document AI for trade finance). The value moved is the price gap times the quantity; a price check works against a reference, and the criminal stays inside its tolerance, as in Simulation 1.

    Real TBML detection adds price history per trader, consistency between shipping, customs and payment data, and name screening. The reference price stays a data problem, not a modelling one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Treating "reported" as "criminal"

      The model predicts reports, so it learns what investigators found, and "performance" is agreement with past decisions. Training only on old alerts makes it a copy of the rules.

      Spot itAsk what the label is and who produced it. Ask for the miss rate from a random or below-the-line sample, and whether training included unalerted customers.
    • Cutting false positives and calling it detection

      Alert volume drops 60%, and nobody checks whether the removed alerts included real cases.

      Spot itAsk for a back-test of the removed alerts: how many would have been reported?
    • A threshold that is known and static

      Rules tuned once and left alone. The ceiling from Simulation 1 becomes the operating norm.

      Spot itLook for the date of last tuning and alert-volume trends per scenario. A scenario with near-zero alerts for a year may be dead, not clean.
    • Leakage from the case outcome

      Features built from case-management fields or post-alert activity look predictive because they were created after the decision.

      Spot itCheck each feature exists at alert time (point-in-time correctness). An AUC above 0.95 on SAR outcomes is a warning.
    • Coverage gaps hidden by a good average, and unexplainable alerts

      Good overall, useless for trade-based activity or one segment. Or the alert says "score 0.93" and the analyst has no reason for the case file.

      Spot itReport performance by segment and scenario. Give an investigator ten alerts and ask them to explain each in a sentence.
    • Customer-group harm

      Higher alert rates for certain nationalities or cash-heavy trades reflect profile, not behaviour, and lead to account restrictions.

      Spot itCompare alert rates across groups (fairness definitions). Differences need a risk-based justification.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A bank produces 20,000 alerts a month and 300 become reports. Why is "98.5% false" not quite right, and what would halving the alerts change?

    "Not reported" is not the same as "pointless": some alerts were reasonable to review. Halving them saves a lot of analyst time, but only helps if the real cases are not among those removed.

    2. Why does lengthening a rule's window not solve structuring?

    A longer window shrinks what a rule-aware criminal can move unseen, but it also alerts far more innocent cash-heavy customers. They spread deposits one per window, so the trade-off never goes away.

    3. An importer is invoiced well above the normal unit price. What is moving, and in which direction?

    Value above fair price leaves with the payment (over-invoicing). Whether it is laundering, capital flight or an odd contract is for investigation; the price gap is the flag.

    4. Why can a hybrid (rules admit, model re-ranks) never find more than the rules admitted?

    The model can only re-rank what the rules let through. Real cases the rules missed never reach it. That is why rules and model need to be judged together.

    5. Why does splitting money across layers of accounts defeat a per-transfer rule?

    Each split shrinks individual transfers until they fall under the limit, even though the total is unchanged. Only a view of the whole structure shows it.

    6. Why is "agreement with past SARs" a weak proof that a model beats the rules?

    Past reports come from the old rules and the investigators who worked them. A model that mimics the rules scores well; one that finds something new scores badly. Use unbiased evidence: below-the-line samples, injected typologies, outside referrals.

    7. Name two reasons a graph view helps with layering, and one limit.

    It sees structure across accounts (fan-out, fan-in, circular flows) that no single account shows, and it survives splitting amounts. Limit: it only uses edges you can observe, and one bank sees part of the network.
