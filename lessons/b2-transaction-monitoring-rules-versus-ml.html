    Transaction Monitoring: Why Rules Still Run, Where ML Helps, and the Patterns Behind the Alerts

    How an anti-money-laundering alert is born and what it costs, why a threshold rule is both useful and easy to dodge, what machine learning really adds on top, and the three patterns (structuring, layering, trade-based laundering) that investigators are trained to see.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTransaction monitoring is a triage problem under a fixed analyst budget, against opponents who adapt: rules give coverage you can explain and defend, ML gives better ranking inside that budget, and neither can be judged without knowing what each one cannot see.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take a rule: "Alert on any cash deposit of AED 50,000 or more." (Illustrative figure; each bank sets its own.) It is cheap and easy to explain, and it has a hole. Someone with AED 250,000 of unexplained cash deposits AED 49,000 on five mornings. No deposit trips the rule. This is structuring, and it exists because the rule does.

    So add a rule: "Alert if 7-day cash deposits total AED 50,000." The criminal deposits every 8 days. Widen the window to 30 days and the queue fills with shopkeepers, jewellers and taxi owners whose normal business is cash. Every fix moves the problem.

    That is the field: a contest with a budget. The bank has a fixed number of investigators and a legal duty to report suspicion; the opponents study the controls and adapt.

    Hold onto thisAlmost every alert is a false alarm, so the real product is the ranking of what gets looked at. Labels come from past investigations, so they inherit past blind spots. Rules are known to the people evading them, so a rule's safe throughput can be calculated (Part 2 does it).

    Money laundering makes criminal proceeds look legitimate, in three stages: placement (cash enters the system), layering (moving it to break the trail) and integration (back as apparently clean wealth). Structuring is a placement technique, layering is its own pattern, and trade-based laundering uses commerce as cover. Knowing these typologies lets you judge whether a model's features and a rule library really cover the threats.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    From transaction to report: the pipeline and its economics

    A monitoring system does not decide who is a launderer. It decides whom a human looks at next.

    1
    Monitor.

    All transactions and customer profiles run through scenarios (and sometimes models). Output: alerts, each tagged with the scenario that fired.

    2
    Triage (first line).

    An analyst clears obvious false alarms in minutes using profile, expected activity and documents. Most alerts die here.

    3
    Investigate (second line).

    Survivors become cases. Investigators pull history, counterparties, KYC and open sources, and write why the activity is or is not explained.

    4
    Decide and report.

    If suspicion stands, the bank files a suspicious transaction report with the national FIU (UAE: goAML; Saudi Arabia: its FIU) and must not tip off the customer. Check current local rules.

    5
    Feed back.

    Outcomes should tune scenarios and train models. In practice the loop is slow and biased (Part 3).

    The economics drive every design choice. Say the bank raises 20,000 alerts a month and 300 end as reports: 1.5% conversion, 98.5% false alarms (industry commentary often puts false positives above 90%; definitions vary). At 45 minutes per first-line review that is 15,000 hours, about 107 analysts at 140 productive hours each. Halving false alerts saves over 50 salaries. Two different goals follow:

    • Efficiency: fewer wasted reviews.
    • Effectiveness: more real cases found.

    Efficiency can improve while effectiveness quietly drops, for instance by raising thresholds. Always ask which one a claimed improvement is about.

    What rules are, and why they persist

    A scenario is a rule over transactions and time windows. Think of a smoke alarm: simple, loud, explainable, and sometimes set off by toast. Families:

    • Threshold: one cash deposit or transfer above a limit.
    • Velocity and aggregation: total value or count over a rolling window, per customer, account or counterparty.
    • Rapid movement: money in, most of it out within hours.
    • Geography and counterparties: flows to or from higher-risk jurisdictions or listed entities.
    • Profile deviation: activity far above what the customer declared, or far from similar customers (see customer risk rating).
    • Dormant-account reactivation and new-account surges.

    Banks still run them, even though ML is routine in fraud, because:

    • Explainable. An analyst, auditor and supervisor can all read "7-day cash total above AED 50,000", and it maps to a typology in the risk assessment.
    • Coverage you can show. Supervisors ask which typologies you cover and why thresholds sit where they do. A documented library answers directly.
    • No labels needed. Rules encode typologies from regulators, the FATF and peers, so they catch patterns never seen in your data.
    • Stable and controlled. Changing a threshold is a testable, change-managed act.
    • Regulatory comfort. Moving beyond rules is allowed, but you must show the new approach is at least as good.

    The weakness is the same property: rules are transparent and static. Anyone who learns the threshold learns its safe throughput.

    Remember the ceiling. For an aggregation rule with limit T and window w days, a criminal who knows it moves about:

    unseen per 30 days≈T×(⌊29w⌋+1)

    One deposit just under T per window, spaced so no window holds two. With w = 7 and T = AED 50,000: five deposits, about AED 250,000 a month. Read it as the price of a rule: a longer window shrinks the ceiling but interrupts more innocent customers. No threshold makes both small, hence the need for behaviour that is hard to fake.

    Where machine learning actually helps

    ML does not replace monitoring. It does six specific jobs:

    1. Alert prioritisation. Like an ER triage nurse, a supervised model trained on past alert outcomes (cleared, escalated, reported) scores new alerts so analysts work the likely ones first. Most common and least controversial: rules still decide what alerts, the model decides order.
    2. Behavioural detection. Score every customer-period on many features (flows, counterparties, timing, channel mix, peers) and alert at the top. Finds what no single rule describes; needs labels or an unsupervised method, and is harder to explain.
    3. Anomaly and peer-group methods. No labels: flag customers unlike their peers (isolation forests and autoencoders). Good for discovery, but unusual is not suspicious: a new business, a wedding or a property sale are all unusual.
    4. Network analytics. Laundering lives in relationships: shared devices, addresses, beneficiaries, circular flows. Graph features and graph neural networks (F9.2) expose rings that look ordinary account by account (Simulation 3).
    5. Entity resolution. Linking records of the same person or company, across Arabic and English spellings (name matching). It quietly improves every other method.
    6. Investigator assistance. Summarising history, drafting narratives, retrieving similar cases. An LLM task (internal copilots) under human sign-off.

    The central difficulty is the label. In fraud, a chargeback says "this was fraud". In AML nobody says who the launderers were; your positive is "an investigator escalated it" or "a report was filed", which reflects what old rules surfaced and what people believed. Three consequences:

    • Selection bias. Outcomes exist only for customers the rules alerted (selection bias). A model trained on them imitates the rules and is blind to their misses.
    • Circular benchmark. Finding "more reported cases" than the rules may only mean predicting investigator behaviour better.
    • Few positives. Everything in imbalanced data applies: intervals on tiny counts, time-ordered splits, caution with resampling. Labels also arrive late (delayed labels).

    Rules, ML, or both: a capacity-limited comparison

    Three designs are common. Rules only: alerts are what scenarios flag, tuned until the queue fits the team. ML ranks everyone: score the whole population, review the top. Hybrid: rules cast a wide net, a model re-ranks it. The test is how many real cases land inside the analyst budget.

    The simulation assumes the model is equally good on every typology. In reality a model trained on past cases is weakest where criminals changed method, while rules written from fresh typology guidance can cover a new pattern fastest. They fail in different places. That is the honest case for keeping both, stronger than "supervisors like rules".

    Tuning, validation and governance

    Three practices make a monitoring system defensible.

    • Above- and below-the-line testing. Check alerts just above a threshold (is it noise?) and sample transactions just below it (are we missing real activity?). Below-the-line sampling is the only direct measure of what the system does not see, like the random review sample in imbalanced data.
    • Documented segmentation and tuning. Retail, SME, corporate and private banking behave differently, so set thresholds per segment with a rationale and change log.
    • Model risk management. Monitoring systems, vendor ones included, are models: they need documentation, independent validation (G3), drift monitoring (G3) and a reason per alert. US supervisors said so in a 2021 interagency statement; check what CBUAE or SAMA expects, as I have not verified the local text.

    Measure what the business can act on: share of reviewed alerts escalated, cases found at fixed capacity, ranking stability over time, and the miss rate from below-the-line samples. Accuracy and AUC mean little at this prevalence (see F7).

    Typology 1: structuring (smurfing)

    Structuring (smurfing) is like paying a large bill in many small instalments to avoid a form. Cash is broken into pieces below a limit, across days, branches, ATMs or several people's accounts (the carriers are "smurfs"). Visible signs:

    • Deposits clustered just under a round limit, of similar size.
    • Several branches in a short period.
    • Money wired or withdrawn straight after.
    • Cash that the declared business does not explain.

    Simulation 1 gave the maths. Detection follows: aggregate over windows and across linked accounts; look for a hump in deposit sizes just below the limit (an honest business has no reason to avoid exactly AED 50,000); compare with expected activity from onboarding; join device, branch and counterparty data. Each beats a single threshold because the criminal must change several things at once.

    Typology 2: layering

    Layering is passing a parcel through many hands so nobody can trace where it started. Cash already in the system moves through accounts, products, companies and countries. Patterns:

    • Pass-through or funnel accounts: money arrives from many places and leaves within hours.
    • Money mules: ordinary holders recruited or tricked into lending an account.
    • Rapid cross-border hops, and shell companies with no real activity.
    • Round-tripping: money leaves and returns as an apparent loan or investment.
    • High-value goods such as gold or real estate, relevant in a hub like Dubai.
    • Informal value transfer such as hawala: legitimate in many communities, also abused, so a red flag to examine, not a finding.

    The key point: each hop can look unremarkable. The signal is the structure across many accounts, often at several banks. The simulation shows what each detector observes.

    What each detector sees:

    • A per-transfer rule is beaten by splitting. It is structuring again, in a network.
    • A per-account pass-through feature (share of inflow leaving within hours) survives splitting but not patience, and flags innocents such as payroll processors. It needs peer context.
    • A graph view sees one source reaching one destination through fan-out and fan-in. It is the natural tool, but only on edges you can observe. That is why regulators, FIUs and industry projects explore shared, privacy-preserving analytics.

    Typology 3: trade-based money laundering

    Think of a fake receipt: pay AED 160 for goods worth AED 100 and AED 60 per unit has moved quietly. Trade-based money laundering (TBML) uses trade paperwork (invoices, bills of lading, letters of credit) the same way. The FATF lists the main techniques:

    • Over- and under-invoicing of goods.
    • Multiple invoicing of one shipment.
    • Over- and under-shipment.
    • False description of quality or type. Phantom shipments, with no goods at all, are the extreme case.

    It is hard to catch because trade finance is document-heavy and slow to automate; real prices vary with grade, timing and freight, so "wrong price" is noisy; the exporter's and importer's banks each see half; and legitimate trade is huge. Gulf banks sit on busy trade and re-export corridors, so supervisors focus here. For documents, see document AI for trade finance. The mechanic is arithmetic. With invoice price p, Q units and fair market price F:

    value moved=(p−F)×Q

    Over-invoicing (p above F) ships extra value abroad with the payment. Under-invoicing leaves part of the real price settled outside the paperwork. The gap is the laundering or capital flight. The check compares to a reference price; the criminal stays inside its tolerance, as in Simulation 1.

    Real TBML detection adds: unit-price history per trader and counterparty; consistency between shipping documents, customs data and payment; unusual routes and transshipment; counterparties with no trading profile; and name screening on all parties and vessels (B2 sanctions screening). ML helps most at linking documents and parties and at spotting repeats. The reference price stays a data problem, not a modelling one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Treating "reported" as "criminal"

      The model predicts reports, so it learns what investigators found, and "performance" is agreement with past decisions.

      Spot itAsk what the label is and who produced it. Ask for the miss rate from a random or below-the-line sample, not only recall on reported cases.
    • Cutting false positives and calling it detection

      Alert volume drops 60%, and nobody checks whether the removed alerts included real cases.

      Spot itAsk for a back-test of the removed alerts: how many would have been reported? With few positives, ask for the interval.
    • A threshold that is known and static

      Rules tuned once and left alone. The ceiling from Simulation 1 becomes the operating norm.

      Spot itLook for the date of last tuning, a change log and alert-volume trends per scenario. A scenario with near-zero alerts for a year may be dead, not clean.
    • Model trained on old alerts only

      Positives exist only where earlier rules fired, so the model copies them and cannot see what they missed.

      Spot itAsk whether training included unalerted customers with outcomes (sampling, outside referrals). If not, expect a copy of the rules.
    • Leakage from the case outcome

      Features built from case-management fields, narratives or post-alert activity look predictive because they were created after the decision.

      Spot itCheck each feature exists at alert time (point-in-time correctness). An AUC above 0.95 on SAR outcomes is a warning.
    • Coverage gaps hidden by a good average

      Good overall, useless for trade-based activity or one customer segment.

      Spot itMap the model to the typology library; report performance by segment and scenario. Ask which typology would pass unseen.
    • Unexplainable alerts

      The alert says "score 0.93". The analyst has no reason for the case file.

      Spot itGive an investigator ten alerts and ask them to explain each in a sentence using only what the system shows.
    • Customer-group harm

      Higher alert rates for certain nationalities, occupations or cash-heavy trades reflect profile, not behaviour, and lead to account restrictions.

      Spot itCompare alert and escalation rates across groups and check the reasons (fairness definitions). Differences need a risk-based justification.
    • Believing the reference price

      Price checks assume a reliable reference. For differentiated goods there may be none.

      Spot itAsk where the reference comes from, how old it is and how the spread was estimated per product class.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A bank produces 20,000 alerts a month; 300 become reports. First-line review takes 45 minutes. How many analysts at 140 productive hours a month, and what share of alerts are false?

    20,000 × 0.75 h = 15,000 hours; ÷ 140 ≈ 107 analysts. Conversion is 300 ÷ 20,000 = 1.5%, so 98.5% did not become a report. (Not reported is not quite "false": some were reasonable to review.)

    2. A rule aggregates cash over 14 days with a limit of AED 50,000. How much can a rule-aware criminal move per 30 days?

    ⌊29 ÷ 14⌋ + 1 = 3 deposits just under 50,000, so about AED 150,000 (days 0, 14 and 28).

    3. An importer is invoiced AED 140 a unit for 5,000 units normally worth AED 100. What value moves, and in which direction?

    (140 − 100) × 5,000 = AED 200,000 above fair value leaves with the payment (over-invoicing). Whether it is laundering, capital flight or an odd contract is for investigation; the price gap is the flag.

    4. Rules catch 60% of the suspicious customers they admit; a model re-ranks those and keeps 80% at your queue limit. What share of all suspicious customers does the hybrid find, and what is the assumption?

    0.6 × 0.8 = 48%, if the model's choices are independent of the rules' given the class. In reality scores are correlated. Either way, the hybrid can never exceed the rules' 60%.

    5. AED 900,000 is split into 3 at each of 2 layers, then merged into one account. What is the smallest transfer, how many transfers, how many accounts?

    Layer 1: 3 transfers of 300,000. Layer 2: 9 of 100,000. Fan-in: 9 of 100,000. Total 21 transfers, smallest AED 100,000. Accounts: 1 + 3 + 9 + 1 = 14.

    6. Why is "agreement with past SARs" a weak proof that a model beats the rules?

    Past reports come from the old rules and the investigators who worked them. A model that mimics the rules scores well; one that finds something new scores badly. Use unbiased evidence: below-the-line samples, injected typologies, outside referrals.

    7. Name two reasons a graph view helps with layering, and one limit.

    It sees structure across accounts (fan-out, fan-in, circular flows) that no single account shows, and it survives splitting amounts. Limit: it only uses edges you can observe, and one bank sees part of the network.
