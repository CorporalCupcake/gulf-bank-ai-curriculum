    B2.1 · B2.2 Day one ★ Priority About 40 minutes

    Transaction Monitoring: Why Rules Still Run, Where ML Helps, and the Patterns Behind the Alerts

    How an anti-money-laundering alert is born and what it costs, why a threshold rule is both useful and easy to dodge, what machine learning really adds on top, and the three patterns (structuring, layering, trade-based laundering) that investigators are trained to see.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTransaction monitoring is a triage problem under a fixed analyst budget, against opponents who adapt: rules give coverage you can explain and defend, ML gives better ranking inside that budget, and neither can be judged without knowing what each one cannot see.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a bank rule written in plain words: "Alert on any cash deposit of AED 50,000 or more." (The figure is illustrative; each bank sets its own, and regulators set some reporting limits.) It is clear, cheap and easy to explain to a supervisor. It also has an obvious hole. A person with AED 250,000 of cash they cannot explain deposits AED 49,000 on five different mornings. No single deposit trips the rule. This is structuring, and it exists because the rule exists.

    So the bank adds a second rule: "Alert if cash deposits over any 7 days add up to AED 50,000." Now the criminal deposits once every 8 days. The bank widens the window to 30 days, and the alert queue fills with shopkeepers, jewellers and taxi owners whose ordinary business is cash. Every fix moves the problem somewhere else.

    That small story is the whole field. Monitoring is a contest with a budget. The bank has a fixed number of investigators and a legal duty to report suspicion. The people it is looking for study the controls and adjust. A good system is one that stays useful anyway.

    Hold onto thisThree facts shape everything below. First, almost every alert is a false alarm, so the real product of a monitoring team is the ranking of what gets looked at. Second, the labels (which alerts turned out to be crime) come from past investigations, so they carry the blind spots of past systems. Third, rules are known to the people trying to evade them, so a rule's safe throughput can be calculated. Part 2 does that calculation.

    The second half of the lesson, B2.2, is the vocabulary of what investigators are looking for. Money laundering is the process of making criminal proceeds look legitimate. It is usually described in three stages: placement (getting cash into the system), layering (moving it around to break the trail) and integration (bringing it back as apparently clean wealth). Structuring is a placement technique, layering is its own pattern, and trade-based laundering uses commerce itself as the cover. Knowing the typologies is what lets you judge whether a model's features and a rule library actually cover the threats, instead of just producing scores.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    From transaction to report: the pipeline and its economics

    A monitoring system does not decide who is a money launderer. It decides who a human should look at next. The path from payment to regulator looks like this.

    1
    Monitor.

    Every transaction, plus profiles of each customer, run through scenarios (rules) and sometimes models. Output: a set of alerts, each with the scenario that fired.

    2
    Triage (first line).

    An analyst clears the obvious false alarms in minutes using customer profile, expected activity and documents. Most alerts die here.

    3
    Investigate (second line).

    Surviving alerts become cases. Investigators pull history, counterparties, KYC files, open sources, and write a narrative of why the activity is or is not explained.

    4
    Decide and report.

    If suspicion stands, the bank files a suspicious transaction report with the national financial intelligence unit (in the UAE, filed through the FIU's goAML platform; in Saudi Arabia, to its FIU), and must avoid tipping off the customer. Check the current local rules for details.

    5
    Feed back.

    Outcomes are meant to tune scenarios and train models. In practice this loop is slow and biased, as Part 5 explains.

    Now the economics, which drive every design choice. Suppose the bank produces 20,000 alerts a month, 300 of which end as reports. That is a conversion of 1.5%, or 98.5% false alarms. Industry commentary commonly puts false-positive rates above 90%, though figures vary by bank and by how "alert" is defined. If a first-line review takes about 45 minutes, that is 15,000 hours a month, around 107 full-time analysts at roughly 140 productive hours each. Halving false alerts saves more than 50 salaries. Doubling real detection finds more crime. Both matter, and they are different goals: efficiency (fewer wasted reviews) and effectiveness (more real cases found). A system can improve the first while quietly damaging the second, for instance by raising thresholds. Always ask which one a claimed improvement is about.

    What rules are, and why they persist

    A monitoring scenario is a rule over transactions and time windows. The common families:

    • Threshold: a single cash deposit or transfer above a limit.
    • Velocity and aggregation: total value or count over a rolling window, per customer, account or counterparty.
    • Rapid movement: money in and, within hours, most of it out again.
    • Geography and counterparties: transfers to or from higher-risk jurisdictions or listed entities.
    • Profile deviation: activity far above what the customer said at onboarding, or far from a peer group of similar customers. Compare with customer risk rating.
    • Dormant-account reactivation and new-account surges.

    Why do banks still run these, years after ML became routine in fraud? Several reasons, all practical.

    • Explainability to the letter. An analyst, an auditor and a supervisor can all read "7-day cash total above AED 50,000". The alert comes with its own reason, and the reason maps to a typology in the bank's risk assessment.
    • Coverage you can demonstrate. Supervisors ask "which typologies do you cover, and why are the thresholds where they are?" A scenario library with documented tuning answers that directly. A model's coverage is harder to argue.
    • No labels needed. Rules encode typologies from regulators, the FATF and peers. They can detect a pattern the bank has never seen in its own data.
    • Stability and control. Behaviour is predictable. Changing a threshold is a change-managed, testable act.
    • Regulatory comfort. Rules are the baseline everyone understands. Moving beyond them is allowed and encouraged in many jurisdictions, but you carry the burden of showing the new approach is at least as good.

    The weakness is the same property seen from the other side: rules are transparent and static. Anyone who learns the threshold learns its safe throughput. The next simulation computes it.

    The ceiling formula is worth remembering. A pure aggregation rule with limit T and window w days lets a criminal who knows it move about

    unseen per 30 days≈T×(⌊29w⌋+1)

    One deposit just under T per window, spaced so no window ever holds two. With w = 7 and T = AED 50,000 that is five deposits, about AED 250,000 a month. Rule tuning is therefore a trade between this ceiling and the number of innocent customers you interrupt. No threshold makes both small, which is the argument for adding behaviour that is hard to fake.

    Where machine learning actually helps

    It helps to be precise. ML does not "replace" monitoring; it does a few specific jobs, each with its own difficulty.

    1. Alert prioritisation (triage scoring). Train a supervised model on past alerts labelled by outcome (cleared at triage, escalated, reported) to score new alerts, so analysts work the likely ones first and low-scoring ones are reviewed more lightly. This is the most common and least controversial use, because the rules still decide what alerts; the model only decides order. Rules stay as the safety net.
    2. Behavioural detection on the whole population. Score every customer-period using many features (flows, counterparties, timing, channel mix, peer comparison), and alert on the top of the ranking. This can find activity no single rule describes. It needs labels or an unsupervised method, and it is harder to explain.
    3. Anomaly and peer-group methods. No labels needed: flag customers who differ from similar customers (isolation forests and autoencoders). Fine for discovering unknown patterns, but "unusual" is not "suspicious": a new business, a wedding, a property sale are all unusual.
    4. Network and graph analytics. Money laundering is about relationships between accounts: shared devices, addresses, beneficiaries, circular flows. Graph features and graph neural networks (F9.2) can expose a ring that looks ordinary account by account. Simulation 3 shows why.
    5. Entity resolution and enrichment. Linking records that belong to the same real person or company, including across Arabic and English spellings (see name matching). This quietly improves every other method.
    6. Investigator assistance. Summarising an account's history, drafting a case narrative, retrieving similar cases. This is the newest area and an LLM task (see internal copilots), kept under human sign-off.

    The central difficulty is the label. In fraud, a chargeback tells you the transaction was fraud. In AML, nobody tells you who the launderers were. Your "positive" is "an investigator escalated this" or "a report was filed". That reflects what the old rules surfaced and what investigators believed, not underlying truth. Three consequences follow.

    • Selection bias. You only have outcomes for customers the rules alerted on (F2.6, selection bias). A model trained on them learns to imitate the old rules and is blind to what they missed.
    • The benchmark is circular. A model that finds "more of the reported cases" than the rules may only be better at predicting investigator behaviour.
    • The positives are few. Reports are a tiny fraction of customers, so everything in imbalanced data applies: intervals on tiny counts, honest time-ordered splits, and caution with resampling. Delays are long as well; see delayed labels and feedback loops.

    Rules, ML, or both: a capacity-limited comparison

    Three designs are on the table in most banks. Rules only: alerts are whatever the scenarios flag, tuned until the queue is the size the team can handle. ML ranks everyone: score the whole population and review the top of the list. Hybrid: rules cast a wide net, then a model re-ranks the net so analysts see the best first. The question for each is how many real cases land inside the analyst budget.

    Notice what the simulation does not show, because it matters in the room. It assumes the model is as good on every typology. In reality a model trained on past cases is weakest exactly where the criminals have changed method, and rules written from fresh typology guidance can be quickest to cover a new pattern. Rules and models fail in different places. That is the honest argument for keeping both, and it is stronger than "supervisors like rules".

    Tuning, validation and governance

    Whatever the design, three practices decide whether a monitoring system is defensible.

    • Above-the-line and below-the-line testing. To tune a threshold you look at alerts just above it (are we reviewing noise?) and, crucially, sample transactions just below it (are we missing real activity?). Below-the-line sampling is the only direct measurement of what the system does not see. It is the monitoring equivalent of the random review sample in imbalanced data.
    • Documented segmentation and tuning. Retail, SME, corporate and private banking behave differently, so thresholds are set per segment with a recorded rationale and a change log.
    • Model risk management. Monitoring systems, including vendor ones, are models. They need development documentation, independent validation (G3), ongoing monitoring of performance and drift (G3), and an explanation route for each alert. US supervisors said as much in a 2021 interagency statement on model risk management for BSA/AML systems; check what your own regulator (CBUAE or SAMA) currently expects, because I have not verified the local text.

    Performance measures for a monitoring model should use what the business can act on: share of reviewed alerts that are escalated, cases found at fixed review capacity, stability of rankings over time, and the miss rate estimated from below-the-line samples. Accuracy and AUC alone mean little at this prevalence (see F7).

    Typology 1: structuring (smurfing)

    Breaking a large cash amount into pieces below a reporting or monitoring limit, often across days, branches, ATMs or several people's accounts (the people who carry the deposits are sometimes called smurfs). Typical visible features: deposits clustered just under a round limit; many similar-sized deposits; several branches in a short period; deposits that are immediately wired or withdrawn; a sudden rise in cash for a customer whose declared business does not explain it.

    You have already met its mathematics in Simulation 1. The detection ideas that follow from it are the ones banks use: aggregate over windows and across linked accounts; look at the distribution of deposit sizes for a hump just below the limit (a legitimate business has no reason to avoid exactly AED 50,000); compare to the customer's expected activity from onboarding; and join with device, branch and counterparty data. Each is stronger than a single threshold because the criminal has to keep changing more than one thing.

    Typology 2: layering

    After cash is in, layering moves it through accounts, products, companies and countries to separate it from its source. Patterns include: pass-through or funnel accounts (money arrives from many places and leaves within hours); money mules (ordinary account holders recruited or tricked into lending an account); rapid cross-border hops; shell or front companies with no real activity; round-tripping (money leaves and returns as an apparent loan or investment); and use of high-value goods such as gold or real estate, which matter in a trading hub like Dubai. Informal value transfer systems, including hawala, can also appear. They are legitimate in many communities, regulated and abused, so treat the label as a red flag to examine, not a finding.

    The key technical point: each hop can look unremarkable. The signal is in the structure across many accounts, often at several banks. The simulation draws a layering chain so you can see what each detector gets to observe.

    What the picture tells you about detection design:

    • A per-transfer rule is defeated by splitting. This is structuring again, in a network.
    • A per-account pass-through feature (share of inflow that leaves within hours) is robust to splitting but not to patience, and it also flags innocent accounts such as a payroll processor or a shared-rent account. It needs context and peer comparison.
    • A graph view sees that one source reaches one destination through a fan-out and fan-in. It is the natural tool, but it only works with the edges you can observe. Cross-institution visibility is the reason regulators, FIUs and some industry projects have explored shared analytics and privacy-preserving collaboration (Part 4).

    Typology 3: trade-based money laundering

    Trade-based money laundering (TBML) uses the paperwork of international trade to move value: invoices, bills of lading, letters of credit. The FATF describes the main techniques as over- and under-invoicing of goods, multiple invoicing of the same shipment, over- and under-shipment, and falsely describing the goods' quality or type. Phantom shipments, where no goods move at all, are the extreme case.

    Why it is hard: trade finance is document-heavy and slow to automate; prices of real goods vary a lot (quality, grade, timing, freight terms), so "wrong price" is a noisy signal; value is split across the exporter's and importer's banks, each seeing half; and legitimate trade is enormous. Gulf banks sit in some of the world's busiest trade and re-export corridors, so the topic is a supervisory focus. For the document side, see document AI for trade finance.

    The core mechanic is plain arithmetic. If an importer pays an invoice price p for Q units whose fair market price is F:

    value moved=(p−F)×Q

    Over-invoicing (p above F) sends extra value abroad with the payment. Under-invoicing leaves part of the real price to be settled outside the paperwork. In both cases the difference is the laundering or the capital flight. The detection tool is a price check against a reference, and the criminal's countermeasure is to stay inside whatever tolerance the check uses, just as in Simulation 1.

    Real TBML detection combines the price check with other things: unit-price history of the same trader and counterparty; consistency between shipment documents, customs data and payment; unusual routes and transshipment; counterparties with no plausible trading profile; and name screening on all parties and vessels (B2 sanctions screening). ML helps most at linking documents and parties across files and at spotting repeated patterns. The reference-price problem remains a data problem, not a modelling one.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Monitoring is where the statistics from Foundations meet regulation and operations. These are the neighbours.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataThe same arithmetic, rarer positives

    Suspicious customers are far rarer and the labels worse than in card fraud. Base rates, intervals on tiny counts and honest splits all carry over.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, thresholdsChoosing the cut with a queue limit

    Monitoring is "top k at fixed capacity". Precision at k and recall at k are the numbers that matter, not AUC.

    F4.7 · Anomaly detectionUnlabelled discovery

    Peer-group and isolation methods find the unusual. Alert reasons must still be stated in AML terms.

    F9.2 · GraphsSeeing who is connected to whom

    Layering and mule networks are graph problems. Features such as fan-in, fan-out and shared devices come straight from here.

    F8 · ExplanationsWhy was this customer alerted?

    An investigator needs reasons in words. Local explanations of a score are an aid, not a substitute for a rule-based or narrative justification.

    F2.6 · Selection biasOutcomes exist only where someone looked

    All AML labels are downstream of earlier alerting. Below-the-line sampling is the antidote.

    B1 · Fraud detectionCousins with different clocks

    Fraud is real-time with fast labels and a victim who complains. AML is batch-ish with slow, subjective labels and no complaining victim. Techniques transfer; evaluation does not. See also delayed labels.

    B2 · Sanctions screeningNames and lists

    Monitoring watches behaviour; screening checks identity against lists. They share entity resolution and a flood of false positives.

    B3 · Customer due diligenceExpected activity and risk rating

    Profile-deviation rules compare behaviour to what the customer declared. Poor onboarding data means poor monitoring.

    G3 · Validation and monitoringModels the regulator will test

    A monitoring model faces independent validation and periodic review. Design for explanations, change logs and coverage evidence from the start.

    A9 · Scams and mule accountsWhere fraud and AML meet

    Scam proceeds flow through mule accounts; the same graph and behavioural signals serve both teams. See also synthetic identities.

    A8 · Foundation models for transactionsLearning from the raw sequence

    Pretrained sequence models on transaction histories are an active route to better behavioural features.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Retail and SME cash activity. Structuring, cash-intensive businesses, and the false-alert flood from honest traders.
    • Correspondent and cross-border payments. Layering through high-risk corridors, with the visibility limit of seeing only your own leg.
    • Trade finance desks. Letters of credit, collections, invoices and TBML red flags, mostly reviewed by people today.
    • Precious metals, real estate and other designated sectors. Customers in higher-risk businesses that call for enhanced monitoring; check current local sector rules.
    • The case management team. The consumer of every model output. Their time budget is the design constraint, so talk to them early.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    The failures below are the ones seen in bank projects and in vendor claims. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Treating "reported" as "criminal"

      The model is trained to predict reports. It learns what investigators found, not what exists, and "performance" is agreement with past decisions.

      Spot itAsk what the label is and who produced it. Ask for the miss rate from a random or below-the-line sample, not only recall against reported cases.
    • Cutting false positives and calling it detection

      An alert volume drop of 60% is announced. Nobody checks whether the alerts removed included real cases.

      Spot itAsk for a back-test of the removed alerts: how many would have been reported? With few positives, ask for the interval.
    • A threshold that is known and static

      Rules tuned once and left alone. Criminals and ordinary customers both change; the ceiling from Simulation 1 becomes the operating norm.

      Spot itLook for date of last tuning, a change log, and trends in alert volume per scenario. A scenario with near-zero alerts for a year may be dead, not clean.
    • Model trained on old alerts only

      Positives exist only where earlier rules fired, so the model reproduces them and cannot see activity those rules missed.

      Spot itAsk whether the training population included unalerted customers with outcomes (from sampling or referrals from other channels). If not, expect a copy of the rules.
    • Leakage from the case outcome

      Features built from case-management fields, narratives or post-alert activity. They look predictive because they were created after the decision.

      Spot itCheck each feature is available at alert time (point-in-time correctness). An AUC above 0.95 on SAR outcomes is a warning.
    • Coverage gaps hidden by a good average

      A model that does well overall but nothing for trade-based activity, for a customer segment, or for a typology.

      Spot itMap the model to the typology library and report performance by segment and scenario. Ask which typology would pass unseen.
    • Unexplainable alerts

      The alert says "score 0.93". The analyst has no reason to write in the case file, and a supervisor cannot follow the logic.

      Spot itGive an investigator ten alerts and ask them to explain each in a sentence using only what the system shows.
    • Customer-group harm

      Higher alert rates for certain nationalities, occupations or cash-intensive trades that reflect profile rather than behaviour, leading to account restrictions.

      Spot itCompare alert and escalation rates across groups and check the reasons (fairness definitions). Differences need a justification tied to risk.
    • Believing the reference price

      Price checks assume a reliable reference. For differentiated goods there may be none.

      Spot itAsk where the reference comes from, how old it is, and how the spread was estimated per product class.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a head of financial crime · 30 seconds
    “We have a fixed number of investigators and a queue where most alerts are false. My proposal keeps the current rules as the coverage baseline, and adds a model that ranks alerts so your people see the likelier ones first. I'd prove it by taking a random sample of the alerts we would not have reviewed, and checking how many were real, so we know what we gain and what we risk. We would not turn any rule off until that evidence is in and validated.”
    To a model validator or regulator
    “The label is escalation or report, so it inherits the selection of the old rules. I'm correcting for that with a below-the-line sample and by reporting the miss rate with an interval, not only recall on reported cases. Performance is shown as cases found at fixed review capacity, by segment and by typology, forward in time. Every alert carries a reason code an investigator can state. Rules remain in place for typology coverage, and I can show where the model adds or loses coverage relative to them.”
    “Why do we still use rules? Can't ML do all of this?”

    Rules give coverage we can explain, document and show to a supervisor, and they work with no labels, including for patterns we have never seen. ML ranks better inside a fixed analyst budget and can find behaviour no rule describes. They fail in different places, so the strong design uses both: rules for coverage, ML for ranking and discovery.

    “A vendor says its AI cuts false positives by 60%. Should we buy?”

    Maybe, but a lower alert count is not a better system. Ask: against which baseline, on whose data, how was the removed 60% checked for real cases, and was it independently tested? Public figures from large-bank pilots are self-reported. I'd run it in shadow on our alerts, sample the alerts it would suppress, and compare cases found at the same capacity.

    “How do you know what the model misses if nobody knows who the launderers are?”

    You can't know exactly, but you can estimate. Sample transactions and customers below the alert line, have experienced investigators review them blind, and use the proportion found as a miss-rate estimate with an interval. You can also test with injected known typologies and seeded synthetic cases, and compare with referrals from outside the monitoring system, such as law-enforcement requests.

    “Structuring: why not just lower the threshold?”

    Lowering it buys a small ceiling at the cost of many more false alerts, because ordinary cash businesses sit near any sensible limit. The arithmetic: a rule-aware criminal can move roughly the limit times the number of non-overlapping windows in a month. Better to add what is hard to fake: aggregation across linked accounts, deposit-size distributions, and comparison with expected activity.

    “Can we use an LLM to write the case narratives?”

    As a drafting aid with a named investigator reviewing and owning every report, yes, in principle. The risks are invented facts, leakage of customer data to a model host, and subtle tipping-off in wording. I'd constrain it to facts from the case file with source links, log every draft, measure edit rates and factual errors on a sample, and get compliance and legal sign-off before use.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A bank produces 20,000 alerts a month; 300 become reports. Each first-line review takes 45 minutes. How many analysts, at 140 productive hours a month, and what share of alerts are false?

    20,000 × 0.75 h = 15,000 hours; ÷ 140 ≈ 107 analysts. Conversion is 300 ÷ 20,000 = 1.5%, so 98.5% did not become a report. (Not becoming a report is not quite the same as being "false": some were reasonable to review. The label matters.)

    2. A rule aggregates cash over 14 days with a limit of AED 50,000. About how much can a rule-aware criminal move per 30 days?

    ⌊29 ÷ 14⌋ + 1 = 3 deposits, each just under 50,000, so about AED 150,000. Windows that do not overlap deposits is the trick: deposits on days 0, 14 and 28.

    3. An importer is invoiced AED 140 a unit for 5,000 units of goods that normally cost AED 100. What value is moved, and which direction?

    (140 − 100) × 5,000 = AED 200,000 more than fair value, which leaves the country with the payment (over-invoicing). Whether it is laundering, capital flight or just an unusual contract is for investigation; the price gap is the flag.

    4. Rules catch 60% of the suspicious customers they admit; a model re-ranks those and keeps 80% of them at your queue limit. What share of all suspicious customers does the hybrid find, and what is the assumption behind that?

    0.6 × 0.8 = 48%, if the model's choices are independent of the rules' (given the class). In reality scores are correlated, which changes the figure. The more important point: the hybrid can never exceed the rules' own 60%.

    5. AED 900,000 is split into 3 at each of 2 layers, then merged into one account. What is the smallest transfer, how many transfers, how many accounts?

    Layer 1: 3 transfers of 300,000. Layer 2: 9 transfers of 100,000. Fan-in: 9 transfers of 100,000. Total 21 transfers, smallest AED 100,000. Accounts: 1 source + 3 + 9 + 1 destination = 14.

    6. Why is "agreement with past SARs" a weak way to prove a model is better than the rules?

    Past reports are produced by the old rules and the investigators who worked them. A model that mimics the rules scores well, and a model that finds something new scores badly. You need unbiased evidence, such as below-the-line samples, injected typologies and outside referrals.

    7. Name two reasons a graph view helps with layering, and one limit.

    It sees structure across accounts (fan-out, fan-in, circular flows) that no single account shows, and it is robust to splitting amounts. The limit: it can only use edges you can observe, and one bank sees only part of the network, so some hops are invisible without cross-institution data sharing.
