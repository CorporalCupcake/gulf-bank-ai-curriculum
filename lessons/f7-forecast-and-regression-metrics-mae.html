    F7.5 · F7.6 Day one About 40 minutes

    Scoring Forecasts and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE, Quantile Loss, NDCG

    Every error measure quietly chooses what the model should aim at: the median, the mean, or a biased-low guess. How to pick the measure that matches what a miss costs, how to score a forecast that gives a range, and how to grade an ordered list such as an alert queue.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn error metric is a statement about which mistakes hurt: MAE aims at the median, RMSE at the mean and punishes big misses, MAPE rewards under-forecasting, quantile loss lets you say that running short costs more than holding spare, and ranking metrics (precision@k, NDCG, MRR) grade only the order of a list, so choose the one whose answer you would be happy to be optimised for.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A treasury team forecasts how much cash customers will withdraw from a cluster of ATMs tomorrow, so that vans can load the right amount. Three analysts build models. One reports "average error AED 6,000". Another reports "RMSE AED 9,500". A third reports "MAPE 4.8%". All three are honest. None of them can be compared with the others, and the one that looks best on its own number may be the worst for the bank.

    The reason is that each number is a different question about the errors. MAE asks how far off you are on a typical day. RMSE squares the misses first, so one terrible day counts as much as many small ones. MAPE divides each miss by the actual value, so a 10,000 miss on a quiet day counts far more than the same miss on a payday. And none of them knows that a machine running empty on the Thursday before Eid is far worse than one holding extra cash overnight.

    The deeper point is what happens when you train against the number, or choose between models by it. A metric has a built-in target. Minimise squared error and the model learns to predict the mean. Minimise absolute error and it learns the median. Minimise percentage error and it learns something smaller than the median, on purpose, because that is how the arithmetic works. The first simulation shows this happening.

    Three things to take away.

    • The metric you pick is the target you train towards. Choosing it after the fact, to make a model look good, is the quiet form of cheating in forecasting.
    • "Which error is best?" has no general answer. It depends on what a miss costs: is a big miss much worse than two small ones? Is under-forecasting worse than over-forecasting? Is a 5% miss on a small account as bad as 5% on a large one?
    • Ranking is a different problem. When the output is an ordered list (alerts for investigators, documents for a search, offers for a customer), nobody cares about the score itself. They care about whether the right items sit near the top, because only the top gets looked at. That needs its own metrics, covered from Part 2.
    Hold onto thisBefore choosing a metric, finish the sentence "a miss is expensive when ...". If the answer is "when it is big", use RMSE. If it is "when it is on the short side", use quantile loss. If it is "when it is large relative to the customer", use a percentage or scaled error, carefully. If the answer is "when the right item is not near the top", use a ranking metric.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    MAE and RMSE: the median and the mean

    Let yt be what actually happened and ŷt what you forecast, over n periods. The two workhorses are:

    MAE=1n∑t|yt−y^t|RMSE=1n∑t(yt−y^t)2

    Both are in the units of the thing forecast (AED, customers, days), which makes them easy to explain. MAE is the average size of a miss. RMSE squares first, averages, then takes the root. RMSE is always at least as large as MAE; the gap between them tells you how uneven the misses are. If every miss were the same size they would be equal. Misses of 10 and 50 give MAE 30 and RMSE 36.

    What each one rewards is the interesting part, and Simulation 1 shows it. For any set of outcomes, the single number that minimises squared error is the mean, and the one that minimises absolute error is the median. A model trained on squared error is therefore aiming at the conditional average; one trained on absolute error is aiming at the conditional middle. For symmetric, well-behaved data they coincide. For skewed data (cash demand with paydays, transaction amounts, claim sizes, time to repay) they do not, and the choice is a real modelling decision, not a detail. This is the same story as the loss functions in F6: squared error is Gaussian maximum likelihood, absolute error is Laplace.

    Practical guidance:

    • RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly (a large unexpected cash call, a large mis-forecast of deposits outflow) and the data has no wild outliers that are errors rather than events.
    • MAE when the cost grows in proportion to the miss, or when a few extreme days would otherwise dominate model selection. It is also the easiest for a business reader.
    • Report both, and the ratio. RMSE/MAE well above 1.25 or so signals heavy-tailed errors: a few days are causing most of the pain. Ask which days.
    • Neither says whether the model beats a sensible alternative. Always show the same metric for a naive baseline (yesterday, or the same weekday last week). A model with MAE 6,000 sounds fine until the naive rule scores 5,500.

    Percentage errors and scale-free alternatives

    To compare across series of different sizes (a Dubai flagship branch against a small Fujairah one) you need errors that are scale-free. The obvious fix is to divide by the actual, giving MAPE = the average of |y − ŷ| ÷ y. It is popular because "4.8%" needs no units. It also has three known defects:

    • It explodes near zero. A branch with AED 200 of a rare product sold, forecast 1,000, scores 400% on its own and swamps the average. At exactly zero it is undefined, which is common for sparse series (new products, small segments, weekends in a closed branch).
    • It is lopsided. Over-forecasting has no ceiling (forecast 300 when truth is 100: 200% error), while under-forecasting is capped at 100% (forecast 0). So if you minimise MAPE, the safe move is to forecast low. In Simulation 1, the red curve bottoms out below the median. The minimiser is the median of the values weighted by 1/y, which pulls it towards the small values.
    • It treats every observation alike. A 20% miss on a day with AED 10m of flow counts the same as 20% on a day with AED 100k.

    The alternatives that practitioners actually use:

    One more practical point: when you average across many series, you are choosing weights. A simple mean of MASE treats a tiny branch as equal to a flagship; a volume-weighted number does not. Neither is wrong. Both must be stated. In the M5 forecasting competition, which used 42,840 Walmart sales series, scores were weighted by the dollar sales of each series for exactly this reason.

    Last, remember the forecast horizon and the calendar. Error on day 1 and error on day 14 are different quantities; report them per horizon. For anything Islamic-calendar driven (Ramadan, Eid, school holidays) the dates move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year and the Gulf weekend differs by country and by year, so a plain "same day last year" baseline is quietly wrong. Check the baseline is aligned to the right occasion before judging a model against it. Time series evaluation done properly is covered in F9, and the temporal splits it needs in F4.

    Quantile loss: when running short costs more than holding spare

    The three metrics above score a single "best guess". Many bank decisions are not about the best guess. How much cash to load? How large a liquidity buffer to hold? Capacity for a call centre on Monday? In each, one side of the error is more expensive. An ATM that runs dry loses customers and fees and triggers an emergency run; cash that sits idle for a night costs a little in funding. If the shortfall is nine times as costly as the excess, you do not want the mean or the median. You want a number that is exceeded only about 1 time in 10.

    That number is a quantile of the demand distribution, and the right score for it is the pinball loss (or quantile loss). For a target quantile τ between 0 and 1, with miss u = y − ŷ:

    ρτ(u)=τ·uif u≥0 (actual above forecast: you were short)(1−τ)·|u|if u<0 (actual below forecast: you held spare)

    At τ = 0.5 this is half the absolute error, so the median is just the 0.5 quantile. At τ = 0.9, being 20 short costs 0.9 × 20 = 18, while being 20 over costs 0.1 × 20 = 2. The forecast that minimises the average pinball loss is exactly the τ-quantile of the outcomes. That is why a model trained on it learns "the level I will exceed only 10% of the time".

    The connection to money is direct. If a shortfall costs cu per unit and surplus costs co per unit, the cost-minimising plan is the quantile with

    τ* = cu ÷ (cu + co)

    This is the classic "newsvendor" result from inventory theory, and it is the same logic as the cost-based alert threshold in F7.3: turn business costs into a probability level, then read the decision off the distribution. Shortfall nine times worse than surplus gives τ* = 0.9. Equal costs give the median.

    Several extensions are worth knowing.

    • Forecast intervals are two quantiles. A 80% interval is the 10% and 90% quantile forecasts together. Score each with pinball loss at its own τ. Check coverage too: about 80% of actuals should fall inside. A model with great pinball loss but 60% coverage is overconfident in a way that matters.
    • Averaging pinball loss over many τ values gives the CRPS (continuous ranked probability score), which grades an entire predictive distribution, not one point. It is the standard single number for probabilistic forecasts and sits in a family of "proper" scoring rules, in which your best strategy is to report your true beliefs (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Pinball loss itself is proper for the quantile it targets. MAPE is not proper for anything useful, and that is the formal version of "it rewards forecasting low".
    • Quantile loss trains models directly. Gradient boosting, linear models and neural networks all accept it as an objective, so you can fit a 90% quantile without a distributional assumption. Quantiles fitted separately can cross (the 90% line dipping below the 50%); sort them or use a method that forbids it. For guaranteed coverage regardless of the model, see conformal prediction.
    • Where it appears: liquidity buffers and funding needs (B5), cash logistics, staffing of call centres and branches, and loss forecasts where the tail drives capital. In each, the point forecast is the least useful output.

    For expected-loss work such as IFRS 9, the accounting value is the probability-weighted mean, so RMSE-like or likelihood-based scores and calibration (F7.4) are the right tools there, not a 90% quantile. The metric follows the decision.

    Ranking metrics: precision@k, NDCG and MRR

    Now change the problem. A transaction-monitoring model scores 4,000 alerts overnight. Twenty investigators can review 300 before lunch. A search tool returns 10 passages from a policy library. A recommender shows a customer 5 offers. In all of these the order is the product. The score value hardly matters; what matters is whether the items that deserve attention sit in the part that is actually read. (If you also need the score to be a true probability, that is a separate question: calibration, F7.4.)

    The metrics all start from the same picture: a ranked list, each item marked relevant or not (or given a grade), and a cut-off k for how deep anyone looks.

    MetricQuestion it answersHow it is computedBlind to
    Precision@kOf the k items shown, how many were worth it?Relevant items in the top k ÷ k.Order within the top k, and anything beyond it. Never above 1; bounded by how many relevant items exist.
    Recall@kOf everything worth finding, how much was in the top k?Relevant in the top k ÷ all relevant.How much junk you made people wade through.
    MRR (mean reciprocal rank)How far down is the first good item?Average over queries of 1 ÷ rank of the first relevant item. First at rank 1 gives 1, rank 2 gives 0.5, rank 10 gives 0.1.Everything after the first hit. Right when there is a single answer to find.
    Average precision (MAP over queries)Are relevant items packed towards the top?Average of precision@r taken at each rank r where a relevant item sits.Gradations: relevant is yes or no.
    NDCG@kHow good is the ordering, given that some items are more valuable than others, relative to the best possible order?See below. Between 0 and 1; 1 is a perfect ordering.Needs graded labels; the gain and discount are choices.

    NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain; Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002) is the one to understand properly, because it is what retrieval and recommendation benchmarks report. It has three moves. Give each item a gain from its grade, so a critical alert counts for more than a minor one. Discount that gain by position, so being at rank 1 counts for more than rank 8. Then normalise by the best score achievable for that list, so queues of different quality and size are comparable.

    DCG@k=∑i=1k2gi−1log2(i+1)NDCG@k=DCG@kideal DCG@k

    gi is the grade of the item at rank i (say 0 = benign, 1 = minor, 2 = suspicious, 3 = confirmed). Gain 2g − 1 makes a grade-3 item worth 7 and a grade-1 item worth 1. The discount 1 ÷ log2(i + 1) is 1 at rank 1, 0.63 at rank 3 and 0.5 at rank 7. Worked example: a list with grades [0, 3, 1] scores 7/1.585 + 1/2 = 4.92; the ideal order [3, 1, 0] scores 7 + 1/1.585 = 7.63; NDCG = 0.64.

    The simulation brings out the judgement calls.

    • Choose k from the operation, not from habit. If investigators can review 300 alerts, precision@300 is the number the business will experience. NDCG@10 is a convention from web search; borrowing it for an alert queue measures something nobody is doing.
    • Precision@k has a ceiling. If a queue has only 4 true cases and k = 10, precision@10 can never exceed 0.4 even for a perfect model. Compare against the ideal for that queue, which is what NDCG's normalisation does.
    • MRR suits "find the one right answer" (retrieving the single policy paragraph that answers a question, matching a name on a sanctions list), and punishes everything else too little. For alerts with many cases to find, use precision, recall and NDCG.
    • Grades and gains are policy. Whether a confirmed case is worth 3 times or 7 times a suspicious one changes model rankings. Agree it with the business before looking at results.
    • These metrics ignore the score. Two models with the same ordering score identically even if one outputs 0.99 for everything. They say nothing about calibration or about whether to review item 301 at all; that depends on the cost threshold from F7.3.
    • Averages hide queries. A mean NDCG of 0.7 can be many good queries and a few disasters. Look at the spread, and cut by segment (Arabic and English queries, retail and corporate customers).

    How it connects

    Error measures are the point where modelling, business cost and honest evaluation meet. They come back in these places.

    Cost-based thresholds there and the newsvendor quantile here are the same idea: costs become a probability level, and the decision follows.

    F6 · Loss functionsMetrics versus training losses

    Squared error, absolute error and pinball loss are all training losses too. Training and evaluating on the same loss is the easy case; mismatch is where surprises live.

    F4 · Cross-validation and baselinesForward-in-time evaluation

    Every forecast metric needs a split that respects time, and a baseline to divide by (MASE) or compare against.

    F9 · Time series and forecastingWhat the metrics are scoring

    Seasonality, horizons and rolling-origin back-tests. This lesson says how to grade a forecast; F9 says how to make one.

    F9 · Recommenders and rankingRanking in practice

    Next-best-offer systems are judged by NDCG and precision@k offline, but the true test is an online experiment.

    M3 · RetrievalSearch quality is NDCG and MRR

    Retrieval for document assistants is tuned and compared on NDCG@10, recall@k and MRR. Fusing rankings (reciprocal rank fusion) is judged by these same numbers.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · IntervalsError metrics are estimates

    A MAE or NDCG on 60 days or 80 queries has a margin of error. Bootstrap the days or queries before claiming one model wins.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringWatching the metric over time

    Forecast bias creeping away from zero, or precision@capacity decaying, are monitoring signals in themselves.

    R8 · Conformal predictionIntervals with a guarantee

    When a quantile forecast's coverage cannot be trusted, conformal methods wrap any model to restore it.

    A4 · Statistics for evalsComparing scores honestly

    The same paired-difference and interval logic applies to NDCG across queries and to LLM-judge scores.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Cash and ATM logistics. Quantile forecasts with asymmetric costs; MASE or WAPE across hundreds of machines; careful alignment of Eid and payday effects.
    • Liquidity and deposit forecasting. Outflow quantiles (the bad tail) matter more than the mean; the same forecast also gets a point metric for reporting. See B5.
    • Loss and balance forecasting for finance. RMSE or bias on aggregated portfolios, with WAPE for the story told to executives. For provisioning, calibration and expected value rather than quantiles.
    • Alert prioritisation for fraud and AML. Precision@capacity, recall of confirmed cases in the top slice, NDCG when cases have severity grades (see B2). The labels are delayed and selective (B1), so mature them before scoring.
    • Search, copilots and recommendations. Staff or customer assistants backed by document retrieval, judged by recall@k and NDCG@k on questions in both Arabic and English; next-best-offer ranking judged by precision@k offline and by experiment online.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basics are settled. The live developments are the move from point forecasts to probabilistic ones scored with proper rules, the standardisation of benchmarks around scale-free metrics, and a growing awareness that ranking evaluation shortcuts can mislead.

    Written in November 2026 from established literature. I did not run a fresh web check for this lesson, so recent items below are from memory and worth a quick look at the source before you quote them.

    1. 2002
      NDCG

      Järvelin and Kekäläinen introduced cumulative gain with a positional discount for graded relevance. It became the default retrieval metric.

      Järvelin & Kekäläinen, ACM TOIS, 2002
    2. 2006
      MASE: a scale-free error that avoids MAPE's problems

      Hyndman and Koehler proposed scaling errors by those of a naive forecast, which works across series and with zeros.

      Hyndman & Koehler, International Journal of Forecasting, 2006

      Proper scoring rules and "make the metric match the question"

      Gneiting and Raftery set out strictly proper scoring rules for probabilistic forecasts. Gneiting then showed that a point forecast should be requested for a stated functional (mean, median, a quantile), and scored with a loss that is consistent for it. Squared error for the mean, absolute error for the median, pinball for quantiles.

      Gneiting & Raftery, JASA, 2007; Gneiting, JASA, 2011
    4. 2013
      NDCG is consistent for ranking

      Theory work showed that NDCG-based evaluation reliably distinguishes ranking functions, which helped justify its use as an optimisation target.

      Wang et al., COLT, 2013
    5. 2018–22
      The M4 and M5 competitions

      M4 (100,000 series) scored by a mix of sMAPE and MASE, found that combinations of methods beat single ones and that a hybrid of statistical and machine learning won. M5 (retail sales for Walmart) used a weighted RMSSE for point forecasts and a weighted scaled pinball loss for its uncertainty track, with gradient boosting among the leaders.

      Makridakis et al., Int. J. Forecasting, 2020 and 2022
    6. 2020
      Sampled ranking metrics can mislead

      Many recommender papers rank the true item against a small random sample of items rather than the full catalogue. Krichene and Rendle showed these sampled versions of recall, NDCG and others are not consistent with the full metrics and can reverse model rankings. Use full ranking where feasible, or corrected estimators.

      Krichene & Rendle, KDD, 2020
    7. 2019+
      Reproducibility concerns in neural recommenders

      A widely cited study found most reviewed neural recommendation methods could be beaten by simple, well-tuned baselines under careful evaluation. The lesson for banks: always include a tuned simple baseline.

      Dacrema et al., RecSys, 2019
    8. 2024+
      Forecasting foundation models are benchmarked on MASE and quantile scores

      Pretrained time series models such as Chronos report results with scaled errors and weighted quantile loss against statistical and deep learning baselines, and benchmark suites such as GIFT-Eval compare models with MASE and CRPS. These models produce quantile forecasts, so understanding pinball loss is the way to read their results. See R9.

      Ansari et al., Chronos, 2024; Aksu et al., GIFT-Eval, 2024 (from memory; check the papers)
    9. 2021+
      Retrieval benchmarks for LLM systems

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Optimising MAPE and getting a low-biased model

      The model "wins" on MAPE by under-forecasting, then branches run out of cash.

      Spot itReport the bias (mean signed error). If forecasts sit systematically under actuals, check what the training loss was.
    • Averaging MAPE over series with small or zero values

      One sparse product at 400% makes the portfolio average meaningless.

      Spot itLook at the largest individual percentage errors. If a handful dominate, switch to WAPE or MASE.
    • Choosing the metric after seeing the results

      RMSE favoured model A, MAE favoured B, so the report uses whichever flatters the favourite.

      Spot itAsk when the metric was fixed and why. It should be tied to a cost, written down before comparison.
    • No naive baseline

      "MAE of 6,000" with nothing to compare against. A same-weekday-last-week rule may score 5,200.

      Spot itAsk for the same metric on a naive forecast. Prefer a skill score or MASE.
    • A quantile forecast that never gets coverage-checked

      The "90% level" is exceeded 25% of the time, so buffers are far too thin.

      Spot itCompute the share of actuals above the forecast, by month. It should sit near 10%.
    • Mismatched metric and decision

      The model is scored on RMSE but used to set a 95% liquidity buffer, or scored on mean error and used to rank customers.

      Spot itWrite down the decision the forecast feeds and ask which functional (mean, quantile, ordering) that decision needs.
    • Wrong calendar baseline

      "Same day last year" compared across Ramadan, Eid or a changed weekend, flattering or damning the model.

      Spot itPlot errors by calendar event. Spikes at moving holidays mean the baseline is misaligned.
    • Precision@k compared across queues of different richness

      Queue A has 30 true cases, queue B has 3. Precision@10 is not comparable.

      Spot itShow the ceiling (best possible precision@k) or use NDCG, which normalises by it.
    • Sampled ranking evaluation

      Ranking the true item against 100 random others, then reporting NDCG as if it were over the full catalogue.

      Spot itAsk how many candidates each query was ranked against. Only full-catalogue or properly corrected numbers should be compared across papers or vendors.
    • Judging alert rankings on selective, immature labels

      Only reviewed alerts have outcomes, so unreviewed positives are scored as misses or ignored.

      Spot itAsk how many alerts in the evaluated slice actually received a label, and whether a random sample was reviewed.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our cash forecast is off by about AED 6,000 a day on average, against 8,000 for the rule we use today, so roughly a quarter better. But the average is not what hurts: running out costs us much more than holding spare. So I'd have the model give us a level we exceed on only one day in ten, and I'd measure it by how often we actually run short and what that costs, not by the average error.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd decide the metric from the decision. For a buffer set against shortfall, a quantile forecast at the cost-implied level, scored with pinball loss, plus a coverage check by month. For aggregated reporting, MAE and WAPE next to a seasonal-naive baseline, with MASE across series. I avoid plain MAPE because it rewards under-forecasting and breaks near zero. For alert queues, precision at review capacity and NDCG with agreed grades, computed on matured labels with bootstrap intervals over days or queries.”
    “Which is better, MAE or RMSE?”

    Neither; they answer different questions. MAE is the typical miss and targets the median; RMSE punishes large misses and targets the mean. I'd pick by cost: if a big miss is much worse than two small ones, RMSE. I'd report both, because a large RMSE/MAE ratio shows a few bad days are driving the result.

    “Why not just use MAPE? Everyone understands a percentage.”

    It's easy to read, but it explodes near zero, treats a miss on a small day like one on a large day, and favours low forecasts: over-forecasts can be unboundedly bad, under-forecasts are capped at 100%. For a communicable percentage I'd use WAPE, total error over total volume, and MASE for comparing across series.

    “Why does the model need to predict a range? We need one number for planning.”

    You still get one number, but it is chosen on purpose. If a shortfall costs nine times a surplus, the cost-minimising number is the 90th percentile, not the average. The model learns it directly with quantile loss, and we can verify it by checking it was exceeded on about 10% of days.

    “Our new search tool has NDCG@10 of 0.62. Is that good?”

    Not on its own. I'd ask what the labelled question set is, whether it includes Arabic questions and the hard ones, what a keyword baseline scores on it, and how wide the interval is across questions. Then I'd look at recall@k for the cases where the answer wasn't retrieved at all, because a downstream model cannot use what was never found.

    “The alert model has precision@300 of 18%. The vendor claims 30%.”

    I'd check they were measured on the same queues, the same k, the same label definition and maturity, and similar case density. Precision@k depends heavily on how many true cases a queue contains. I'd ask for NDCG or the ceiling, and rerun the vendor on our matured labels with a bootstrap interval before comparing.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A forecast misses by 10 on one day and by 50 on another. What are the MAE and the RMSE, and what does the gap tell you?

    MAE = (10 + 50) ÷ 2 = 30. RMSE = √((100 + 2500) ÷ 2) = √1300 ≈ 36.1. RMSE is above MAE because the misses are uneven; the larger one counts for more. If both days missed by 30, the two would be equal at 30.

    2. Actual sales are 10 and you forecast 20. Next day actual is 20 and you forecast 10. What are the two percentage errors, and what does that show about MAPE?

    First day: 10 ÷ 10 = 100%. Second day: 10 ÷ 20 = 50%. The absolute miss is identical but the over-forecast scores twice as badly, so a model minimising MAPE is pushed to forecast low.

    3. You forecast the 90% quantile at 80 and the actual is 100. Then you forecast 120 and the actual is 100. What are the pinball losses at τ = 0.9?

    First: u = 100 − 80 = 20, short, so loss = 0.9 × 20 = 18. Second: u = −20, spare, so loss = 0.1 × 20 = 2. Running short is nine times as expensive, which is what makes the optimal forecast sit high.

    4. An empty ATM costs AED 9 per AED 1,000 of unmet demand; spare cash costs AED 1 per AED 1,000 held overnight. Which quantile should you load to, and how would you check it works?

    τ* = 9 ÷ (9 + 1) = 0.9. Load to the 90th percentile of the demand forecast. Check by counting: over a back-test, actual demand should exceed the load on about 10% of days. Much more than that means the forecast is overconfident.

    5. A ranked list of three alerts has grades [0, 3, 1] in the order the model returned them. Compute NDCG@3 with gain 2g − 1.

    DCG = 0 + 7 ÷ log23 + 1 ÷ log24 = 4.417 + 0.5 = 4.917. Ideal order is [3, 1, 0]: 7 + 1 ÷ 1.585 + 0 = 7.631. NDCG = 4.917 ÷ 7.631 ≈ 0.64.

    6. Across three queries, the first relevant document is at rank 1, 3 and 2. What is the MRR, and when is it the wrong metric?

    (1 + 1/3 + 1/2) ÷ 3 = 1.833 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.61. It is the wrong metric when there are many things to find and what matters is how many of them the user sees, as in alert review, because it ignores everything after the first hit.

    7. The model team report MAPE of 5% across 800 branches and the model beats last year's by 1 point. What do you ask before accepting that?

    How are zeros and tiny branches handled? Is the average weighted by volume? What is the bias, and does a naive same-weekday or same-occasion baseline score similarly? Is the 1-point gain larger than its interval across days or branches? And does the metric reflect a cost the bank actually bears?
