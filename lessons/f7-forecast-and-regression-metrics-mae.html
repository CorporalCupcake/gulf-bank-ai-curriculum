    Scoring Forecasts and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE, Quantile Loss, NDCG

    Every error measure quietly picks what the model aims at. How to match it to what a miss costs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePick the error metric that matches what a miss costs: MAE aims at the median, RMSE at the mean, MAPE rewards under-forecasting, quantile loss lets shortfalls cost more.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three analysts forecast ATM withdrawals and report MAE, RMSE and MAPE. All are honest, none are comparable. MAE is the typical miss, RMSE squares misses so one terrible day outweighs many small ones, and MAPE scales each miss by the actual.

    A metric is an exam: the model studies for whatever it is graded on. Squared error teaches the mean, absolute error the median, percentage error something below the median. Simulation 1 shows this.

    Fix the metric before seeing results, because it is the target you train towards. Ranked lists are a separate problem: only the top gets looked at.

    Hold onto thisFinish the sentence "a miss is expensive when ...". "It is big": RMSE. "It is on the short side": quantile loss. "It is large relative to the customer": a percentage or scaled error. "The right item is not near the top": a ranking metric.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    MAE and RMSE: the median and the mean

    MAE is the average size of a miss, direction ignored. Judge it against a naive baseline such as yesterday.

    RMSE punishes big misses harder, like a fine that quadruples when the excess doubles. A wide gap above MAE means a few bad days drive the pain. Squared error is minimised by the mean, absolute error by the median, and on skewed data (paydays, claim sizes) they differ. See F6.

    Use RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly, MAE otherwise. Report both, plus a naive baseline.

    Percentage errors and scale-free alternatives

    MAPE scores each miss as a percentage of the actual: 10 AED matters on a 20 AED coffee, not on a laptop. It needs no units, but it explodes near zero and is undefined at zero. It is also lopsided: over-forecasting has no ceiling, under-forecasting is capped at 100%, so minimising it pushes forecasts low (the red curve in Simulation 1).

    Better options are WAPE (total error over total actual, "we missed 7% of cash") and MASE (MAE relative to a naive forecast; below 1 beats naive). Also report bias, the average signed miss, since errors cancel.

    Report error per horizon. Ramadan and Eid move about 11 days a year, so "same day last year" is quietly wrong. See F9.

    Quantile loss: when running short costs more than holding spare

    You leave for the airport earlier than the average journey time, because late costs a flight and early costs a coffee. Cash loading and liquidity buffers work the same way. If a shortfall costs nine times a surplus, you want a number exceeded only 1 day in 10.

    That number is a quantile, scored by pinball loss, which charges short and spare misses at different rates. Nine times worse gives the 0.9 quantile; equal costs give the median. Same logic as F7.3.

    • Intervals are two quantiles. An 80% interval is the 10% and 90% forecasts. Check coverage: about 80% of actuals should fall inside, or the model is overconfident.

    For expected-loss work such as IFRS 9 you need the mean, not a 90% quantile. The metric follows the decision.

    Ranking metrics: precision@k, NDCG and MRR

    Investigators review 300 of 4,000 alerts, so the order is the product. Each metric uses a ranked list and a cut-off k.

    • Precision@k: of the k items shown, how many were worth it? 0.3 at k = 10 means 3 of 10.
    • Recall@k: of everything worth finding, how much was in the top k?
    • MRR: how far down is the first good item? 1 means always first. Suits a single answer.
    • NDCG@k: how close is the ordering to the best possible, with valuable items and high ranks counting more? 1 is perfect. The grades are a policy choice.
    • Choose k from the operation. If investigators review 300 alerts, use precision@300.
    • These metrics ignore the score. Whether to review item 301 is a cost threshold (F7.3).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • MAPE misleads

      Optimising it gives a low-biased model that runs branches out of cash, and one sparse series at 400% ruins the average.

      Spot itReport the bias. Switch to WAPE or MASE if a few errors dominate.
    • Metric chosen after the results, or no naive baseline

      The report uses whichever metric flatters the favourite, with no naive comparison.

      Spot itAsk when the metric was fixed and what a naive forecast scores.
    • Metric and decision do not match

      Scored on RMSE but used to set a 95% liquidity buffer.

      Spot itAsk whether the decision needs a mean, a quantile or an ordering, and check quantile coverage.
    • Wrong calendar baseline

      "Same day last year" compared across Ramadan or Eid.

      Spot itPlot errors by calendar event.
    • Ranking scores that flatter

      Precision@k compared across queues with different numbers of true cases.

      Spot itShow the best possible precision@k, or use NDCG.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A forecast misses by 10 on one day and by 50 on another. Is RMSE higher or lower than MAE, and what does the gap tell you?

    Higher. MAE is 30; RMSE is about 36. A wide gap means a few bad days drive the pain.

    2. Actual is 10 and you forecast 20; next day actual is 20 and you forecast 10. What does that show about MAPE?

    The first scores 100%, the second 50%, though both are 10 off. Over-forecasting is punished harder, so models learn to forecast low.

    3. Running short of cash costs nine times what holding spare does. Which quantile do you load to, and how do you check it?

    The 90th percentile, so you run short about 1 day in 10. Back-test that demand exceeds the load on roughly 10% of days.

    4. Why does a high-NDCG model not tell you whether to review alert number 301?

    Ranking metrics grade only the order, not the scores. Where to stop reviewing is a cost question, as in F7.3.

    5. The model team report MAPE of 5% across 800 branches. What do you ask before accepting it?

    How are zeros and tiny branches handled, is the average volume-weighted, what is the bias, and does a naive baseline score similarly?
