    Scoring Numbers and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE and NDCG

    Part 1

    What these scores are

    In one sentenceA metric is a rule for turning a model's misses into one number, so you can compare models and know if one is good enough.

    Our running example is a café forecasting daily coffee sales. The forecast says 110 coffees every day. Here is how the week really went:

    Friday was a festival day, so its miss of 60 dwarfs the others. Each metric squeezes the five misses into one number, in its own way.

    How the three number scores differ

    • MAE (mean absolute error) is the average size of a miss, ignoring direction. Here: (10 + 10 + 0 + 10 + 60) ÷ 5 = 18 coffees. It reads as "a typical day is off by 18".
    • RMSE (root mean squared error) squares each miss first, so big misses count much more. Here: about 28 coffees. Friday's 60 becomes 3,600, which swamps the rest.
    • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) divides each miss by what really happened, so it is a percentage. Here: about 13%. It works across big and small shops, because "10% off" means the same everywhere.

    RMSE is never smaller than MAE. A big gap between them (28 against 18) tells you a few days are doing most of the damage. Ask which days.

    Part 2

    How to score a forecast

    1. Keep test days aside. Score on days the model never learned from.
    2. Work out each miss: forecast minus actual, for every day.
    3. Squeeze the misses into one number with MAE, RMSE or MAPE.
    4. Score a naive baseline the same way, such as "same as yesterday" or "same weekday last week".
    5. Compare. The model only earns its place if it beats the baseline.

    How to read the numbers

    • MAE of 18 coffees is only good or bad against the baseline. If "same as yesterday" scores 15, the model is worse than doing nothing clever.
    • RMSE of 28 beside MAE of 18 means misses are uneven. A ratio above about 1.25 is a prompt to look at the worst days.
    • MAPE of 13% means the forecast is typically 13% off, whatever the shop size. Lower is better for all three.

    Which one should you choose?

    The metric is an exam, and the model studies for whatever it is graded on. Squared error teaches the model to chase the average day, so it bends towards festival days. Absolute error teaches it to aim at the middle day, which ignores them.

    • RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly, and extreme days are real events.
    • MAE when cost grows in step with the miss, or a few freak days shouldn't steer everything.
    • MAPE when you compare series of very different sizes. Beware its flaws, below.
    Part 3

    When MAPE misleads, and what to use instead

    MAPE is popular, but it has three defects:

    • It explodes near zero. Selling 200 against a forecast of 1,000 scores 400%, which swamps the average. At zero sales it is undefined.
    • It favours low forecasts. Over-forecasting has no ceiling (300 against 100 is 200%), but under-forecasting is capped at 100%. Minimise MAPE and the model learns to guess low.
    • Every day counts equally. 20% off on a huge day weighs the same as 20% off on a tiny one.

    Two fixes are widely used:

    • WAPE (weighted MAPE): total miss divided by total actual. "We missed 7% of all coffees." Zeros are fine.
    • MASE (mean absolute scaled error): your MAE divided by the naive baseline's MAE. Below 1 beats the baseline, and 0.8 means 20% fewer errors.
    Part 4

    When running short costs more than spare

    In one sentenceQuantile loss scores a forecast that is deliberately high or low, because one kind of miss is more costly than the other.

    A café that runs out of coffee loses customers. One with spare milk loses a few pennies. So it should not bake for the average day. It should bake for a busy one.

    • The idea: if running short costs nine times as much as having spare, aim for a number that demand beats only about 1 day in 10. That is the 90th quantile of past demand.
    • The rule: target quantile = shortfall cost ÷ (shortfall cost + spare cost). Nine to one gives 0.9. Equal costs give 0.5, the median.
    • The score: pinball loss charges short misses at one rate and spare misses at another. The forecast that scores best is exactly the quantile you asked for.
    • Intervals: an 80% range is the 10% and 90% quantile forecasts together. Check that about 80% of real days fall inside.
    Part 5

    Scoring a ranked list

    In one sentenceA ranking metric asks whether the good items sit near the top of a list, because that is where people look.

    A fraud model scores 4,000 alerts overnight and investigators check the top 300 before lunch. Here the order is the product. Compare two models that put the same three useful items in their top five:

    Shaded boxes are useful items. Precision@5 cannot tell the models apart. NDCG can.

    • Precision@k: of the top k items, how many were useful? 0.6 at k = 5 means 3 of 5. It ignores the order inside the top k.
    • MRR (mean reciprocal rank): 1 divided by the position of the first useful item. A first-place hit scores 1, second place 0.5. Model B scores 0.33. Use it only when one good answer is enough.
    • NDCG@k (normalised discounted cumulative gain): gives each item credit for how useful it is, shrinks that credit the lower it sits, then divides by the best order possible. 1 is a perfect list.

    Choosing k: set it from the real job. If investigators review 300 alerts, use precision@300.

    Part 6

    Which to use

    ScoreAnswersStrong atWeak at
    MAETypical miss, in coffeesSimple, robust to freak daysHides rare large misses
    RMSETypical miss, big ones weighted upPunishes costly large missesOne bad day can dominate
    MAPE / WAPEMiss as a percentageCompares shops of different sizeMAPE breaks near zero and favours low guesses
    MASEMiss against a naive baselineWorks across scales and zerosNeeds a sensible baseline
    Pinball lossCost of a deliberately high or low forecastMatches uneven costs, gives intervalsOnly comparable at the same quantile
    Precision@k, NDCG, MRRAre good items near the top?Lists where only the top is readIgnore the scores themselves; averages hide bad queues

    In practice: report MAE with a naive baseline, add RMSE if big misses hurt, and use a percentage form only when you must compare sizes.

    Show the formulas

    MAE = average of |actual − forecast|. RMSE = square root of the average of (actual − forecast)². MAPE = average of |actual − forecast| ÷ actual.

    NDCG: an item at position p earns its grade divided by log₂(p + 1), so lower positions earn less. Add these up for your list, then divide by the same sum for the best possible order.

    In banking, the metric follows the decision: RMSE for cash-call risk, quantile loss for ATM cash and buffers, precision@k for alert queues sized to the review team.

    Part 7

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting an error with no baseline

      "MAE of 6,000" means nothing alone. Score a naive rule with the same metric and compare.

    • Using MAPE on small or zero values

      It explodes or is undefined, and it pushes forecasts low. Use WAPE or MASE.

    • Picking the metric after seeing results

      The metric steers training. Choose it from the decision, up front.

    • Trusting an average ranking score

      A mean NDCG of 0.7 can hide a few disastrous queues. Cut it by segment.

    1. MAE is 18 and RMSE is 28. What does the gap tell you?

    A few large misses drive the error, since squaring weights them up. Look at the worst days.

    2. Why does minimising MAPE push forecasts low?

    Under-forecasting is capped at 100% error, but over-forecasting has no ceiling, so guessing low is safer for the score.

    3. Running out costs four times as much as having spare. Which quantile do you forecast?

    4 ÷ (4 + 1) = 0.8, the 80th quantile.

    4. Two models have the same precision@10. Why might NDCG still differ?

    Precision ignores order within the top 10. NDCG rewards putting the best items first.

    5. A model has MASE of 0.8. Is that good?

    Yes. It makes 20% less error than the naive baseline.
