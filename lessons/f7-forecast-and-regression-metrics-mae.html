    Scoring Forecasts and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE, Quantile Loss, NDCG

    Every error measure quietly picks what the model aims at. How to match it to what a miss costs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePick the error metric that matches what a miss costs: MAE aims at the median, RMSE at the mean, MAPE rewards under-forecasting, quantile loss lets shortfalls cost more.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three analysts forecast ATM withdrawals and report MAE, RMSE and MAPE. All are honest, none are comparable. MAE is the typical miss, RMSE squares misses so one terrible day outweighs many small ones, and MAPE scales each miss by the actual.

    A metric is an exam: the model studies for whatever it is graded on. Squared error teaches the mean, absolute error the median, percentage error something below the median. Simulation 1 shows this.

    Fix the metric before seeing results, because it is the target you train towards. Ranked lists are a separate problem: only the top gets looked at.

    Hold onto thisFinish the sentence "a miss is expensive when ...". "It is big": RMSE. "It is on the short side": quantile loss. "It is large relative to the customer": a percentage or scaled error. "The right item is not near the top": a ranking metric.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    MAE and RMSE: the median and the mean

    MAE (mean absolute error) is the average size of a miss, direction ignored. MAE of 6,000 means a typical day is off by AED 6,000, and every AED of miss counts the same. Whether 6,000 is good depends on a naive baseline such as yesterday or the same weekday last week. If the naive rule scores 5,500, the model is worse than doing nothing clever.

    RMSE (root mean squared error) is the same idea with big misses punished harder, like a fine that quadruples when the excess doubles. It is always at least MAE, and the gap shows how uneven the misses are. RMSE 9,500 beside MAE 6,000 means a few bad days drive the pain. A ratio above about 1.25 is a prompt to ask which days.

    The single number that minimises squared error is the mean. The one that minimises absolute error is the median. On symmetric data they coincide. On skewed data (paydays, transaction amounts, claim sizes) they do not, so the metric is a modelling decision, not a reporting detail. It is the same story as F6: train a model on squared error and it chases the mean, so it bends towards outliers.

    • RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly (a large unexpected cash call) and extreme days are real events, not data errors.
    • MAE when cost grows in proportion to the miss, or a few extreme days would dominate model selection.
    • Always report a naive baseline scored with the same metric.

    Percentage errors and scale-free alternatives

    A 10 AED error on a 20 AED coffee is bad; on a 2,000 AED laptop it is trivial. MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) scores that way: each miss as a percentage of the actual, averaged. It lets you compare a Dubai flagship with a small Fujairah branch, and "4.8%" needs no units. It has three defects.

    • It explodes near zero. Sales of AED 200 forecast at 1,000 score 400% and swamp the average. At zero it is undefined, which is common in sparse series.
    • It is lopsided. Over-forecasting has no ceiling (300 against a truth of 100 is 200%); under-forecasting is capped at 100%. Minimising MAPE therefore pushes forecasts low, as the red curve in Simulation 1 shows.
    • It treats every observation alike. 20% on a day with AED 10m of flow counts the same as 20% on a day with AED 100k.

    The usual fixes:

    • WAPE (weighted MAPE) is total absolute error over total actual: "we missed 7% of cash". Zeros are fine. It is dominated by the largest series, so small segments can be badly forecast unseen.
    • MASE (mean absolute scaled error) is MAE divided by the MAE of a naive forecast. Below 1 beats naive; 0.8 means 20% fewer errors. It works across scales and with zeros.
    • Skill versus a benchmark is one minus the model's error over the baseline's. Skill 0.17 means "17% lower error than the naive rule". Choose the baseline before seeing results.
    • Bias is the average signed miss. Errors cancel, so zero is not proof of accuracy. Report it beside MAE, never instead.

    Report error per horizon, since day 1 and day 14 are different quantities. Ramadan and Eid move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, and the Gulf weekend differs by country and year, so "same day last year" is quietly wrong. Align the baseline to the occasion. See F9.

    Quantile loss: when running short costs more than holding spare

    You leave for the airport earlier than the average journey time, because late costs a flight and early costs a coffee. Cash loading, liquidity buffers and call-centre staffing work the same way. An ATM that runs dry loses customers and fees; idle cash for a night costs a little funding. If a shortfall costs nine times a surplus, you want a number exceeded only about 1 day in 10.

    That number is a quantile, scored by pinball loss. A miss on the short side is charged at one rate and a miss on the spare side at another, and the forecast that minimises the total is exactly the quantile you asked for. At the 0.5 quantile both sides cost the same and you get the median. Pinball loss is a cost (lower is better) and is only comparable at the same quantile.

    The cost-minimising quantile is shortfall cost ÷ (shortfall cost + surplus cost). Nine times worse gives 0.9; equal costs give the median. Same logic as the cost-based threshold in F7.3: costs become a probability level.

    • Intervals are two quantiles. An 80% interval is the 10% and 90% forecasts together. Check coverage: about 80% of actuals should fall inside. Great pinball loss with 60% coverage means overconfidence.
    • CRPS (continuous ranked probability score) is pinball loss averaged over many quantile levels, so it grades a whole predictive distribution. Lower is better, and it rewards reporting your true beliefs. MAPE has no such property, which is the formal version of "it rewards forecasting low".
    • Models can train on it directly (gradient boosting, linear models, neural networks). Separately fitted quantiles can cross, so sort them. For guaranteed coverage see conformal prediction.

    For expected-loss work such as IFRS 9 you need the mean, not a 90% quantile. The metric follows the decision.

    Ranking metrics: precision@k, NDCG and MRR

    Nobody reads page 2 of search results. Likewise a monitoring model scores 4,000 alerts overnight and investigators review 300 before lunch. Here the order is the product: what matters is whether deserving items sit where people look. Each metric starts from a ranked list and a cut-off k.

    • Precision@k: of the k items shown, how many were worth it? 0.3 at k = 10 means 3 of 10. Raising k usually lowers it. Blind to order within the top k.
    • Recall@k: of everything worth finding, how much was in the top k? It rises with k and ignores how much junk people waded through.
    • MRR (mean reciprocal rank): how far down is the first good item? 1 means always first, 0.5 typically second. Right only when there is one answer to find, because it ignores everything after the first hit.
    • NDCG@k (normalised discounted cumulative gain): how close is the ordering to the best possible? It gives each item credit by its grade, discounts by position, and divides by the best score that queue could have got, so queues are comparable. 1 is perfect. It needs graded labels, and the grades are a policy choice.
    • Choose k from the operation. If investigators review 300 alerts, use precision@300.
    • These metrics ignore the score, and averages hide queries. Two models with the same ordering score identically; whether to review item 301 is a cost threshold (F7.3). A mean NDCG of 0.7 can be many good queues and a few disasters, so cut by segment.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • MAE and RMSE are cheap and interpretable. Both are in the units of the thing forecast, so "off by AED 6,000" needs no translation. They are also smooth enough to train on directly.
    • RMSE tracks tail risk. Squaring means one disastrous day outweighs many small ones, which is what a treasurer fears.
    • Scale-free measures (WAPE, MASE) compare across series. Dividing by actuals or by a naive error removes the units, so a flagship and a small branch can sit in one table.
    • Quantile loss encodes asymmetric cost. It turns "short is nine times worse" into a forecast you can act on, and scores whole ranges.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • MAPE with zeros, small values or a goal of unbiased forecasts. It explodes near zero and rewards under-forecasting.
    • RMSE with dirty data. A single bad record can pick the winning model.
    • Any single-number summary of a skewed or multi-segment book. The average hides which branches, days or horizons fail. Cut by segment.
    • Point-error metrics when the decision needs a range. Safety stock or a liquidity buffer needs a quantile and a coverage check.
    • Ranking metrics for threshold decisions. They grade order only, so they cannot say where to stop reviewing.

    In practice

    • What to try first. Write down the decision and what a miss costs, then fix one primary metric. Report MAE, RMSE, bias and a naive baseline (MASE or skill) together. Add quantile loss only if costs are asymmetric.
    • Data and compute. Cost is negligible. The real need is enough back-test history to include the awkward periods: Ramadan, Eid, month-end and salary dates. Score on rolling-origin splits, not a random shuffle.
    • In banks. Cash and liquidity forecasting uses quantile loss and coverage checks. Branch and call-centre demand uses WAPE or MASE. AML and fraud alert queues use precision@capacity and NDCG. Expected-loss work uses mean-based scores.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • MAPE misleads

      Optimising it gives a low-biased model that runs branches out of cash, and one sparse series at 400% ruins the average.

      Spot itReport the bias. Switch to WAPE or MASE if a few errors dominate.
    • Metric chosen after the results, or no naive baseline

      The report uses whichever metric flatters the favourite, with no naive comparison.

      Spot itAsk when the metric was fixed and what a naive forecast scores.
    • Metric and decision do not match

      Scored on RMSE but used to set a 95% liquidity buffer.

      Spot itAsk whether the decision needs a mean, a quantile or an ordering, and check quantile coverage.
    • Wrong calendar baseline

      "Same day last year" compared across Ramadan or Eid.

      Spot itPlot errors by calendar event.
    • One average hides the failures

      Overall MAE looks fine while the small branches, the Ramadan weeks or the long horizons are badly wrong.

      Spot itBreak errors out by segment, horizon and calendar event, and look at the worst few.
    • Ranking scores that flatter

      Precision@k compared across queues with different numbers of true cases.

      Spot itShow the best possible precision@k, or use NDCG.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A forecast misses by 10 on one day and by 50 on another. Is RMSE higher or lower than MAE, and what does the gap tell you?

    Higher. MAE is 30; RMSE is about 36. A wide gap means a few bad days drive the pain.

    2. Actual is 10 and you forecast 20; next day actual is 20 and you forecast 10. What does that show about MAPE?

    The first scores 100%, the second 50%, though both are 10 off. Over-forecasting is punished harder, so models learn to forecast low.

    3. Running short of cash costs nine times what holding spare does. Which quantile do you load to, and how do you check it?

    The 90th percentile, so you run short about 1 day in 10. Back-test that demand exceeds the load on roughly 10% of days.

    4. Why does a high-NDCG model not tell you whether to review alert number 301?

    Ranking metrics grade only the order, not the scores. Where to stop reviewing is a cost question, as in F7.3.

    5. The model team report MAPE of 5% across 800 branches. What do you ask before accepting it?

    How are zeros and tiny branches handled, is the average volume-weighted, what is the bias, and does a naive baseline score similarly?

    6. You forecast daily branch cash demand. Some branches are near zero on quiet days. Which would you choose to compare models, MAPE, WAPE or MASE, and why?

    MASE or WAPE. Both survive zeros. MASE also compares against a naive forecast and treats each series on its own scale; WAPE is simpler to explain but is dominated by the biggest branches. MAPE would be swamped by the quiet days.

    7. Your data has occasional keying errors that produce absurd values. Would you pick MAE or RMSE for model selection, and why?

    MAE, ideally with the errors cleaned too. Squaring lets one absurd value dominate RMSE and decide the winner. If the extremes were real events, RMSE would be the right choice.
