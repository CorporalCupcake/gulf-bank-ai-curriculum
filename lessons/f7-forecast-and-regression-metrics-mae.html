    Scoring Forecasts and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE, Quantile Loss, NDCG

    Every error measure quietly chooses what the model should aim at: the median, the mean, or a biased-low guess. How to pick the measure that matches what a miss costs, how to score a forecast that gives a range, and how to grade an ordered list such as an alert queue.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn error metric is a statement about which mistakes hurt: MAE aims at the median, RMSE at the mean and punishes big misses, MAPE rewards under-forecasting, quantile loss lets you say that running short costs more than holding spare, and ranking metrics (precision@k, NDCG, MRR) grade only the order of a list, so choose the one whose answer you would be happy to be optimised for.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three analysts forecast tomorrow's cash withdrawals from a cluster of ATMs. One reports "average error AED 6,000", another "RMSE AED 9,500", a third "MAPE 4.8%". All are honest, none are comparable, and the best-looking one may be worst for the bank. Each number asks a different question about the misses:

    • MAE: how far off are you on a typical day?
    • RMSE: squares each miss first, so one terrible day outweighs many small ones.
    • MAPE: divides each miss by the actual, so a 10,000 miss on a quiet day counts far more than on a payday.

    None of them knows that an empty machine before Eid is far worse than spare cash overnight. A metric is like an exam: the model studies for whatever it is graded on. Grade it on squared error and it learns the mean; on absolute error, the median; on percentage error, something below the median. Simulation 1 shows this.

    • The metric you pick is the target you train towards. Choosing it after seeing results, to flatter a model, is quiet cheating.
    • "Which error is best?" depends on what a miss costs. Is one big miss worse than two small ones? Is under-forecasting worse than over-forecasting?
    • Ranking is a different problem. For an ordered list (alerts, search results), only the top gets looked at, so what matters is whether the right items sit near the top. See ranking metrics.
    Hold onto thisFinish the sentence "a miss is expensive when ...". "It is big": RMSE. "It is on the short side": quantile loss. "It is large relative to the customer": a percentage or scaled error, carefully. "The right item is not near the top": a ranking metric.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    MAE and RMSE: the median and the mean

    MAE is the average size of a miss, direction ignored. MAE 6,000 means a typical day is off by AED 6,000. Whether that is good depends on the naive baseline (yesterday, or the same weekday last week): if the naive rule scores 5,500, a model at 6,000 is worse than doing nothing clever. Every AED of miss counts equally, so a drop from 6,000 to 5,000 means each day is 1,000 closer on average.

    RMSE is the same idea with big misses punished harder, like a speeding fine that quadruples when the excess doubles. RMSE 9,500 beside MAE 6,000 means a few bad days drive the pain. It is not "the average error"; it is always at least MAE, and the gap shows how uneven the misses are.

    The single number that minimises squared error is the mean; the one that minimises absolute error is the median. On symmetric data they coincide. On skewed data (paydays, transaction amounts, claim sizes) they do not, so the choice is a modelling decision. It is the same story as F6.

    • RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly (a large unexpected cash call) and extreme days are real events, not data errors.
    • MAE when cost grows in proportion to the miss, or a few extreme days would dominate model selection. It is also the easiest for a business reader.
    • Report both, and always the same metric for a naive baseline. A ratio of RMSE to MAE above about 1.25 signals heavy-tailed errors: ask which days.

    Percentage errors and scale-free alternatives

    A 10 AED error on a 20 AED coffee is bad; on a 2,000 AED laptop it is trivial. MAPE scores that way: each miss as a percentage of the actual, averaged. It lets you compare a Dubai flagship with a small Fujairah branch, and "4.8%" needs no units. Three defects:

    • It explodes near zero. Sales of AED 200 forecast at 1,000 score 400% and swamp the average. At zero it is undefined, common in sparse series.
    • It is lopsided. Over-forecasting has no ceiling (300 against a truth of 100 is 200%); under-forecasting is capped at 100%. Minimising MAPE therefore pushes forecasts low, as the red curve in Simulation 1 shows.
    • It treats every observation alike. 20% on a day with AED 10m of flow counts the same as 20% on a day with AED 100k.

    The alternatives in practice:

    MeasureWhat it isHow to read it, and what a change means
    WAPE (weighted MAPE)Total absolute error ÷ total actual."We missed 7% of total cash." Zeros are fine. Dominated by the largest series, so small segments can be badly forecast unseen.
    MASEMAE ÷ the MAE of a naive forecast.1 means no better than naive; 0.8 means 20% fewer errors; above 1 is worse. Works across scales and with zeros.
    Skill vs a benchmark1 − (model MAE ÷ baseline MAE).Skill 0.17 means "17% lower error than the naive rule". The simplest honest statement, but only as meaningful as the baseline. Choose it before seeing results.
    Bias (mean error)Average signed miss.+2,000 means forecasts run AED 2,000 under actuals on average. Zero does not mean accurate: errors cancel. Report beside MAE, never instead of it.

    Averaging across series chooses weights. A simple mean treats a tiny branch as equal to a flagship; a volume-weighted number does not. State which.

    Report error per horizon, since day 1 and day 14 are different quantities. Islamic-calendar dates (Ramadan, Eid) move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, and the Gulf weekend differs by country and year, so "same day last year" is quietly wrong. Align the baseline to the occasion. See F9.

    Quantile loss: when running short costs more than holding spare

    Leaving for the airport, you do not leave at the average journey time: arriving late costs a flight, arriving early costs a coffee. Bank decisions work the same way: how much cash to load, how big a liquidity buffer, Monday call-centre capacity. An ATM that runs dry loses customers and fees; idle cash for a night costs a little funding. If a shortfall costs nine times a surplus, you want a number exceeded only about 1 day in 10.

    That number is a quantile, scored by pinball loss: a miss on the short side is charged at one rate, a miss on the spare side at another, and the forecast that minimises the total is exactly the quantile you asked for. At the 0.5 quantile both sides cost the same and you get the median. Pinball loss is a cost, lower is better, and only comparable at the same quantile.

    The cost-minimising quantile is simply shortfall cost ÷ (shortfall cost + surplus cost). Nine times worse gives 0.9; equal costs give the median. It is the same logic as the cost-based threshold in F7.3: costs become a probability level.

    • Intervals are two quantiles. An 80% interval is the 10% and 90% forecasts together. Check coverage: about 80% of actuals should fall inside. Great pinball loss with 60% coverage means overconfidence.
    • CRPS is pinball loss averaged over many quantile levels, grading a whole predictive distribution. Lower is better, and it rewards reporting your true beliefs. MAPE has no such property, which is the formal version of "it rewards forecasting low".
    • Models train on it directly (boosting, linear models, neural networks). Separately fitted quantiles can cross, so sort them; for guaranteed coverage see conformal prediction.

    For expected-loss work such as IFRS 9, the accounting value is the probability-weighted mean, so RMSE-like or likelihood scores and calibration (F7.4) fit, not a 90% quantile. The metric follows the decision.

    Ranking metrics: precision@k, NDCG and MRR

    Nobody reads page 2 of search results. Likewise a monitoring model scores 4,000 alerts overnight and 20 investigators review 300 before lunch. Here the order is the product: what matters is whether deserving items sit where people look. Each metric starts from a ranked list and a cut-off k.

    MetricWhat it means, how to read itBlind to
    Precision@kOf the k items shown, how many were worth it? 0.3 at k = 10 means 3 of 10 reviewed were productive. Raising k usually lowers it.Order within the top k, and anything beyond.
    Recall@kOf everything worth finding, how much was in the top k? 0.6 means 60% found. Rises with k.How much junk people waded through.
    MRR (mean reciprocal rank)How far down is the first good item? 1 means always first, 0.5 means typically second. Slipping from first to second hurts more than slipping from tenth to eleventh.Everything after the first hit. Right only when there is one answer to find.
    NDCG@kHow close is the ordering to the best possible, with valuable items counted more? 1 is perfect; 0.64 means 64% of the best achievable credit.Needs graded labels; the grades are a policy choice.

    NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) is what retrieval and recommendation benchmarks report. It does three things: gives each item credit by its grade (a confirmed case counts more than a minor one), discounts by position (rank 1 counts more than rank 8), and divides by the best score that queue could have got, so different queues are comparable. Moving a confirmed case from rank 2 to rank 1 gains more than moving a minor one.

    • Choose k from the operation. If investigators review 300 alerts, use precision@300. NDCG@10 is a web-search convention that measures something nobody here is doing.
    • Precision@k has a ceiling. With only 4 true cases and k = 10, precision@10 cannot exceed 0.4 even for a perfect model. NDCG normalises for this.
    • These metrics ignore the score, and averages hide queries. Two models with the same ordering score identically; whether to review item 301 is a cost threshold (F7.3). A mean NDCG of 0.7 can be many good queues and a few disasters, so cut by segment.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Optimising MAPE and getting a low-biased model

      The model "wins" by under-forecasting, then branches run out of cash.

      Spot itReport the bias. If forecasts sit systematically under actuals, check the training loss.
    • Averaging MAPE over series with small or zero values

      One sparse product at 400% makes the average meaningless.

      Spot itLook at the largest individual percentage errors. If a handful dominate, switch to WAPE or MASE.
    • Choosing the metric after seeing the results, or with no naive baseline

      RMSE favoured A, MAE favoured B, so the report uses whichever flatters the favourite. Or "MAE of 6,000" stands alone when same-weekday-last-week scores 5,200.

      Spot itAsk when the metric was fixed (tied to a cost, written down first) and for the same metric on a naive forecast.
    • Metric and decision do not match

      Scored on RMSE but used to set a 95% liquidity buffer, or a quantile forecast never coverage-checked so the "90% level" is exceeded 25% of the time.

      Spot itWrite down the decision and ask whether it needs a mean, a quantile or an ordering. For quantiles, check the share of actuals above the forecast: it should sit near 10%.
    • Wrong calendar baseline

      "Same day last year" compared across Ramadan, Eid or a changed weekend.

      Spot itPlot errors by calendar event. Spikes at moving holidays mean the baseline is misaligned.
    • Ranking scores that flatter

      Precision@k compared across queues with different numbers of true cases, ranking evaluated against a small sample of candidates, or only reviewed alerts having labels.

      Spot itShow the best possible precision@k or use NDCG, ask how many candidates each query was ranked against, and ask how many alerts actually received a label.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A forecast misses by 10 on one day and by 50 on another. Would RMSE be higher or lower than MAE, and what does the gap tell you?

    Higher. MAE is the plain average miss (30); RMSE punishes the big miss harder, so it comes out around 36. The wider the gap, the more a few bad days drive the pain. Two equal misses of 30 would give 30 for both.

    2. Actual sales are 10 and you forecast 20. Next day actual is 20 and you forecast 10. What does that show about MAPE?

    The first miss scores 100%, the second 50%, though both are 10 off. Over-forecasting is punished harder, so a model trained on MAPE learns to forecast low.

    3. Running short of cash costs nine times what holding spare does. Which quantile of demand should you load to, and how do you check it?

    The 90th percentile: you want to run short only about 1 day in 10. In a back-test, demand should exceed the load on roughly 10% of days; much more means the forecast is overconfident.

    4. Why does a high-NDCG model still not tell you whether to review alert number 301?

    Ranking metrics grade only the order, not the scores. Two models with the same order score the same, even if one gives every alert 0.99. Where to stop reviewing is a cost question, as in F7.3.

    5. Across three queries, the first relevant document is at rank 1, 3 and 2. When is MRR the wrong metric?

    MRR here is about 0.61. It is wrong when there are many things to find, as in alert review, because it ignores everything after the first hit.

    6. The model team report MAPE of 5% across 800 branches and the model beats last year's by 1 point. What do you ask before accepting that?

    How are zeros and tiny branches handled? Is the average volume-weighted? What is the bias, and does a naive same-weekday or same-occasion baseline score similarly? Does the metric reflect a cost the bank bears?
