    Scoring Forecasts and Rankings: MAE, RMSE, MAPE, Quantile Loss, NDCG

    Every error measure quietly chooses what the model should aim at: the median, the mean, or a biased-low guess. How to pick the measure that matches what a miss costs, how to score a forecast that gives a range, and how to grade an ordered list such as an alert queue.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn error metric is a statement about which mistakes hurt: MAE aims at the median, RMSE at the mean and punishes big misses, MAPE rewards under-forecasting, quantile loss lets you say that running short costs more than holding spare, and ranking metrics (precision@k, NDCG, MRR) grade only the order of a list, so choose the one whose answer you would be happy to be optimised for.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three analysts forecast tomorrow's cash withdrawals from a cluster of ATMs. One reports "average error AED 6,000", another "RMSE AED 9,500", a third "MAPE 4.8%". All are honest, none are comparable, and the best-looking one may be worst for the bank. Each number asks a different question about the misses:

    • MAE: how far off are you on a typical day?
    • RMSE: squares each miss first, so one terrible day outweighs many small ones.
    • MAPE: divides each miss by the actual, so a 10,000 miss on a quiet day counts far more than on a payday.

    None of them knows that an empty machine before Eid is far worse than spare cash overnight.

    A metric is like an exam: the model studies for whatever it is graded on. Minimise squared error and it learns the mean. Minimise absolute error and it learns the median. Minimise percentage error and it learns something below the median, because of how the arithmetic works. Simulation 1 shows this.

    • The metric you pick is the target you train towards. Choosing it after seeing results, to flatter a model, is quiet cheating.
    • "Which error is best?" depends on what a miss costs. Is one big miss worse than two small ones? Is under-forecasting worse than over-forecasting? Is 5% on a small account as bad as 5% on a large one?
    • Ranking is a different problem. For an ordered list (alerts, search results, offers), only the top gets looked at, so the score matters less than whether the right items sit near the top. See ranking metrics.
    Hold onto thisFinish the sentence "a miss is expensive when ...". "It is big": RMSE. "It is on the short side": quantile loss. "It is large relative to the customer": a percentage or scaled error, carefully. "The right item is not near the top": a ranking metric.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    MAE and RMSE: the median and the mean

    Let yt be the actual and ŷt the forecast over n periods.

    MAE=1n∑t|yt−y^t|RMSE=1n∑t(yt−y^t)2

    Both are in the units of the thing forecast (AED, customers, days). RMSE is always at least MAE; the gap shows how uneven the misses are. Misses of 10 and 50 give MAE 30 and RMSE 36. Equal misses give equal values.

    MAE is the average size of a miss, direction ignored. MAE 6,000 means a typical day is off by AED 6,000. Whether that is good depends on the naive baseline (yesterday, or the same weekday last week) and on what a miss costs: if the naive rule scores 5,500, a model at 6,000 is worse than doing nothing clever. A drop from 6,000 to 5,000 means every day's miss is 1,000 smaller on average, and each AED of miss counts equally.

    RMSE is the same idea with big misses punished harder, like a speeding fine that quadruples when the excess doubles. RMSE 9,500 beside MAE 6,000 (ratio about 1.6) means a few bad days drive the pain. Fixing the worst day moves RMSE far more than fixing a small one. Misreading to avoid: RMSE is not "the average error"; it is always at least MAE.

    The single number minimising squared error is the mean; the one minimising absolute error is the median. On symmetric data they coincide. On skewed data (paydays, transaction amounts, claim sizes, time to repay) they do not, so the choice is a modelling decision. Same story as F6: squared error is Gaussian maximum likelihood, absolute error is Laplace.

    • RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly (a large unexpected cash call) and extreme days are real events, not data errors.
    • MAE when cost grows in proportion to the miss, or a few extreme days would dominate model selection. It is also the easiest for a business reader.
    • Report both, and the ratio. RMSE/MAE above about 1.25 signals heavy-tailed errors. Ask which days.
    • Always show the same metric for a naive baseline. Neither number says whether the model beats a sensible alternative.

    Percentage errors and scale-free alternatives

    A 10 AED error on a 20 AED coffee is bad; on a 2,000 AED laptop it is trivial. MAPE scores that way: the average of |y − ŷ| ÷ y. It lets you compare a Dubai flagship with a small Fujairah branch, and "4.8%" needs no units. Read it as: a typical day is off by 4.8% of the actual. Going from 5% to 4% means misses shrink by a fifth in relative terms, but that gain can come from simply forecasting lower. Three defects:

    • It explodes near zero. Sales of AED 200 forecast at 1,000 score 400% and swamp the average. At zero it is undefined, common in sparse series (new products, small segments).
    • It is lopsided. Over-forecasting has no ceiling (300 against a truth of 100 is 200%); under-forecasting is capped at 100%. Minimising MAPE therefore pushes forecasts low, as the red curve in Simulation 1 shows. The minimiser is the median weighted by 1/y, pulled towards small values.
    • It treats every observation alike. 20% on a day with AED 10m of flow counts the same as 20% on a day with AED 100k.

    Averaging across series chooses weights. A simple mean of MASE treats a tiny branch as equal to a flagship; a volume-weighted number does not. State which. The M5 competition (42,840 Walmart sales series) weighted by dollar sales for this reason.

    Report error per horizon: day 1 and day 14 are different quantities. Islamic-calendar dates (Ramadan, Eid, school holidays) move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, and the Gulf weekend differs by country and year, so "same day last year" is quietly wrong. Align the baseline to the occasion. See F9 and the temporal splits in F4.

    Quantile loss: when running short costs more than holding spare

    Leaving for the airport, you do not leave at the average journey time: arriving late costs a flight, arriving early costs a coffee. Bank decisions work the same way (how much cash to load, how big a liquidity buffer, Monday call-centre capacity). An ATM that runs dry loses customers and fees; idle cash for a night costs a little funding. If a shortfall costs nine times a surplus, you want a number exceeded only about 1 day in 10.

    That number is a quantile, scored by pinball loss. For target quantile τ between 0 and 1, with miss u = y − ŷ:

    ρτ(u)=τ·uif u≥0 (actual above forecast: you were short)(1−τ)·|u|if u<0 (actual below forecast: you held spare)

    At τ = 0.5 this is half the absolute error, so the median is the 0.5 quantile. At τ = 0.9, being 20 short costs 0.9 × 20 = 18; being 20 over costs 0.1 × 20 = 2. The forecast minimising average pinball loss is exactly the τ-quantile, the level you exceed only 10% of the time.

    Reading it: pinball loss is a cost, lower is better, and only comparable at the same τ. A fall means the forecast sits closer to the right level for that τ. If a shortfall costs cu per unit and surplus co, the cost-minimising plan is the quantile

    τ* = cu ÷ (cu + co)

    This is the "newsvendor" result, and the same logic as the cost-based threshold in F7.3: costs become a probability level. Nine times worse gives τ* = 0.9; equal costs give the median.

    • Intervals are two quantiles. An 80% interval is the 10% and 90% forecasts together, each scored at its own τ. Check coverage: about 80% of actuals should fall inside. Great pinball loss with 60% coverage means overconfidence.
    • CRPS (continuous ranked probability score) is pinball loss averaged over many τ, grading a whole predictive distribution. Lower is better. It belongs to the "proper" scoring rules, where your best strategy is to report your true beliefs (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Pinball is proper for its quantile. MAPE is not proper for anything useful, the formal version of "it rewards forecasting low".
    • Models train on it directly. Gradient boosting, linear models and neural networks accept it as an objective. Separately fitted quantiles can cross (90% line below the 50%); sort them. For guaranteed coverage see conformal prediction.
    • Where it appears: liquidity buffers (B5), cash logistics, call-centre staffing, tail-driven loss forecasts.

    For expected-loss work such as IFRS 9, the accounting value is the probability-weighted mean, so RMSE-like or likelihood scores and calibration (F7.4) fit, not a 90% quantile. The metric follows the decision.

    Ranking metrics: precision@k, NDCG and MRR

    Nobody reads page 2 of search results. Likewise a monitoring model scores 4,000 alerts overnight and 20 investigators review 300 before lunch. Here the order is the product; what matters is whether deserving items sit where people look. (Whether the score is a true probability is a separate question: calibration, F7.4.) Each metric starts from a ranked list, items marked relevant or graded, and a cut-off k.

    NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain; Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002) is what retrieval and recommendation benchmarks report. Three moves: give each item a gain from its grade (a critical alert counts more than a minor one); discount by position (rank 1 counts more than rank 8); normalise by the best achievable score for that list, so different queues are comparable.

    DCG@k=∑i=1k2gi−1log2(i+1)NDCG@k=DCG@kideal DCG@k

    gi is the grade at rank i (0 = benign, 1 = minor, 2 = suspicious, 3 = confirmed). Gain 2g − 1 makes grade 3 worth 7 and grade 1 worth 1. The discount 1 ÷ log2(i + 1) is 1 at rank 1, 0.63 at rank 3, 0.5 at rank 7. Grades [0, 3, 1] score 7/1.585 + 1/2 = 4.92; the ideal order [3, 1, 0] scores 7 + 1/1.585 = 7.63; NDCG = 0.64.

    • Choose k from the operation. If investigators review 300 alerts, use precision@300. NDCG@10 is a web-search convention that measures something nobody here is doing.
    • Precision@k has a ceiling. With only 4 true cases and k = 10, precision@10 cannot exceed 0.4 even for a perfect model. NDCG normalises for this.
    • MRR suits "find the one right answer" (one policy paragraph, one sanctions-list match). For many cases to find, use precision, recall and NDCG.
    • Grades and gains are policy. Whether a confirmed case is worth 3 or 7 times a suspicious one changes model rankings. Agree it before looking at results.
    • These metrics ignore the score. Two models with the same ordering score identically, even if one outputs 0.99 for everything. Whether to review item 301 depends on the cost threshold from F7.3.
    • Averages hide queries. A mean NDCG of 0.7 can be many good queries and a few disasters. Look at the spread and cut by segment (Arabic and English, retail and corporate).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Optimising MAPE and getting a low-biased model

      The model "wins" by under-forecasting, then branches run out of cash.

      Spot itReport the bias. If forecasts sit systematically under actuals, check the training loss.
    • Averaging MAPE over series with small or zero values

      One sparse product at 400% makes the average meaningless.

      Spot itLook at the largest individual percentage errors. If a handful dominate, switch to WAPE or MASE.
    • Choosing the metric after seeing the results

      RMSE favoured A, MAE favoured B, so the report uses whichever flatters the favourite.

      Spot itAsk when the metric was fixed. It should be tied to a cost and written down before comparison.
    • No naive baseline

      "MAE of 6,000" with nothing to compare against. Same-weekday-last-week may score 5,200.

      Spot itAsk for the same metric on a naive forecast. Prefer a skill score or MASE.
    • A quantile forecast never coverage-checked

      The "90% level" is exceeded 25% of the time, so buffers are far too thin.

      Spot itCompute the share of actuals above the forecast, by month. It should sit near 10%.
    • Mismatched metric and decision

      Scored on RMSE but used to set a 95% liquidity buffer, or scored on mean error but used to rank customers.

      Spot itWrite down the decision and ask which functional (mean, quantile, ordering) it needs.
    • Wrong calendar baseline

      "Same day last year" compared across Ramadan, Eid or a changed weekend.

      Spot itPlot errors by calendar event. Spikes at moving holidays mean the baseline is misaligned.
    • Precision@k compared across queues of different richness

      Queue A has 30 true cases, queue B has 3, so precision@10 is not comparable.

      Spot itShow the ceiling (best possible precision@k) or use NDCG.
    • Sampled ranking evaluation

      Ranking the true item against 100 random others, then reporting NDCG as if over the full catalogue.

      Spot itAsk how many candidates each query was ranked against. Compare only full-catalogue or corrected numbers across papers or vendors.
    • Judging alert rankings on selective, immature labels

      Only reviewed alerts have outcomes, so unreviewed positives count as misses or are ignored.

      Spot itAsk how many alerts in the evaluated slice received a label, and whether a random sample was reviewed.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the Part 2 formulas.

    1. A forecast misses by 10 on one day and by 50 on another. What are the MAE and RMSE, and what does the gap tell you?

    MAE = (10 + 50) ÷ 2 = 30. RMSE = √((100 + 2500) ÷ 2) = √1300 ≈ 36.1. RMSE is higher because the misses are uneven and the larger counts more. Two misses of 30 would give 30 for both.

    2. Actual sales are 10 and you forecast 20. Next day actual is 20 and you forecast 10. What are the percentage errors, and what does that show about MAPE?

    10 ÷ 10 = 100%, then 10 ÷ 20 = 50%. The miss is identical but the over-forecast scores twice as badly, so minimising MAPE pushes forecasts low.

    3. You forecast the 90% quantile at 80 and the actual is 100. Then you forecast 120 and the actual is 100. What are the pinball losses at τ = 0.9?

    First: u = 20, short, loss = 0.9 × 20 = 18. Second: u = −20, spare, loss = 0.1 × 20 = 2. Running short is nine times as expensive, so the optimal forecast sits high.

    4. An empty ATM costs AED 9 per AED 1,000 of unmet demand; spare cash costs AED 1 per AED 1,000 overnight. Which quantile should you load to, and how do you check it?

    τ* = 9 ÷ (9 + 1) = 0.9. Load to the 90th percentile. In a back-test, demand should exceed the load on about 10% of days; much more means overconfidence.

    5. Three alerts have grades [0, 3, 1] in the model's order. Compute NDCG@3 with gain 2g − 1.

    DCG = 0 + 7 ÷ log23 + 1 ÷ log24 = 4.417 + 0.5 = 4.917. Ideal [3, 1, 0]: 7 + 1 ÷ 1.585 + 0 = 7.631. NDCG = 4.917 ÷ 7.631 ≈ 0.64.

    6. Across three queries, the first relevant document is at rank 1, 3 and 2. What is the MRR, and when is it the wrong metric?

    (1 + 1/3 + 1/2) ÷ 3 ≈ 0.61. It is wrong when there are many things to find, as in alert review, because it ignores everything after the first hit.

    7. The model team report MAPE of 5% across 800 branches and the model beats last year's by 1 point. What do you ask before accepting that?

    How are zeros and tiny branches handled? Is the average volume-weighted? What is the bias, and does a naive same-weekday or same-occasion baseline score similarly? Is the 1-point gain larger than its interval? Does the metric reflect a cost the bank bears?
