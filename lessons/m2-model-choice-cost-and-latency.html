    Choosing a Model: Quality, Cost and Speed

    Part 1

    What choosing a model means

    In one sentenceChoosing a model means picking the cheapest, fastest model that still does your task well enough, and proving it on your own examples.

    Running example for this lesson: a tool that summarises customer emails, 1,000 emails a day, each about 500 tokens long, with a summary of about 100 tokens. (A token is a word-sized chunk of text; you pay by the token.)

    How it differs from picking a model by reputation

    • Reputation: "it is the top model on the leaderboard." That says how it does on general tests, not on your emails.
    • Measurement: you run your own examples through two or three tiers and compare accuracy, cost and delay. That is the only evidence that counts.

    Think of it as hiring. You pay for the least seniority that does the job reliably, and you test candidates on real work first.

    Two other kinds of model exist. Reasoning models think at length before answering (next lesson). Open-weight models are ones you can download and run yourself, needed when data cannot leave the bank.

    Part 2

    How to choose, step by step

    1. Write the requirement. The task, how costly a mistake is, the delay you can accept, the volume, where data may go, and the languages.
    2. Build a test set from real work. 100 to 300 emails with agreed correct summaries, including Arabic ones and awkward ones. It outlives every model release.
    3. Run the same prompt on two or three tiers, cheapest first. Measure accuracy, cost per task and delay. Move up only if the cheap one fails.
    4. Pick the cheapest model that clears the bar with margin. Pin the exact version, and rerun the test set whenever it changes. A "latest" alias can move underneath you.

    Small models do more than people expect: classification, routing, extracting fixed fields and short summaries. Bigger ones earn their price on multi-step reasoning and long documents with subtle cross-references.

    Leaderboard gaps of a point or two are noise. A model that tops a coding table can still misread a Gulf-dialect complaint.

    Part 3

    Where the money and the seconds go

    Cost: tokens times price

    Like a taxi meter with two rates, you pay for input (what you send) and output (what the model writes). Output costs several times more per token, because the model writes it one token at a time. Cost is input tokens times input price, plus output tokens times output price, times your volume.

    Here is the email tool with illustrative prices (not any vendor's real list) per million tokens: small $0.25 in and $1.25 out, mid $3 and $15, frontier $10 and $50. Each day that is 500,000 tokens in and 100,000 out.

    Output is a fifth of the tokens but half of each bill. So shortening the answer saves more than shortening the prompt. Two further savings need no change of model:

    • Prompt caching: the provider reuses its work on the start of a prompt that is identical across requests, such as your instructions. Cached input costs a fraction. Put fixed material first and the customer's email last: a date or name at the top breaks the cache.
    • Batch processing: work nobody is waiting for, such as overnight back-filling, runs within hours at roughly half price.

    Cost per token is not cost per task. A cheap model that needs two retries, or human review of its mistakes, can cost more. Measure cost per correct result.

    Delay: first token, then speed

    The model first reads your prompt (nothing appears yet), then writes one token at a time at a steady speed. A 100-token summary at 50 tokens a second takes 2 seconds to write, however quick the first token was. So long outputs hurt delay far more than long inputs.

    • Stream the output so words appear at once. It feels faster, though the total time is the same. It does not suit output a program must receive whole, such as JSON.
    • Ask for less: a label or three lines beats a paragraph.
    • Watch the slow tail: an average of 2 seconds hides the one call in fifty that takes 30. Set timeouts and a fallback.
    Part 4

    Routing: not every request needs the big model

    Like a GP who refers only difficult cases to a specialist, a cascade sends every email to the small model first and escalates only the doubtful ones. A router is the same idea decided up front.

    Everything rests on the "this one is hard" signal. It can be a classifier trained on past requests, a failed output check (invalid format, a total that does not add up), or a simple rule (document over a set length). The small model's own confidence is a weak signal: it is often over-confident.

    A cascade adds moving parts: two models to test and a router to monitor. Use it when your volume pays for that.

    Part 5

    Documents, images and audio

    Models can take a page image or audio directly. It is convenient, but you lose the audit trail. The question is not "can it read this?" but "can we check what it read?"

    • Parse to text first. A digital PDF has a text layer you can extract free and exactly. Scans need OCR (optical character recognition) first. You keep the text, so every value traces to a string.
    • Send the page image to a vision model. Simple, and good with layout, stamps and handwriting. But the reading is invisible: "1,250,000" can become "1,250,00" with the same confidence as everything else.
    • Audio is usually turned into a transcript by a speech-to-text model, then the LLM reads that. The transcript is your evidence. Accuracy falls with noise, overlapping speakers and Arabic-English mixing, so test on a sample of your own calls.

    Where a number matters, read it twice by different routes and compare, like two clerks keying the same cheque. Send disagreements to a person. Treat text extracted from uploaded files as data, never as instructions: a scanned letter can hide a line telling the model to "approve this payment".

    Part 6

    Which option to use

    OptionStrong atWeak at
    Small modelCheap, fast; labels, routing, fixed fieldsMulti-step reasoning, subtle long documents
    Mid-size modelGood all-rounder for summaries and draftingHardest reasoning
    Frontier modelHard, multi-step workPrice and delay
    CascadeNear big-model quality at a fraction of costNeeds a good router and extra monitoring

    In banking, the deciding factors are usually where the data may go, how costly a wrong answer is, and whether you can show a regulator which model version produced a result.

    Part 7

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Choosing by leaderboard or brand

      Nobody tested it on your emails. Ask for your test set, a score per candidate and the cost per correct answer.

    • Budgeting from the prototype

      Ten test calls say nothing about production volume, real input lengths and retries. Forecast from measured tokens per request and requests per day.

    • A cache that never hits

      A timestamp or customer name at the start makes every prompt unique. Check cached tokens in the usage report.

    • Trusting averages and "latest" aliases

      Look at the slowest 1 to 5 per cent of calls, and pin the model version so quality does not shift without a code change.

    1. Output is a fifth of the tokens in a request but about half the cost. Why?

    Output tokens cost several times more than input tokens, because each is written one at a time. Shortening the answer is usually the biggest saving.

    2. Why try the cheap model first rather than the best?

    You want the cheapest model that clears the bar. Starting at the top only shows that the top works, and teaches you nothing about which cases fail.

    3. Does streaming make a 300-token answer finish sooner?

    No, the total time is the same. But words appear almost at once, so it feels fast.

    4. A cascade sends 15% of requests to the large model. When does that save money without losing quality?

    When the 15% are the genuinely hard cases. Quality depends on the router, so test it by category.

    5. A vision model reads invoices and 98% of fields match human labels. Why is that not yet enough to automate payment?

    The 2% may sit in the amount and account fields, where errors cost most. Check accuracy on those fields alone, add a second reading and arithmetic checks, and keep the extracted text as the record.
