    Modern Experimentation: CUPED, Anytime-Valid Tests, Interference and Off-Policy Evaluation

    How to get the same answer from a third fewer customers, how to look at a running test without cheating, what breaks when customers share a scarce resource, and how to judge a new decision policy from old logs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA classic A/B test ignores what you knew before the test, forbids peeking, assumes customers do not affect each other and needs a live test per idea; each modern fix comes with a condition you must check, not assume.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take a new app nudge ("you are AED 200 short of your monthly goal"). The textbook test (F2.3, F3.6) splits customers at random, waits four weeks and compares average spend. It has four quiet weaknesses; each has a fix.

    WeaknessCostFix
    Ignores what you know. A customer who spent AED 15,000 last month spends a lot this month whatever you do.Customer-to-customer noise hides a modest effect, so you need many customers or weeks.CUPED: subtract the part of each result that the customer's history predicts.
    Forbids looking. Check daily, stop when significant, and a 5% error rate becomes 20-30%.Wait for a fixed date, or cheat without knowing.Anytime-valid tests and e-values: honest however often you look.
    Assumes customers do not affect each other. If treated customers use up appointment slots, control customers get fewer.A big measured gap, but zero true effect of rolling out.Cluster randomisation and switchbacks: randomise the unit that interacts (branch, time block).

    Every gain rests on an assumption you can break. CUPED needs the covariate measured before treatment. Sequential tests need the right stopping rule. Cluster and switchback designs need interference to stay inside the unit. Off-policy evaluation needs the old policy to have tried what the new one wants to do. Know the assumption, not just the formula.

    Hold onto thisThe randomised experiment is still the gold standard. These methods make it cheaper, safer to monitor and correct under interaction, and let you shortlist ideas before spending a live test.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    CUPED: variance reduction from pre-experiment data

    Compare a heavy-spending crowd to a light-spending one and the nudge's effect drowns in who they are. Comparing each runner's time to their own last race removes that. CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data; Deng et al., 2013) does this: let Y be spend during the test and X spend in the four weeks before. Replace Y with:

    Ycuped=Y−θ(X−X¯),θ=Cov(X,Y)Var(X)

    θ is the slope of Y regressed on X; X̄ is the average of X over both arms combined. X predates assignment, so it differs between arms only by chance: the average effect stays unbiased and the spread shrinks to

    Var(Ycuped)=Var(Y)·(1−ρ2)

    ρ is the correlation between pre-test and in-test spend.

    How to read ρ. ρ² is the share of the variance that history already explains. ρ = 0.7 removes 49% of the variance, so you need about half the customers or half the time. Going from 0.6 to 0.7 moves the saving from 36% to 49%; at 0.3 it is only 9%. Spend and balances usually correlate strongly with their own past; one-off outcomes ("took a new product") do not. Common misreading: CUPED does not change the answer, only the noise around it.

    It is ordinary regression adjustment (F2.5): regressing Y on treatment and X gives essentially the same estimate. In a randomised test, adding pre-treatment covariates can only help precision.

    • Covariate must predate assignment. Anything measured after the nudge can be changed by it, and adjusting for it biases the result (F3.1).
    • Measure ρ before promising anything.
    • New customers have no history. Give them X = the average plus an indicator, or use other pre-test traits. It helps them less.
    • Skewed metrics. A few huge spenders drive variance and θ. Cap at a percentile or use logs first.
    • More covariates or a model. CUPAC (DoorDash) uses an ML prediction of the outcome from pre-test data as X. The more of Y it explains, the bigger the gain.

    Looking early without cheating: anytime-valid tests and e-values

    Flip a fair coin 30 times and check after every flip: at some point it will look biased. A p-value assumes one look at a planned sample size; each extra look is another chance to see a fluke. A daily check on a 30-day test turns a nominal 5% error rate into roughly 25-30% (F2.3, F2.4). Three honest options:

    • Group-sequential designs. Fix, say, four looks and spend the 5% budget across them with stricter thresholds. Reliable, but the schedule is fixed.
    • Always-valid p-values and confidence sequences (Johari et al.; Howard et al.). An interval valid at every sample size at once. It is wider than a fixed-sample one at any moment.
    • E-values (Grünwald, Ramdas and others): a bet against the null.

    An e-value E ≥ 0 has expected value at most 1 if the null is true. Think of a gambler staking one dirham against "no effect": wealth grows only when the data surprise the null. Big wealth is rare under the null (Markov's inequality: the chance E ever reaches 1/α is at most α). So "reject when E ≥ 20" is a 5% test.

    How to read it. E = 20 means the bet grew twentyfold; E = 1 means no evidence; E below 1 means the data favoured the null. Common misreading: it is not a probability, and 20 is not "95% sure".

    En=11+nτ2·exp(τ2Sn22(1+nτ2))

    A standard example for two means with known unit variance (a normal mixture, related to the mSPRT of Johari et al.). Sn is the running sum of standardised differences after n units of information; τ² is how large an effect you think plausible. It averages likelihood ratios over effect sizes, so under the null its expectation is exactly 1 at every n (a martingale), which makes stopping at any time legitimate. A different τ² changes power, never validity.

    • Optional stopping and continuation are safe. Stop whenever you like, or extend the test.
    • Independent evidence multiplies. E = 4 and 5 give 20, a clean way to combine pilot and rollout.
    • Easy to explain. "20 to 1 against the null" beats "p = 0.049 after four peeks".
    • Cost. At a fixed planned sample size you need stronger evidence than the classical test: flexibility for some power.

    Sequential testing is also a safety feature: a harmful treatment (a bad pricing message, a broken checkout) can be stopped early, and guardrail metrics (complaints, failed payments) can be watched continuously while the main metric is read once. Check which method your platform actually ships.

    When customers interact: interference, clusters and switchbacks

    Picture 1,000 people queuing for 250 appointment slots. Give half of them priority and their booking rate rises, but only because the rest lose slots. A standard test assumes SUTVA (F9): your result depends only on your own assignment. When it fails you have interference (spillover), and a customer-level test is wrong, not just noisy. Where it happens:

    • Shared capacity: relationship-manager slots, collections agents, callbacks, fraud analysts.
    • Limited budgets: a cashback pot, a rate quota, new-card allocations.
    • Networks: peer-to-peer transfers, referrals, household accounts.
    • Adversaries: block a fraud pattern for half the traffic and the attack shifts to the other half.
    • Shared prices: auctions, margins, FX spreads.

    The test estimates "treated vs control in the same crowded world". The decision is "everyone vs nobody". They can differ wildly.

    DesignWhat is randomisedFixesCosts and cautions

    Cluster randomisationBranches, regions, advisor books, households, or graph communities (Ugander et al., 2013).Spillover inside the cluster.Few units, wide intervals; clusters must truly contain the interaction. 20 branches is a 20-unit experiment.

    Switchback block length is the dial. Short blocks give many repeats but more carryover contamination. Long blocks are clean but give few comparisons. Handle carryover with a burn-in (discard the start of each block), explicit modelling, or blocks longer than the system's memory.

    The customer-level test is not noisy: its 300 repeats agree almost perfectly. That is the trap. A tight interval around the wrong quantity looks like strong evidence, and only the design protects you, not more data. The answer also depends on the treated share q: a 5% pilot overstates less than a 50% split, but still overstates.

    Checklist. Is there a shared, scarce resource? Does the treatment change how customers reach it? Do arms share a queue, budget or network? If yes to any, use a cluster or switchback design, or at least run two different treated shares and see whether the estimate moves.

    Off-policy evaluation: judging a new policy from old logs

    A policy maps a customer's situation to an action (which offer, whether to call, how large a limit increase). Banks hold years of "situation, action, outcome" logs. Off-policy evaluation (OPE) asks what a different policy would have earned, without deploying it. It is like grading a new exam strategy using only old papers where you answered a different way.

    It is hard because you see only the outcome of the action taken. If the old policy never offered the premium card to high-risk customers, the logs are silent on them (the missing counterfactual of F9). Three estimators:

    • Direct method (DM). Fit a model r̂(x, a) of reward, then ask it what the new policy would earn. Low variance, but only as good as the model, especially for rarely-logged actions.
    • Importance weighting (IPS). Keep logged rows where the old action matches the new policy's, and up-weight by how unlikely it was under the old policy. Unbiased, but variance explodes with large weights.
    • Doubly robust (DR). Start from the model, correct with weighted residuals. Accurate if either the model or the logged probabilities are right.
    V^IPS=1n∑i=1nπ1(ai|xi)π0(ai|xi)ri

    π0 is the old policy, π1 the new one, ri the observed reward. For a deterministic new policy only rows where the logged action equals its choice survive, each scaled by 1/π0: if the old policy offered the premium card to a customer type with probability 5%, each matching row counts 20 times. DR replaces ri with r̂(xi, π1) + wi(ri − r̂(xi, ai)), where wi is the weight.

    Conditions, in order of how often they bite:

    • Overlap. What the new policy would do must have had non-zero chance in the logs. If the old rule was deterministic, no estimator can recover the answer without assumptions. Keep a small randomised exploration share to stay evaluable.
    • Logged propensities. You need π0(a|x), recorded at decision time (best) or estimated (then it is a model with errors).
    • No unmeasured confounding. If staff acted on hunches you did not record, the logs mix their judgement into outcomes (F2.6).
    • Effective sample size. (Σw)² / Σw² is the number of equal-weight rows giving the same variance. It collapses when exploration is thin.
    • Stable world. If behaviour shifts or the policy changes the environment, estimates drift (F7).

    Variants: clip weights (small bias, less variance); self-normalise by Σw instead of n; bootstrap the rows for intervals (F2.2). Over many steps, weights multiply and variance explodes, so long-horizon OPE is still a research problem.

    The trade-off: a precise but possibly biased model, or unbiased but noisy weights; DR tries to keep the best of both. It does not remove the need for overlap: with no logged trials the weights are infinite and the model has nothing to correct it. In this toy the model errs the same way everywhere; in a real portfolio errors differ by segment and harm can concentrate in the segment you least wanted to hurt.

    Use OPE as a screening tool: it picks the three of thirty candidates worth a live test and sizes the prize. It rarely replaces the test where real money or customer impact is involved, and it must report an interval. The Open Bandit Pipeline offers realistic logs to practise on.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor claims and AI-generated analyses alike.

    • CUPED with a post-treatment covariate

      "Adjusted for logins" where logins were counted during the test and the nudge affects them.

      Spot itAsk for the date range of every covariate; all must end before assignment. Check balance across arms.
    • Promising a variance cut without measuring ρ

      "CUPED halves the sample size" for a binary outcome with a weak pre-period link.

      Spot itAsk for historical pre-post correlation. The cut is ρ²; at ρ = 0.3 it is 9%.
    • Peeking with a fixed-sample test

      Daily p-values; the team stops when p < 0.05.

      Spot itAsk when the stopping rule was fixed and how many looks occurred. Run an A/A test through the process and see how often it "wins".
    • Calling e-value tests "free"

      Claiming sequential methods are strictly better.

      Spot itCompare sample size for equal power against a fixed-horizon plan. Anytime-validity costs speed if you would only have looked once.
    • Customer-level split with a shared resource

      A tight, large effect on a product drawing on limited slots, budget, queue time or a network.

      Spot itAsk what control experiences. Rerun at a different treated share; if the estimate moves, there is interference.
    • Clusters that leak

      Randomising by branch when customers use several branches and the app.

      Spot itMeasure the share of activity outside the assigned cluster. High leakage means partial isolation.
    • Switchback carryover ignored

      Daily flipping on a slow metric such as balances or churn.

      Spot itPlot the metric around switch points. If it takes days to settle, lengthen blocks or add a burn-in.
    • OPE without overlap

      Evaluating actions the old policy never took, with a flexible model filling the gap.

      Spot itTabulate, per segment, how often the logged policy took the new action. Zero cells mean the estimate is model opinion.
    • Unlogged or guessed propensities

      Weights from a model of "what the policy probably did" when staff used judgement.

      Spot itAsk how π0 was recorded. If estimated, the estimator inherits its errors; check for extreme weights.
    • Reporting an OPE point estimate

      "The new policy raises acceptance by 3.1 points" from heavy weights on a few rows.

      Spot itAsk for an interval and the effective sample size. If a handful of rows carry the weight, treat it as a hypothesis.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Pre-test and in-test spend correlate at 0.6. By how much does CUPED cut the variance, the interval width and the customers needed?

    Variance falls by ρ² = 36%, to 64%. Width scales with the square root, so ×0.8: 20% narrower. Customers needed scale with variance: 36% fewer.

    2. Why is it safe to adjust for pre-test spend but not for in-test logins?

    Pre-test spend was fixed before randomisation, so it is independent of treatment. In-test logins can be changed by the treatment, so adjusting for them removes part of the real effect and biases the estimate.

    3. You look at a 5% test every day for 30 days and stop at the first p < 0.05. How often is a no-effect test called a winner?

    Roughly a quarter to a third (the simulation shows about 26%). Repeated looks give noise many chances to cross the line.

    4. A test ends with an e-value of 12. At what error level can you reject? Can you reject at 5%?

    At 1/12 ≈ 8.3%. At 5% you need E ≥ 20, so no. You may keep collecting data and multiply in further independent evidence.

    5. 250 appointment slots exist for 1,000 customers. A feature gives treated customers priority. Half are treated. Why does the treated booking rate rise but not the total?

    Slots are fixed, so total bookings stay capped at 25%. Priority only changes who gets them: treated rate up, control down, average about 25%. A customer-level test reports the gap, though rolling out to everyone leaves the total unchanged.

    6. What is the trade-off when choosing a switchback block length?

    Short blocks give many comparisons but more carryover, biasing toward zero. Long blocks cut carryover but give few blocks and a noisier estimate. Choose blocks longer than the system's memory, or discard a burn-in.

    7. The old rule never offered product B to high-risk customers. Can OPE tell you what would happen if it did?

    No. There are no logged cases to reweight, so weights are infinite. Any answer is the reward model's extrapolation, an assumption and not evidence. You need some randomised exposure first.

    8. The old policy took the new policy's preferred action with probability 4%. Of 10,000 customers, about 400 rows match, each scaled by 25. What does this do, and what helps?

    The estimate rests on about 400 heavily weighted rows, so variance is high (effective sample size at most 400). Remedies: more logs, deliberate exploration, clipped or self-normalised weights, or DR with a good reward model. Always bootstrap an interval.
