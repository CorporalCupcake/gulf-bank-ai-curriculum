    Modern Experimentation: CUPED, Anytime-Valid Tests, Interference and Off-Policy Evaluation

    How to get the same answer from a third fewer customers, how to look at a running test without cheating, what breaks when customers share a scarce resource, and how to judge a new decision policy from old logs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA classic A/B test wastes information (it ignores what you knew about each customer before the test), forbids peeking, assumes customers do not affect each other, and needs a live experiment for every idea; the modern toolkit fixes each of these, and each fix comes with a condition that you must check rather than assume.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank wants to know whether a new app nudge ("you are AED 200 short of your monthly goal") raises card spend. The textbook test (F2.3, F3.6) is simple: split customers at random, wait four weeks, compare average spend. It works, and it has four quiet weaknesses. This lesson is one fix for each.

    Weakness of the classic testWhat it costs youThe modern fix
    It ignores what you already know. A customer who spent AED 15,000 last month will spend a lot this month whatever you do.Huge customer-to-customer noise hides a modest effect, so you need many customers or many weeks.CUPED: subtract the part of each customer's result that their own history predicts.
    It forbids looking. Check daily, stop when it turns significant, and your 5% error rate is really 20-30%.Either you wait for a fixed date, or you cheat without knowing it.Anytime-valid inference and e-values: tests that stay honest however often you look.
    It assumes customers do not affect each other.     It needs a live test for every idea. You cannot try twenty collections policies on real customers.Slow, costly, and sometimes not allowed.Off-policy evaluation: estimate how a new policy would have done, using logs of the old one.

    Two ideas run through all four.

    • Information is the currency. Every technique here either squeezes more signal from the same customers (CUPED), lets you act as soon as the evidence is strong enough (sequential tests), or reuses data you already paid for (off-policy evaluation).
    • Every gain rests on an assumption you can break. CUPED needs the covariate to be measured before treatment. Sequential tests need the right stopping rule. Cluster and switchback designs need the interference to stay inside the unit. Off-policy evaluation needs the old policy to have tried what the new one wants to do. The skill is knowing the assumption, not the formula.
    Hold onto thisThe randomised experiment is still the gold standard. These methods do not replace it. They make it cheaper, safer to monitor, correct when customers interact, and let you shortlist ideas before spending a live test on them.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    CUPED: variance reduction from pre-experiment data

    The comparison of two group means is noisy mostly because customers differ from each other, not because of the nudge. Some spend AED 2,000 a month, some AED 15,000. That spread is already visible before the test starts. CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data; Deng et al., 2013) uses it.

    Let Y be a customer's spend during the test and X their spend in the four weeks before it. Replace Y with an adjusted value:

    Ycuped=Y−θ(X−X¯),θ=Cov(X,Y)Var(X)

    θ is the slope of a regression of Y on X, and X̄ is the average of X over both arms combined. The adjustment shifts each customer's result by the amount their history predicts. Because X was measured before assignment, it cannot differ between arms except by chance, so the average treatment effect is unchanged (no bias) while the spread of the adjusted values shrinks to

    Var(Ycuped)=Var(Y)·(1−ρ2)

    ρ is the correlation between pre-test and in-test spend. With ρ = 0.7, the variance falls by 49%. Since the sample size needed for a given precision is proportional to variance, you need about half the customers, or you reach the same answer in half the time.

    This is ordinary regression adjustment (F2.5) in disguise: the treatment effect from "regress Y on treatment and X" is essentially CUPED. The experimenter's version is popular because it is simple to run at scale in SQL and easy to explain: "we remove the predictable part of each customer's behaviour." In a randomised test, adding covariates is safe because treatment is independent of them, so it can only help precision.

    What to remember when you use it.

    • The covariate must come from before assignment. A pre-period metric is safe. Anything measured after the nudge could itself be changed by it, and adjusting for it biases the result. This is point-in-time discipline (F3.1) applied to experiments.
    • The gain is set by ρ. Spend, balances and logins usually correlate strongly with their own past; one-off outcomes such as "took a new product this month" do not, so CUPED may give almost nothing. Measure ρ before promising anything.
    • New customers have no history. Give them X = the average and an indicator, or use other pre-test traits. The method still works; it just helps less for them.
    • Skewed metrics need care. A few very large spenders drive both the variance and θ. Cap extreme values at a percentile, or work on a log scale, before adjusting.
    • You can use more than one covariate, or a model. CUPAC (the name used by DoorDash) replaces X with a machine-learning prediction of the outcome trained on pre-test data, and the same recipe works with several covariates. The more of Y the prediction explains, the larger the gain. Post-stratification and regression adjustment are close cousins.

    Looking early without cheating: anytime-valid tests and e-values

    A p-value is built for one look at a pre-planned sample size. Look more often and each look is another chance to see a fluke. A daily check across a 30-day test turns a nominal 5% error rate into something like 25-30%, because random noise wanders across the line at some point (F2.3, F2.4 cover the logic of tests and multiple chances). Business people do look, and they do stop early when a dashboard turns green. The question is how to make that legitimate.

    There are three families of answer.

    • Group-sequential designs (classical, from clinical trials). Decide in advance on, say, four looks and spend the 5% error budget across them with a stricter threshold at each. Reliable and well understood, but you must fix the schedule.
    • Always-valid p-values and confidence sequences (Johari et al.; Howard et al.). A confidence interval that is valid at every sample size simultaneously, so you can look continuously. The price is a wider interval than a fixed-sample one at any given moment.
    • E-values (Grünwald, Ramdas and others). A different foundation: a bet against the null hypothesis.

    An e-value is a number E ≥ 0 whose expected value is at most 1 if the null is true. Think of it as the wealth of a gambler who stakes one dirham against "no effect" and wins when the data surprise the null. Under the null, big wealth is rare: by Markov's inequality, the chance that E ever reaches 1/α is at most α. So "reject when E ≥ 20" is a 5% test, and an e-value of 20 means the bet grew twentyfold.

    En=11+nτ2·exp(τ2Sn22(1+nτ2))

    A standard example for comparing two means with known unit variance (a "normal mixture" test, related to the mSPRT of Johari et al.). Sn is the running sum of standardised differences after n units of information, and τ² is how large an effect you consider plausible (a prior width). This En averages likelihood ratios over all effect sizes, so it has expectation exactly 1 at every n under the null. That property makes it a "martingale", and it is what makes stopping at any time legitimate. Choosing a different τ² changes power but never validity.

    Three properties make e-values attractive in practice. They survive optional stopping and continuation: you may stop whenever you like, or extend a test, and the guarantee holds. Evidence from independent experiments multiplies: two studies with E = 4 and 5 give 20, which is the clean way to combine a pilot and a rollout. And they stay interpretable ("20 to 1 against the null" is easier to explain than "p = 0.049 after four peeks"). The honest cost: at a fixed planned sample size they need stronger evidence than the classical test, so you gain flexibility and lose some power. The simulation shows both sides.

    Two practical notes. First, sequential testing is also a safety feature: a harmful treatment (a bad pricing message, a broken checkout) can be stopped early without ruining the statistics. Second, the same ideas power safe stopping for guardrail metrics (complaints, failed payments) that you want to watch continuously while the main metric is read once. Several commercial experimentation platforms now ship some form of sequential testing; check the vendor's documentation for exactly which method they use.

    When customers interact: interference, clusters and switchbacks

    A standard test relies on SUTVA, the "stable unit treatment value assumption" (F9 covers causal inference more broadly). In plain terms: one customer's result depends only on their own assignment, not on what anybody else got. When it fails you have interference or spillover, and a customer-level A/B test can be badly wrong, not just noisy.

    Where this happens in a bank:

    • Shared capacity. Relationship-manager appointment slots, collections agents, call-centre callbacks, a fixed pool of fraud analysts. Give treated customers a better chance of a slot and control customers lose out.
    • Limited budgets and inventory. A cashback pot, a promotional rate quota, a fixed number of new-card allocations.
    • Networks. Peer-to-peer transfers, referral bonuses, household accounts, salary-advance products spreading by word of mouth. A treated customer influences untreated friends.
    • Adversaries. Fraudsters adapt. Block one pattern for half the traffic and the attack may simply shift to the other half. Control customers then look worse than they would in a world where everything is blocked.
    • Prices and markets. Anything that moves a shared price, such as an auction, a margin or an FX spread.

    The danger is a precise one. The test estimates "treated customers versus control customers who live in the same crowded world". The decision you face is "everyone gets it versus nobody gets it". Those can differ wildly. The simulation below makes it concrete.

    DesignWhat is randomisedFixesCosts and cautions
    Customer-level A/BIndividual customers.Nothing: assumes no interference.Biased when customers compete for a resource or influence each other, and the bias can be large and confident.
    Cluster randomisationWhole groups: branches, regions, advisor books, households, or communities found in a transaction graph (graph cluster randomisation; Ugander et al., 2013).Spillover that stays inside the cluster.Far fewer independent units, so wide intervals. Clusters must truly contain the interaction. With 20 branches you have a 20-unit experiment.
    SwitchbackTime blocks.     Two-sided or budget-split designsBoth sides of a marketplace, or budget shares.Competition for a shared resource, by estimating the effect at several treatment shares.Needs bespoke modelling; used in large marketplaces more than in banks.

    Switchbacks buy validity with block length as the dial. Short blocks give many repeats and little noise from slow-moving trends, but more of each block is contaminated by the previous state (carryover). Long blocks are clean but give few independent comparisons. Practitioners handle carryover by discarding the first part of each block (a "burn-in"), by modelling it explicitly, or by designing blocks longer than the memory of the system.

    Notice what is and is not wrong with the customer-level test. It is not "noisy": its 300 repeats agree almost perfectly. That is the trap. A very tight interval around the wrong quantity looks like strong evidence. Only the design can protect you, not more data. Notice also that the answer depends on the treated share q: a 5% pilot overstates the benefit less than a 50% split but still overstates it, and the effect of "everyone treated" is not recoverable from a single split without a model of how customers compete.

    Checklist before trusting a customer-level test: is there a shared, scarce resource? Does the treatment change how customers reach it? Do treated and control customers share a queue, a budget or a network? If yes to any, plan a cluster or switchback design, or at least run the test at two different treated shares and see whether the estimate moves.

    Off-policy evaluation: judging a new policy from old logs

    A policy is a rule that maps a customer's situation to an action: which offer to show, whether to call, how large a limit increase to give. Banks accumulate years of logs of "situation, action taken, outcome". The question: if we had used a different policy, what would the outcome have been? Answering it from the logs, without deploying, is off-policy evaluation (OPE).

    It is hard for one reason. You only see the outcome of the action that was taken. If the old policy never offered the premium card to high-risk customers, the logs say nothing about how they would have responded. This is the missing-counterfactual problem of causal inference (F9), and OPE is its decision-making version. Three estimators, each balancing bias and variance differently:

    • Direct method (DM). Fit a model r̂(x, a) predicting the reward for each customer and action, then ask it what the new policy would have earned. Low variance, but it is only as good as the model, especially for actions the logs rarely contain.
    • Importance weighting (IPS, inverse propensity scoring). Keep only the logged rows where the old action happens to match what the new policy would do, and re-weight them by how unlikely that action was under the old policy. Unbiased, but variance explodes when the weights are large.
    • Doubly robust (DR). Start from the model, then correct it with weighted residuals. It stays accurate if either the model or the logged probabilities are right.
    V^IPS=1n∑i=1nπ1(ai|xi)π0(ai|xi)ri

    π0 is the old (logging) policy, π1 the new one, and ri the observed reward. If the new policy is deterministic, only rows where the logged action equals its choice survive, each scaled by 1/π0. Example: if the old policy offered the premium card to a customer type with probability 5%, each matching row counts 20 times. The estimator is unbiased provided π0 was truly random enough (see below) and you recorded its probabilities. The doubly robust version replaces ri with r̂(xi, π1) + wi(ri − r̂(xi, ai)), where wi is the weight above.

    The conditions that matter, in order of how often they bite:

    • Overlap (positivity). Whatever the new policy would do must have had a non-zero chance of being tried in the logs. If the old rule was deterministic ("high-risk customers never get offer 2"), the data are silent and no estimator can recover the answer without assumptions. This is why teams keep a small randomised exploration share: it is the price of being able to evaluate later.
    • Logged propensities. You need π0(a|x), either recorded at decision time (best) or estimated from the logs (then it is a model, with a model's errors).
    • No unmeasured confounding. The logging policy's choices may depend on things you did not record. If the call centre rang the customers it "had a feeling" about, the logs mix its judgement into the outcome (F2.6).
    • Effective sample size. High weights mean few rows do the work. A useful quantity is (Σw)² / Σw², the number of equally-weighted rows that would give the same variance. The simulation shows it collapsing when exploration is thin.
    • Stable world. Logs describe customers as they were. If behaviour shifts, or the policy changes the customers' environment, the estimate drifts (F7).

    Practical variants: clip the weights at a ceiling (introduces small bias, cuts variance); self-normalise by dividing by the sum of weights rather than n (stabilises when many rows get a low weight); report intervals by bootstrapping the logged rows (F2.2). For sequential decisions over many steps (collections plans over months), the weights multiply across steps and variance grows rapidly, which is why long-horizon OPE is still a research problem.

    The picture makes the central trade-off visible. You can have a model that is precise but possibly biased, or weights that are unbiased but noisy, and doubly robust estimators try to keep the best of both. They do not remove the need for overlap: if the old policy never tried the new action, the weights are infinite and the estimate leans entirely on the model, with nothing to correct it. In this toy, the new policy premium-offers high-risk customers, who rarely saw it; it looks fine on average because the model errs in the same direction everywhere. In a real portfolio, errors differ by segment and the harm can concentrate in the segment you least wanted to hurt.

    In practice OPE is a screening tool. It tells you which three of thirty candidate policies deserve a live test, and gives a rough size of the prize. It rarely replaces the test for a decision with real money or customer impact, and it must report an interval. Open-source toolkits and benchmark data (the Open Bandit Pipeline, for example) exist for trying the estimators on realistic logs.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • App and notification nudges, offer design, onboarding flows. Classic CUPED territory: lots of customers, noisy spend, strong history. A 30-50% saving in test duration is realistic when pre-post correlation is high.
    • Contact-centre scripts and collections strategies. Shared agent capacity makes interference real. Randomise by team, branch or time block rather than by customer, or test at two treated shares.
    • Fraud and AML alert models. A new model changes the analyst queue and the fraudsters' behaviour. Customer-level splits understate how much a full rollout changes the world, and labels arrive late (F3.5). Use sequential monitoring for harm; use time-block or channel-level designs where you can.
    • Credit limits, pricing and next-best-action. Cost and fairness constraints make live tests hard, and decisions are policy-shaped. OPE helps shortlist candidate policies from existing logs, provided the old policy explored enough. Take any plan to randomise credit decisions through compliance and legal first.
    • Pilot governance. For model risk, being able to say "we pre-registered the stopping rule and the guardrails" is much stronger than "we looked when it seemed ready".
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Variance reduction and sequential testing are mainstream in large online experimentation platforms. Anytime-valid inference has grown into a mature statistical theory. Interference-aware design is an active area, and off-policy evaluation has a strong theory for single decisions and a much weaker one for long sequences.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor claims and in AI-generated analyses alike. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • CUPED with a post-treatment covariate

      "Adjusted for the customer's logins" where logins were counted during the test, and the nudge affects logins.

      Spot itAsk for the date range of every covariate. All of it must end before assignment. Check that the covariate is balanced across arms.
    • Promising a variance cut without measuring ρ

      "CUPED halves the sample size" for a binary outcome with a weak pre-period link.

      Spot itAsk for the pre-post correlation on historical data. The reduction is 1 − ρ²; at ρ = 0.3 it is 9%.
    • Peeking with a fixed-sample test

      The dashboard shows p-values daily and the team stops when p drops below 0.05.

      Spot itAsk when the stopping rule was fixed and how many looks occurred. Run an A/A test through the same process and see how often it "wins".
    • Declaring e-value tests "free" of cost

      Claiming that sequential methods are strictly better.

      Spot itCompare the sample size needed for the same power against a fixed-horizon plan. Anytime-validity costs some speed if you would only have looked once.
    • Customer-level split with a shared resource

      A tight, large effect on a product that draws on limited slots, budget, queue time or a network.

      Spot itAsk what the control group experiences. If treated customers can use up something control customers need, rerun with a different treated share; if the estimate moves, there is interference.
    • Clusters that leak

      Randomising by branch, when customers use several branches and the app.

      Spot itMeasure what share of a customer's activity occurs outside their assigned cluster. High leakage means the design only partly isolates arms.
    • Switchback carryover ignored

      Daily flipping on a metric that responds slowly, such as balances or churn.

      Spot itPlot the metric around switch points. If it takes days to settle, use longer blocks or drop a burn-in period.
    • OPE without overlap

      Evaluating a new policy whose actions the old policy never took, with a flexible model filling the gap.

      Spot itFor each segment, tabulate how often the logged policy took the new policy's action. Zero cells mean the estimate is model opinion.
    • Unlogged or guessed propensities

      Weights computed from a model of "what the policy probably did" when staff actually used judgement.

      Spot itAsk how π0 was recorded. If it was estimated, the estimator inherits that model's errors; check whether weights are extreme.
    • Reporting a point estimate from OPE

      "The new policy would raise acceptance by 3.1 points" from heavy weights on a few rows.

      Spot itAsk for an interval and the effective sample size. If a handful of rows carry most of the weight, treat the result as a hypothesis.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a product or business lead · 30 seconds
    “We can usually finish this test about a third sooner by using each customer's own spending history to remove the noise that has nothing to do with the nudge. I'd also like us to agree the stopping rule up front, so that checking the dashboard mid-test doesn't weaken the result. And since agents have limited time, I'd split the test by team, not by customer, otherwise the group getting the nudge simply takes appointments from the group that doesn't.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For randomised digital tests I'd use regression-adjusted estimators with pre-period covariates, which are CUPED in effect, with the covariate window fixed before assignment and ρ checked on history. Monitoring would use a pre-registered group-sequential or anytime-valid procedure, such as a mixture e-process, with guardrails monitored continuously. Where customers share capacity I'd move to cluster or switchback randomisation and report the block length and any carryover treatment. For policy changes I'd screen candidates with doubly robust off-policy estimates, but only where logged propensities exist and overlap is adequate, with bootstrap intervals and an effective sample size, and then confirm the shortlist live.”
    “Isn't CUPED just a trick that changes the answer?”

    It does not change the expected effect; it removes noise. Because the adjustment uses data from before assignment, which is independent of treatment by randomisation, the estimate stays unbiased while its variance shrinks by a factor 1 − ρ². It is regression with a pre-period covariate. If it changes the point estimate a lot, that is a sign that the arms were imbalanced on history by chance, which is exactly what it should correct.

    “Can we stop the test as soon as it looks significant?”

    Not with a standard p-value: each look is another chance for noise, and a daily check on a 30-day test makes a nominal 5% error rate more like a quarter. We have two honest options: pre-plan interim looks with a stricter threshold, or use a sequential or e-value test that is valid at any stopping time. The latter needs somewhat more evidence than a single planned look, so we trade a little power for flexibility.

    “What is an e-value in one sentence?”

    A number that is on average at most 1 if nothing is going on, so if it reaches 20 the data are twenty times more surprising than chance would allow, and that holds however many times and whenever we look. Evidence from independent tests can be multiplied.

    “The A/B test shows +17 points on bookings. Why won't you recommend rollout?”

    Because bookings draw on a fixed pool of appointment slots. The treated customers can only be booking faster by taking slots the control customers would have had. The 17 points is the gap between two groups sharing a crowded system, not the gain from improving the system. I'd rerun as a switchback or by team and measure total bookings and wait times for everyone.

    “Can we evaluate the new collections policy using last year's data instead of a pilot?”

    Maybe for screening, if last year's policy had some randomness, we recorded its probabilities, and it tried the actions the new policy wants. I'd check overlap by segment first. If the old rules were deterministic, the data cannot answer the question. If it passes, I'd report a doubly robust estimate with an interval and effective sample size, shortlist the best two, and test those live.

    “Why are the intervals so wide on the off-policy estimate?”

    Because only the logged rows that match the new policy do the work, each scaled up by the inverse of how unlikely it was under the old one. If the old policy rarely did that, a few rows carry large weights and the effective sample size is small. More logs or a bit of deliberate exploration in the current policy would fix it; a fancier estimator would not.

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Pre-test and in-test spend correlate at 0.6. By how much does CUPED cut the variance, the width of the interval, and the customers needed?

    Variance falls by ρ² = 36%, to 64% of the original. The interval width scales with the square root, so ×0.8: a 20% narrower interval. The sample size needed is proportional to variance, so 36% fewer customers.

    2. Why is it safe to adjust for pre-test spend but not for in-test logins?

    Pre-test spend was fixed before random assignment, so it is independent of treatment and cannot create bias. In-test logins can be changed by the treatment itself; adjusting for them removes part of the real effect (or introduces selection) and biases the estimate.

    3. You look at a 5% test every day for 30 days and stop at the first p < 0.05. Roughly how often will a test with no real effect be called a winner?

    Very roughly a quarter to a third (the simulation shows about 26%). Repeated looks give noise many chances to cross the line, so the overall false-positive rate is far above the nominal 5%.

    4. A test ends with an e-value of 12. At what error level can you reject the null? Can you reject at 5%?

    The null can be rejected at level 1/12 ≈ 8.3%. At 5% you need E ≥ 20, so no. You may keep collecting data and multiply in further independent evidence, because e-values remain valid when a test is continued.

    5. 250 appointment slots exist for 1,000 customers. A feature gives treated customers priority. Half are treated. Explain why the treated group's booking rate rises, and why the total does not.

    The number of slots is fixed, so total bookings are capped at 25% of customers. Priority only changes who gets them: the treated group's rate rises, the control group's falls, and the average stays about 25%. A customer-level test reports the gap between the groups, even though rolling out to everyone would leave the total unchanged.

    6. What is the trade-off when choosing the block length of a switchback?

    Short blocks give many comparisons and so less noise from trends, but a larger share of each block is contaminated by the previous state (carryover), which biases the estimate toward zero. Long blocks reduce carryover but give few independent blocks, so the estimate is noisier. Choose blocks longer than the system's memory, or discard a burn-in at the start of each block.

    7. The old rule never offered product B to high-risk customers. Can off-policy evaluation tell you what would happen if it did?

    No. There are no logged cases to reweight, so the importance weights are infinite. Any answer would come purely from the reward model's extrapolation, which is an assumption and not evidence. You need some randomised exposure first.

    8. In the log, the old policy took the action the new one prefers with probability 4%. Of 10,000 customers, about 400 rows match, each scaled by 25. What does this do to the estimate, and what would reduce the problem?

    The estimate rests on about 400 rows with weight 25, so its variance is high (the effective sample size is at most 400, and less if some rows have even larger weights). Remedies: more logs, deliberate exploration in the current policy, clipped or self-normalised weights, or the doubly robust form with a good reward model. Always bootstrap an interval.
