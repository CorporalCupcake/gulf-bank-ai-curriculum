    Modern A/B Testing: CUPED and Always-Valid Tests

    Part 1

    What these two fixes are

    In one sentenceCUPED makes an A/B test sharper by using each customer's own past, and an always-valid test lets you check results every day without inflating false alarms.

    Running example: a café texts a voucher to half its customers (group B) and compares weekly spend with the other half (group A).

    A classic A/B test splits customers at random and compares averages. It has two quiet weaknesses.

    • Noise: customers differ hugely in how much they spend, which hides a small voucher effect. CUPED fixes this.
    • No peeking: a normal test allows one look at a planned sample size. Always-valid tests fix this.

    Randomisation stays the gold standard. These methods make it cheaper and safer to monitor.

    Part 2

    CUPED: compare each customer with their own past

    It is like timing a runner against their last race instead of against strangers. The average effect stays the same. The noise around it shrinks.

    How it works

    1. Before the test, record each customer's spend (the "pre-test" spend).
    2. Run the test as usual and record in-test spend.
    3. Fit a straight line predicting in-test spend from pre-test spend.
    4. Subtract the part of each customer's result that the line explains.
    5. Compare group A and B on the adjusted numbers.

    How to read it

    The gain depends on how well past spend predicts in-test spend. The share of spread removed is the correlation squared.

    Correlation 0.7
    About half the spread goes. You need roughly half as many customers for the same precision.
    Correlation 0.3
    Under a tenth goes. Barely worth it.
    One-off outcomes (a first purchase)
    Past behaviour predicts little, so CUPED helps little. Spend and balances correlate strongly with their own past, so measure the correlation first.
    • Use data from before assignment only. Anything measured after can be changed by the voucher, and adjusting for it eats part of the real effect.
    • New customers have no history. Give them the average plus a "no history" flag. They gain less.
    • A few huge spenders can dominate the line. Cap at a high percentile or take logs first.
    • CUPAC goes a step further: use a machine-learning prediction of the outcome, built from pre-test data, as the adjustment.
    Part 3

    Always-valid tests: looking early without cheating

    A p-value assumes one look at a planned sample size. Each extra look is another chance to see a fluke. Checking daily and stopping at the first p-value below 0.05 turns a 5% false-alarm rate into roughly 25%.

    Three honest options

    • Group-sequential design: fix a few looks in advance and spend the 5% error budget across them with stricter thresholds. Reliable but rigid.
    • Confidence sequence: an interval valid at every sample size at once, so you can read it daily. It is wider than a one-look interval at any moment.
    • E-value test: a running score for evidence against "no effect", explained below.

    How to read an e-value

    • Stake one dirham against "no effect". Your wealth grows only when the data surprise that claim. If there really is no effect, big wealth is rare.
    • The stop rule: the chance wealth ever reaches 20 is at most 5%, so "stop when E reaches 20" is a 5% test however often you check.
    • E of 1 means no evidence. Below 1 means the data favoured "no effect".
    • It is not a probability. 20 does not mean "95% sure".
    • Combining: independent evidence multiplies. E of 4 from a pilot and 5 from the rollout gives 20.

    What you gain: stop whenever you like, extend a test, and watch guardrail metrics (complaints, failed payments) continuously. A broken change can be stopped early. What you pay: if you would only have looked once, you need somewhat stronger evidence than the classic test.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    MethodFixesCatch
    Classic testNothing, it is the baselineOne look only
    CUPEDNoise, so fewer customers neededNeeds pre-test history that predicts the outcome
    Group-sequentialA few planned looksRigid schedule
    Confidence sequenceDaily reading of an intervalWider at any moment
    E-valueStop any time, combine evidenceNeeds more evidence than a one-look test
    • CUPED is strong at: cutting cost with no bias. Weak at: new customers and one-off outcomes.
    • Always-valid tests are strong at: continuous monitoring. Weak at: a little power if you only ever look once.
    • Both assume customers do not affect each other. If they share something scarce (appointment slots, a cashback budget), the test is wrong, not just noisy. Assign whole branches or switch the product on and off in time blocks instead.
    Show the formulas

    CUPED adjusted result = in-test spend − θ × (pre-test spend − average pre-test spend). The slope θ is the slope of the fitted line.

    Spread left = original spread × (1 − correlation²). A correlation of 0.7 leaves 51%.

    In banking, CUPED suits campaigns on card spend or balances, and e-values suit daily monitoring of guardrails such as complaints or failed payments.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Adjusting for something measured after assignment

      The voucher may have changed it, so the adjustment removes real effect. Use pre-test data only.

    • Peeking at a classic test

      Stopping at the first p below 0.05 gives roughly 25% false alarms, not 5%.

    • Reading an e-value as a probability

      E of 20 is a stopping threshold, not "95% sure".

    • Ignoring interaction between customers

      With shared capacity, a tight interval around the wrong quantity looks like strong evidence.

    1. Past spend correlates 0.7 with in-test spend. What does CUPED give you?

    About half the spread removed, so roughly half the customers needed.

    2. Can you use last week's spend, measured during the test, as the CUPED covariate?

    No. Only data fixed before assignment is safe, because later data can be affected by the voucher.

    3. Why is daily peeking at a classic test a problem?

    Each look is another chance for a fluke, so false alarms rise from 5% to about 25%.

    4. An e-value reads 0.5. What does that mean?

    Below 1, so the data have slightly favoured "no effect". It is not evidence for the voucher.

    5. Two independent e-values of 4 and 5. What is the combined evidence?

    20, since independent e-values multiply. That reaches the stop line.
