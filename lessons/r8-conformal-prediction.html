    Conformal Prediction and Causal ML: Honest Ranges and Who Actually Responds

    Two ideas that turn a model's output into something you can defend. Conformal prediction wraps any model in a range with a coverage guarantee. Double machine learning and causal forests ask a different question: not who will buy, but who buys because we asked.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceConformal prediction turns any model's point estimate into a range that contains the truth a stated share of the time, by checking how wrong the model was on data it did not train on; double ML strips out what the model can already predict so that what is left isolates a treatment's effect, and lets you estimate that effect customer by customer.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two requests land on your desk in the same week.

    The first is from Treasury. Your model forecasts tomorrow's net cash outflow at a branch cluster as AED 12.4 million. Treasury asks: how wrong could that be? A single number invites false confidence. They need a range they can plan reserves around, and they need to know that "90% range" really means nine times in ten.

    The second is from Retail Marketing. They have a model that scores customers by likelihood to take up a personal finance top-up. They propose to call the top 30%. You notice the top 30% are customers who would have taken the offer anyway. The calls cost AED 18 each and change almost nothing. The people worth calling are in the middle, and your score cannot tell you who they are because it answers "who will buy", not "who will buy because of the call".

    These are the two halves of this lesson.

    • Conformal prediction (R8.1) answers "how wrong could this be?" without trusting the model. Hold some data back. See how far off the model was on it. Use the size of those past misses to draw the range around tomorrow's prediction. The guarantee comes from fair counting, not from the model being right.
    • Double machine learning and heterogeneous effects (R8.2) answer "what does acting change, and for whom?". Machine learning is good at predicting. Causal questions need an effect, a difference between two worlds. The trick is to use ML for the prediction parts and a small, careful step for the causal part.

    Both are about being honest regarding what a model output does and does not tell you. The first asks for an error bar that can be checked. The second refuses to confuse a prediction with a cause.

    Hold onto thisA model gives you a guess. Conformal prediction gives the guess an error bar that holds up in a meeting. Causal ML gives you the effect of your action, which is a different number from the guess. In both cases the quality of the answer rests on checks you can run, not on how clever the model is.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Conformal prediction: ranges with a guarantee

    The most common form is split conformal prediction. Four steps, none of which depends on what the model is.

    1. Train any model on a training set. Neural net, boosted trees, a linear model, a vendor black box.
    2. Calibrate. Take a separate calibration set of n cases the model has not seen. For each, compute a nonconformity score: how surprising the true answer was. For a forecast, the simplest score is the absolute error, |actual − predicted|.
    3. Take a quantile. Sort the n scores. Pick the one at position k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉. Call it q. For α = 0.1 and n = 99 this is the 90th smallest score.
    4. Predict. For a new case, return the prediction ± q. In general: every answer whose score would be at most q.
    P(Y∈C(X))≥1−α

    Here C(X) is the range returned for a new case and α is the miss rate you accept (0.1 for a "90% range"). The guarantee is marginal: averaged over new cases and over the random draw of the calibration set. It needs only that the calibration cases and the new case are exchangeable, meaning that if you shuffled them together nobody could tell which was which. No assumption about the model, and none about the shape of the errors.

    Why does it work? Take the new case and the n calibration cases together. That is n+1 scores. If they are exchangeable, the new one is equally likely to be the smallest, the second smallest, and so on. The chance it lands in the top α share is therefore at most α, apart from rounding. The "+1" in the formula is the new case counting itself in. It is pure counting, which is why no distribution is needed.

    The interval can still be useless in two ways. It can be too wide to act on because the model is poor. And a single constant width ignores that some cases are harder than others. Simulation 1 shows both.

    What you saw is the central distinction. Split conformal guarantees marginal coverage: right on average across all cases. It does not guarantee conditional coverage: right for each kind of case. Conditional coverage cannot be guaranteed in general without assumptions, which is a theorem, not a gap in the method. In practice you get close by choosing a score that reflects difficulty. Three common ways:

    • Normalised scores. Divide the error by an estimate of local spread, as in the simulation.
    • Conformalised quantile regression (CQR). Train a model for the 5th and 95th percentiles (F7.2 covers quantile loss), then calibrate the gap between them so the guarantee holds. Ranges widen and narrow with the case, and they carry the guarantee even when the quantile model is wrong.
    • Classification sets. For a classifier, return a set of labels, for example {"complaint: unauthorised access", "complaint: card issue"}, sized so the true label is in the set 95% of the time. A single clear case returns one label. A confusing one returns several, which is the model saying "send this to a human".

    There is a second subtlety. The guarantee is about the random draw of the calibration set too. With a small calibration set, the coverage you actually got is itself random.

    The rule of thumb: for a 90% range you want at least a few hundred calibration cases, and for a 99% range (a regulator-style tail) you need many more, since the 99th percentile of 100 scores is just the largest one. With too few cases the method honestly returns an infinite range rather than pretending.

    When data shift: weighted and adaptive conformal

    Exchangeability is the one assumption that matters, and banking data break it often. Deposit behaviour in Ramadan differs from other months. A new product brings a different customer mix. Fraudsters adapt. The calibration data are from last year; tomorrow is not exchangeable with it.

    Two families of repair exist.

    • Covariate shift: reweight. If only the mix of customers changes (the inputs X) while the relationship between inputs and outcome stays put, weight each calibration case by how much more common it is under the new mix: w(x) = new density ÷ old density. This is weighted conformal prediction. It needs the density ratio, which you estimate with a classifier that tells old data from new, and it works only when the ratio is not extreme.
    • Drifting over time: adapt. Adaptive conformal inference watches whether recent ranges missed, and nudges α up or down online. If the last 100 days missed 15% when you wanted 10%, it widens the range. It guarantees the long-run miss rate, without per-day guarantees, even when the data are arbitrary.

    Two cautions. First, reweighting fixes only covariate shift. If the relationship itself changes (a new fraud tactic, a rate change that alters repayment behaviour), no reweighting from old data can repair it, and you need fresh labelled data or the online adaptive approach. Second, the effective sample size shrinks when the weights are uneven, so a bank that shifts a lot needs a bigger calibration set, not a smaller one.

    Double ML: isolating the effect of an action

    Now the second half. Suppose the bank gave some customers a pre-approved top-up offer (T = 1) and not others (T = 0), and you observe their next-quarter spend or balance Y. The treated customers were not chosen at random: the marketing rules picked wealthier, more active customers. If you just compare the averages, you attribute their higher spend to the offer. This is confounding (F2.6). A randomised experiment (F3.6) is the clean answer. Where you only have history, you adjust for observed customer features X and hope they capture the reasons for selection.

    You could adjust with a regression. But the relationship between X and spend is messy and nonlinear, and a wrong regression form biases the answer. The temptation is to use a flexible ML model for the adjustment. Naively plugged in, ML introduces its own bias (regularisation shrinks things in just the wrong direction). Double/debiased ML solves this with a small recipe.

    1. Predict the outcome from X alone, ignoring treatment: m(x) = expected spend for this kind of customer.
    2. Predict the treatment from X: e(x) = chance this kind of customer gets the offer (the propensity).
    3. Take residuals. Residual spend = actual − m(x). Residual treatment = T − e(x). These are what is left after removing everything the customer's features already explain.
    4. Regress residual spend on residual treatment. The slope is the effect of the offer.
    τ^=∑(T−e^)(Y−m^)∑(T−e^)2

    This is the Frisch-Waugh idea from regression (F2.5): to find the effect of T once X is accounted for, strip X out of both T and Y and look at what remains. The two nuisance models, m̂ and ê, can be any ML method. The formula is orthogonal (Neyman-orthogonal) to their errors, so small mistakes in them only have a second-order effect on τ̂.

    The word "double" refers to the two prediction models. The word "debiased" refers to two further details. Orthogonality, as above. And cross-fitting: the nuisance models for a customer are trained on other customers' data, never their own. Without this, an overfitted nuisance model quietly eats part of the signal and biases the estimate. With it, you can use random forests, boosting or neural nets for the nuisance parts and still get an estimate with a valid standard error.

    Heterogeneous effects. The average effect hides who responds. Let the effect depend on features: τ(x). Two approaches dominate.

    • Meta-learners. Build the effect estimator from ordinary ML models. The S-learner fits one model with treatment as a feature. The T-learner fits separate models for treated and untreated and subtracts. The X-learner and R-learner add corrections for imbalance between groups. The DR-learner uses a doubly robust pseudo-outcome. The R-learner is essentially DML with a flexible final stage: regress the outcome residual on treatment residual × a model of the effect.
    • Causal forests. A random forest whose splits are chosen to separate customers with different treatment effects, not different outcomes, and whose leaves average within-leaf residual comparisons. The "honest" variant uses one half of the data to choose splits and the other half to estimate effects, which gives valid confidence intervals for τ(x).

    Simulation 4 uses a deliberately simple setting, one customer feature and a straight-line effect, so you can see every moving part.

    The result to remember is the last row. A ranking by "who is likely to spend" is a different ranking from "who spends more because of the offer". The best targets often sit in the middle of the spend distribution: not already sold, not hopeless. The same logic is behind uplift modelling in B6.2.

    What DML does not fix: unobserved confounders. If something that drives both the offer and the spend is missing from X, such as a relationship manager's hunch, the estimate is still biased, however fancy the model. It also needs overlap: customers like each other should be found in both treated and untreated groups. If the rules always offered to anyone above AED 50,000, there is nobody untreated to compare the rich with. DML cannot invent that data.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Conformal is the checkable cousin of the uncertainty ideas earlier in the curriculum. Causal ML is where prediction ends and decisions begin.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and the bootstrapParameter uncertainty versus prediction uncertainty

    A confidence interval is about an average. A conformal range is about the next single case. Different question, wider answer.

    F7 · Uncertainty quantificationThe wider toolbox

    Ensembles, Bayesian methods and calibration are the alternatives. Conformal is the one that carries a finite-sample guarantee. Drift monitoring tells you when its exchangeability assumption is in trouble.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · CalibrationCalibrated probabilities versus covered sets

    A calibrated 80% probability is a statement per score. A conformal set is a statement about coverage. They complement each other.

    F7 · Forecast metrics, quantile lossThe engine of CQR

    Quantile regression gives the raw ranges that conformal then corrects.

    F4 · Cross-validationTime-ordered splits

    Calibration data must come from the same era as use. A random calibration split on drifting data repeats the classic CV mistake.

    F2.6 · Confounding and selectionThe problem DML is built for

    DML adjusts for the observed reasons for selection. It does nothing for the unobserved ones.

    F2.5 · RegressionFrisch-Waugh in disguise

    "Regress residuals on residuals" is a regression result you may already know. DML makes the residual step flexible.

    B6.2 · Propensity and upliftChurn, retention and uplift

    Uplift models are heterogeneous treatment effect models. This lesson supplies the estimators and the validity conditions.

    F3.6 · ExperimentsRandomise when you can

    An experiment removes confounding by design and gives the best validation of any effect estimate. Causal forests on experimental data are also how you find subgroups.

    R8 · Synthetic control and DiDOther ways to estimate effects from history

    When a policy hits a branch or segment and you cannot randomise, these compete with the adjustment approach here.

    F8 · CounterfactualsExplanations are not effects

    A SHAP value or partial dependence plot describes the model, not the world. Only a causal estimate describes what an action does.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Liquidity and cash forecasting. A forecast of ATM cash demand or deposit outflow with a 95% range that planners can size buffers against.
    • Credit and expected loss. Ranges on loss-given-default or on exposure at default, and prediction sets for rating-grade assignment. Conformal gives an auditable statement of how often the truth falls outside.
    • Triage with abstention. Complaint, KYC document or adverse-media classifiers that return a set of labels. A one-label set goes straight through; a multi-label or empty set goes to a person. The automation rate becomes a dial linked to a stated error rate.
    • LLM pipelines. Conformal methods can calibrate when a language-model answer should be trusted or withheld, relevant for staff copilots and Arabic-English document extraction.
    • Marketing and collections. Which customers actually respond to a call, an offer, a payment holiday or a restructure, as opposed to those who would pay or buy anyway. This is also the place where cost-per-contact economics make the difference between profitable and wasteful.
    • Pricing and limits. The effect of a rate change or limit increase on balances and defaults, estimated from history with the usual warnings about selection.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Both areas have solid foundations and active frontiers. The foundations are mature enough to use; the frontiers are mostly about relaxing the assumptions.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with two light searches; the earlier items are cited from the literature as I know it, so verify details against the linked papers.

    1. 2005
      Conformal prediction is born

      Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer's book set out the framework and its distribution-free guarantee. It stayed a niche for 15 years, partly because early versions were computationally heavy.

      Vovk, Gammerman, Shafer, Algorithmic Learning in a Random World, 2005
    2. 2018
      Split conformal and double ML

      Lei et al. popularised split conformal prediction: cheap, simple, and usable with any model. In the same year Chernozhukov and co-authors published the double/debiased ML paper, giving orthogonal scores and cross-fitting for estimating effects with flexible nuisance models.

      Lei et al., JASA, 2018; Chernozhukov et al., Econometrics Journal, 2018
    3. 2018–19
      Causal forests and meta-learners

      Wager and Athey proved that honest causal forests give asymptotically valid confidence intervals for individual effects; generalized random forests extended them. Künzel et al. introduced the X-learner and compared meta-learners; Nie and Wager proposed the R-learner. Microsoft's EconML and the R package grf made these routine tools.

      Wager & Athey, JASA, 2018; Athey, Tibshirani, Wager, Annals of Statistics, 2019; Künzel et al., PNAS, 2019; Nie & Wager, Biometrika, 2021
    4. 2019
      Conformalised quantile regression and weighted conformal

      CQR gave adaptive-width ranges with the guarantee. Tibshirani et al. extended conformal prediction to covariate shift with the likelihood-ratio weights used in Simulation 3.

      Romano, Patterson, Candès, NeurIPS 2019; Tibshirani et al., NeurIPS 2019
    5. 2021–23
      Online and beyond-exchangeability

      Adaptive conformal inference tracked the miss rate over time. Barber et al. gave guarantees for non-exchangeable data that degrade gracefully with how far the data depart from exchangeable. Angelopoulos and Bates's gentle introduction became the standard on-ramp.

      Gibbs & Candès, NeurIPS 2021; Barber et al., Annals of Statistics, 2023; Angelopoulos & Bates, Found. Trends ML, 2023
    6. 2024
      Which causal estimator works best?

      A systematic comparison of the modified causal forest, grf and DML under selection-on-observables found that forest-based approaches with outcome centring did better at picking up finer heterogeneity. Results depend on the data design, so treat any "winner" as provisional.

      Lechner & Mareckova, arXiv 2405.10198
    7. 2026
      Conformal methods for language models under shift

      CoFact (ICLR 2026) argues that conformal factuality guarantees for LLM outputs rely on exchangeability that real prompt streams break, and reweights calibration data with an online density-ratio estimate. Another preprint reweights calibration samples by closeness to the test prompt. A 2026 scoping review groups over 100 conformal-for-LLM studies into factuality, abstention and retrieval themes. I only saw summaries of these, so check the papers.

      Hu et al., CoFact, ICLR 2026; arXiv 2510.05566 (preprint)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Calibrating on data that are not like the future

      The calibration window is a calm quarter; the next one contains a market shock or Ramadan seasonality. Coverage is 90% on paper and 75% in use.

      Spot itTrack realised coverage on recent cases weekly. Compare it with the target. A persistent gap is the alarm.
    • Reading marginal coverage as coverage for every customer

      "90% of ranges are right" is said as though it applied to each segment. Small business or new-to-bank customers may be badly under-covered.

      Spot itReport coverage by segment, by range width, and by amount band. If it differs widely, use a difficulty-aware score or separate calibration per group.
    • Tiny calibration sets, extreme confidence levels

      99% ranges from 80 calibration cases.

      Spot itCheck that n(1−α) is comfortably above, say, 10. Otherwise the range is the maximum score or infinite.
    • Calibration leakage

      The calibration set was also used for tuning or early stopping, or contains the same customers as training. Coverage is optimistic.

      Spot itAsk which data trained, tuned and calibrated, and whether customers repeat across them.
    • A guaranteed range so wide it is useless

      The guarantee holds; the model is just poor. "Between AED 2m and AED 40m" is valid and no help.

      Spot itAlways show the average range width beside coverage and compare it to the naive baseline's width.
    • Treating a propensity-to-buy score as a causal effect

      Calling the likeliest buyers wastes money on sure things.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what? If there is no untreated comparison group, the score is not an effect.
    • Unobserved confounding behind a sophisticated estimator

      The model is cross-fitted, orthogonal and still wrong, because relationship-manager judgement drove the offers and is not in the data.

      Spot itAsk how treatment was assigned. Check that a variable that should not matter (a placebo outcome from before the offer) shows no "effect".
    • No overlap

      Treated customers occupy a region of the feature space where no untreated customers exist. The estimator extrapolates silently.

      Spot itPlot the estimated propensity for treated and untreated. If the untreated never reach high values, effects there are guesses.
    • Chasing heterogeneity in noise

      With 400 customers and 30 features, a causal forest will find a "high responder" segment that is mostly luck.

      Spot itLook at the confidence intervals on individual effects, test on held-out data, and confirm the top-ranked group in a fresh randomised test.
    • Bad nuisance models, no cross-fitting

      The ML adjustment overfits and absorbs the effect, or the same rows train and score.

      Spot itCheck that residuals are out-of-fold, and compare the estimate under two different nuisance learners. Large differences are a warning.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our forecast says AED 12.4 million outflow tomorrow. What Treasury needs is a range, and I can give one with a promise attached: we check how wrong the model was on recent cases it hadn't seen, and size the range so that nine days in ten the truth falls inside. We monitor whether that is still true. Separately, for the marketing campaign, the question isn't who is likely to take the offer but who takes it because we called. I'd test that on a small random hold-out before we spend on the full list.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For ranges I'd use split conformal on a time-ordered calibration set, with a normalised or quantile-based score so widths track difficulty, and report marginal and segment-level coverage together with average width. Under drift I'd monitor realised coverage and consider weighted or adaptive variants. For treatment effects I'd use cross-fitted DML or a causal forest with overlap diagnostics and a placebo check, treat the output as a hypothesis, and validate the targeted group in a randomised hold-out. Neither fixes unobserved confounding.”
    “Is a conformal 90% range the same as a Bayesian credible interval?”

    No. A credible interval is only as good as the model and prior behind it. A conformal range makes a frequency statement that holds whatever the model, provided the data are exchangeable. The price: it is marginal, not tailored to each customer, and it says nothing about the model's internal beliefs.

    “Why can't we guarantee 90% for every customer type?”

    For an individual case there is only one outcome, so a guarantee for it alone would need assumptions about the data. Conditional guarantees are impossible in general without them. We can approach it with scores that reflect difficulty, and we can audit by segment. We guarantee the average, and measure the rest.

    “What happens if the customer base changes?”

    The guarantee relies on new data resembling calibration data. If only the mix of customers changes, reweighting restores it, at the cost of effective sample size. If behaviour changes, we need fresh calibration data or an online method that adjusts the miss rate. Either way we track realised coverage so we see trouble early.

    “Our propensity model already ranks customers. Why do we need a causal model?”

    Because it answers a different question. Propensity says who will buy. We want who buys because of our action. The top-propensity group is full of customers who would buy anyway, so contacting them has a cost and little gain. In simulation, targeting by effect earns many times more per contact than targeting by size.

    “Can double ML prove the offer worked?”

    It can give a good estimate if the customer features we adjust for capture why customers were chosen and there is overlap. It cannot rule out hidden reasons. It is strong evidence for prioritising, not proof. A randomised hold-out is the proof, and it is cheap to hold out a small slice of the list.

    “Do these methods satisfy the regulator?”

    I am not aware of a CBUAE or SAMA requirement that names either method, and I would not claim one. What they offer a validator is a checkable property: a coverage number that can be monitored, and an effect estimate with a randomised cross-check. I'd confirm expectations with model risk and compliance.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You have 99 calibration errors and want a 90% range (α = 0.1). Which error do you use as the half-width? What if you only had 9?

    k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉ = ⌈100 × 0.9⌉ = 90, so the 90th smallest error. With n = 9, k = ⌈10 × 0.9⌉ = 9: the largest error, which is the best you can do. With n = 8, k = ⌈9 × 0.9⌉ = 9 > 8, so no finite range gives the guarantee.

    2. A range is "90% conformal" and covers 90% overall, but only 70% for new-to-bank customers. Is the method broken?

    No. The guarantee is marginal. The remedy is a score that reflects difficulty, such as normalised errors or CQR, or a separate calibration for that group (which needs enough cases in it), and reporting coverage by segment.

    3. Which assumption does split conformal need, and when does a bank typically break it?

    Exchangeability of calibration and future cases. It is broken by seasonality (Ramadan, salary-day cycles), changes in customer mix, new products, and adversaries who adapt, such as fraudsters.

    4. After a shift, the effective calibration size in the weighted method falls from 400 to 60. What does that tell you?

    A few calibration cases carry most of the weight, so the estimated quantile is noisy and the realised coverage will vary a lot from set to set. The shift is too large for the old data to stand in for the new. Gather recent data.

    5. In DML, why take residuals of both the outcome and the treatment?

    To remove everything the customer's features already predict about each. What remains in the treatment residual is variation in treatment not explained by features, which behaves as if randomly assigned. Regressing outcome residual on it recovers the effect, and the construction is insensitive to small errors in the nuisance models.

    6. What does cross-fitting prevent?

    The nuisance model memorising a customer's own outcome and then removing part of the true signal when predicting it. With out-of-fold predictions the residuals are honest, and standard errors remain valid even with very flexible learners.

    7. Treated customers spend AED 150 more than untreated, but DML estimates AED 45. What is the likely explanation, and what could still make 45 wrong?

    The offer was given to bigger customers, who spend more anyway; the naive gap mixes selection with effect. DML removes the part explained by observed features. It could still be wrong if an unobserved driver (RM judgement, a recent salary change) influenced both who got the offer and spend, or if overlap is poor.

    8. A causal forest says the top decile of customers has an effect of AED 120. How do you decide whether to believe it?

    Check the interval around that estimate, compare with an out-of-sample test of ranking quality (does the predicted top decile really show a larger gap in held-out data?), and then run a randomised test on that decile before scaling. Choosing the best-looking group from many guarantees some optimism.
