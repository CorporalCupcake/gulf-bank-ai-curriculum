    Conformal Prediction and Causal ML: Honest Ranges and Who Actually Responds

    Two ideas that turn a model's output into something you can defend. Conformal prediction wraps any model in a range with a coverage guarantee. Double machine learning and causal forests ask a different question: not who will buy, but who buys because we asked.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceConformal prediction turns any model's point estimate into a range that contains the truth a stated share of the time, by checking how wrong the model was on data it did not train on; double ML strips out what the model can already predict so that what is left isolates a treatment's effect, and lets you estimate that effect customer by customer.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two requests land in the same week.

    • Treasury gets a forecast of AED 12.4 million net outflow at a branch cluster and asks: how wrong could that be? A single number invites false confidence. They need a range to size reserves against, and "90%" must really mean nine times in ten.
    • Retail Marketing wants to call the top 30% of customers by likelihood to take a personal finance top-up. Those customers would have taken it anyway, and each call costs AED 18. The ones worth calling sit in the middle, but the score answers "who will buy", not "who will buy because of the call".

    These are the two halves of the lesson.

    • Conformal prediction (R8.1) answers "how wrong could this be?" without trusting the model. Hold data back, measure how far off the model was, and use those past misses to size the range. The guarantee comes from fair counting, not from the model being right.
    • Double ML and heterogeneous effects (R8.2) answer "what does acting change, and for whom?". ML does the prediction parts; a small, careful step does the causal part.
    Hold onto thisA model gives a guess. Conformal gives the guess an error bar that holds up in a meeting. Causal ML gives the effect of your action, a different number from the guess. Both rest on checks you can run, not on how clever the model is.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Conformal prediction: ranges with a guarantee

    Think of a tailor who checks how far off past fittings were, then pads tomorrow's estimate by that typical miss. Split conformal prediction does this in four steps, whatever the model.

    1. Train any model: neural net, boosted trees, a vendor black box.
    2. Calibrate. On n unseen cases, compute a nonconformity score, how surprising the truth was. For a forecast: |actual − predicted|.
    3. Take a quantile. Sort the scores and pick position k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉; call it q. For α = 0.1 and n = 99 that is the 90th smallest.
    4. Predict. Return prediction ± q: every answer whose score would be at most q.
    P(Y∈C(X))≥1−α

    C(X) is the returned range; α is the miss rate you accept (0.1 for "90%"). The guarantee is marginal: averaged over new cases and calibration draws. It needs only exchangeability: shuffle calibration and new cases together and nobody can tell which is which. No assumption about the model or the error shape.

    Reading it. A 90% range means about 9 in 10 future actuals land inside; the guarantee says nothing about how wide the range is. Width is the model's quality: a poor model gives a valid but useless range. A single constant width also ignores that some cases are harder. Simulation 1 shows both.

    Why it works. Put the new case's score with the n calibration scores: n+1 exchangeable scores. The new one is equally likely to rank anywhere, so it lands in the top α share with probability at most α. The "+1" is the new case counting itself. Pure counting, so no distribution needed.

    Split conformal guarantees marginal coverage: right on average across all cases, not conditional coverage for each kind of case. Like a hospital with 90% survival overall that is far worse for the sickest patients. Conditional coverage cannot be guaranteed in general (a theorem). You get close with a score that reflects difficulty:

    • Normalised scores. Divide the error by an estimate of local spread, as in the simulation.
    • Conformalised quantile regression (CQR). Train 5th and 95th percentile models (F7.2), then calibrate the gap so the guarantee holds. Ranges widen and narrow with the case, even if the quantile model is wrong.
    • Classification sets. Return a set of labels, such as {"complaint: unauthorised access", "complaint: card issue"}, sized so the truth is inside 95% of the time. A clear case gets one label; a confusing one gets several, meaning "send to a human".

    The coverage you actually got from one calibration set is also random, especially when the set is small.

    Rule of thumb: a 90% range needs at least a few hundred calibration cases. A 99% range (regulator-style tail) needs many more, since the 99th percentile of 100 scores is just the largest. With too few cases the method honestly returns an infinite range.

    When data shift: weighted and adaptive conformal

    Exchangeability is the assumption that matters, and bank data break it often: Ramadan deposit behaviour, a new product's customer mix, adapting fraudsters. Like a weather forecast calibrated on summer and used in winter. Two repairs exist.

    • Covariate shift: reweight. If only the customer mix (inputs X) changes and the input-outcome relationship holds, weight each calibration case by w(x) = new density ÷ old density. This is weighted conformal prediction. You estimate the ratio with a classifier that tells old data from new; it fails if the ratio is extreme.
    • Drift over time: adapt. Adaptive conformal inference watches recent misses and nudges α online. If the last 100 days missed 15% against a 10% target, it widens the range. It guarantees the long-run miss rate, not per-day coverage, even for arbitrary data.

    Reweighting fixes only covariate shift. If the relationship itself changes (a new fraud tactic, a rate change altering repayment), you need fresh labelled data or the online approach. Uneven weights also shrink the effective sample, so a bank that shifts a lot needs a bigger calibration set, not a smaller one.

    Double ML: isolating the effect of an action

    Suppose some customers got a pre-approved top-up offer (T = 1) and others did not (T = 0), and you observe next-quarter spend Y. Marketing picked wealthier, more active customers, so comparing averages credits the offer with spend that was coming anyway. That is confounding (F2.6). Like judging a gym by its members' fitness. A randomised experiment (F3.6) is the clean fix; with only history, you adjust for observed features X.

    A regression adjustment breaks if the X-to-spend relationship is nonlinear. A plain ML adjustment adds its own bias (regularisation shrinks the wrong things). Double/debiased ML fixes this in four steps.

    1. Predict the outcome from X alone: m(x) = expected spend for this kind of customer.
    2. Predict the treatment from X: e(x) = chance this kind of customer gets the offer (the propensity).
    3. Take residuals: actual spend − m(x), and T − e(x). What is left after removing what features explain.
    4. Regress residual spend on residual treatment. The slope is the offer's effect.
    τ^=∑(T−e^)(Y−m^)∑(T−e^)2

    This is Frisch-Waugh (F2.5): strip X out of both T and Y, then look at what remains. τ̂ = AED of extra spend per offer; 45 means "an offer adds about AED 45", not "treated customers spend 45 more". The nuisance models m̂ and ê can be any ML method, because the formula is Neyman-orthogonal: small errors in them have only a second-order effect on τ̂.

    "Double" means two prediction models. "Debiased" means orthogonality plus cross-fitting: each customer's nuisance predictions come from models trained on other customers, never themselves. Otherwise an overfitted model quietly eats part of the signal.

    Heterogeneous effects. The average effect hides who responds, so let it depend on features: τ(x). Two approaches dominate.

    • Meta-learners build effect estimators from ordinary ML models. S-learner: one model with treatment as a feature. T-learner: separate treated and untreated models, subtracted. X-, R- and DR-learners add corrections for group imbalance. The R-learner is DML with a flexible final stage.
    • Causal forests are random forests whose splits separate customers with different effects, not different outcomes. The "honest" variant picks splits on one half of the data and estimates effects on the other, giving valid confidence intervals for τ(x).

    Simulation 4 uses one feature and a straight-line effect so every part is visible.

    Reading the result. "Who is likely to spend" is a different ranking from "who spends more because of the offer". The best targets often sit mid-distribution: not already sold, not hopeless. Uplift modelling (B6.2) rests on the same logic.

    What DML does not fix. Unobserved confounders: if something driving both offer and spend is missing from X (a relationship manager's hunch), the estimate is biased however fancy the model. It also needs overlap: similar customers must appear in both groups. If the rules always offered to anyone above AED 50,000, there is nobody untreated to compare the rich with.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Calibrating on data unlike the future

      The calibration window is a calm quarter; the next has a market shock or Ramadan. Coverage is 90% on paper, 75% in use.

      Spot itTrack realised coverage on recent cases weekly. A persistent gap against target is the alarm.
    • Reading marginal coverage as coverage for every customer

      "90% of ranges are right" does not mean each segment gets 90%. Small-business or new-to-bank customers may be badly under-covered.

      Spot itReport coverage by segment, range width and amount band. If it differs widely, use a difficulty-aware score or calibrate per group.
    • Tiny calibration sets, extreme confidence levels

      A 99% range from 80 calibration cases.

      Spot itCheck n(1−α) is comfortably above about 10. Otherwise the range is the maximum score or infinite.
    • Calibration leakage

      The calibration set was also used for tuning or early stopping, or shares customers with training. Coverage looks too good.

      Spot itAsk which data trained, tuned and calibrated, and whether customers repeat across them.
    • A guaranteed range so wide it is useless

      The guarantee holds; the model is poor. "Between AED 2m and AED 40m" is valid and no help.

      Spot itShow average width beside coverage and compare with a naive baseline's width.
    • Treating a propensity-to-buy score as a causal effect

      Calling the likeliest buyers wastes money on sure things.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what? With no untreated comparison group, the score is not an effect.
    • Unobserved confounding behind a sophisticated estimator

      The model is cross-fitted and orthogonal, and still wrong, because relationship-manager judgement drove the offers and is not in the data.

      Spot itAsk how treatment was assigned. Run a placebo outcome from before the offer; it should show no "effect".
    • No overlap

      Treated customers sit where no untreated ones exist, so the estimator extrapolates silently.

      Spot itPlot estimated propensity for treated and untreated. If untreated never reach high values, effects there are guesses.
    • Chasing heterogeneity in noise

      With 400 customers and 30 features, a causal forest will find a "high responder" segment that is mostly luck.

      Spot itCheck intervals on individual effects, test on held-out data, and confirm the top group in a fresh randomised test.
    • Bad nuisance models, no cross-fitting

      The ML adjustment overfits and absorbs the effect, or the same rows train and score.

      Spot itCheck residuals are out-of-fold, and compare estimates under two different nuisance learners. Large differences are a warning.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. You have 99 calibration errors and want a 90% range (α = 0.1). Which error is the half-width? What if you had only 9?

    k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉ = ⌈100 × 0.9⌉ = 90, so the 90th smallest error. With n = 9, k = 9: the largest error, the best you can do. With n = 8, k = 9 > 8, so no finite range gives the guarantee.

    2. A "90% conformal" range covers 90% overall but only 70% for new-to-bank customers. Is the method broken?

    No. The guarantee is marginal. Fix it with a difficulty-aware score (normalised errors, CQR) or separate calibration for that group (with enough cases), and report coverage by segment.

    3. Which assumption does split conformal need, and when does a bank typically break it?

    Exchangeability of calibration and future cases. Seasonality (Ramadan, salary-day cycles), customer-mix changes, new products and adaptive fraudsters all break it.

    4. After a shift, the effective calibration size in the weighted method falls from 400 to 60. What does that tell you?

    A few cases carry most of the weight, so the quantile is noisy and realised coverage will swing from set to set. The shift is too big for old data to stand in for new. Gather recent data.

    5. In DML, why take residuals of both the outcome and the treatment?

    To remove everything the features already predict about each. The treatment residual is the variation features cannot explain, which behaves as if random. Regressing the outcome residual on it recovers the effect, and small errors in the nuisance models barely matter.

    6. What does cross-fitting prevent?

    The nuisance model memorising a customer's own outcome and removing part of the true signal. Out-of-fold predictions keep residuals honest, so standard errors stay valid even with flexible learners.

    7. Treated customers spend AED 150 more than untreated, but DML estimates AED 45. Why, and what could still make 45 wrong?

    The offer went to bigger customers, who spend more anyway; the naive gap mixes selection with effect. DML removes the part explained by observed features. It is still wrong if an unobserved driver (RM judgement, a recent salary change) affected both the offer and spend, or if overlap is poor.

    8. A causal forest says the top decile has an effect of AED 120. Do you believe it?

    Check the interval, test ranking quality out of sample (does the predicted top decile show a bigger gap in held-out data?), then run a randomised test on that decile before scaling. Picking the best-looking group from many guarantees some optimism.
