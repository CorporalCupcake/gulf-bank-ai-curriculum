    Conformal Prediction and Causal ML: Honest Ranges and Who Actually Responds

    Two ways to make a model's output defensible. Conformal prediction gives a range with a coverage guarantee. Causal ML asks who buys because we asked, not who will buy.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceConformal prediction wraps any model in a range that holds a stated share of the time; double ML isolates the effect of an action, customer by customer.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Treasury gets a forecast of AED 12.4 million net outflow and asks how wrong it could be. A single number invites false confidence, and they need a range where "90%" really means nine times in ten. That is conformal prediction: hold data back, measure how far off the model was, and size the range from those misses.

    Retail Marketing wants to call the customers most likely to take a top-up. Many would have taken it anyway. A score says who will buy, not who buys because of the call. That is double ML.

    Hold onto thisA model gives a guess. Conformal gives it an error bar. Causal ML gives the effect of your action, which is a different number from the guess.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Conformal prediction: ranges with a guarantee

    Think of a tailor who checks how far off past fittings were, then pads tomorrow's estimate by the typical miss. Split conformal prediction does this in four steps, whatever the model. Train any model: a neural net, boosted trees, a vendor black box. Hold back some cases it has not seen (the calibration set) and score each by how surprising the truth was, for a forecast simply the absolute error. Sort those scores and pick the one that sits at the 90th percentile, nudged up slightly to allow for the new case itself. Finally return the prediction plus or minus that value.

    A 90% range means about 9 in 10 future actuals land inside. The only assumption is exchangeability: new cases look like the calibration cases, so nobody could tell them apart if you shuffled them together. There is no assumption about the model or the shape of the errors. The reason it works is plain counting. The new case's score is equally likely to rank anywhere among the calibration scores, so it lands in the worst 10% only about 10% of the time.

    The guarantee says nothing about width. Width is the model's quality: a poor model gives a valid but useless range. It also holds on average, not for every kind of case, like a hospital with 90% survival overall that is far worse for the sickest patients. This is called marginal coverage, against conditional coverage for each kind of case, and the second cannot be guaranteed in general. Simulation 1 shows the gap.

    You get closer to per-case coverage by choosing a score that reflects difficulty.

    • Normalised scores. Divide the error by an estimate of local spread, as in Simulation 1. The guarantee still holds even if that estimate is rough; a better estimate just gives tighter ranges.
    • Conformalised quantile regression (CQR). Train models for the 5th and 95th percentiles, then calibrate the gap so the guarantee holds. Ranges widen and narrow with the case, even if the quantile model is wrong.
    • Classification sets. Return a set of labels, such as {"complaint: unauthorised access", "complaint: card issue"}, sized so the truth is inside 95% of the time. A clear case gets one label; a confusing one gets several, which is a natural signal to send it to a human.

    The coverage you get from any one calibration set is itself random, as Simulation 2 shows. As a rule, a 90% range wants at least a few hundred calibration cases. A 99% range needs far more, because the 99th percentile of 100 scores is just the largest. With too few cases the method honestly returns an infinite range. The calibration data must be separate from anything used to train or tune the model, otherwise coverage looks better than it is.

    When data shift

    Bank data break exchangeability often: Ramadan deposits, a new product's customer mix, adapting fraudsters. It is like a forecast calibrated on summer and used in winter. Two repairs exist, for two different kinds of change.

    Covariate shift means only the customer mix changes, while the link between inputs and outcome holds. Here you reweight: give each calibration case a weight equal to how much more likely it is in the new mix than the old one. This is weighted conformal prediction. You estimate the weights with a classifier that tells old data from new, so they carry error, and extreme weights are a warning sign. Uneven weights also shrink the effective sample, so a bank that shifts a lot needs a bigger calibration set, not a smaller one.

    Drift over time means behaviour keeps moving. Adaptive conformal inference watches recent misses and adjusts the miss rate online. If the last 100 days missed 15% against a 10% target, it widens the range. It guarantees the long-run miss rate, not coverage on any given day, and works even for arbitrary data.

    Neither fixes a changed relationship, such as a new fraud tactic or a rate change altering repayment. That needs fresh labelled data. In every case, track realised coverage on recent cases: a persistent gap against target is the alarm.

    Double ML: isolating the effect of an action

    Marketing offered a top-up to wealthier, more active customers, so comparing treated and untreated credits the offer with spend that was coming anyway. That is confounding, like judging a gym by its members' fitness. A randomised test is the clean fix. With only history, you adjust for observed customer features.

    A plain regression adjustment breaks if the feature-to-spend link is nonlinear, and a plain ML adjustment adds its own bias because regularisation shrinks the wrong things. Double (debiased) ML works in four steps. Predict spend from the features. Predict who gets the offer from the features (the propensity). Take the leftovers in each: actual spend minus predicted, and offer minus predicted chance. Then regress leftover spend on leftover offer. The slope is the offer's effect in AED, so 45 means "an offer adds about AED 45", not "treated customers spend 45 more".

    "Double" means two prediction models, and they can be any ML method. That is safe because the final step is insensitive to small errors in them (the technical word is Neyman-orthogonal). "Debiased" adds cross-fitting: each customer is scored by models that never saw them, so overfitting cannot absorb the signal.

    The average effect hides who responds, so you let it depend on features. Two approaches dominate. Meta-learners build effect estimators from ordinary ML models: an S-learner fits one model with the offer as a feature, a T-learner fits separate treated and untreated models and subtracts them, and the R-learner is double ML with a flexible final stage. Causal forests are random forests whose splits separate customers with different effects, not different outcomes. The "honest" variant picks splits on one half of the data and estimates effects on the other, which gives valid confidence intervals. The best targets often sit mid-distribution: not already sold, not hopeless.

    Double ML cannot fix unobserved confounders, such as a relationship manager's hunch. It also needs overlap: similar customers must appear in both groups, or the estimator extrapolates silently.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Conformal prediction is strong at:

    • Model-agnostic guarantees. It only looks at the model's errors on held-out data, so it wraps a neural net, a boosted tree or a vendor API equally well.
    • Few assumptions. No normal-errors assumption and no correct-model assumption. Exchangeability is the one thing you need to defend.
    • Cheap to add. It needs one calibration pass and a sort. No retraining, which suits models you do not own.
    • Honest uncertainty in classification. Label sets flag confusing cases for review rather than forcing a single answer.

    Conformal prediction is weak at, or wrong when:

    • The data drift. The guarantee is only as good as exchangeability. Regime changes quietly break it.
    • You need per-segment coverage. The guarantee is on average. A small segment can be badly under-covered unless you calibrate per group or use difficulty-aware scores.
    • The model is poor. You get a valid range that is too wide to use. Conformal cannot fix a bad model.
    • You have very little labelled data or want an extreme level such as 99.9%.

    Causal ML is strong at separating the effect of an action from selection, using flexible models for the adjustment, and finding who responds. It is weak when a randomised test is cheap (run the test), when key drivers of treatment are unobserved, when overlap is poor, or when the sample is small relative to the number of features.

    In practice. For ranges, start with split conformal around your existing model, using 500 to 2,000 calibration cases, and report coverage and width by segment before anything fancier. Move to CQR or normalised scores if width varies a lot by case. The key knob is the miss rate α: pick it from the cost of being wrong, not habit. Compute is trivial. For effects, confirm the data can answer the question first: check how the offer was assigned and plot overlap. Use double ML with gradient-boosted nuisance models and cross-fitting, and compare against a second learner. It wants thousands of customers, and heterogeneity wants many more. Always validate the chosen group in a randomised test.

    In banking, conformal ranges suit treasury cash forecasts, loss and exposure estimates from vendor models, and routing in complaint or KYC classification where a multi-label set means "ask a human". Causal ML suits campaign targeting, pricing and limit-increase decisions, and measuring whether a collections nudge really works.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Calibrating on data unlike the future

      A calm calibration quarter, then a market shock or Ramadan. Coverage is 90% on paper, 75% in use.

      Spot itTrack realised coverage weekly.
    • Reading average coverage as coverage for everyone

      New-to-bank customers may be badly under-covered even at 90% overall.

      Spot itReport coverage and width by segment.
    • A valid range so wide it is useless

      "Between AED 2m and AED 40m" holds and helps nobody.

      Spot itShow width beside coverage, against a naive baseline.
    • Treating a propensity score as an effect

      Calling the likeliest buyers wastes money on sure things.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what?
    • Tiny calibration sets, extreme confidence

      A 99% range from 80 calibration cases is the largest error or infinite.

      Spot itCheck the calibration size times the miss rate is comfortably above about 10.
    • Chasing heterogeneity in noise

      With 400 customers and 30 features, a causal forest finds a "high responder" segment that is mostly luck.

      Spot itTest on held-out data, and check intervals on effects.
    • Hidden confounding or no overlap

      A sophisticated estimator is still wrong if the real driver of the offer is not in the data.

      Spot itAsk how treatment was assigned, and confirm winners in a fresh randomised test.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A "90% conformal" range covers 90% overall but 70% for new-to-bank customers. Is the method broken?

    No. The guarantee is on average. Use a difficulty-aware score or calibrate per group, and report coverage by segment.

    2. What does conformal assume, and when does a bank break it?

    That future cases look like the calibration cases. Seasonality, customer-mix changes, new products and adaptive fraudsters all break it.

    3. Why take leftovers of both outcome and treatment in double ML?

    It removes what the features already explain about each. What remains of the treatment behaves as if random, so relating the leftovers isolates the effect.

    4. Treated customers spend AED 150 more, but double ML says AED 45. Why, and what could still make 45 wrong?

    The offer went to bigger customers who spend more anyway. It is still wrong if an unobserved driver affected both offer and spend, or overlap is poor.

    5. A causal forest says the top decile responds by AED 120. Do you believe it?

    Not yet. Test it on held-out data, then run a randomised test on that group. Picking the best-looking group from many guarantees some optimism.

    6. Treasury wants a 90% range around a vendor's cash forecast, with 400 labelled past days. Would you choose conformal or quantile regression, and why?

    Conformal. You do not own the vendor model, so you cannot retrain it, and conformal needs only its predictions and the 400 errors. Quantile regression would need your own model and gives no guarantee. If widths vary a lot by day, calibrate a normalised score.

    7. Marketing can run a two-week randomised test, but wants you to use double ML on last year's data instead. Which would you choose and why?

    The randomised test, if two weeks is acceptable. Randomisation removes confounding by design, while double ML relies on every driver of the offer being recorded. Use the history for planning and to choose whom to test, then confirm in the experiment.
