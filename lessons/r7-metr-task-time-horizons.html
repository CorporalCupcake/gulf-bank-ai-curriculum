    How Long a Task Can AI Finish? METR Time Horizons and Frontier Benchmarks

    Why "how long a job a person would need" has become the most quoted yardstick for AI agents, what the steady doubling really says (and does not), and how to read ARC-AGI, Humanity's Last Exam and SWE-bench without being fooled by a headline score.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMETR measures AI by the length of task, in human working time, that a model completes about half the time, and that length has doubled every few months; benchmark scores near 100% stop telling you anything, so read every score with its ceiling, its error bars and the reliability level you actually need.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Ask about a new analyst and you hear: "She can take a two-hour ticket off my plate, but not a month-long migration yet." That is trust measured in length of task, not a percentage.

    Most AI benchmarks fix a question set and report the share correct. Like a school exam everyone aces, the score hits the ceiling and stops moving while models keep improving. It also hides whether the agent can do the whole job or only a slice.

    METR (a non-profit that evaluates frontier models) proposed another yardstick in 2025. Give a model tasks of many lengths, record how long a skilled human takes on each, and plot success against that human time. Short tasks are nearly always solved, long ones almost never. The length where success crosses 50% is the 50% time horizon.

    The unit is concrete (minutes and hours of professional work), and across successive models the horizon grew exponentially, doubling every few months. R7.2 covers the fixed-question benchmarks (ARC-AGI, Humanity's Last Exam, SWE-bench) and why each saturates.

    Hold onto thisA horizon means "at 50% reliability, on this kind of task, measured in human time". A 50% horizon of 10 hours does not mean "can do a 10-hour job unsupervised". It means it gets about half of such jobs right, with nobody checking. Like a bowler who hits 50% of strikes: fine for a game, not for a job where every miss costs money.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R7.1 · From pass/fail results to a time horizon

    The method (Kwa et al., 2025) has four steps.

    1. Build a task suite with human times. Software, cybersecurity and reasoning tasks from seconds to many hours. Timed skilled people set each task's length.
    2. Run each model many times in an agent harness (tools, a shell, room to iterate). An automatic check scores each attempt pass or fail.
    3. Fit a logistic curve of success against the logarithm of human time.
    4. Read off where the curve crosses 50%. That is the 50% horizon. Reading at 80% gives the much shorter 80% horizon.

    In symbols, with task length t:

    P(success|t)=11+(t/h50)k

    h50 is the 50% horizon: at t = h50 success is exactly one half. k is the steepness: high means a sharp cliff from "reliably solves" to "reliably fails", low means a gentle slope. Same logistic shape as F2.5. The 80% horizon is h80 = h50 × (1/4)1/k.

    Reading a horizon. Higher reliability pulls you back along the curve. At typical steepness the 80% horizon is about a fifth of the 50% one, and the 95% horizon shorter still. A bank that wants an agent to run unattended needs the 95%+ horizon, a small fraction of the headline.

    "Human time" means a skilled professional who already has the context. A new hire would take longer. Models did worse on messier tasks with unclear scoring or heavy context dependence. So a horizon from well-specified software tasks does not transfer one-for-one to an AML investigation needing judgement and ten internal systems.

    R7.1 · The doubling trend, and what extrapolation does

    Plot each frontier model's 50% horizon against release date, horizon on a log scale, and the points line up. Like compound interest, a fixed doubling time means growth that looks slow, then sudden.

    • March 2025 analysis: doubling about every seven months over 2019 to 2025, from seconds in 2019 to about an hour in early 2025.
    • Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026): roughly 131 days (about four months) for post-2023 models, shorter still on 2024 onward.

    The number depends on which dates you fit. If the horizon doubles every d months from h0:

    h(m)=h0·2m/d

    m is months from now. Reaching target H takes d × log2(H/h0) months. One hour to a 40-hour week is log2 40 ≈ 5.3 doublings: about 21 months at a 4-month doubling, 37 at 7. Small changes in doubling time swing the date by years, so never quote a single date.

    Three caveats on the line:

    • It is a fit to a handful of frontier models, so uncertainty is wide.
    • METR says the suite is running out of room: the strongest models pass most tasks, so the top of the scale rests on few long tasks with wide error bars.
    • It says how long a task a model can complete, not whether the work is useful or safe unchecked. It measures one dimension, not general ability.

    R7.2 · What the frontier benchmarks test, and what saturation means

    Fixed-question benchmarks still headline most launches. Three matter.

    BenchmarkWhat it testsFormatKnown weaknesses
    SWE-bench (Verified)Fixing real GitHub issues in open-source Python repos. Hidden unit tests decide.500 human-vetted tasks (Verified); original ~2,300.Public repos models may have seen; one language; weak or over-specific tests; scores depend on the scaffold. Newer variants (e.g. SWE-bench Pro) exist because Verified nearly saturated.
    Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)Expert-written, closed-ended academic questions, built to be hard for models.About 2,500 questions, exact-match and multiple choice, plus a private held-out set.Disputed key: reviews estimated a sizeable minority of chemistry and biology answers are wrong; a re-verified version exists.     ARC-AGI (1, 2, 3)Novel puzzles: infer a rule from a few coloured-grid examples and apply it. Resists memorisation.     Saturation means top models score so near the maximum that gaps between them are smaller than the noise. A test everyone gets 98% on can't tell the best student from the second best. It happens three ways:

    • The ceiling is not 100%. If 3% of the key is wrong, no model can honestly exceed about 97%. Above that, the model learned the key's mistakes.
    • Error bars swallow the gap. On a few hundred questions, 92% and 94% are statistically indistinguishable. (Statistics for evals goes deeper.)
    • Contamination and overfitting. If questions appeared in training data, or teams tune against the public set, the score measures exposure as well as ability.

    Expect months to a couple of years from release to near-ceiling, then a new benchmark.

    Mid-curve, labs report big gains; near the ceiling, they quietly switch benchmarks. Ask: where on the curve are we, how big is the key's error, and how many questions separate the models?

    The two families differ. A benchmark asks "what fraction of this fixed set?" and saturates by construction. A horizon asks "how long a task, at fixed reliability?" and keeps growing while you can build longer tasks. Those are costly to build and grade, which is why METR is shifting to new evaluations.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most mistakes come from dropping a qualifier.

    • Reading a 50% horizon as "can do the job"

      "The model handles 8-hour tasks." Like a driver who passes half their tests, it also fails often on 4-hour jobs, and nobody notices without a check.

      Spot itAsk for the reliability level and the 80% or 95% horizon. If none, divide the headline by 5 to 10.
    • Confusing the unit

      The horizon is human time for the task, not model run time. A model may finish a "2-hour" task in 5 minutes, or loop for three hours and fail.

      Spot itAsk "two hours of whose time?" and check the baseline's context assumptions.
    • Extrapolating one line for years

      "In 24 months it does month-long projects." Uncertainty in doubling time, start point and suite compounds exponentially.

      Spot itAsk for a range, not a date, and whether a slowdown scenario is included.
    • Assuming software tasks transfer to yours

      The suite is mostly well-scored coding and reasoning. Messy, judgement-heavy work is harder to measure and models do worse on it.

      Spot itAsk whether the tasks match yours in clarity of success criteria and context.
    • Quoting a score without its ceiling

      "95% on an HLE-style set" when the key has errors, or 94% and 95% are within noise.

      Spot itAsk for label error and question count. Standard error: √(p(1−p)/n).
    • Comparing scores from different harnesses

      Two labs report SWE-bench with different scaffolds, tools, retries and compute.

      Spot itCheck for an independent verified run. Treat self-reported scores as upper bounds.
    • Contamination and tuning to the public set

      The model has seen the questions, or the team iterated against them.

      Spot itLook for a private held-out score and a large public-private gap.
    • Treating benchmark gains as productivity gains

      In METR's 2025 randomised trial, experienced open-source developers on their own repositories were 19% slower with early-2025 AI tools while believing they were faster.

      Spot itAsk for a field result with real users on the real process before quoting savings.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the Part 2 formulas.

    1. A model's 50% horizon is 4 hours and k = 1. What task length gives 80% success, and what is the success rate on a 16-hour task?

    80% horizon = 4 × (1/4)1 = 1 hour. At 16 hours, t/h = 4, so P = 1/(1 + 4) = 20%.

    2. Starting from a 1-hour horizon with a 5-month doubling time, when does it reach 32 hours?

    32 = 25, so five doublings: 25 months. At 7 months it is 35, a 10-month gap from a 2-month change.

    3. Why does a time horizon of "8 hours" not mean an agent can replace a person for a working day?

    50% means it fails about half the time; the baseline assumes a skilled person with full context; the tasks are mostly well-specified software; and review, handoffs and judgement are not measured.

    4. A benchmark has 300 questions. Two models score 90% and 92%. Are they different?

    Each standard error is √(0.9 × 0.1 / 300) ≈ 1.7 points, so the 2-point gap has a standard error of about 2.3 (unpaired). The gap is under one standard error: you cannot say. A paired comparison helps, but 300 items is few.

    5. A test has 4% wrong answer keys. A model scores 97%. What does that suggest?

    The honest maximum is about 96%. Above it, the model is reproducing the key's mistakes, a flag for contamination.

    6. Why can a time-horizon measure outlast a fixed benchmark, and what limits it?

    A fixed benchmark has a maximum score; task length has none, so you can add longer tasks. The limit is cost: long tasks are expensive to design, run and grade, so the top end becomes statistically thin.

    7. What is the difference between METR's field trial and its horizon measurement?

    The horizon measures model success on prepared, auto-scored tasks. The trial measured real developers' speed on their own work, with and without tools. Different questions: in 2025 the developers were slower, so benchmark gains do not automatically become productivity.
