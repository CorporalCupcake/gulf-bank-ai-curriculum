    How Long a Task Can AI Finish?

    Part 1

    What a time horizon is

    In one sentenceThe 50% time horizon is the length of task, measured by how long a skilled human takes, that an AI completes correctly about half the time.

    Think of a new analyst. You might say: "She can take a two-hour ticket off my plate, but not a month-long migration yet." That is trust measured in length of task, not in a percentage. METR, a research group that evaluates AI, applies the same idea to models.

    The dot is the horizon. Short tasks sit near the top, long ones near the bottom, and the point where the curve crosses 50% is the number METR reports.

    How it differs from a normal benchmark

    • A fixed benchmark (SWE-bench, Humanity's Last Exam, ARC-AGI) reports the share of one fixed question set answered correctly. Once top models score near the maximum, the number stops moving even though models keep improving. This is called saturation.
    • A time horizon has no ceiling, because you can always build longer tasks. It also uses a unit people understand: minutes and hours of professional work.
    Part 2

    How it is measured

    1. Build a task suite. Software, cybersecurity and reasoning tasks, from seconds to many hours, each with an automatic pass/fail check.
    2. Time skilled humans. People attempt the tasks, and their time sets each task's length.
    3. Run the model many times. It works inside an "agent harness": the tools, shell and retry budget it is given. Each attempt is scored pass or fail.
    4. Fit a curve. A smooth falling curve of success against the logarithm of task length. Short tasks sit near 100%, long ones near 0%.
    5. Read off the horizon. The task length where the curve crosses 50%. Reading at 80% gives a much shorter figure.

    How to read a horizon

    • A 50% horizon of 2 hours means: on tasks that take a skilled person about 2 hours, it succeeds about half the time. It does not mean "can do a 2-hour job unsupervised". Half the time it fails, and nobody notices without a check.
    • The 80% horizon is shorter, often around a quarter to a fifth of the 50% one. An agent that runs with no reviewer needs the 95% horizon, which is shorter still.
    • "Two hours" is human time, not how long the model runs. Ask "two hours of whose time?"
    • The trend: plotted against release date on a log scale, frontier horizons line up. METR's March 2025 analysis found a doubling about every seven months. Its January 2026 update found roughly four months for recent models.

    Doubling is like compound interest: slow, then sudden. Small changes in doubling time move a milestone date by years, so never quote a single date.

    When to use it

    MeasureWhat it tells youWeak at

    SWE-benchFixing real GitHub issues, judged by hidden testsPublic repos models may have seen; scores depend on the scaffold
    Humanity's Last ExamHard expert-written academic questionsSome answer keys are wrong; rewards trivia as well as reasoning
    ARC-AGINovel grid puzzles: infer a rule from a few examplesNarrow format; results depend on compute
    Your own task suiteWhether an agent can run your processCosts expert time to build and grade

    Time horizons are strong at:

    • A unit people understand. "Two hours of work" means more to a manager than "78%".
    • No fixed ceiling, so it keeps separating models after exam-style benchmarks saturate.
    • Agent-shaped work: multi-step tasks with tools, which is how agents are really used.

    They are weak at:

    • Judging your workflow. The suite is mostly well-scored software and reasoning. Messy, judgement-heavy work is under-represented, and models do worse on it.
    • Anything graded by taste, such as writing quality or client tone. With no automatic pass/fail check there is nothing to fit.
    • Safety or usefulness. Finishing a long task does not show the output was correct in context or worth the review time.

    Is it usable today? As a planning and vendor-question tool, yes. It is a research measure, not a replacement for testing on your own process: shortlist models with public numbers, then test on 50 to 200 of your own tasks graded by the people who do them now.

    In banking, treat a horizon as a ceiling on what is plausible for work like reconciliation, ticket triage or AML investigations, never as a promise.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading a 50% horizon as "can do the job"

      It fails about half of such tasks. Ask for the 80% or 95% horizon, or divide the headline by 5 to 10.

    • Extrapolating one line for years

      Doubling time is uncertain and the suite is running out of long tasks. Ask for a range, not a date.

    • Assuming software results transfer to your work

      Tidy, well-scored coding tasks are easier to measure than judgement-heavy bank work.

    • Trusting self-reported scores

      The same model scores differently with different tools, retries and compute. Treat them as upper bounds until someone independent reruns them.

    1. What does a 50% time horizon of 4 hours mean?

    On tasks a skilled human needs about 4 hours for, the model succeeds about half the time.

    2. Why is the 80% horizon shorter than the 50% horizon?

    Demanding higher reliability pulls you back along the curve to easier, shorter tasks.

    3. Why can a horizon keep growing when a fixed benchmark has saturated?

    A fixed benchmark has a ceiling at 100% (or lower if keys are wrong). Task length has no ceiling: you can always build longer tasks.

    4. Two models share a 50% horizon. What else do you ask for?

    The 80% and 95% horizons, which show how steep the cliff is. A gentle curve means erratic behaviour.

    5. Is the horizon how long the model takes to run?

    No. It is the time a skilled human needs for the same task.
