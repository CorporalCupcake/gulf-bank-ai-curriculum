    How Long a Task Can AI Finish? METR Time Horizons and Frontier Benchmarks

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Ask about a new analyst and you hear: "She can take a two-hour ticket off my plate, but not a month-long migration yet." That is trust measured in length of task, not a percentage.

    Most AI benchmarks report the share of a fixed question set answered correctly. Like an exam everyone aces, the score hits the ceiling and stops moving while models keep improving. METR instead records how long a skilled human takes on each task and plots success against that time. The length where success crosses 50% is the 50% time horizon, and across models it has doubled every few months.

    Hold onto thisA horizon means "at 50% reliability, on this kind of task, measured in human time". A 50% horizon of 10 hours does not mean "can do a 10-hour job unsupervised". It means it gets about half of such jobs right, with nobody checking. Like a bowler who hits 50% of strikes: fine for a game, not for a job where every miss costs money.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R7.1 · From pass/fail results to a time horizon

    METR builds a suite of tasks timed by skilled humans, runs each model many times in an agent harness, and scores every attempt pass or fail. It then fits a success curve against human time. Where it crosses 50% is the 50% horizon; reading at 80% gives a much shorter figure.

    Four moving parts matter. First, the task suite: software, cybersecurity and reasoning tasks from seconds to many hours, each with an automatic check. Second, the human baseline: skilled people attempt the tasks, and their time sets each task's length. Third, the agent harness: the tools, shell and retry budget the model gets. Change the harness and the horizon changes. Fourth, the fit: a logistic curve of success against the logarithm of task length, which is why short tasks sit near 100% and long ones near 0%, with a smooth slide between.

    Reading a horizon. Higher reliability pulls you back along the curve: the 80% horizon is roughly a fifth of the 50% one, and the 95% horizon is shorter still. An agent that runs unattended needs the 95%+ horizon. "Human time" means a skilled professional with context, so a horizon from tidy software tasks does not transfer one-for-one to an AML investigation.

    The steepness knob. A gentle curve (small k) means the model sometimes solves long tasks and sometimes fails short ones: erratic, with a flat tail. A sharp curve means a clear cliff. Two models with the same 50% horizon but different steepness have very different 80% horizons, so always ask for both numbers. A horizon also assumes the tasks have a clean pass/fail check. Where success is a matter of taste, the method has nothing to fit.

    R7.1 · The doubling trend, and what extrapolation does

    Plot each frontier model's 50% horizon against release date on a log scale and the points line up. A fixed doubling time is like compound interest: slow, then sudden. METR's March 2025 analysis found a doubling about every seven months; its January 2026 update found roughly four months for recent models.

    The number depends on which dates you fit, and small changes in doubling time swing a milestone date by years. Never quote a single date. Two things can bend the line. Compute, data and algorithm gains may keep paying off, or they may hit limits; and the suite's long tasks are few, so the top end rests on thin evidence. The slowdown button in the simulation shows the second possibility: both shapes fit what we have seen.

    Treat the line with care: it fits a few frontier models, the suite is running out of long tasks, and it measures task length only, not whether the work is useful or safe.

    R7.2 · What the frontier benchmarks test, and what saturation means

    Fixed-question benchmarks still headline most launches. Each tests something different, and each has a known way to mislead.

    BenchmarkWhat it testsKnown weakness
    SWE-benchFixing real GitHub issues; hidden unit tests decide.Public repos models may have seen; one language; scores depend on the scaffold.
    Humanity's Last ExamExpert-written, hard academic questions.Some answer keys are wrong; rewards memorised trivia as much as reasoning.
    ARC-AGINovel grid puzzles: infer a rule from a few examples.Narrow format; results depend on compute; self-reported scores run above verified ones.

    Saturation means top models score so near the maximum that gaps between them are smaller than the noise. It happens three ways: the ceiling is not 100% (wrong keys), error bars swallow the gap (see Statistics for evals), or contamination inflates the score.

    Near the ceiling, labs quietly switch benchmarks. A horizon keeps growing while you can build longer tasks, but those are costly to build and grade.

    Two more distortions are worth knowing. Contamination means the questions or near copies were in the training data, so the score partly measures memory. A private held-out set, or a large gap between public and private scores, is the tell. Scaffold dependence means the same model scores very differently with different tools, retries and compute per task, which is why self-reported numbers should be treated as upper bounds until someone independent reruns them.

    R7.2 · Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at.

    • A unit people understand. Minutes and hours of professional work mean something to a manager. A 78% score does not.
    • No fixed ceiling. Task length can always grow, so the measure keeps separating models after exam-style benchmarks have saturated.
    • Trend-friendly. One number per model, on a log scale, makes progress over years easy to compare and argue about.
    • Reliability is explicit. Reading the curve at 50%, 80% and 95% forces the question "how often is it right?" rather than hiding it in an average.
    • Agent-shaped. It tests multi-step work with tools, which is how agents are actually used, unlike a single-answer quiz.

    Weak at, or don't use when.

    • Judging your own workflow. The suite is mostly well-scored software and reasoning. Messy, judgement-heavy bank work is under-represented, and models do worse on it.
    • Anything needing a taste-based grade. Writing quality, client tone or risk appetite have no automatic pass/fail check, so the method cannot fit them.
    • Forecasting a date. The doubling time is uncertain, and a few months either way moves a milestone by years.
    • Safety or usefulness claims. Completing a long task says nothing on whether the output is correct in context, safe, or worth the review time. METR's own 2025 trial found developers slower with AI tools.
    • Comparing across harnesses. Different tools and retry budgets make horizons from different labs only loosely comparable.
    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Time horizon (METR)You want a trend in agent capability, in units a non-specialist understands.You need a verdict on your own tasks, or a precise forecast date.
    Fixed benchmark (SWE-bench, HLE)You need a cheap, repeatable comparison while models are mid-curve.Top models are within noise of the ceiling, or contamination is likely.
    ARC-AGI-style novel puzzlesYou want to probe generalisation to unseen rules rather than recall.You need to know about real-world tasks, or the compute budget differs between entries.
    Your own task suiteYou are deciding whether to deploy an agent on a specific process.You cannot afford labelled examples and expert time to grade them.
    Field trial or A/B testThe question is productivity or business value.You need an answer before deployment, or cannot randomise users.

    In practice. Use public horizons and benchmarks to shortlist models, then test on 50 to 200 of your own real tasks, graded by the people who do them today. Report success at 80% and 95% reliability, not just the average, and record cost and time per attempt. Check the agent's output on the sampled cases before widening scope. A small private suite costs days, not months, and it will tell you more than any public leaderboard.

    In industry this reads as: model choice for a coding assistant, deciding which internal workflows (reconciliation, ticket triage, report drafting) an agent can run with light review, and sizing the review burden. For compliance-heavy work such as AML investigations, treat horizons as a ceiling on what is plausible, not a promise. This is a research measure: useful for planning and vendor questions today, not a replacement for evaluating on your own process. What would change that: horizon suites that cover judgement-heavy professional work, with independent reruns.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most mistakes come from dropping a qualifier.

    • Reading a 50% horizon as "can do the job"

      It fails about half of such tasks, and nobody notices without a check.

      Spot itAsk for the 80% or 95% horizon. If none, divide the headline by 5 to 10.
    • Confusing the unit

      The horizon is human time for the task, not model run time.

      Spot itAsk "two hours of whose time?"
    • Extrapolating one line for years

      Uncertainty in the doubling time compounds exponentially, and the suite itself is running out of room.

      Spot itAsk for a range, not a date, and whether a slowdown case is included.
    • Assuming software tasks transfer to yours

      The suite is mostly well-scored coding. Messy, judgement-heavy work is harder to measure and models do worse on it.

      Spot itAsk whether the tasks match yours in clarity and context.
    • Quoting a score without its ceiling or noise

      "95% on an HLE-style set" when the key has errors, or 94% and 95% on a few hundred questions are within noise.

      Spot itAsk for label error and question count. The standard error of a score is roughly the square root of p(1−p)/n.
    • Comparing scores from different harnesses

      Two labs report SWE-bench with different scaffolds, tools, retries and compute. The model may be the same; the setup is not.

      Spot itLook for an independent verified run. Treat self-reported scores as upper bounds.
    • Contamination and tuning to the public set

      The model has seen the questions, or the team iterated against them, so the score measures exposure as well as ability.

      Spot itLook for a private held-out score and a large public-to-private gap.
    • Treating benchmark gains as productivity gains

      In METR's 2025 randomised trial, experienced developers on their own repositories were 19% slower with AI tools yet believed they were faster.

      Spot itAsk for a field result with real users on the real process before quoting savings.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a time horizon of "8 hours" not mean an agent can replace a person for a working day?

    50% means it fails about half the time, the baseline is a skilled person with full context, and the tasks are mostly well-specified software. Review and judgement are not measured.

    2. Why can a small change in doubling time matter so much?

    Growth compounds, so a few months more or less per doubling moves the date of a long-task milestone by years. Quote a range, not a date.

    3. Two models score 90% and 92% on a 300-question benchmark. Are they different?

    Probably you cannot say. With a few hundred questions, a two-point gap is within the noise.

    4. A test has 4% wrong answer keys and a model scores 97%. What does that suggest?

    The honest maximum is about 96%. Above it, the model is reproducing the key's mistakes, which hints at contamination.

    5. Why can a time-horizon measure outlast a fixed benchmark, and what limits it?

    A benchmark has a maximum score, but task length has none, so you can add longer tasks. The limit is cost: long tasks are expensive to build and grade.

    6. Model X has a 50% horizon of 4 hours with a sharp curve; model Y has 5 hours with a gentle one. You need an agent to run a 30-minute reconciliation unattended. Which do you test first, and why?

    X, probably. The sharp curve means its success on a 30-minute task is likely very high, while Y's gentle curve means a longer tail of failures on short tasks. But the horizon alone does not settle it: run both on 100 of your own reconciliations and compare success at 95%.

    7. A vendor says "doubling every four months, so month-long projects in two years". A colleague says "plan for much slower". Which would you plan around, and why?

    Plan around a range. The four-month figure is a fit to a few recent models, the suite has few long tasks, and the date is highly sensitive to the doubling time. Build a process that works with today's capability and gets cheaper if the optimistic case arrives, rather than one that needs it.
