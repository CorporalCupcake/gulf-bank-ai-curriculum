    How Long a Task Can AI Finish? METR Time Horizons and Frontier Benchmarks

    Why "how long a job a person would need" has become the most quoted yardstick for AI agents, what the steady doubling really says (and does not), and how to read ARC-AGI, Humanity's Last Exam and SWE-bench without being fooled by a headline score.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMETR measures AI by the length of task, in human working time, that a model completes about half the time, and that length has doubled every few months; benchmark scores near 100% stop telling you anything, so read every score with its ceiling, its error bars and the reliability level you actually need.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Ask a colleague how good a new analyst is and you rarely get a percentage. You hear: "She can take a two-hour ticket off my plate, but I would not hand her a month-long migration yet." That is a statement about the length of task someone can be trusted with.

    Most AI benchmarks do the opposite. They fix a set of questions and report the share answered correctly. That works until the number hits the ceiling, and then it stops moving while models keep improving. It also hides what a bank cares about: can the agent do the whole job, or only a slice of it?

    METR, a non-profit that evaluates frontier models, proposed a different yardstick in 2025. Give a model tasks of many different lengths. For each task, record how long a skilled human takes. Plot the model's success against that human time. Short tasks are nearly always solved, long ones almost never. The length where success crosses 50% is the model's 50% time horizon.

    Two things make it striking. The unit is concrete (minutes and hours of professional work). And when METR plotted the horizon for successive models, it grew along a clean exponential line: the length of task roughly doubled every several months. Section R7.2 is the companion piece: the fixed-question benchmarks (ARC-AGI, Humanity's Last Exam, SWE-bench) and why each one eventually saturates.

    Hold onto thisA time horizon is a statement of the form "at 50% reliability, on this kind of task, measured against human time". Every one of those qualifiers matters. A 50% horizon of 10 hours does not mean "can do a 10-hour job unsupervised". It means it gets about half of such jobs right, with nobody checking.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R7.1 · From pass/fail results to a time horizon

    The method (Kwa et al., 2025) has four steps.

    1. Build a task suite with human times. METR combined software, cybersecurity and reasoning tasks, from seconds to many hours: short multiple-choice style items, real-world coding tasks and long research-engineering problems. Skilled people attempted the tasks under timing, and the time of successful attempts became the task's length.
    2. Run each model many times in an agent harness (tools, a shell, the ability to iterate). Each attempt is simply scored success or failure by an automatic check.
    3. Fit a logistic curve of success probability against the logarithm of human time. Longer tasks, lower success.
    4. Read off the length at which the curve passes 50%. That is the 50% horizon. Reading it at 80% gives the 80% horizon, which is much shorter.

    In symbols, success probability falls with the log of task length t:

    P(success|t)=11+(t/h50)k

    h50 is the 50% horizon: at t = h50 the bracket equals 1 and success is exactly one half. k is the steepness: a high k means a sharp cliff from "reliably solves" to "reliably fails"; a low k means a long, gentle slope. This is the same logistic shape as in F2.5, with log task length as the input. The 80% horizon follows: h80 = h50 × (1/4)1/k.

    Look at what happened to reliability. For a typical steepness, the 80% horizon is about five times shorter than the 50% horizon, and the 95% horizon shorter again. A bank that wants an agent to run unattended needs something like the 95%+ horizon, which can be a small fraction of the number in the headline. This is the first thing to say when someone quotes "the model can do 8-hour tasks".

    What counts as "human time" also needs care. It is the time for a skilled professional who already has the right context to do the task. A new employee unfamiliar with your systems would take much longer. METR found models were weaker on tasks that are messier, with less clear scoring or more dependence on context. So a horizon measured on well-specified software tasks does not transfer one-for-one to, say, an AML investigation that needs judgement and access to ten internal systems.

    R7.1 · The doubling trend, and what extrapolation does

    Plot the 50% horizon of each frontier model against its release date, on a log scale for the horizon. The points line up. METR's original March 2025 analysis reported a doubling time of about seven months over 2019 to 2025, with models of 2019 managing tasks of seconds and early-2025 models managing tasks of around an hour. The pace looked faster in the most recent period: METR's Time Horizon 1.1 update (January 2026) estimates a doubling time of roughly 131 days (about four months) for post-2023 models, and a shorter figure still if you look only at 2024 onward. The number depends on which dates you fit.

    The trend line is just exponential growth: if the horizon doubles every d months, starting at h0, then

    h(m)=h0·2m/d

    m is months from now. To reach a target H you need d × log2(H/h0) months. From one hour to a 40-hour working week is log2 40 ≈ 5.3 doublings: about 21 months at a 4-month doubling, but 37 months at 7 months. Small changes in the doubling time swing the date by years, which is why you should never quote a single date.

    Three honest caveats about the line. First, it is a fit to a handful of frontier models, so it has wide uncertainty. Second, METR's own report says the suite is running out of room: the strongest models now pass most of the tasks, so the top of the scale rests on few, long tasks and the estimates grow wide error bars. Third, the trend says how long a task a model can complete, not whether the work is economically useful or safe to deploy unchecked. Treat it as a good measure of one dimension, not of general ability.

    R7.2 · What the frontier benchmarks test, and what saturation means

    Fixed-question benchmarks are still how most model launches are reported. Three matter now.

    BenchmarkWhat it testsFormatKnown weaknesses
    SWE-bench (Verified)Fixing real GitHub issues in open-source Python repositories. The agent edits the code; hidden unit tests decide.500 human-vetted tasks in the Verified subset; the original has ~2,300.Public repositories the models may have seen; one language; tests can be weak or over-specific; scores depend heavily on the scaffold around the model. Newer variants (for example SWE-bench Pro) exist because Verified got close to saturated.
    Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)Expert-written, closed-ended academic questions across many fields, built to be hard for models.About 2,500 questions, a mix of exact-match and multiple choice, with a private held-out set.Part of the answer key is disputed: independent reviews estimated a sizeable minority of chemistry and biology answers are wrong, and a re-verified version has been published. Exact-match questions reward memorised trivia as much as reasoning.
    ARC-AGI (1, 2, 3)Novel puzzles that a person solves from a few examples: infer a rule from coloured-grid demonstrations and apply it. Designed to resist memorisation. ARC-AGI-3 moves to interactive, game-like environments where the agent must discover the goal.Public training set plus semi-private and private evaluation sets; an official leaderboard with cost per task.Narrow format; results depend on how much compute the system spends per task; unverified self-reported scores run far above verified ones.

    The word that matters is saturation. A benchmark saturates when the top models score so close to the maximum that differences between them are smaller than the noise. It happens three ways:

    • The ceiling is not 100%. If 3% of the answer key is wrong, no model can honestly exceed about 97%. Above that, a higher score means the model learned the key's mistakes.
    • The error bars swallow the gap. With a few hundred questions, a model at 92% and one at 94% are statistically indistinguishable. (Statistics for evals goes deeper.)
    • Contamination and overfitting. If questions, or close variants, appeared in training data, or if teams tune against the public set, the score measures exposure as well as ability.

    Benchmarks are designed to be hard at birth and to last a few years. Each new one is a response to the previous one saturating; the field has learned to expect a lifespan of months to a couple of years from release to near-ceiling, then move on.

    The picture explains a lot of launch-day behaviour. When a benchmark is mid-curve, labs report big gains and press coverage follows. When it is near the ceiling, labs quietly switch to a new one. A good reader asks: where on this curve are we, how big is the key's error, and how many questions separate the models?

    Time horizons and fixed benchmarks measure different things. A benchmark asks "what fraction of this fixed set?". A horizon asks "how long a task, at fixed reliability?". The first saturates by construction; the second keeps growing as long as you can build longer tasks, but those are expensive to build and to grade, which is why METR itself is now shifting to new evaluations.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson sits between model capability and the question the bank cares about: what can we safely hand to a system, and how would we know?

    R7.3 · R7.4 · Item response theoryThe statistics under both ideas

    The 50% horizon is a one-parameter logistic curve in disguise. IRT does the same job per question and explains why scores bunch up at the ceiling.

    A4.1 · A4.2 · Statistics for evalsError bars on a benchmark

    Standard errors, paired comparisons and how many questions you need. Use it before believing any 1-point gap.

    A4 · Simulation testing for agentsLong, multi-step reliability

    Agent benchmarks test repeated success over many turns, which is exactly what time horizons are about.

    M5.5 · M5.6 · Evaluating agentsTrajectories and tool use

    How a single agent run is scored, step by step, before you aggregate it into a horizon.

    R2 · Scaling lawsWhy capability trends are smooth

    Compute and data have grown on an exponential path; the horizon trend rides on top of it. If the inputs slow, so may the curve.

    A5 · Reasoning modelsLonger thinking, longer tasks

    Much of the recent horizon growth coincides with reasoning models and better tool use over long contexts.

    M4 · The agent loopWhy long tasks compound errors

    If each step succeeds with probability s, a 100-step job succeeds with s100. That is the mechanism behind steep falling curves.

    C1 · BaselinesCompare to a person, not a leaderboard

    The time horizon is human-relative by design. The same discipline applies in-house: measure the agent against the analyst you have today.

    G3 · ValidationWhat a validator will ask

    Model risk teams will want in-house, task-specific tests, not a public benchmark number.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Choosing what to automate. Map candidate workflows to rough human time and error cost. Five-minute, high-volume, low-stakes steps (triage a complaint, classify a document) are inside today's reliable range. Day-long investigations are not, without review.
    • Vendor claims. A vendor quoting a coding-agent benchmark percentage says little about your legacy COBOL, Arabic documents or approval workflows. Ask for results on your tasks.
    • Planning and headcount. The trend is a reason to expect the range of automatable work to widen, but dates are uncertain by years. Plan in scenarios.
    • Governance. Reliability level, not just average ability, sets how much human review a process needs. The CBUAE and SAMA expectations on model risk and human oversight (A12) are about exactly this trade-off, though I have not seen either regulator reference time horizons.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The time-horizon idea is little more than a year old and already the default way to describe agent progress. The live arguments are whether the trend continues, whether it carries over to messy real work, and what replaces benchmarks that have saturated.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most mistakes come from dropping a qualifier. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Reading a 50% horizon as "can do the job"

      "The model handles 8-hour tasks." At 50%, it fails four in ten on 4-hour jobs too, and nobody notices without a check.

      Spot itAsk for the reliability level. Ask for the 80% or 95% horizon. If none is given, divide the headline by 5 to 10 for a first guess.
    • Confusing the unit

      The horizon is human time to do the task, not how long the model runs. A model may finish a "2-hour" task in 5 minutes, or fail after three hours of looping.

      Spot itAsk "two hours of whose time?" and check the context assumptions of the human baseline.
    • Extrapolating one line for years

      "In 24 months it does month-long projects." The doubling time, the start point and the suite all carry uncertainty that compounds exponentially.

      Spot itAsk for a range, not a date. Notice whether the forecast includes a slowdown scenario. Check how few tasks sit at the top of the scale.
    • Assuming software tasks transfer to your tasks

      The suite is mostly well-scored coding and reasoning. Messy, judgement-heavy work is harder to measure and models do worse on it.

      Spot itAsk whether the tasks resemble yours in clarity of success criteria and context.
    • Quoting a benchmark score without its ceiling

      "95% on HLE-style question set" when the key is known to have errors, or when 94% and 95% are within noise.

      Spot itAsk for the estimated label error and the number of questions. Compute the standard error: √(p(1−p)/n).
    • Comparing scores from different harnesses

      Two labs report SWE-bench numbers using different scaffolds, tool access, retries and compute.

      Spot itCheck whether the number is from a verified independent run and what the agent was allowed to do. Treat self-reported scores as upper bounds.
    • Contamination and tuning to the public set

      The model has seen the questions, or the team iterated against them.

      Spot itLook for a private held-out score and for a large gap between public and private performance.
    • Treating benchmark gains as productivity gains

      A higher horizon did not speed up experienced developers in METR's trial.

      Spot itAsk for a field result, with real users, on the real process, before quoting savings.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The best independent measure we have is how long a task, in human working time, an AI agent can finish about half the time. That length has been doubling every four to seven months. It is a good sign that more of our work will become automatable, but half the time is not good enough to run unattended. For work where mistakes are costly we need reliability closer to 95%, and at that level the tasks are much shorter. So we start with short, checkable, high-volume tasks and test on our own work.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “The 50% time horizon comes from fitting a logistic curve of agent success against log human completion time; it grew roughly exponentially, with a doubling time of about seven months over 2019 to 2025 and nearer four months since 2023 on METR's updated suite. I'd treat it as a trend with wide uncertainty and a saturating suite, and I'd care about the 80 to 95% horizons, the human baseline's context, and transfer to our tasks. For public benchmarks I look at the key's error rate, the number of items against the gap, contamination and the scaffold.”
    “If the doubling continues, will agents replace our analysts in two years?”

    I would not forecast that. The doubling time is uncertain, the benchmark is mostly software, and a 50% success rate is far from deployable. The honest statement is that the set of tasks agents can handle with review is widening fast. I'd plan scenarios and track our own task suite every quarter instead of relying on one extrapolation.

    “Why is the 80% horizon so much shorter?”

    Success falls smoothly with task length, so demanding higher reliability pulls you back along the curve. In a typical shape the 80% horizon is about a fifth of the 50% one. Long tasks are chains of steps: with 98% per step, a 50-step task succeeds only about 36% of the time.

    “The vendor says 85% on SWE-bench. Should we buy?”

    It tells us the model is strong at fixing issues in open-source Python repositories under the vendor's setup. I'd ask which subset, what scaffold and compute, whether it was independently reproduced, and then run a pilot on 50 real tickets from our own codebase, scored against the time our engineers take and the rework needed.

    “What does it mean that a benchmark is saturated?”

    The top models are near the maximum possible score, so differences are smaller than noise or label error and the benchmark no longer ranks models. It does not mean the models are perfect, only that the test has stopped discriminating. Then labs move to harder tests.

    “Is ARC-AGI measuring general intelligence?”

    It measures one thing well: working out a new rule from a few examples. That is a useful property, and it resists memorisation, but it is a narrow puzzle format and scores depend on compute spent per task. I'd use it as one signal of novel reasoning, not a verdict on general intelligence.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A model's 50% horizon is 4 hours and k = 1. What task length gives 80% success, and what is the success rate on a 16-hour task?

    80% horizon = 4 × (1/4)1/1 = 1 hour. At 16 hours, t/h = 4, so P = 1/(1 + 4) = 20%.

    2. Starting from a 1-hour horizon with a 5-month doubling time, when does it reach 32 hours?

    32 hours is 25, so five doublings: 25 months. At a 7-month doubling it would be 35 months, which is a 10-month difference from a 2-month change in doubling time.

    3. Why does a time horizon of "8 hours" not mean an agent can replace a person for a working day?

    The 50% level means it fails about half the time, the human baseline assumes a skilled person with full context, the tasks are mostly well-specified software work, and the process around it (review, handoffs, judgement) is not measured.

    4. A benchmark has 300 questions. Two models score 90% and 92%. Are they different?

    The standard error of each is √(0.9 × 0.1 / 300) ≈ 1.7 points, so the difference of 2 points has a standard error of about 2.3 (unpaired). The gap is under one standard error; you cannot say. Paired comparison on the same questions helps, but 300 items is still few.

    5. A test has 4% wrong answer keys. A model scores 97%. What does that suggest?

    The maximum honest score is about 96%. A result above that means the model is reproducing the key's mistakes, perhaps from having seen the data. It is a flag for contamination or for the key being wrong in a predictable way.

    6. Why can a time-horizon measure outlast a fixed benchmark, and what limits it?

    A fixed benchmark has a maximum score, so it saturates. Task length has no maximum: you can always add longer tasks. The limit is cost: long tasks are expensive to design, run and grade, and with few long tasks the top end becomes statistically thin.

    7. What is the difference between METR's field trial and its horizon measurement?

    The horizon measures model success on prepared tasks with automatic scoring. The trial measured real developers' speed on their own work with and without tools. They answer different questions, and in the 2025 trial the developers were slower, so benchmark gains do not automatically become productivity gains.
