    Regularising Neural Networks: Dropout, Augmentation and Loss Curves

    Part 1

    What regularising means

    In one sentenceRegularising means making it harder for a network to memorise its training data, so it learns the pattern instead.

    Running example: a network that predicts exam scores from study habits, trained on 500 students. A big network can memorise those 500 students exactly: perfect on them, poor on the next cohort. That gap is overfitting. The opposite, underfitting, is a model too simple to learn the pattern, so it is poor on both.

    How the main tools differ

    • More data is the best fix and the least available. In fraud, the number of fraud cases counts, not the number of rows.
    • Weight decay (an L2 penalty) charges the network for large weights, which keeps the fitted curve from wiggling.
    • Early stopping stops training when validation loss stops improving.
    • Dropout switches parts of the network off at random during training.
    • Data augmentation makes extra training examples by changing existing ones.
    Part 2

    Dropout and augmentation

    Dropout

    Think of a team where anyone might be off sick on a given day. No one person can become indispensable.

    1. At each training step, pick a random share of units (say 20%) and switch them off.
    2. Scale the survivors up so the average output stays the same.
    3. Train on that step with the thinned network.
    4. At test time, switch every unit back on and drop nothing.

    Dropout adds noise, and the network learns to be right despite it. You can also see it as training a huge crowd of thinned networks that share weights. The full network then acts like their average.

    • Typical rates: about 0.1 for transformers, 0.2 to 0.5 for older dense networks.
    • Raise it when the gap between training and validation loss is large. Lower it when training loss itself will not fall.
    • Limits: it does little for tree ensembles, which have their own randomness, and it sits awkwardly with batch normalisation, so many vision models use less of it.

    Data augmentation

    Augmentation makes new examples by changing existing ones in ways that should not change the label. A photo of a cat rotated two degrees is still a cat. You are telling the network which differences do not matter.

    • Works well for images (small rotation, blur, noise) and text (for example back-translation, which translates a sentence out and back).
    • Rarely suits tabular data, where a changed amount can turn a genuine row into a fraud-like one.
    • Can break labels: flipping the digit 6 makes a different shape, and cropping the cat out leaves a label with nothing to match.
    • Rule: augment only the training set, after splitting, and only with changes that could really happen in production.
    Part 3

    How to read loss curves

    A loss curve is a car dashboard. Plot training and validation loss together and ask four questions.

    • Is training loss falling? If not, it is an optimisation problem or a bug. Validation tells you nothing yet.
    • Is the gap widening? A small steady gap is normal. Validation loss turning upward is the point where early stopping should fire.
    • Where does it start and level off? A model with no skill sits near the loss of a constant guess (0.693 for two balanced classes). Both curves on a high plateau means underfitting.
    • Is it smooth? Heavy zig-zag usually means the learning rate is too high. Spikes that recover are common. Spikes that do not are divergence.

    Worked readings. Training 0.10 and validation 0.60, with validation rising: memorising, so add regularisation or stop earlier. Training 0.55 and validation 0.57, both flat: underfitting, so use a bigger model or train longer. Validation below training: often just dropout being on during training, but it can also mean leakage or an easy validation set.

    A first test, popularised by Andrej Karpathy: overfit one small batch. If the network cannot drive the loss near zero on 10 examples, there is a bug, and no tuning will fix it.

    A curve shows a symptom, not a cause. Also log the metric you really care about, such as recall at a fixed alert volume, because loss can improve while the business metric does not.

    Part 4

    Which tool to use

    ToolWhat it doesStrong atWeak at
    Early stoppingStops when validation loss turns upNearly free, so turn it on firstWastes the final data if you do not retrain
    Weight decayPenalises large weightsWorks almost everywhereToo strong and the model underfits
    DropoutRandom units off in trainingWide dense layersSlows training, less useful elsewhere
    AugmentationAdds changed copies of examplesImages and text, adds real varietyTabular data, or labels that change

    In practice: turn on early stopping, keep the best checkpoint, then add weight decay. Add dropout or augmentation if the gap is still large. Keep a final test set untouched: if you try 200 settings against the validation set, its best score is optimistic.

    Show the formula

    Penalised loss = ordinary loss + strength × (sum of squared weights). The strength is a setting: too small and it does nothing, too large and the model underfits.

    In banking, overfitting is the credit model that looks superb in training and disappoints on next quarter's applicants, so validate on a later time period, not a random slice.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging by training loss

      It only ever falls as the model gets bigger, so it cannot tell you when to stop. Use validation loss.

    • Leaking validation data

      Augmenting before splitting, or tuning heavily on one validation set, makes scores look better than they are.

    • Leaving dropout on at test time

      Predictions become noisy. Dropout belongs to training only.

    • Augmenting with changes that alter the label

      Check that each change could happen in production and keeps the right answer.

    1. Training loss is 0.10 and validation loss is rising from 0.60. What is happening?

    Overfitting: the network is memorising. Stop earlier, add weight decay or dropout, or get more data.

    2. Why are the surviving units scaled up in dropout?

    So the average output stays the same as when nothing is dropped, which lets you use the full network unchanged at test time.

    3. Why is augmentation risky for tabular bank data?

    A small change to an amount can turn a normal transaction into one that looks like fraud, so the label may no longer be true.

    4. Both curves are high and flat. Which problem is it?

    Underfitting. Use a bigger model, train longer or reduce regularisation.

    5. A network cannot get near zero loss on 10 examples. What does that tell you?

    There is a bug, such as wrong labels or a broken pipeline. Tuning will not help until it is fixed.
