    Keeping a Model Honest: Overfitting, Dropout, Augmentation, Loss Curves and Low-Precision Training

    Why a model that memorises looks brilliant on its own data, how dropout and augmentation push against that, how to read a loss curve like a doctor reads a chart, and what lets very large models be trained at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model fits the training data more easily than it fits the future, so you spend your effort on narrowing that gap (more data, less capacity, noise, early stopping), you watch training and validation loss together to see which way it is going, and when the model is too big for the hardware you trade numerical precision for speed in a way that is safe only if you keep a high-precision master copy.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A student prepares for an exam by memorising last year's paper, answers included. On that paper she scores 100%. On this year's paper she scores 55%. Nothing is wrong with her memory. She learned the questions, not the subject.

    A model does the same. Given enough flexibility it can store the training rows, noise and all. A credit model with 400 features and 3,000 defaults can learn that "customer 81,203 defaulted" through some odd combination of fields, and report a spectacular training score. That combination says nothing about next quarter's applicants. This gap between how well the model does on data it has seen and data it has not is overfitting. F4.1 explained it as variance; here we look at the tools that fight it in neural networks and how to see it happening.

    Three practical questions follow, and they are the three topics of this lesson.

    • How do I stop it? More data, a smaller or more constrained model, noise injected during training (dropout), more varied data made from the data you have (augmentation), and stopping early.
    • How do I see it? By plotting loss on the training data and on held-out data over the course of training. The shape of the two curves tells you what is wrong, and what is not wrong.
    • What if the model is simply too large to train? Then numbers are stored with fewer bits, and the work is split over many chips. Both bring their own ways to go wrong.
    Hold onto thisTraining loss tells you whether the model can learn. Validation loss tells you whether what it learned is true outside the training set. You always need both, on the same plot, and the validation set must be one the model has never influenced.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Overfitting: capacity against data

    Two quantities control it. Capacity is how many different shapes the model can bend itself into: polynomial degree, tree depth, number of weights. Information is how many independent examples pin that shape down, and how noisy they are. When capacity is small compared with information, the model cannot bend enough: it underfits, and errors are high on training and validation alike. When capacity is large compared with information, the model uses its spare flexibility to follow noise: training error keeps falling while validation error turns up. Validation error is then a U shape, and the best model sits at the bottom.

    The cleanest way to feel this is with a model small enough to fit exactly: a polynomial. The simulation below fits one to noisy points from a smooth curve (dashed), using real least squares. The "ridge penalty" is weight decay, the classical form of regularisation: it adds a cost for large coefficients, which stops the curve from wiggling to catch every point.

    Notice what this shows that a textbook sentence does not. Training error only ever falls as capacity rises, so it can never tell you where to stop. And "more data" and "a stronger penalty" are two answers to the same problem: they both reduce how much the noise in the sample can move the fit.

    The honest ways to cut the gap, roughly in order of how much they usually give:

    • More real data. Always best, and seldom available. In fraud and default modelling it is the number of positives that counts (F3.5).
    • Less capacity. Fewer features, shallower trees, narrower layers.
    • Weight decay (L2). The ridge penalty above. In deep learning this is nearly always on; the correct form for Adam-type optimisers is decoupled "AdamW" decay (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019).
    • Early stopping. Stop training when validation loss stops improving and keep the best checkpoint. It acts like a penalty because weights start small and grow as training goes on.
    • Noise during training: dropout and augmentation, below.

    One wrinkle for later. Very large networks can fit pure random labels perfectly (Zhang et al., 2017), so "capacity vs data" is not the whole story, and the U shape can become a double dip. R6 covers double descent and grokking. For tabular banking models at normal sizes, the classical picture above holds.

    A subtler overfit is on the validation set itself. Every time you look at validation results and change something, you leak a little information into the model. Try 200 hyperparameter settings and the best validation score is optimistic. That is why a final, untouched test set is kept (F4.5, F4.9), and why time-ordered splits are needed in banking (F3.1).

    Dropout and data augmentation

    Dropout. During each training step, every unit in a layer is switched off at random with probability p. The next step uses a different random set. The network cannot rely on any single unit, or on two units that only work as a pair (co-adaptation), because either might vanish. It has to spread the evidence across many units. The usual picture is an ensemble: each step trains a different thinned sub-network, and the full network at test time behaves like their average (Srivastava et al., 2014).

    The scaling matters. Modern code uses inverted dropout: when a unit survives (probability 1−p), its value is divided by 1−p, so the expected output of the layer is unchanged. At test time the mask is simply not applied. For one output formed from contributions ci:

    E[y]=∑ci,Var(y)=p1−p∑ci2

    The average output is untouched; only noise is added, and it grows quickly with p (at p = 0.5 the noise variance equals the sum of squared contributions). The noise is what does the regularising: the network is trained to give the right answer despite it, so it must make the answer robust to losing parts of its evidence.

    Practical notes. Typical rates are 0.1 for transformers and 0.2–0.5 for older dense networks; convolutional layers use it less. Dropout slows training (you need more epochs) and does little for tree ensembles, which have their own forms of randomness. Training loss measured with dropout on is higher than validation loss measured with it off, which is one honest reason for a validation curve sitting below the training curve. A related use: leaving dropout on at prediction time and averaging many passes gives a rough uncertainty estimate (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), though how well calibrated that is remains debated; F7.4 is the way to check.

    Data augmentation makes new training examples by changing existing ones in ways that should not change the label. A cheque image rotated by two degrees is still the same cheque. The model is taught that these differences do not matter, which is knowledge you are supplying by hand. It is regularisation by prior belief.

    Data typeAugmentations that usually keep the labelWhere it breaks
    Images: cheques, IDs, trade documentsSmall rotation and shift, blur, brightness, JPEG noise, simulated scan skew and stamps.Flipping an Arabic cheque changes script direction; flipping digits changes amounts. Cropping off the signature box removes the label.

    Three rules. Augment only the training set, never validation or test. Augment after splitting, so a rotated copy of a test image is not in training. And let each augmentation answer a question: "could this happen in production?" Augmentations that produce impossible inputs teach the model to ignore signal. More advanced forms (mixing two images and their labels, or learning augmentation policies) help vision models; the idea reappears in self-supervised learning, where the augmentations define what the model must treat as the same thing (F5.7).

    Reading loss curves

    A loss curve is the cheapest diagnostic you have. Plot training loss and validation loss against steps (or epochs) on the same axes. What you are asking:

    • Is training loss going down? If not, the problem is optimisation or a bug, and validation tells you nothing yet.
    • How big is the gap, and is it widening? A small steady gap is normal. A gap that opens, then widens, is overfitting. Validation turning up while training falls marks the point where early stopping should have fired.
    • Is the curve smooth? Heavy zig-zag usually means a learning rate too high (F6.2) or a batch too small. Spikes that recover are common in large-model training; spikes that do not recover are divergence.
    • Where does it start? A model with no skill starts near the loss of a constant guess. For two classes that is ln 2 ≈ 0.693; for ten, ln 10 ≈ 2.30; for a language model with vocabulary V, ln V. A first loss far above that suggests a bug in the labels or scale; a curve that never leaves it means nothing is being learned.
    • Where does it level off? A plateau at a high value with training and validation together is underfitting: more capacity or training time, a better learning rate, better features.

    Karpathy's well-known advice (2019) is to overfit a single small batch first: if the model cannot drive loss to nearly zero on 10 examples, there is a bug, and no amount of tuning will find it. Try the quiz below. Each of the eight scenarios is generated from a simple formula, but they are drawn the way you will see them.

    Two cautions. First, a curve diagnoses a symptom. A validation curve that sits well below training loss might be dropout, but it might also be leakage, a validation set that is easier (or smaller) than training, or a time split where the validation months were calm. Second, do not average away the noise you want to see: smooth for reading trends, but look at the raw curve for spikes. Log more than loss: the metric you care about (recall at a fixed alert volume), gradient size, and learning rate.

    Mixed precision and distributed training

    A computer stores a real number in a fixed number of bits: a sign, an exponent (how large) and a mantissa (how many significant digits). The usual formats:

    FormatBitsExponent / mantissa bitsGist
    FP32328 / 23The classic default. About 7 decimal digits. Safe.
    FP16165 / 10More digits than BF16, but a narrow range (largest value about 65,504). Small gradients vanish to zero, so training needs loss scaling.
    BF16168 / 7Same range as FP32, fewer digits. No loss scaling needed. The workhorse for large-model training.
    FP8 (E4M3, E5M2)84 / 3 and 5 / 2Used for matrix multiplies on recent GPUs, with per-tensor or per-block scaling factors.
    FP4 (e.g. NVFP4)4block-scaledFrontier research and early production; see Part 4.

    Fewer bits means less memory, less data moved and faster arithmetic: 16-bit multiplies run at several times the rate of 32-bit on modern accelerators. The danger is in accumulating small changes. A weight is nudged each step by a tiny amount (learning rate times gradient). If the weight is stored in a format whose spacing is larger than twice that nudge, the addition rounds back to the old value and the weight never moves. The next simulation shows it.

    Mixed precision is the fix (Micikevicius et al., 2018). Keep a master copy of the weights in FP32. Do the forward and backward passes, which are the expensive part, in a low-precision copy. Apply the update to the FP32 master, then re-round to low precision for the next step. Sums that need accuracy (softmax, normalisation, loss, optimiser state) stay in higher precision. For FP16 add loss scaling: multiply the loss by, say, 1,024 before backpropagation so small gradients land in range, then divide the gradients by the same factor before the update.

    Where the memory goes. For every parameter, a typical mixed-precision Adam setup holds the BF16 weight (2 bytes), a BF16 gradient (2), the FP32 master weight (4) and two FP32 optimiser moments (4 + 4): about 16 bytes. A 7-billion-parameter model therefore needs roughly 7×10⁹ × 16 = 112 GB for weights and optimiser state alone, before activations. One 80 GB accelerator cannot hold it. That arithmetic is the whole reason for distributing training.

    StrategyWhat is split across chipsCost
    Data parallelEach chip holds a full model copy and sees different examples; gradients are averaged (all-reduce) every step.Needs the whole model to fit on one chip. Communication grows with model size.
    Sharded data parallel (ZeRO, FSDP)Optimiser state, gradients and then weights are divided across chips and gathered when needed (Rajbhandari et al., 2020).More communication; removes most of the memory duplication.
    Tensor parallelEach matrix multiplication is cut across chips.Needs very fast links, so usually inside one server.
    Pipeline parallelDifferent layers on different chips; micro-batches flow through.Idle gaps ("bubbles") while the pipeline fills.
    Expert parallelIn mixture-of-experts models, each chip holds some experts (R1).Load balancing and all-to-all traffic.

    Two facts help in conversation. Doubling the number of chips rarely doubles speed, because chips wait for each other; "scaling efficiency" is the real number. And a large effective batch (examples per update, summed over chips) changes the training dynamics, so learning rate and warm-up have to be re-tuned when you scale out (F6.2). For a bank, this level of detail matters only if you pre-train or heavily fine-tune your own large models; most teams call a hosted model or fine-tune a small one on a single node.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit and fraud neural nets on tabular data. Small positive counts, high capacity. Weight decay, early stopping and an out-of-time test set are standard. Gradient-boosted trees often win here anyway, with their own overfitting controls.
    • Document and cheque models. Image augmentation with realistic scan artefacts; check that augmentation does not remove the field you need.
    • Arabic and English text classifiers. Fine-tuning a pre-trained model on a few thousand labelled complaints overfits within a few epochs; watch the validation curve and keep a per-language breakdown.
    • Fine-tuning large language models. Adapter methods train a small number of weights on a small dataset: the overfitting risk is high, and the loss curve is your main early warning. Memory arithmetic decides whether it fits on the bank's GPUs or needs a cloud region (see sovereign and on-premises choices in P4).
    • Model validation. Independent validators (G3) will ask for the train and validation curves and for how the stopping point was chosen. Have them saved with the model.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basics are stable. Weight decay and early stopping are universal; dropout has faded in the largest language-model pre-training, where a single pass over a huge dataset leaves little room to memorise, but remains common in fine-tuning and smaller models. Augmentation is central in vision, speech and self-supervised learning. The movement is in precision and scale.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing the epoch on the test set

      Training is stopped at the epoch with the best test score, and that same score is reported.

      Spot itAsk which data picked the stopping point. It must be a separate validation set; the test set is used once.
    • Reporting training performance

      "The model reaches 99% AUC." On what data?

      Spot itAsk for the validation and out-of-time numbers beside the training numbers. A big gap is the finding.
    • Augmenting before the split

      Rotated or noised copies of the same cheque land on both sides. Validation looks excellent.

      Spot itCheck that group identifiers (document, customer) never cross the split, and that augmentation code runs only on the training loader.
    • Label-changing augmentation

      Back-translation flips a negation; a crop drops the signature; tabular noise turns a normal transaction into an outlier.

      Spot itRead fifty augmented examples with their labels. If you would relabel any, the augmentation is wrong.
    • Dropout left on at prediction time (by accident)

      The same input gives different scores on each call.

      Spot itScore one input twice. A deployed model should return identical scores unless uncertainty sampling is intended.
    • Validation set too small to read

      A wobbly validation curve is mistaken for overfitting, or an early-stopping point is picked from noise.

      Spot itCount the positives in validation. With 40 cases, recall moves by several points from sampling alone (F3.5).
    • Misreading a smoothed or clipped curve

      Smoothing hides spikes, or the y-axis is cut so a diverging run looks flat.

      Spot itRequest raw curves with the full axis, and the learning-rate schedule beside them.
    • Silent loss of small updates

      FP16 training with no loss scaling, or low-precision optimiser state: loss plateaus early or becomes NaN.

      Spot itCompare against a short FP32 run. Check for NaN and inf counts and gradient norms in the logs.
    • "Scaling out" that doesn't scale

      Eight times the chips, 3 times the speed, and a different final loss because the effective batch changed.

      Spot itAsk for tokens per second per chip and for the learning-rate settings used at each scale.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The risk with any learning model is that it memorises our past instead of learning something that holds up next quarter. We guard against that by testing on months the model never saw, and by stopping training when performance on those months stops improving. So the number I would show you is the out-of-time one, not the training one, and I will tell you how big the gap between them is.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I log training and validation loss and the business metric per epoch, with the learning-rate schedule. Regularisation is weight decay, early stopping on a time-ordered validation set, and dropout or augmentation where the data type supports it, with augmentation only inside the training split. Before tuning I check that the model can overfit a tiny batch. For fine-tuning I use BF16 with an FP32 master copy and sharded optimiser state if the memory arithmetic needs it, and I compare against a higher-precision run before trusting any lower-precision recipe.”
    “Training accuracy is 99% and validation is 82%. Is the model bad?”

    It is overfitting, which is a different question from being bad. 82% on held-out data may still beat the baseline. I would check whether the gap widens with training (stop earlier), then try more data, a smaller model, weight decay or dropout, and judge each by the validation number, not the training one. I would also confirm the validation set reflects the future, not a shuffled slice of the past.

    “Validation loss is lower than training loss. Isn’t that impossible?”

    No, but it deserves a check. Training loss is often averaged over the epoch while weights are changing, and measured with dropout on and augmentation applied. Validation is measured once, cleanly. If it persists with those switched off, suspect leakage, or a validation set that is simply easier.

    “Why not just train for as long as possible?”

    Training loss always keeps improving; validation loss does not. Past the minimum we are fitting noise. Early stopping with a saved best checkpoint is free regularisation, and it saves compute.

    “What does dropout actually do?”

    It randomly removes units during training so the network cannot rely on any one of them, which forces redundancy. It adds noise of known size while leaving the expected output unchanged, and it is switched off at prediction time. It is common in fine-tuning and smaller networks; the largest pre-training runs make a single pass and mostly don't need it.

    “Can we train in 8-bit or 4-bit to save cost?”

    For serving, yes, with checks. For training, BF16 is the safe default. FP8 works on recent hardware with careful scaling, and 4-bit has been shown on a single large run, but those are specialist recipes. For us the cheaper win is usually a smaller model, adapter fine-tuning, or sharding, with a short higher-precision run as the control.

    “Will this 7-billion-parameter model fit on our GPU?”

    Weights alone in BF16 are about 14 GB, so inference fits on one 24 GB card. Full fine-tuning with Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter, near 112 GB, plus activations, so it needs sharding across several GPUs. Adapter methods train a small fraction of the weights and cut optimiser state to match, which is why they fit.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Training loss falls every epoch; validation loss falls for 12 epochs then rises. What is happening, and what do you do?

    Overfitting from about epoch 12. Stop there (early stopping), restore the epoch-12 checkpoint, then try more data, weight decay, dropout or a smaller model, and compare on the validation curve.

    2. A layer uses inverted dropout with p = 0.2. A surviving unit outputs 3.0. What value is passed on, and why?

    3.0 ÷ 0.8 = 3.75. Units survive 80% of the time, so scaling up by 1/0.8 keeps the layer's average output unchanged and nothing needs adjusting at test time.

    3. Contributions to a neuron are 2, 1 and 1. With p = 0.5, what is the standard deviation of its output under dropout?

    Variance = p/(1-p) × Σc² = 1 × (4 + 1 + 1) = 6, standard deviation ≈ 2.45. The mean stays 4.

    4. A loss curve starts at 0.69 for a binary task and stays there. What does it mean?

    0.693 = ln 2 is the loss of predicting 50/50. The model has learned nothing. Suspect a bug (labels shuffled, inputs constant, learning rate zero or far too small, frozen weights, dead activations). Check by overfitting one small batch.

    5. For cheque images, which of these augmentations is safe: 3° rotation, horizontal flip, JPEG compression, crop that may remove the amount box?

    Rotation and compression are label-preserving and realistic. Flipping mirrors the writing and creates cheques that don't exist. A crop that can remove the amount box destroys the evidence the label depends on.

    6. A weight of 1.0 stored in FP16 gets an update of +0.0001. Why does it not change, and how does mixed precision fix this?

    Near 1.0 FP16 numbers are spaced 2-10 ≈ 0.00098 apart. An update below half the spacing rounds back to 1.0. Mixed precision applies the update to an FP32 master copy, whose spacing is about 10-7, so tiny updates accumulate; the low-precision copy is re-derived each step.

    7. How much memory do weights and optimiser state need for a 3-billion-parameter model at 16 bytes per parameter?

    3×10⁹ × 16 = 48 GB, before activations and batch memory. It would not fit on a 40 GB card; sharding or an adapter approach would.

    8. You tried 300 hyperparameter settings and report the best validation score. Why is it optimistic, and what protects against it?

    The maximum of many noisy scores is biased upwards: some of the winner's lead is luck, and the choice was made on that very data. Keep a final test set, ideally later in time, that is touched once after all choices are made.
