    Keeping a Model Honest: Overfitting, Dropout, Augmentation, Loss Curves and Low-Precision Training

    Why a model that memorises looks brilliant on its own data, how dropout and augmentation push against that, how to read a loss curve like a doctor reads a chart, and what lets very large models be trained at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model fits its training data more easily than the future, so you narrow that gap (more data, less capacity, noise, early stopping), watch training and validation loss together, and when the model is too big for the hardware, trade precision for speed safely by keeping a high-precision master copy.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A student memorises last year's exam paper, answers included. She scores 100% on it and 55% on this year's. She learned the questions, not the subject.

    A flexible model does the same. A credit model with 400 features and 3,000 defaults can "learn" that customer 81,203 defaulted through some odd combination of fields. Its training score is spectacular and useless for next quarter's applicants. This gap between seen and unseen data is overfitting (variance, as in F4.1). Three questions follow:

    • How do I stop it? More data, a smaller model, noise during training (dropout), varied copies of the data (augmentation), early stopping.
    • How do I see it? Plot training and held-out loss over training. The shape of the two curves tells you what is wrong, and what isn't.
    • What if the model is too big to train? Store numbers with fewer bits and split the work over many chips. Both bring new ways to fail.
    Hold onto thisTraining loss tells you whether the model can learn. Validation loss tells you whether what it learned is true outside the training set. Plot both together, and keep the validation set one the model has never influenced.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Overfitting: capacity against data

    Two quantities control overfitting. Capacity is how many shapes the model can bend into (polynomial degree, tree depth, number of weights). Information is how many independent, clean examples pin the shape down. Fitting a curve through two points with a wiggly ruler is easy; the ruler needs many points before its wiggles mean anything.

    • Capacity small, information large: the model underfits. Error is high on training and validation alike.
    • Capacity large, information small: the model follows noise. Training error keeps falling while validation error turns up, a U shape with the best model at the bottom.

    Read it by the gap: a small steady gap is normal; a gap that grows as you add capacity is memorisation. The simulation fits a polynomial to noisy points from a smooth curve (dashed), using real least squares. The "ridge penalty" is weight decay, the classical regularisation: a cost on large coefficients that stops the curve wiggling to catch every point.

    Two things to take from this. Training error only ever falls as capacity rises, so it can never tell you where to stop. And more data and a stronger penalty fix the same problem: both reduce how far noise in the sample can move the fit.

    Ways to cut the gap, roughly in order of how much they usually give:

    • More real data. Best, and seldom available. In fraud and default modelling, the number of positives is what counts (F3.5).
    • Less capacity. Fewer features, shallower trees, narrower layers.
    • Weight decay (L2). The ridge penalty above. Nearly always on in deep learning; for Adam-type optimisers use decoupled "AdamW" decay (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019).
    • Early stopping. Stop when validation loss stops improving and keep the best checkpoint. It acts like a penalty because weights start small and grow as training goes on.
    • Noise during training: dropout and augmentation, below.

    Very large networks can fit pure random labels (Zhang et al., 2017), so the U can become a double dip. R6 covers double descent and grokking. For ordinary tabular banking models, the classical picture holds.

    The validation set can overfit too. Each time you look at it and change something, a little information leaks in. Try 200 hyperparameter settings and the best score is optimistic, like a student who sits the mock exam 200 times. That is why a final untouched test set is kept (F4.5, F4.9) and why banking needs time-ordered splits (F3.1).

    Dropout and data augmentation

    Dropout. A team where anyone might be off sick on any day must make sure no one person is indispensable. Dropout does that: at each training step every unit in a layer is switched off at random with probability p, and the next step uses a different set. The network can't rely on one unit, or on two that only work as a pair (co-adaptation), so it spreads evidence across many. Picture an ensemble: each step trains a different thinned sub-network, and the full network at test time behaves like their average (Srivastava et al., 2014).

    Modern code uses inverted dropout: a surviving unit (probability 1−p) is divided by 1−p, so the layer's expected output is unchanged. At test time no mask is applied. For one output formed from contributions ci:

    E[y]=∑ci,Var(y)=p1−p∑ci2

    The average output is untouched; only noise is added, and it grows quickly with p (at p = 0.5 the noise variance equals the sum of squared contributions). The network is trained to be right despite the noise, so it must make its answer robust to losing parts of its evidence.

    Typical dropout rates are 0.1 for transformers and 0.2–0.5 for older dense networks; convolutional layers use it less. It slows training (more epochs) and does little for tree ensembles, which have their own randomness. Training loss measured with dropout on can sit above validation loss measured with it off, one honest reason a validation curve dips below training. Leaving dropout on at prediction time and averaging many passes gives a rough uncertainty estimate (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), though its calibration is debated; F7.4 is how to check.

    Data augmentation makes new training examples by changing existing ones in ways that should not change the label. A cheque rotated by two degrees is still the same cheque, like a face photographed from a slightly different angle. You hand the model knowledge it cannot learn from the data: these differences don't matter. It is regularisation by prior belief.

    Data typeAugmentations that usually keep the labelWhere it breaks
    Images: cheques, IDs, trade documentsSmall rotation and shift, blur, brightness, JPEG noise, simulated scan skew and stamps.Flipping an Arabic cheque changes script direction; flipping digits changes amounts. Cropping off the signature box removes the label.
    Text: complaints, KYC notesBack-translation (English to Arabic to English), synonym swap, random word dropout, typos.Arabic dialect and diacritics carry meaning; automatic translation can flip negation. Check a sample by eye.
    Audio: call-centre speechBackground noise, speed change, simulated phone-line compression.Pitch shifts can change apparent speaker or emotion.
    Tabular: transactions, credit filesRarely safe. Small noise on continuous inputs; sometimes mixing rows (mixup).A changed amount or merchant can make a legitimate row look like fraud. Row-level synthetic data needs F3.5-style leakage care.
    Time seriesJitter, window slicing, scaling.Shuffling time destroys the pattern you wanted; augmenting before the split leaks the future.

    Three rules:

    • Augment only the training set, never validation or test.
    • Augment after splitting, so a rotated copy of a test image is not in training.
    • Ask of each augmentation: "could this happen in production?" Impossible inputs teach the model to ignore signal.

    Advanced forms (mixing two images and their labels, learned augmentation policies) help vision models. In self-supervised learning the augmentations define what the model must treat as the same thing (F5.7).

    Reading loss curves

    A loss curve is a car dashboard: a few glances tell you if something is wrong. Plot training and validation loss on the same axes, then ask:

    • Is training loss falling? If not, the problem is optimisation or a bug, and validation tells you nothing yet.
    • How big is the gap, and is it widening? A small steady gap is normal. A gap that opens and widens is overfitting. Validation turning up while training falls is where early stopping should have fired.
    • Is it smooth? Heavy zig-zag usually means a learning rate too high (F6.2) or a batch too small. Spikes that recover are common in large-model training; spikes that don't are divergence.
    • Where does it start? A model with no skill starts near the loss of a constant guess: ln 2 ≈ 0.693 for two classes, ln 10 ≈ 2.30 for ten, ln V for a language model with vocabulary V. Far above that suggests a label or scale bug. Never leaving it means nothing is being learned.
    • Where does it level off? A high plateau with training and validation together is underfitting: more capacity or training time, a better learning rate, better features.

    Karpathy's advice (2019): overfit a single small batch first. If the model can't drive loss near zero on 10 examples, there is a bug, and no tuning will find it. Now try the quiz: eight scenarios, drawn as you will see them.

    A curve shows a symptom, not a cause. A validation curve below training loss might be dropout, but it might also be leakage, an easier or smaller validation set, or calm validation months in a time split. Smooth for trends, but read the raw curve for spikes. Log more than loss: the metric you care about (recall at a fixed alert volume), gradient size and learning rate.

    Mixed precision and distributed training

    A computer stores a real number as a sign, an exponent (how large) and a mantissa (how many significant digits). Fewer mantissa bits means coarser spacing between representable numbers:

    FormatBitsExponent / mantissa bitsGist
    FP32328 / 23The classic default. About 7 decimal digits. Safe.
    FP16165 / 10More digits than BF16, but a narrow range (largest value about 65,504). Small gradients vanish to zero, so training needs loss scaling.
    BF16168 / 7Same range as FP32, fewer digits. No loss scaling needed. The workhorse for large-model training.
    FP8 (E4M3, E5M2)84 / 3 and 5 / 2Used for matrix multiplies on recent GPUs, with per-tensor or per-block scaling factors.
    FP4 (e.g. NVFP4)4block-scaledFrontier research and early production; see Part 4.

    Fewer bits means less memory, less data moved and faster arithmetic: 16-bit multiplies run at several times the rate of 32-bit. The danger is accumulating small changes. Think of a ruler marked only in whole centimetres: you cannot record a 1 mm shift. A weight is nudged each step by a tiny amount (learning rate times gradient). If the format's spacing is more than twice that nudge, the sum rounds back to the old value and the weight never moves. Simulation 4 shows it.

    Mixed precision is the fix (Micikevicius et al., 2018):

    • Keep a master copy of the weights in FP32.
    • Run the expensive forward and backward passes in a low-precision copy.
    • Apply the update to the FP32 master, then re-round for the next step.
    • Keep sensitive sums (softmax, normalisation, loss, optimiser state) in higher precision.
    • For FP16, add loss scaling: multiply the loss by, say, 1,024 before backpropagation so small gradients land in range, then divide the gradients by the same factor before the update.

    Where the memory goes. Per parameter, a typical mixed-precision Adam setup holds a BF16 weight (2 bytes), a BF16 gradient (2), an FP32 master weight (4) and two FP32 optimiser moments (4 + 4): about 16 bytes. A 7-billion-parameter model needs 7×10⁹ × 16 ≈ 112 GB before activations. One 80 GB accelerator cannot hold it, which is the whole reason for distributing training. It is like a sofa that won't fit through the door: you split the job, not the sofa.

    StrategyWhat is split across chipsCost
    Data parallelEach chip holds a full model copy and sees different examples; gradients are averaged (all-reduce) every step.Needs the whole model to fit on one chip. Communication grows with model size.
    Sharded data parallel (ZeRO, FSDP)Optimiser state, gradients and then weights are divided across chips and gathered when needed (Rajbhandari et al., 2020).More communication; removes most of the memory duplication.
    Tensor parallelEach matrix multiplication is cut across chips.Needs very fast links, so usually inside one server.
    Pipeline parallelDifferent layers on different chips; micro-batches flow through.Idle gaps ("bubbles") while the pipeline fills.
    Expert parallelIn mixture-of-experts models, each chip holds some experts (R1).Load balancing and all-to-all traffic.

    Doubling the chips rarely doubles speed, because chips wait for each other. "Scaling efficiency" is the real number. A large effective batch (examples per update, summed over chips) also changes training dynamics, so re-tune learning rate and warm-up when you scale out (F6.2). For a bank this matters only if you pre-train or heavily fine-tune your own large models. Most teams call a hosted model or fine-tune a small one on a single node.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing the epoch on the test set

      Training stops at the epoch with the best test score, and that score is reported.

      Spot itAsk which data picked the stopping point. It must be a separate validation set; the test set is used once.
    • Reporting training performance

      "The model reaches 99% AUC." On what data?

      Spot itAsk for validation and out-of-time numbers beside the training numbers. A big gap is the finding.
    • Augmenting before the split

      Rotated or noised copies of the same cheque land on both sides. Validation looks excellent.

      Spot itCheck that group identifiers (document, customer) never cross the split, and that augmentation runs only on the training loader.
    • Label-changing augmentation

      Back-translation flips a negation; a crop drops the signature; tabular noise turns a normal transaction into an outlier.

      Spot itRead fifty augmented examples with their labels. If you would relabel any, the augmentation is wrong.
    • Dropout left on at prediction time

      The same input gets different scores on each call.

      Spot itScore one input twice. A deployed model should return identical scores unless uncertainty sampling is intended.
    • Validation set too small to read

      A wobbly validation curve is mistaken for overfitting, or the stopping point is picked from noise.

      Spot itCount the positives in validation. With 40 cases, recall moves by several points from sampling alone (F3.5).
    • Misreading a smoothed or clipped curve

      Smoothing hides spikes, or a cut y-axis makes a diverging run look flat.

      Spot itRequest raw curves with the full axis, and the learning-rate schedule beside them.
    • Silent loss of small updates

      FP16 training with no loss scaling, or low-precision optimiser state: loss plateaus early or becomes NaN.

      Spot itCompare against a short FP32 run. Check NaN and inf counts and gradient norms in the logs.
    • "Scaling out" that doesn't scale

      Eight times the chips, 3 times the speed, and a different final loss because the effective batch changed.

      Spot itAsk for tokens per second per chip and the learning-rate settings used at each scale.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Training loss falls every epoch; validation loss falls for 12 epochs then rises. What is happening, and what do you do?

    Overfitting from about epoch 12. Stop there and restore the epoch-12 checkpoint. Then try more data, weight decay, dropout or a smaller model, judged on the validation curve.

    2. A layer uses inverted dropout with p = 0.2. A surviving unit outputs 3.0. What value is passed on, and why?

    3.0 ÷ 0.8 = 3.75. Units survive 80% of the time, so scaling by 1/0.8 keeps the average output unchanged and nothing needs adjusting at test time.

    3. Contributions to a neuron are 2, 1 and 1. With p = 0.5, what is the standard deviation of its output under dropout?

    Variance = p/(1-p) × Σc² = 1 × (4 + 1 + 1) = 6, so the standard deviation is about 2.45. The mean stays 4.

    4. A loss curve starts at 0.69 for a binary task and stays there. What does it mean?

    0.693 = ln 2 is the loss of a 50/50 guess, so nothing is being learned. Suspect a bug: shuffled labels, constant inputs, learning rate zero or tiny, frozen weights, dead activations. Overfit one small batch to check.

    5. For cheque images, which of these augmentations is safe: 3° rotation, horizontal flip, JPEG compression, crop that may remove the amount box?

    Rotation and compression: label-preserving and realistic. Flipping mirrors the writing and makes cheques that don't exist. A crop that can remove the amount box destroys the evidence the label depends on.

    6. A weight of 1.0 stored in FP16 gets an update of +0.0001. Why does it not change, and how does mixed precision fix this?

    Near 1.0, FP16 numbers are spaced 2-10 ≈ 0.00098 apart. An update below half that rounds back to 1.0. Mixed precision applies the update to an FP32 master copy (spacing about 10-7), so tiny updates accumulate; the low-precision copy is re-derived each step.

    7. How much memory do weights and optimiser state need for a 3-billion-parameter model at 16 bytes per parameter?

    3×10⁹ × 16 = 48 GB, before activations. It won't fit on a 40 GB card; sharding or an adapter approach would.

    8. You tried 300 hyperparameter settings and report the best validation score. Why is it optimistic, and what protects against it?

    The maximum of many noisy scores is biased upwards: part of the winner's lead is luck, and the choice was made on that very data. Keep a final test set, ideally later in time, and touch it once after all choices are made.
