    Keeping a Model Honest: Overfitting, Dropout, Augmentation, Loss Curves and Low-Precision Training

    Why a model that memorises looks brilliant on its own data, how dropout and augmentation push against that, how to read a loss curve like a doctor reads a chart, and what lets very large models be trained at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model fits its training data more easily than the future, so you narrow that gap (more data, less capacity, noise, early stopping), watch training and validation loss together, and when the model is too big for the hardware, trade precision for speed safely with a high-precision master copy.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A student memorises last year's exam paper, answers included. She scores 100% on it and 55% on this year's. She learned the questions, not the subject.

    A flexible model does the same. A credit model with 400 features and 3,000 defaults can "learn" that customer 81,203 defaulted through some odd combination of fields: a spectacular training score, useless for next quarter's applicants. That gap between seen and unseen data is overfitting. Three questions follow:

    • How do I stop it? More data, a smaller model, dropout, augmentation, early stopping.
    • How do I see it? Plot training and held-out loss together. The shape of the two curves tells you what is wrong.
    • What if the model is too big to train? Store numbers with fewer bits and split the work over many chips. Both bring new ways to fail.
    Hold onto thisTraining loss tells you whether the model can learn. Validation loss tells you whether what it learned is true outside the training set. Plot both together, and keep the validation set one the model has never influenced.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Overfitting: capacity against data

    Two things control overfitting. Capacity is how many shapes the model can bend into (polynomial degree, tree depth, number of weights). Information is how many clean, independent examples pin the shape down. Too much capacity for the information, and the model follows noise: training error keeps falling while validation error turns up, a U shape with the best model at the bottom. Too little capacity, and it underfits: error is high on both.

    Read it by the gap: a small steady gap is normal; one that grows as you add capacity is memorisation. The "ridge penalty" in the simulation is weight decay: a cost on large coefficients that stops the curve wiggling to catch every point.

    Training error only ever falls as capacity rises, so it can never tell you where to stop. More data and a stronger penalty fix the same problem: both limit how far noise in the sample can move the fit.

    Ways to cut the gap, roughly in order of payoff:

    • More real data. Best, and seldom available. In fraud and default modelling, the number of positives is what counts (F3.5).
    • Less capacity. Fewer features, shallower trees, narrower layers.
    • Weight decay and early stopping. Penalise large weights; stop when validation loss stops improving and keep the best checkpoint.
    • Noise during training: dropout and augmentation, below.

    Very large networks can behave differently: see R6. For ordinary tabular banking models, the classical picture holds.

    The validation set can overfit too. Try 200 hyperparameter settings and the best score is optimistic, like a student who sits the mock exam 200 times. Keep a final untouched test set (F4.5, F4.9) and use time-ordered splits (F3.1).

    Dropout and data augmentation

    Dropout. A team where anyone might be off sick on any day can't have one indispensable person. At each training step, every unit in a layer is switched off at random with probability p, so the network can't rely on any one unit and spreads evidence across many. It behaves like an ensemble of thinned networks, and the full network at test time acts like their average.

    Surviving units are scaled up by 1/(1−p) so the average output is unchanged, and no mask is used at test time. What dropout adds is noise, which grows quickly with p. The network learns to be right despite it.

    Typical dropout rates are 0.1 for transformers and 0.2–0.5 for older dense networks. It does little for tree ensembles, which have their own randomness. Training loss measured with dropout on can sit above validation loss measured with it off, one honest reason a validation curve dips below training.

    Data augmentation makes new training examples by changing existing ones in ways that should not change the label. A cheque rotated by two degrees is still the same cheque. You hand the model knowledge it cannot learn from the data: these differences don't matter.

    Data typeAugmentations that usually keep the labelWhere it breaks
    Images: cheques, IDsSmall rotation and shift, blur, brightness, JPEG noise, simulated scan skew.Flipping an Arabic cheque changes script direction; flipping digits changes amounts. Cropping off the signature box removes the label.
    Text: complaints, KYC notesBack-translation (English to Arabic to English), synonym swap, typos.Arabic dialect and diacritics carry meaning; translation can flip negation. Check a sample by eye.
    Tabular: transactions, credit filesRarely safe. Small noise on continuous inputs.A changed amount or merchant can make a legitimate row look like fraud.

    Three rules:

    • Augment only the training set, never validation or test.
    • Augment after splitting, so copies of a test item are not in training.
    • Ask of each augmentation: "could this happen in production?" Impossible inputs teach the model to ignore signal.

    Reading loss curves

    A loss curve is a car dashboard: a few glances tell you if something is wrong. Plot training and validation loss on the same axes, then ask:

    • Is training loss falling? If not, the problem is optimisation or a bug, and validation tells you nothing yet.
    • Is the gap widening? A small steady gap is normal. A gap that opens and widens is overfitting; validation turning up is where early stopping should have fired.
    • Where does it start and level off? A model with no skill starts near the loss of a constant guess (0.693 for two classes); never leaving that means nothing is being learned. A high plateau for both curves is underfitting.
    • Is it smooth? Heavy zig-zag usually means a learning rate that is too high (F6.2). Spikes that recover are common; spikes that don't are divergence.

    Karpathy's advice: first overfit a single small batch. If the model can't drive loss near zero on 10 examples, there is a bug. Now try the quiz.

    A curve shows a symptom, not a cause. A validation curve below training loss might be dropout, but also leakage or an easier validation set. Log the metric you care about too, such as recall at a fixed alert volume.

    Mixed precision and distributed training

    A computer stores a number with a fixed budget of bits, split between range (how large) and digits (how finely). Fewer bits means less memory and faster arithmetic, but coarser spacing between representable numbers.

    FormatBitsGist
    FP3232The classic default. About 7 decimal digits. Safe.
    FP1616More digits than BF16, but a narrow range. Small gradients vanish to zero, so training needs loss scaling.
    BF1616Same range as FP32, fewer digits. No loss scaling needed. The workhorse for large-model training.
    FP88Used for matrix multiplies on recent GPUs, with scaling factors.

    The danger is accumulating small changes. With a ruler marked only in whole centimetres you cannot record a 1 mm shift. In the same way, if a format's spacing is coarser than a weight's tiny update, the sum rounds back to the old value and the weight never moves. Simulation 4 shows it.

    Mixed precision is the fix (Micikevicius et al., 2018):

    • Keep a master copy of the weights in FP32 and apply updates to it.
    • Run the expensive forward and backward passes in a low-precision copy, re-rounded each step.
    • Keep sensitive sums (softmax, normalisation, loss, optimiser state) in higher precision.
    • For FP16, scale the loss up before backpropagation so small gradients stay in range, then scale the gradients back down.

    Where the memory goes. Mixed-precision Adam holds roughly 16 bytes per parameter (weight, gradient, FP32 master copy, two optimiser values). A 7-billion-parameter model needs around 112 GB before activations, more than one 80 GB card holds. That is why training is distributed.

    The main ways to split the work:

    • Data parallel: every chip holds a full copy and sees different examples; gradients are averaged each step. Needs the whole model to fit on one chip.
    • Sharded data parallel (ZeRO, FSDP): optimiser state, gradients and weights are divided across chips and gathered when needed. Removes most duplication, at the cost of more communication.
    • Tensor and pipeline parallel: cut each matrix multiplication, or put different layers, on different chips. Needs fast links.

    Doubling the chips rarely doubles speed, because chips wait for each other, and a larger effective batch means re-tuning learning rate and warm-up (F6.2). This matters to a bank only if you pre-train or heavily fine-tune your own large models; most teams call a hosted model or fine-tune a small one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing the epoch on the test set, or reporting training numbers

      Training stops at the epoch with the best test score, or "the model reaches 99% AUC" is quoted without saying on what data.

      Spot itAsk which data picked the stopping point and which data the number comes from. Ask for validation and out-of-time figures beside training. A big gap is the finding.
    • Augmentation that leaks or changes the label

      Copies of the same cheque land on both sides of the split, so validation looks excellent. Or back-translation flips a negation, or tabular noise turns a normal transaction into an outlier.

      Spot itCheck that group identifiers never cross the split and that augmentation runs only on the training loader. Read fifty augmented examples: if you would relabel any, the augmentation is wrong.
    • Dropout left on at prediction time

      The same input gets different scores on each call.

      Spot itScore one input twice. A deployed model should return identical scores unless uncertainty sampling is intended.
    • Misreading the curve

      A tiny validation set makes a wobbly curve look like overfitting, or smoothing and a cut y-axis hide spikes and divergence.

      Spot itCount the positives in validation: with 40 cases, recall moves several points from sampling alone (F3.5). Request raw curves with the full axis and the learning-rate schedule.
    • Silent loss of small updates

      FP16 training with no loss scaling: loss plateaus early or becomes NaN.

      Spot itCompare against a short FP32 run. Check NaN counts and gradient norms.
    • "Scaling out" that doesn't scale

      Eight times the chips, 3 times the speed, and a different final loss because the effective batch changed.

      Spot itAsk for tokens per second per chip and the learning-rate settings used at each scale.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Training loss falls every epoch; validation loss falls for 12 epochs then rises. What is happening, and what do you do?

    Overfitting from about epoch 12. Stop there and restore that checkpoint, then try more data, weight decay, dropout or a smaller model.

    2. Why does inverted dropout scale the surviving units up during training, and why is nothing changed at test time?

    Switching units off shrinks the layer's total output. Scaling the survivors up keeps the average the same, so at test time the full network runs as it is.

    3. You raise the dropout rate from 0.2 to 0.7. What happens to the noise the network sees, and what is the risk?

    The average stays the same but the noise grows quickly. A little forces the network to spread its evidence; too much stops it learning, so training loss stays high.

    4. A loss curve starts at 0.69 for a binary task and stays there. What does it mean?

    0.69 is the loss of a 50/50 guess, so nothing is being learned. Suspect a bug: shuffled labels, constant inputs, a tiny learning rate, frozen weights. Overfit one small batch to check.

    5. For cheque images, which of these augmentations is safe: 3° rotation, horizontal flip, JPEG compression, crop that may remove the amount box?

    Rotation and compression: label-preserving and realistic. Flipping makes cheques that don't exist; a crop that can remove the amount box destroys the evidence the label depends on.

    6. A weight stored in FP16 receives a tiny update and does not change. Why, and how does mixed precision fix this?

    Near 1.0, FP16 can only store numbers a little under 0.001 apart, so a smaller update rounds back to the old value. Mixed precision applies updates to an FP32 master copy, whose finer spacing lets tiny updates add up.

    7. Why can a 7-billion-parameter model not be trained on one 80 GB card, when its weights alone take only 14 GB in 16-bit?

    Training also stores gradients, an FP32 master copy and optimiser state, about 16 bytes per parameter, so roughly 112 GB before activations. That is why training is split across chips, or an adapter approach is used.

    8. You tried 300 hyperparameter settings and report the best validation score. Why is it optimistic, and what protects against it?

    The best of many noisy scores is partly luck, and the choice was made on that very data. Keep a final test set, ideally later in time, and touch it once after all choices are made.
