    W2.1 Day one ★ Priority About 8 minutes

    Your First 90 Days

    Part 1

    What the first 90 days are for

    In one sentenceYour first 90 days are for turning skill into a track record: learn the problems, deliver one small win that someone senior cares about, then propose bigger work.

    A new joiner has skill but no reputation. Nobody can judge skill until it is shown on something they can see and check. A small result that holds up beats a large one nobody has tested.

    Picture two data scientists who join a bank in the same month. One spends six weeks on a deep-learning fraud model. The other spends three weeks with investigators and automates a report that wastes one analyst a day each week.

    How it differs from other starts

    • Not "prove you are clever". Impressive but unrequested work gets questions about validation and deployment that you cannot yet answer.
    • Not "just listen for ever". Listening that never ends leaves no time for anyone to see a result.
    • Not a solo launch. In a bank a sponsor must want your model, validation and compliance must allow it, technology must run it and users must adopt it. Any one can stop it.
    Part 2

    How the three months work

    1. Days 1–30: listen and map. Meet your manager, business owners, users, model validation, data governance and IT. Start access requests on day one, because they are slow. Read existing model documents and recent audit findings.
    2. End of month one: write a one-page map of problems, owners and constraints, with a shortlist of 3–5 candidate wins.
    3. Days 31–60: deliver one visible, low-risk win. Agree in writing what "done" means, deliver it, show the number to the person who asked, and hand it over so it keeps working.
    4. Days 61–90: propose the next step. Write a two-page proposal: the problem, a baseline (today's number), the approvals needed, a realistic plan and the risks. Name a sponsor.

    The order matters more than the exact dates.

    What makes a good first win

    • Someone senior already cares. Fixing something nobody cares about is safe and invisible.
    • You can finish it in weeks with data you can reach.
    • Failure is cheap. You have no goodwill in reserve, so a big public failure costs more than it seems.
    • It need not be machine learning. A fixed report or a backtest of existing rules is easy for a non-technical person to check.

    Two or three modest wins beat one big bet. They give several chances to be seen delivering.

    Three habits for all 90 days

    • Show you did the reading. "The audit flagged late sanctions-list updates. Is that still open?" earns more respect than "what do you do here?"
    • Close your commitments. Promise Thursday, send Wednesday. Send your manager a weekly note: done, next, blocked.
    • Do not overpromise. Say what you will find out and by when, not what the model will achieve. You do not yet know the data or the baseline.
    Part 3

    Who can say yes, and who can say no

    Most AI work in a bank fails for organisational reasons, not technical ones: the sponsor lost interest, validation was never planned, or a data owner was never told. List everyone with a stake and sort them by what they can do to your work.

    • Treat vetoes as design constraints. Learn the model risk policy's tiers and timeline this week, not after you have built the model.
    • A meeting confirms; it is not first contact. Before each decision meeting, list the attendees and tick who you have already spoken to. Unticked names are your risk.
    • Order your conversations: sponsor first, then the vetoes, then users, then the informed. End each with "Who else should I speak to?"
    • Roles vary by bank. Ask rather than assume.

    How to read your progress

    • Day 30: you can name the owner, user and constraint for each of 3–5 problems. If not, keep listening.
    • Day 60: a senior person can repeat your result in their own words. If not, it was not visible enough.
    • Day 90: someone senior asks what to try next. That request is the real goal.

    In banking, "low-risk" means low risk of failing, not low importance. Pick a boring-sounding task that a senior person finds annoying.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Building the impressive thing first

      Ask who requested it and who will use it. If you cannot name them, go back to listening.

    • Learning the validation rules after building

      The model then needs documentation, testing and approvals you never planned for.

    • Relying on a single sponsor

      If your sponsor left for two months, would anyone else defend the project?

    • Overpromising, then going silent

      A claim like "cut false alerts by 40%" before seeing the data becomes your yardstick. Send short weekly notes instead.

    1. Why is a fixed weekly report a better first win than a deep-learning model?

    It is quick, low-risk, easy to verify and fixes a real annoyance, so it earns trust fast. The model is slow, may fail in public and needs approvals you do not yet understand.

    2. What is the difference between someone who can veto and someone you must consult?

    A veto holder has formal authority to stop go-live, so their requirements are constraints. A consultee has no formal power, but their advice shapes the design and their goodwill drives adoption.

    3. Why do you start access requests on day one?

    Access is slow, and you cannot deliver a win in month two without data.

    4. The head of compliance has not heard of your project and the decision meeting is next week. What do you do?

    Speak to them before the meeting, explain the idea and ask what they would need to see. If you cannot, ask to move the item.

    5. Why promise what you will find out instead of what the model will achieve?

    You do not yet know the data quality or baseline. A promised outcome becomes the yardstick, and missing it costs credibility. A promise about learning and dates is one you control.
