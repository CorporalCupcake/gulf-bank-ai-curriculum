    Your First 90 Days: Credibility, Wins and the People Who Can Say Yes

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two data scientists join a Gulf bank in the same month. Both are strong.

    The first spends six weeks building a deep-learning fraud model. In week eight the head of fraud operations asks how it differs from his 40 tuned rules, who validated it, and whether it runs on the card platform. She has no answer to the last two. The model sits in a notebook.

    The second spends three weeks with investigators, asking what wastes their Monday. One analyst loses a day a week stitching a report from three systems. By day 45 it is automated and correct. By day 90 the head of fraud asks him what to try next.

    He was not more talented. He knew a new joiner has skill but no reputation, and nobody can judge skill until it is shown on something they can see and check.

    • Trust comes from a track record. A small result that holds up beats a large untested one.
    • A bank model is never yours alone to launch. A sponsor must want it, validation and compliance allow it, technology run it and users adopt it. Any one can stop it.
    • Listening has a return. It decides whether your first win lands on something people care about.
    Hold onto thisMonth one: learn. Month two: deliver one small thing that matters to someone senior. Month three: turn that into a trusted proposal. Ambitious work comes after people have a reason to back you.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Your first 90 days: listen, win, propose

    The order matters more than the dates.

    PhaseAimWhat you actually doWhat you produce
    Days 1–30
    Listen and map    		Learn the business, the data and the politics.One-to-ones with your manager, business owners, users, model validation, data governance and IT. Start access requests on day one. Read existing model documentation and recent audit findings.A one-page map of problems, owners and constraints. A shortlist of 3–5 candidate wins.
    Days 31–60
    Deliver one visible, low-risk win    		Prove you are reliable and useful.Pick one item from the shortlist (Simulation 1), agree in writing what "done" means, deliver it, show the number to the person who asked, and hand it over so it keeps working.A result a senior person can repeat in their own words.
    Days 61–90
    Propose the next step    		Convert trust into a mandate.Propose larger work: the problem, a baseline, the approvals needed, a realistic plan (C3.3) and the risks. Talk to each key person before any meeting.A two-page proposal with a named sponsor.

    Three habits run through all three phases.

    • Show you have done the reading. "I saw the audit flagged late sanctions-list updates. Is that still open?" earns more respect than "what do you do here?"
    • Close your commitments. Promise the extract for Thursday, send it Wednesday. Add a short weekly note to your manager: done, next, blocked.
    • Do not overpromise. Say what you will find out and by when, not what the model will achieve.

    Choosing early wins: visible, valuable, low-risk

    A good first win has four properties: someone senior already cares, you can finish it in weeks, it uses data you can reach, and failure is cheap. It need not be machine learning. A fixed report or a backtest of existing rules is easy for a non-technical person to verify.

    Think of each candidate as a bet. A big failure in month two costs more than it seems, because you have no goodwill in reserve. Two or three modest wins limit the downside and give several chances to be seen delivering.

    The best mix fits your time, includes at least one near-certain item and puts something in front of senior people. "Low-risk" means low risk of failing, not low importance: fixing something nobody cares about is safe and invisible. Aim for a boring-sounding task that a senior person finds annoying.

    Why listening first pays: the trade-off

    Building straight away is like cooking before asking what the guests eat. Listening for ever means dinner is served at midnight. Listening raises the chance your win hits a real pain point, but every day spent leaves fewer days for it to be seen and used before day 90.

    Trust at day 90 therefore rises with listening, peaks, then falls. The exact peak depends on how long the build takes; the shape is the point.

    Listen actively and front-load it, and chase access in parallel. Overlap too: start a small, certain task in week three while you keep learning.

    Stakeholder mapping: who can say yes, who can say no

    Most AI work in a bank fails for organisational reasons: the sponsor lost interest, validation was never planned, or a data owner was not told. List everyone with a stake and sort them by what they can do to your work.

    • Decides. Owns the problem and budget, and sponsors you. Without them you have no mandate.
    • Can veto. Has formal authority to stop go-live: model validation, compliance, information security, data governance. Treat their requirements as design constraints.
    • Must consult. No formal veto, but users and deploying engineers can make or break adoption.
    • Keep informed. Affected, no say. A short regular update is enough.

    For each person, note what they care about and what they fear. Everyone who can say no should have seen the idea before the decision meeting. A meeting confirms; it is not first contact. Roles vary by bank, so the exercise below is practice, not a universal answer.

    Plan the order: sponsor first, then the vetoes whose requirements shape the design, then users, then the informed. End every conversation with "Who else should I speak to?"

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Building the impressive thing first

      A six-week model nobody asked for, shown in week eight.

      Spot itWho asked for this, and who will use it? If you cannot name them, go back to listening.
    • Learning the validation rules after building

      The model needs documentation, independent testing and approvals you never planned for.

      Spot itDo you know the model risk policy's tiering and timeline? If not, ask this week.
    • Single-threaded sponsorship

      One champion leaves or changes priorities and the work loses its mandate.

      Spot itIf your sponsor vanished for two months, would anyone else defend the project?
    • Meeting-as-first-contact

      A key person hears about the idea in committee, feels bypassed and objects.

      Spot itBefore each decision meeting, list attendees and tick who you have already spoken to. Unticked names are the risk.
    • Overpromising and going silent

      "We will cut false alerts by 40%" before seeing the data, then weeks of heads-down work and a reveal.

      Spot itAny claim without a baseline, or a manager who last heard from you over a week ago. Replace it with what you will measure and a short note.
    • Ignoring users, the incumbent and local culture

      Users see the tool at the end, the current rules are presented as foolish, or a senior person is challenged directly in a group meeting.

      Spot itHave you watched a user do the task? Would the previous author be comfortable hearing how you describe their work? Ask a trusted colleague how disagreement is really raised. C3.4 covers this.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is a fixed weekly report a better first win than a new deep-learning model, even though the model is more impressive?

    The report is quick, low-risk, easy for a non-technical person to verify and fixes a real annoyance, so it earns trust fast. The model is slow, likely to fail in public, and needs validation and deployment you may not yet understand.

    2. Why can two or three modest wins beat one big one, and what is the catch in Simulation 1?

    Several chances make at least one clear success very likely, at the price of a higher chance of a visible stumble. The catch is that the simulation treats outcomes as independent, which is optimistic: shared causes such as late data access hit several items at once.

    3. What is the difference between someone who can veto and someone you must consult?

    A veto holder has formal authority to stop go-live (validation, compliance, information security), so their requirements are constraints. A consultee has no formal power, but their advice shapes the design and their goodwill drives adoption.

    4. In Simulation 2, why does trust fall if you listen for 50 days?

    The win is more relevant, but it arrives too late to be used and seen by day 90. Relevance and time in use pull in opposite directions, so there is a sweet spot.

    5. A decision meeting is next week and the head of compliance has not heard about your project. What do you do?

    Speak to them before the meeting. Explain the idea, ask what they would need to see to support it, and note any conditions. If you cannot, ask to move the item.

    6. Why should your early promises be framed as what you will find out, not what the model will achieve?

    You do not yet know the data quality, label problems or baseline. A promised outcome becomes the yardstick, and missing it costs credibility. A promise about learning and dates is one you control.

    7. What does the CBUAE guidance mean for your first 90 days, and what should you not claim?

    Reports describe it as non-binding guidance on AI governance, explanation, human oversight, fairness and data. Engage governance teams in month one. Do not quote detailed requirements until you have read the primary document.
