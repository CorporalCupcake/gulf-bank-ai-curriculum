    Exploratory Analysis, Data Quality and Honest Charts

    How to question a table before trusting its numbers, why summaries can hide the story, and how a chart can argue more than the data supports.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLook at the raw data, hunt for codes and mix-ups that pose as values, and treat every chart as an argument someone chose to make.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An analyst asks for the average salary of salary-transfer customers and gets AED 115,000. It came from the warehouse, so it looks authoritative. It is also absurd, like a bathroom scale with a book on it: right arithmetic, wrong answer.

    Plotting the column shows why. Most customers sit near AED 14,000, a small hump sits 100 times further out (salaries stored in fils by one legacy feed), a spike at 999,999 means "not provided", and a block of rows says 0. The mean averaged them all without complaint.

    Hold onto thisNever summarise a column you haven't looked at. Plot it, sort it, count its most frequent values and ask where each row came from. Most "model problems" are data problems nobody looked for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Profiling: asking the table what it is

    Exploratory data analysis is looking before modelling, like a detective at a scene. The first pass is profiling: a fixed checklist run on every new table. It takes minutes and catches most disasters.

    Ask what one row is, how many rows against distinct keys, the null rate per column, the most frequent values, and whether totals match the ledger. Automate the checks so the next load fails loudly.

    Sentinels, impossible values and unit mix-ups

    Old systems had no way to say "unknown", so people invented values: 0, -1, 9999, 999999, dates like 1900-01-01 or 9999-12-31, text like "N/A". These sentinels look like data and no null check catches them.

    Unit mix-ups are nastier because every value looks reasonable: dirhams against fils, or mixed currencies in one column. A ×100 error doesn't fatten the tail, it makes a second hump on a log axis (Simulation 1).

    Duplicates and joins that quietly change the count

    Duplicates come in three kinds: the same row twice, one key with two rows, and one person under two IDs. The last counts one risk as two in exposure and fraud work.

    The most common wrong number in banking is the fan-out. Join a customer with one AED 40,000 account to their three cards, sum the balance, and you get AED 120,000. The mirror error is an inner join that silently drops customers with no bureau record. Check row counts after every join.

    Missing data: why it's missing matters more than how much

    A null is a measurement that didn't happen, and the fix depends on why. Students skipping questions at random is harmless; skipping the hard ones is not.

    • MCAR (completely at random): random drops. You just have less data.
    • MAR (at random given what you observed): affluent customers skip income more often, and segment is recorded. Fixable by weighting.
    • MNAR (not at random): higher earners decline to state income. Biased, by an unknown amount.

    MAR versus MNAR cannot be tested from the data, so argue it from how the form works. Dropping rows is fine only under MCAR. Mean-filling crushes the spread. A missing-indicator feature is often best, since missingness can be a signal.

    Distributions, not summaries: Anscombe and the outlier question

    Anscombe's four datasets share the same means, spread, correlation and fitted line, yet one is a noisy line, one a curve, and two are hostage to a single point. Plot first, because summaries invite you to imagine a dataset that may not exist.

    What do you do with an outlier? If it is an error, fix it and its source. If it is real and rare, keep it and summarise with medians and percentiles. If it is the thing you're looking for, as in fraud and AML, deleting it deletes the target.

    Choosing a chart by the question

    Start from the question. Position on a common scale (dots, lines) reads best, then length, then angle and area. Bars must start at zero, because their length is the value. Title the chart with the finding.

    Charts that mislead, and how to catch them

    Most misleading charts aren't forged. The numbers are real; the axis, aggregation and window were chosen to favour a conclusion.

    • Truncated axes. Bars starting at 90 make 92 to 95 look like a 150% jump when the data moved about 3%.
    • Dual axes. Axis ends can make any two lines overlap, and two trending series look related anyway.
    • Averages hiding spread. A flat bank-wide average can hide collapsing branches.

    Treat these as choices to declare, not tricks to avoid. If you truncate, say so on the axis. If you aggregate, show spread.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Summarising before looking

      A dashboard says "average salary AED 115,000" and nobody plotted the column.

      Spot itAsk for the log-axis histogram and the ten most frequent values.
    • Mixed units and mishandled blanks

      Fils with dirhams, or blanks replaced by the average and a tight interval reported.

      Spot itA second hump on a log axis, or a spike and too little spread.
    • Fan-out and hidden inner joins

      A total triples after a join, or customers silently vanish.

      Spot itCompare row counts and totals before and after each join.
    • Deleting outliers that are the signal, and misleading charts

      "We removed everything above the 99th percentile" from fraud data; truncated bars; dual axes implying a link.

      Spot itAsk what share of known positives was removed, and check axis starts and ranges.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A few percent of a salary column holds the placeholder 999,999. What happens to the mean and median?

    The mean jumps several times over, while the median barely moves. The gap between them is the alarm, but the median alone would hide the damage.

    2. One customer with a AED 40,000 account has three cards. What does SUM(balance) give after joining?

    AED 120,000, because the balance repeats per card. Aggregate to one row per customer first and check against a control total.

    3. Income is blank for 30% of customers. What can and can't you test?

    Comparing blank and non-blank rows can rule out MCAR. MAR versus MNAR can't be tested, because it depends on the missing values.

    4. A bar chart axis starts at 90 and shows 92 then 95. Why is it misleading?

    The bars are 2 and 5 tall, so the data's 3% move looks like 150%. Redraw from zero, or use a line with the scale marked.
