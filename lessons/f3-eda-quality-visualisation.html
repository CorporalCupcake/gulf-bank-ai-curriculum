    F3.1 Day one About 7 minutes

    Exploring Data, Checking Quality and Honest Charts

    Part 1

    What exploring data means

    In one sentenceExploratory analysis is looking at your data before you trust it or model it: checking what is in each column, what is missing, and what is odd.

    Say a staff survey asks for monthly salary, and the average comes back as 115,000. Most people earn around 14,000, so that is absurd.

    A quick plot shows why. Some salaries were typed in cents (100 times too big), some rows hold the placeholder 999,999 for "not given", and some say 0. The average added them all up without complaint.

    How it differs from modelling and testing

    • Modelling asks "can I predict this?". Exploring asks "is this data what I think it is?". Do it first.
    • Hypothesis testing checks one specific claim. Exploring has no fixed question: you look around and write down what surprises you.
    Part 2

    How to check a new table

    Run the same short checklist on every new table. It takes minutes and catches most disasters.

    1. What is one row? One customer, one account, one transaction? Everything else depends on this.
    2. Count rows against unique ids. If 1,000 rows hold 940 distinct customers, you have duplicates.
    3. Count blanks per column. Note which columns are mostly empty.
    4. Plot each column and list its most common values. Spikes at 0, 999999, 1900-01-01 or "N/A" are placeholders (called sentinels) pretending to be data.
    5. Check totals against a number you trust, such as last month's report.

    What odd values usually mean

    • Placeholders: old systems had no way to say "unknown", so people invented values.
    • Unit mix-ups: dollars against cents. Each value looks fine alone, but a second hump appears in the plot.
    • Outliers: if it is an error, fix it. If it is real and rare, keep it and summarise with the median. If it is the thing you are hunting, as with fraud, deleting it deletes your target.
    Part 3

    How to read what you find

    Mean against median

    The mean is the average. The median is the middle value, so extreme rows barely move it. A big gap between the two is a cheap alarm.

    Rule of thumbPay data is naturally a little skewed, so a mean about 1.2 times the median is normal. A mean 8 times the median means look for bad rows.

    Why a value is missing matters more than how many

    • Missing at random: people skip a question by chance. You just have less data, and dropping those rows is fine.
    • Missing because of something you recorded: homeowners skip the income question more than renters, and you know who owns. You can reweight to fix it.
    • Missing because of the value itself: high earners decline to say. Your answers are biased and no trick can tell by how much.

    You cannot test which case you are in from the data. Argue it from how the form or system works. An extra "was missing" column often helps, since missingness can be a signal.

    Why plot first

    Anscombe's quartet (1973) is four small datasets with the same average, spread, correlation and fitted line. One is a noisy line, one a curve, and two hang on a single point. Plot before you summarise.

    Part 4

    Honest charts

    Most misleading charts use real numbers. The axis, the grouping or the time window was simply chosen to favour a conclusion. Here the same two values, 92 and 95, are drawn twice.

    • Truncated axis: bars must start at zero, because their length is the value. A 3% rise should not look like 150%.
    • Two y-axes: you can slide the ends until any two lines match.
    • Averages hiding spread: a flat overall line can hide a few branches getting much worse. Show them too.

    Which chart to use

    QuestionChartWatch out for
    How does it change over time?LineDo not cut the axis to hide scale
    How do categories compare?Bars from zeroSort them, keep labels short
    Do two measures move together?Scatter plotCheck for one point driving the pattern

    Title the chart with the finding, such as "Complaints doubled in March".

    In banking, a data feed with a quiet placeholder or a doubled join can distort a risk figure for months, so profile every new source before anyone models it.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Summarising a column you never looked at

      Plot it, sort it and list its common values before quoting any average.

    • Deleting every outlier

      Some are errors, but some are the cases you care about, such as fraud.

    • Filling blanks with the average without asking why they are blank

      If high earners are the ones who skipped, the average is already too low and filling it in hides that.

    1. A salary column has mean 115,000 and median 14,000. What do you do?

    Plot it and look for placeholders, zeros and values typed in the wrong units. The big gap is the alarm.

    2. When is it safe to drop rows with missing values?

    When values are missing purely by chance. If missingness depends on other fields or on the value itself, dropping rows biases the result.

    3. A bar chart starts at 90 and B looks three times taller than A. What is wrong?

    The axis does not start at zero, so bar length no longer matches the value. Restart at zero.

    4. You found a bad value by eye. What is the next step?

    Fix it at the source if you can, and add an automated check so the next data load fails loudly.
