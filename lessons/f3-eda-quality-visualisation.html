    Exploratory Analysis, Data Quality and Honest Charts

    How to interrogate a table before you trust a single number from it, why summaries can describe a dataset that doesn't exist, and how a chart can argue a case the data doesn't support.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA number is only as good as the rows behind it and the picture it's shown in, so look at the raw distribution, hunt for the codes and mix-ups that pose as data, and treat every chart as an argument someone chose to make.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new analyst at a retail bank is asked a simple thing: what is the average monthly salary of our salary-transfer customers? She runs the query. The answer is AED 115,000. That's absurd for a mass-market product, but the number came out of the warehouse, so it carries an air of authority.

    Then she plots the column instead of summarising it. Most customers sit near AED 14,000. A second small hump sits a hundred times further out, around AED 1.4 million. Those rows are salaries stored in fils (a hundredth of a dirham) by one legacy feed. A thin spike at AED 999,999 is a placeholder meaning "not provided". Another block of rows says 0. The mean was real arithmetic on rows that were never salaries.

    Nothing in the summary warned her. The mean is perfectly happy to average a dirham with a fils. Only a picture, or a question like "what's the largest value, and how many times does it repeat?", makes the problem visible.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Data quality problems don't announce themselves. A sentinel or a unit mix-up is a valid number in a valid column. No type check, no null check and no model will object.
    • Summaries compress, and compression hides. The mean, the median and the standard deviation each throw away almost everything about the shape. Part 2 shows four datasets with identical summaries and four different stories.
    • The same discipline applies to charts you make. A chart is also a compression, and its design choices (where the axis starts, what is averaged, which window is shown) decide what a reader will conclude. Exploring honestly and presenting honestly are the same skill pointed in two directions.
    Hold onto thisNever summarise a column you haven't looked at. Plot it, sort it, count its most frequent values, and ask where each row came from. Most "model problems" in a bank are data problems that nobody looked for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Profiling: asking the table what it is

    Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is the habit of looking before modelling. John Tukey, who named it, framed it as detective work: you aren't testing a hypothesis yet, you're finding out what's there, including what shouldn't be.

    The first pass is profiling, a fixed checklist you run on every new table. It takes minutes and catches most disasters. The questions below are the checklist.

    QuestionWhat it catchesBank example

    Much of this is two or three lines of SQL, and you don't need pandas to ask it:

    -- grain and duplicates
SELECT COUNT(*) AS n_rows,
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS n_customers,
       COUNT(*) - COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS extra_rows
FROM   customers;

-- the most frequent values in a column: sentinels show up here first
SELECT salary, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM   customers
GROUP  BY salary
ORDER  BY n DESC
LIMIT  10;

    Ask the same questions again over time. A column that was 2% null last year and 40% null this quarter is a pipeline change, and it'll hurt a model trained on the earlier period. Also check the profile against what the business expects. If product says the typical customer has two cards and the table says eleven, one of you is wrong, and it's usually the table.

    A last habit worth building: keep the profile. Write the checks down as automated tests (the field is called data validation) so the next load fails loudly instead of quietly. Part 4 covers the tools.

    Sentinels, impossible values and unit mix-ups

    Old systems had no good way to say "unknown", so people invented values. These are sentinel values. They live inside real columns and look like data.

    • Numbers: 0, −1, 99, 999, 9999, 99999, 999999. A date-of-birth age of 999. A balance of 0 meaning "not loaded" rather than empty. The danger of 9999 is that it's also a plausible salary, so you can't filter it blindly: check how often it repeats.
    • Dates: 1900-01-01 and 1970-01-01 (the zero of Excel-era and Unix clocks), plus 9999-12-31 and 2099-12-31 meaning "no end date". Compute an age from 1900-01-01 and you get a 126-year-old customer. Compute a tenure to 9999-12-31 and you get nonsense, or an overflow.
    • Text: "N/A", "NULL", "-", ".", "UNKNOWN", "TBD", a single space. Each is a different spelling of missing, and none is a null.

    Impossible values are the cousin: a negative age, a transaction dated before the account opened, a closing date earlier than the opening date, a card expiry in the past on an active card. Put these rules in writing. They are the cheapest validation you have, because a domain expert can state them without looking at data.

    Unit and currency mix-ups are the nastiest because every value is individually reasonable. The classic Gulf cases:

    • Dirhams versus fils (1 AED = 100 fils), and riyals versus halalas (1 SAR = 100 halalas). One feed stores minor units, another stores major.
    • Mixed currencies in one amount column, with the currency code in a different table or missing. AED, USD (to which the dirham is pegged at about 3.6725) and a dozen others get summed together. Sums are meaningless; so are fraud thresholds.
    • Thousands versus units after a spreadsheet export, and different decimal conventions in text amounts ("1.250,00" against "1,250.00").
    • Time zones. A "daily" total cut at midnight UTC is not a Gulf calendar day (UTC+4 in the UAE, UTC+3 in Saudi Arabia), so late-evening transactions move into the next day, and month-end totals stop reconciling.
    • Arabic digits and text. The same phone number or amount in Western (0–9) or Arabic-Indic (٠–٩) digits; names with different transliterations. Joins and duplicate checks that rely on exact string matches silently miss these.

    How do you spot a unit problem? Not by reading values, but by looking at the distribution on a log axis. A ×100 error doesn't fatten the tail, it creates a second hump two decades along. Simulation 1 above is exactly that picture. Another trick: if a column is meant to be in one unit, its ratio to a related column (salary to credit limit, transaction to balance) should be stable. Where it jumps by 100, you've found the feed.

    Watch outThe median barely moved when you added sentinels in Simulation 1. That's why robust statistics are comforting, and why they're dangerous. A model fed that column still sees the junk, and the median hides it from your dashboard. Fix the data, don't just choose a statistic that hides the damage.

    Duplicates and joins that quietly change the count

    There are three kinds of duplicate, and they need different cures.

    • Exact duplicates: the same row twice, often from a pipeline that retried after a timeout. SELECT DISTINCT removes them, but ask first why they exist. If retries duplicate rows, they may also be duplicating payments.
    • Key duplicates: the same customer ID with two different rows (two addresses, two versions). You need a rule for which one wins: the latest, the verified one. "Latest" needs a reliable timestamp.
    • Entity duplicates: the same real person under two IDs. One customer with a UAE account opened in 2015 and another in 2022 under a slightly different spelling of the name. This one is a record-linkage problem, with fuzzy matching on name (including Arabic-to-English transliteration), date of birth and Emirates ID or national ID. It matters for exposure limits, KYC and fraud rings, because the bank may be treating one risk as two.

    Then there's the join. The most common wrong number in banking analytics isn't a bad value, it's a fan-out: joining a table at one grain to a table at a finer grain, then summing a column from the coarser side.

    customer_idaccount_balance (AED)card_id
    1049240,000C1
    1049240,000C2
    1049240,000C3

    One customer, one account of AED 40,000, three cards. After joining customers to cards, SUM(account_balance) returns AED 120,000. The query ran, the numbers look plausible, and the deposit base is overstated threefold. The defences are habits. State the grain of every table before joining. Check that row counts after a join match what you expect (a one-to-one join shouldn't add rows). Aggregate to the target grain before joining. And reconcile to a control total.

    The mirror-image error is the dropped join: an inner join silently deletes every row without a match. If 8% of customers have no row in the bureau table, the join removes them, and your population is now "customers with a bureau file". That's a selection effect (F2.6), created by one keyword.

    -- a one-to-one join should not change the row count
SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers)                      AS before_join,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers c
        JOIN bureau b ON b.customer_id = c.customer_id)      AS after_join;

    Missing data: why it's missing matters more than how much

    A null is a measurement that didn't happen. The right treatment depends on why. Donald Rubin's 1976 classification is still how everyone talks about it, and it's worth knowing by name.

    MechanismMeaningBank exampleIs the complete-case average biased?
    MCAR
    missing completely at random    		Missingness is unrelated to anything, observed or not.A batch job drops a random 30% of income fields.No. You just have less data.
    MAR
    missing at random    		Missingness depends only on things you've observed.Affluent-segment customers skip the income question more often, and segment is recorded.Yes, overall; but fixable by modelling or weighting on the observed variable.
    MNAR
    missing not at random    		Missingness depends on the missing value itself.Higher earners decline to state income, even within a segment.Yes, and the data can't tell you how much.

    The formal version is a statement about the missingness indicator R (1 if observed):

    P(R=1∣Yobs,Ymis)=P(R=1∣Yobs)(MAR)

    The chance a value is recorded may depend on what you can see (Yobs), but not on the value that's missing (Ymis). Under MCAR it doesn't depend on either. Under MNAR it depends on Ymis, which by definition you don't have, so MAR versus MNAR cannot be tested from the data alone. It's an assumption you defend with knowledge of the process. This is selection bias (F2.6) with the rows still present but the cells empty.

    What you do about it follows from the mechanism, and each tempting shortcut has a cost:

    • Drop incomplete rows (complete-case analysis). Fine under MCAR. Under MAR or MNAR it changes who is in your data.
    • Fill with the mean or median. It keeps the mean and destroys the variance: the filled column is artificially tight, so intervals shrink and correlations weaken. Simulation 3 shows the size of the shrinkage.
    • Add a missing-indicator feature. Often the best move for prediction, because missingness itself can be a strong signal. A customer who won't state an employer is different from one who does (F3.9). The price is that it learns the process, not the person.
    • Model-based or multiple imputation. Predict the missing value from other columns, and repeat with noise so the uncertainty carries through. Sound under MAR if the model's right.
    • Get the value from elsewhere. For MNAR, an outside source (salary-transfer data, bureau data) is worth more than any clever statistics.

    Distributions, not summaries: Anscombe and the outlier question

    In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets, each of eleven (x, y) pairs. All four have the same mean of x, the same mean of y, the same variances, the same correlation (0.816) and the same fitted line (y ≈ 3.0 + 0.5x). Plotted, one is a plain noisy line, one is a perfect curve, one is a perfect line spoiled by a single outlier, and one is eleven points in a column with a single point far to the right deciding the whole slope. Anscombe's argument: plot first, because summary numbers invite you to imagine a dataset that may not exist.

    In 2017 Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice at Autodesk pushed the idea to its limit. Using simulated annealing (nudge points at random, keep the nudge if the summary statistics hold), they produced the Datasaurus Dozen: a dinosaur, a star, a circle, parallel lines and others, all sharing the same means, standard deviations and correlation to two decimal places.

    Banking has its own versions. Two branches with the same average complaint-resolution time, one consistently 3 days and one split between same-day and 6 weeks. Two fraud models with the same average loss per case, one steady and one dominated by three huge misses. The mean tells you where the middle is, and nothing about the tail, which is where banks lose money.

    That leaves the question every analyst meets: what do you do with an outlier? There is no rule that works without knowing what it is.

    • It's an error (a sentinel, a ×100 feed, a typo): fix or remove it, and fix the source.
    • It's real and rare (a corporate account in a retail table; a genuine AED 4 million property purchase): keep it, but consider whether it belongs in this population, and summarise robustly (median, quantiles).
    • It's the thing you're looking for. In fraud, AML and credit, the extreme rows are often the signal. Winsorising or deleting them to make a model "stable" can delete the target.

    Money amounts are naturally heavy-tailed (F1.5): a few huge values dominate totals. So report medians and percentiles beside the mean, look at amounts on a log axis, and for sums ask what share the top 1% contributes. Check whether the same ten customers account for half the value before you describe a "typical" one.

    Choosing a chart by the question

    The mistake is to start from a chart type. Start from the question the reader needs answered, then choose the encoding the eye reads most accurately. Cleveland and McGill's 1984 experiments ranked how well people judge quantities from different visual features. Roughly, from best to worst: position along a common scale, then position on non-aligned scales, then length, then angle and slope, then area, then volume and colour shade. That's why a dot or line chart often beats a pie, and why bars should start at zero (their length is the value).

    Some practical defaults: label the chart with the finding, not the topic ("Fraud losses rose 8% in Q3, driven by card-not-present"); show the unit and the period; use one highlight colour and grey for the rest; avoid red and green together, which colour-blind readers cannot separate; and always put the base (the number of customers or cases) near the picture. If the story needs a note to be understood, the chart isn't finished.

    Charts that mislead, and how to catch them

    Most misleading charts aren't forged. The numbers are real and the chart is technically correct. The choices (axis, aggregation, window, scale) were made to support a conclusion, sometimes on purpose and often not. The usual suspects:

    • Truncated axes. A bar chart whose axis starts at 90 makes a move from 92 to 95 look like a 150% jump. For bars, truncation is a lie, since the bar's length encodes the value. Line charts may start away from zero when the question is about change, but the reader still needs to see the scale.
    • Dual axes. Two lines with different scales can be made to overlap, cross or diverge by choosing the axis ends. The correlation never changes under rescaling, only the picture does. Two trending series will also look related, which is why this form is so persuasive.
    • Averages that hide spread. A bank-wide average can be flat while some branches improve and others collapse; a mean with no range suggests a tight cluster that may not exist. Show the distribution, or at least the range or quantiles.
    • Aggregation and window. Monthly data may look noisy, yearly data smooth. A window that begins at a seasonal peak will show a decline. A cumulative chart always rises. Choose the grain that matches the decision.
    • Log scales. Honest for growth rates and quantities spanning orders of magnitude, but exponential growth then looks like a steady straight line and a 10× change can seem small. A log axis should always be labelled as one. Never put bars on one.
    • Area and volume. Scale a circle or an icon's width and height by the value and its area grows with the square. Double the number, double the radius, and the picture is four times bigger.
    • Missing context. No base rate, no denominator, no comparison period, a map that shows the number of cases without population. "Fraud cases up 40%" while transactions rose 60%.

    Edward Tufte put a number on distortion, the lie factor:

    Lie factor=size of effect shown in the graphicsize of effect in the data,where effect size=b−aa

    Effect size is the change from the first value (a) to the last (b) as a share of the first, measured separately on the drawn heights or areas and on the data. A lie factor of 1 is honest. Values far above 1 exaggerate; values below 1 hide a change. A bar chart from 92 to 95 on an axis starting at 90: drawn bars are 2 and 5 units tall, so the graphic shows +150%; the data shows +3.3%; the lie factor is about 46.

    A final point about honesty. Don't treat this as a list of tricks to avoid, treat it as a list of choices to declare. When you do truncate a line chart, say so in the axis. When you aggregate, show a spread. When you pick a window, show why. A chart's job is to make the real decision easier, and the reader's trust is the asset you're spending.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson sits at the start of every project and the end of every report. These are the places it comes back.

    F1.4 – F1.5 · Base rates and distributionsHeavy tails, and why the mean misleads

    Transaction amounts and balances are heavy-tailed, so means are fragile and totals are dominated by a few rows. Base rates are what make a chart's counts meaningful.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and the bootstrapShow the uncertainty on the chart

    A bar with no interval claims a precision it doesn't have. Bootstrap intervals are the honest error bar for medians and ratios.

    F2.3 – F2.4 · Tests and many comparisonsExploration is a forking path

    EDA generates hypotheses; the dozens of plots you looked at are comparisons you never counted. Confirm findings on fresh data before you report them.

    F2.6 · Confounding and selectionMissingness is selection

    MNAR is selection bias with the rows kept. An inner join, a filter and a survey non-response all select on something. Averages hiding spread is Simpson's paradox waiting to happen.

    F7 · Precision, recall, calibrationMetrics are only as good as labels

    A mislabelled fraud case, or a duplicate in the test set, inflates or deflates every metric. Reliability diagrams and precision-recall curves are charts to read with the lie factor in mind.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeThe quality check models need most

    A column that quietly holds a post-event value, or a table whose "current" status overwrote history, is a data-quality fault with a modelling cost. Profile time stamps like you profile values.

    F3.3 · SQL thinkingWhere the grain bugs live

    Fan-outs, dropped joins and window functions over duplicates all happen in SQL. Row-count checks after every join are the habit that connects the two lessons.

    F3.4 · F3.9 · Features and encodingsSentinels become signals, or poison

    Once a 999 sneaks into a feature, a tree model will split on it and a linear model will treat it as a very rich customer. Missing-value handling is a feature-engineering decision.

    F3.5 · F3.7 · Imbalanced data and reportingWhat the rare class looks like, and how to write it up

    For rare fraud, you need to see the positives, not just the percentages. And every analysis write-up should carry the population, base and chart choices stated in this lesson.

    Data governance and model validationLineage, ownership and sign-off

    Supervisors expect banks to know where risk data comes from, how accurate it is and who owns it (the BCBS 239 principles). Validators ask for exactly the profiling evidence in Part 2.

    AML, KYC and fraud workEntity resolution and thresholds

    Duplicate customers hide exposure and rings; unit errors break thresholds; transliteration breaks screening matches. Data quality here is a control, not a nicety.

    Where the field is now

    The ideas here are old and the checklists haven't changed much. What has changed is the tooling around them: data checks written as code and run on every load, and large language models joining both sides, cleaning data and judging charts. The evidence so far says they help, with limits.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you'll meet in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Summarising before looking

      A dashboard shows "average salary AED 115,000" and nobody has plotted the column.

      Spot itAsk to see the histogram on a log axis, and the ten most frequent values. If neither exists, the number is unverified.
    • Sentinels treated as values

      1900-01-01 turned into an age, 999 into a tenure, 0 into a balance.

      Spot itLook for spikes: the most common value of any date or amount column should make business sense. Compare the max to what is physically possible.
    • Mixed units and currencies

      Fils and dirhams in one column; AED and USD summed; UTC days used as Gulf days.

      Spot itSecond hump on a log axis, a ×100 jump in a ratio between two columns, month-end totals that miss the ledger by a few percent.
    • Fan-out joins

      A total that triples after joining to a finer table.

      Spot itCompare row counts before and after each join, and compare the total to a control. State the grain of every table.
    • An inner join as a hidden filter

      Customers with no bureau record vanish, and nobody mentions the population changed.

      Spot itAsk how many rows each join drops, and who they are. Prefer a left join and look at the unmatched rows.
    • Mean-filling missing values

      Replacing blanks with the average, then reporting a tight confidence interval and strong correlations.

      Spot itThe imputed column has an unnaturally tall spike at one value and a standard deviation below that of the observed data.
    • Assuming missing means random

      "We dropped the 30% with no income" when the missing ones are mostly the affluent ones.

      Spot itCompare observed attributes of missing and complete rows. If they differ, it isn't MCAR. For MNAR, ask for an outside source or a sensitivity analysis.
    • Deleting the outliers that are the signal

      "We removed anything above the 99th percentile" from a fraud or AML dataset.

      Spot itAsk what share of known positives was among the removed rows.
    • Bars on a truncated axis, or averages without spread

      A small gain drawn as a leap; a branch league table of means with no range.

      Spot itCheck where the axis starts and whether the bars' ratios match the numbers' ratios. Ask for the spread, the count and an interval.
    • A dual-axis chart used to imply a link

      Complaints and card spend tracking each other perfectly.

      Spot itAsk for the two axis ranges and for the correlation after removing trend. Rescaling the axes can produce any picture.
    • AI-generated analysis that skips the looking

      An LLM writes confident code that groups, averages and plots, and never checks for duplicates, units or sentinel codes. Its charts may default to truncated axes or 3D.

      Spot itTypical signs: no row counts shown, no missingness table, every column assumed clean. Ask what profiling was done, and ask to see the code that did it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The headline average salary of AED 115,000 is inflated by about 2% of rows holding a placeholder and 5% stored in fils. Cleaned, the typical salary-transfer customer earns about AED 14,000 a month, and the average is closer to AED 16,000. I'd like to fix the feed and add an automatic check, so this can't recur, before we size the campaign on these numbers.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Before modelling I profile every table: grain, key uniqueness, null rate over time, most frequent values, log-scale histograms and a reconciliation to ledger totals. I look for sentinels and unit mixes, check join row counts, and classify missingness as MCAR, MAR or MNAR with evidence. Those checks become automated tests that run on every load. For reporting, I choose a chart from the question, keep bars on zero, show spread, state the base, and I'd compute the lie factor on anything contentious.”
    “Can't we just drop the bad rows and move on?”

    Sometimes, but first we need to know why they're bad and who they are. If the bad rows are random, dropping them costs us only sample size. If they are concentrated in one channel, product or period, removing them changes the population and we'd be building a model for customers who happen to have clean records. I'd quantify who they are, fix the source if possible, and record what we did.

    “The model handles missing values automatically, so does it matter?”

    The software handles them mechanically, but it can't know why they're missing. Tree libraries route nulls down a branch, which can be fine, and the pattern of missingness can even be predictive. But it can also learn the quirks of one data feed or a past process, and then break when the feed changes. I'd still want a missingness table by segment and over time.

    “Why do you keep asking for the median and the distribution? The mean is what finance uses.”

    Both, and the mean is fine for totals. But for a typical customer, bank amounts are skewed, and the mean is dominated by a few large values or by bad rows. If the mean is far above the median, I want to know why before we use either. The distribution plot costs nothing and has caught more problems than any other check.

    “The chart is accurate. Every number is right. What's the problem?”

    A chart can be accurate and still persuade people of something untrue. If the bars start at 90, a 3% change looks like a threefold rise. I'd redraw it with the axis at zero or switch to a line with the scale shown, and add the range across branches. Then it supports the decision rather than steering it.

    “Can we get an AI tool to clean the data and build the dashboards?”

    For drafting, yes. It can propose profiling queries, spot inconsistent spellings and write validation rules faster than I can. But surveys and benchmarks suggest that these tools aren't reliable on subtle faults, and one 2025 benchmark found multimodal models weak at spotting misleading charts. So I'd use it with review, make it show its checks, and test its output with the same profile I'd use on a person's work.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the arithmetic in Part 2.

    1. Of 1,000 salary rows, 960 have an average of AED 15,000 and 40 hold the placeholder 999,999. What is the mean, and what happens to the median?

    Mean = (960 × 15,000 + 40 × 999,999) ÷ 1,000 = 14,400 + 40,000 ≈ AED 54,400, more than three and a half times the true figure. The median stays near the middle of the genuine rows, around AED 15,000 or slightly above, because 4% of rows can't move it far. A mean-to-median ratio of 3.6 is the alarm.

    2. A customer has one AED 40,000 account and three cards. After joining the customer to cards, SUM(balance) gives what? What would you do instead?

    AED 120,000: the balance is repeated on all three card rows (a fan-out). Aggregate cards to one row per customer (or sum balances before the join), keep the grain at customer level, and check the result against a control total.

    3. In 100 transactions, 95 are in AED averaging 400 and 5 are in fils with the same real average. What is the mean of the column, and how many times too high is it?

    The 5 fils rows average 40,000. Mean = (95 × 400 + 5 × 40,000) ÷ 100 = (38,000 + 200,000) ÷ 100 = 2,380, which is 5.95 times the true 400. Five percent bad rows nearly sextupled the average. On a log histogram you would see a second hump two decades to the right.

    4. Income is blank for 30% of customers. How do you decide between MCAR, MAR and MNAR, and what can't you test?

    Compare the observed variables (segment, age, product, channel, branch) of blank and non-blank rows. Differences rule out MCAR. If a model on those variables explains the blanks well, MAR is plausible, and reweighting or imputing from them can help. MAR versus MNAR can't be tested from the data, because it depends on the incomes you don't have. You argue it from how the form works, and test sensitivity or bring in an outside source such as salary-transfer records.

    5. A bar chart axis starts at 90 and shows 92 then 95. What is the lie factor?

    The drawn bars are 2 and 5 units tall: effect +150%. The data moved from 92 to 95: +3.26%. Lie factor ≈ 150 ÷ 3.26 ≈ 46. Redraw the bars from zero (the lie factor is then 1), or use a line chart with the scale clearly marked.

    6. An infographic shows a figure twice as big in dirhams by doubling both a circle's width and height. How misleading is it?

    Doubling the radius multiplies area by four, so the graphic shows +300% for a +100% change, lie factor 3. Scale by area instead (radius × √2 ≈ 1.41), or better, use bars and compare lengths.

    7. A dual-axis chart shows complaints and card spend moving in lockstep. Your manager says "they're clearly related". What do you check?

    The two axis ranges, which can be chosen to make any pair overlap; the correlation, which doesn't depend on axis choice; and whether both series simply trend upward (any two trending series correlate). Then compare changes, or detrended values, and put the series side by side rather than overlaid. If the relationship matters, test it properly (F2) and ask about confounders (F2.6).
