    Exploratory Analysis, Data Quality and Honest Charts

    How to interrogate a table before you trust a single number from it, why summaries can describe a dataset that doesn't exist, and how a chart can argue a case the data doesn't support.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA number is only as good as the rows behind it and the picture it's shown in, so look at the raw distribution, hunt for the codes and mix-ups that pose as data, and treat every chart as an argument someone chose to make.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An analyst asks for the average salary of salary-transfer customers and gets AED 115,000. Absurd for a mass-market product, but it came from the warehouse, so it looks authoritative. Think of a bathroom scale with a book on it: the arithmetic is right and the answer is wrong.

    She plots the column instead. Most customers sit near AED 14,000. A small hump sits 100 times further out: salaries stored in fils (a hundredth of a dirham) by one legacy feed. A spike at 999,999 stands for "not provided". A block of rows says 0. The mean averaged dirhams with fils and never complained. Only a picture, or the question "what is the largest value and how often does it repeat?", exposes this.

    • Quality problems don't announce themselves. A sentinel (a placeholder code) or a unit mix-up is a valid number in a valid column, so no type or null check objects.
    • Summaries compress, and compression hides. Mean and median throw away the shape. Part 2 shows four datasets with identical summaries and four different stories.
    • Charts are compression too. Where the axis starts and what is averaged decide what the reader concludes.
    Hold onto thisNever summarise a column you haven't looked at. Plot it, sort it, count its most frequent values, and ask where each row came from. Most "model problems" are data problems nobody looked for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Profiling: asking the table what it is

    Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is looking before modelling, like a detective at a scene: you aren't testing anything yet, you're finding what is there, including what shouldn't be. The first pass is profiling, a fixed checklist run on every new table. It takes minutes and catches most disasters.

    QuestionWhat it catchesBank example
    What is one row? (the grain)Joins that multiply rows; counting the wrong thing.A customer, an account, a card or a customer-month? "Active customers" depends on it.

    Much of it is a few lines of SQL. This one shows sentinels first:

    SELECT salary, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM   customers
GROUP  BY salary
ORDER  BY n DESC
LIMIT  10;

    Repeat the checks over time. A column 2% null last year and 40% null this quarter is a pipeline change, and it will hurt a model trained on the earlier period. Turn the checks into automated tests so the next load fails loudly.

    Sentinels, impossible values and unit mix-ups

    Old systems had no way to say "unknown", so people invented values. These sentinel values sit inside real columns and look like data.

    • Numbers: 0, −1, 99, 9999, 999999. 9999 is also a plausible salary, so check how often it repeats before filtering.
    • Dates: 1900-01-01 and 1970-01-01 (clock zeros), 9999-12-31 meaning "no end date". A birth date of 1900-01-01 gives a 126-year-old customer.
    • Text: "N/A", "UNKNOWN", "-", a single space. Each is a spelling of missing, and none is a null.

    Impossible values are the cousin: a negative age, a closing date before the opening date. A domain expert can state these rules without seeing data, so they are the cheapest validation you have.

    Unit and currency mix-ups are nastiest because every value looks reasonable. Gulf cases:

    • Major versus minor units: dirhams and fils (1 AED = 100 fils), riyals and halalas.
    • Mixed currencies in one amount column, with the code elsewhere. Totals and fraud thresholds become meaningless.
    • Time zones: a "daily" total cut at midnight UTC is not a Gulf day, so month-end stops reconciling.

    To spot a unit problem, plot the log-axis distribution. A ×100 error doesn't fatten the tail, it makes a second hump two decades along (Simulation 1).

    Watch outThe median barely moved when you added sentinels in Simulation 1. That makes it comforting and dangerous: a model still sees the junk while the median hides it on your dashboard. Fix the data; don't pick a statistic that hides the damage.

    Duplicates and joins that quietly change the count

    Three kinds of duplicate need three cures.

    • Exact duplicates: the same row twice, often a retry after a timeout. SELECT DISTINCT removes them, but ask why they exist: retries that duplicate rows may duplicate payments.
    • Key duplicates: one customer ID, two rows (two address versions). You need a rule for which wins, such as the latest verified one.
    • Entity duplicates: one person under two IDs, with slightly different spellings. Fuzzy matching on name, date of birth and national ID finds them. It matters for exposure limits, KYC and fraud rings, where one risk is counted as two.

    The most common wrong number in banking analytics is a fan-out: join a coarse table to a finer one, then sum a column from the coarse side. Like counting a family's one bank balance once per child.

    customer_idaccount_balance (AED)card_id
    1049240,000C1
    1049240,000C2
    1049240,000C3

    One customer, one AED 40,000 account, three cards. After the join, SUM(account_balance) gives AED 120,000. The query ran and the number looks plausible, but deposits are overstated threefold. Defences: state each table's grain before joining, check that row counts match your expectation afterwards, and reconcile to a control total.

    The mirror error is the dropped join: an inner join deletes every row without a match. If 8% of customers have no bureau record, your population silently becomes "customers with a bureau file". That is a selection effect (F2.6) made by one keyword.

    Missing data: why it's missing matters more than how much

    A null is a measurement that didn't happen, and the right fix depends on why. Think of an exam where some students skip questions: skipping at random is harmless, skipping the hard ones is not.

    MechanismMeaningBank exampleIs the complete-case average biased?
    MCAR
    missing completely at random    		Missingness is unrelated to anything, observed or not.A batch job drops a random 30% of income fields.No. You just have less data.
    MAR
    missing at random    		Missingness depends only on things you've observed.Affluent-segment customers skip the income question more often, and segment is recorded.Yes, overall; but fixable by modelling or weighting on the observed variable.
    MNAR
    missing not at random    		Missingness depends on the missing value itself.Higher earners decline to state income, even within a segment.Yes, and the data can't tell you how much.

    MAR versus MNAR cannot be tested from the data, because the answer depends on the values that are missing. You defend it with knowledge of how the form or process works. The mechanism decides the fix:

    • Drop incomplete rows. Fine under MCAR. Otherwise it changes who is in your data.
    • Fill with the mean or median. Keeps the average but crushes the spread: intervals shrink and correlations weaken (Simulation 3).
    • Add a missing-indicator feature. Often best for prediction, since missingness can itself be a signal.
    • Model-based imputation, or an outside source. Predicting from other columns is sound under MAR. For MNAR, salary-transfer or bureau data beats clever statistics.

    Distributions, not summaries: Anscombe and the outlier question

    Two football teams can both average 1.5 goals a game, one scoring 1-2 every week and one alternating 0 and 5. The average can't tell them apart. Anscombe (1973) made this point with four datasets that share the same means, spread, correlation (0.816) and fitted line, yet look like a noisy line, a perfect curve, a line spoiled by one outlier, and a column of points with one far-right point deciding the whole slope. Lesson: plot first, because summaries invite you to imagine a dataset that may not exist.

    Banking versions: two branches with the same average resolution time, one steady at 3 days and one split between same-day and 6 weeks. The mean locates the middle and says nothing about the tail, where banks lose money.

    What do you do with an outlier? It depends on what it is.

    • An error (sentinel, ×100 feed, typo): fix or remove it, and fix the source.
    • Real and rare (a corporate account in a retail table): keep it, ask whether it belongs in this population, and summarise robustly with medians and quantiles.
    • The thing you're looking for. In fraud, AML and credit the extreme rows are often the signal. Deleting them to make a model "stable" can delete the target.

    Money is heavy-tailed, so a few values dominate totals. Report medians and percentiles beside the mean, use a log axis, and check whether ten customers hold half the value before describing a "typical" one.

    Choosing a chart by the question

    Start from the question, then pick the encoding the eye reads best. Position on a common scale (dots, lines) reads best, then length, then angle and area. So dots or lines often beat pies, and bars must start at zero, because their length is the value.

    The questionUsually the right chartTraps
    How big is each category?Sorted horizontal bars from zero.Pies with many slices; 3D bars.
    How does it change over time?Line chart, same scale and window for comparisons.Cherry-picked start and end; dual axes.
    What is the shape of the values?Histogram or box plot; log axis for money.A bar of the mean alone.
    Do two measures move together?Scatter plot with a smoothed trend.Correlation read as cause; overplotting.
    How do groups differ, including spread?Box or strip plots.Bars of averages with no spread.

    Defaults: title the chart with the finding ("Fraud losses rose 8% in Q3, driven by card-not-present"); show unit and period; use one highlight colour and grey for the rest; avoid red with green; show the base (number of customers or cases) beside the picture.

    Charts that mislead, and how to catch them

    Most misleading charts aren't forged. The numbers are real; the choices of axis, aggregation and window were made, often unthinkingly, to favour a conclusion.

    • Truncated axes. Bars starting at 90 make 92 to 95 look like a 150% jump when the data moved about 3%. For bars this is a lie, since length encodes value. Tufte's lie factor is the change the picture shows divided by the change in the data: 1 is honest, here it is about 46.
    • Dual axes. Choosing the axis ends makes two lines overlap, cross or diverge. Correlation never changes under rescaling, only the picture. Two trending series look related anyway.
    • Averages hiding spread. A bank-wide average can be flat while some branches improve and others collapse.
    • Aggregation, window and context. Yearly looks smooth where monthly is noisy; a window starting at a seasonal peak shows decline; "fraud cases up 40%" means little if transactions rose 60%.
    • Area and volume. Double a circle's width and height and its area quadruples.

    The circles show the area trap. Both right-hand circles mean "twice as much"; the faded one was scaled the common, wrong way.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes in bank analyses and AI-generated ones, each with the quickest check.

    • Summarising before looking

      A dashboard says "average salary AED 115,000" and nobody plotted the column. An LLM writes confident group-average code and never checks duplicates, units or sentinels.

      Spot itAsk for the log-axis histogram, the ten most frequent values and the profiling done. The most common value of any date or amount column should make business sense.
    • Mixed units and currencies

      Fils with dirhams, AED summed with USD, UTC days used as Gulf days.

      Spot itA second hump on a log axis, a ×100 jump in a column ratio, month-end totals missing the ledger by a few percent.
    • Fan-out joins and hidden inner-join filters

      A total triples after joining to a finer table, or customers with no bureau record vanish and nobody mentions the population changed.

      Spot itCompare row counts and totals before and after each join, and ask who each join drops. Prefer a left join and inspect the unmatched.
    • Mishandling missing values

      Blanks replaced by the average, then a tight interval reported; or "we dropped the 30% with no income" when most were affluent.

      Spot itThe imputed column has a tall spike and too little spread. Compare observed attributes of missing and complete rows: differences mean not MCAR.
    • Deleting outliers that are the signal

      "We removed anything above the 99th percentile" from fraud or AML data.

      Spot itAsk what share of known positives was among the removed rows.
    • Misleading charts

      Truncated bars, averages without spread, a dual axis that implies a link between two trending series.

      Spot itCheck where the axis starts, ask for spread and counts, and ask for both axis ranges. Rescaling can produce any picture.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. In a salary column, 4% of rows hold the placeholder 999,999 and the rest are around AED 15,000. What happens to the mean and to the median, and which one raises the alarm?

    The mean jumps to several times the truth (about AED 54,400), because each placeholder row pulls it hard. The median stays near AED 15,000 because 4% of rows can't move the middle. The large gap between mean and median is the alarm, but the median alone would hide the damage.

    2. A customer has one AED 40,000 account and three cards. After joining customer to cards, what does SUM(balance) give? What would you do instead?

    AED 120,000: the balance repeats on three card rows (a fan-out). Aggregate cards to one row per customer, or sum balances before the join, and check against a control total.

    3. A few percent of a transaction column is stored in fils instead of AED. What does it do to the mean, and how do you spot it?

    Each bad row is 100 times too large, so a handful nearly sextuples the mean (five in a hundred does it). On a log-axis histogram it shows up as a second hump two decades to the right, not just a fatter tail.

    4. Income is blank for 30% of customers. How do you decide between MCAR, MAR and MNAR, and what can't you test?

    Compare observed variables (segment, age, product, channel) between blank and non-blank rows. Differences rule out MCAR. If those variables explain the blanks, MAR is plausible, and reweighting or imputing helps. MAR versus MNAR can't be tested, because it depends on the incomes you don't have. Argue it from how the form works, run sensitivity checks, or bring in an outside source.

    5. A bar chart axis starts at 90 and shows 92 then 95. Why is it misleading, and what do you do?

    The bars are drawn 2 and 5 tall, so the second looks two and a half times the first when the data moved about 3%. The picture exaggerates the change roughly 46-fold (the lie factor). Redraw from zero, or use a line chart with the scale marked.

    6. An infographic shows a figure twice as big by doubling a circle's width and height. What goes wrong?

    Doubling the radius quadruples the area, so the eye sees four times as much. Scale the area instead (radius up by about 1.41), or better, use bars.

    7. A dual-axis chart shows complaints and card spend moving in lockstep. Your manager says "clearly related". What do you check?

    The axis ranges (which can make any pair overlap); the correlation (which ignores axis choice); and whether both simply trend upward (any two trending series correlate). Compare changes or detrended values, side by side. If it matters, ask about confounders (F2.6).
