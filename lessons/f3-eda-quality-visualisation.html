    Exploratory Analysis, Data Quality and Honest Charts

    How to interrogate a table before you trust a single number from it, why summaries can describe a dataset that doesn't exist, and how a chart can argue a case the data doesn't support.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA number is only as good as the rows behind it and the picture it's shown in, so look at the raw distribution, hunt for the codes and mix-ups that pose as data, and treat every chart as an argument someone chose to make.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An analyst asks for the average salary of salary-transfer customers and gets AED 115,000. Absurd for a mass-market product, but it came from the warehouse, so it looks authoritative. Think of a bathroom scale that works perfectly but has a book on it: the arithmetic is right and the answer is wrong.

    She plots the column instead. Most customers sit near AED 14,000. A small hump sits 100 times further out: salaries stored in fils (a hundredth of a dirham) by one legacy feed. A spike at AED 999,999 is a placeholder for "not provided". A block of rows says 0. The mean averaged a dirham with a fils and never complained.

    Only a picture, or a question like "what is the largest value and how often does it repeat?", exposes this.

    • Quality problems don't announce themselves. A sentinel (a placeholder code) or a unit mix-up is a valid number in a valid column. No type check, null check or model objects.
    • Summaries compress, and compression hides. Mean, median and standard deviation throw away the shape. Part 2 shows four datasets with identical summaries and four different stories.
    • Charts are compression too. Where the axis starts, what is averaged and which window is shown decide what the reader concludes. Exploring honestly and presenting honestly are one skill.
    Hold onto thisNever summarise a column you haven't looked at. Plot it, sort it, count its most frequent values, and ask where each row came from. Most "model problems" are data problems nobody looked for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Profiling: asking the table what it is

    Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is looking before modelling, like a detective at a scene: you aren't testing a hypothesis yet, you're finding what is there, including what shouldn't be. The first pass is profiling, a fixed checklist run on every new table. It takes minutes and catches most disasters.

    Date ranges and gapsMissing months, late-arriving data, time-zone shifts, test records.No transactions on the 31st of any month: a batch job dropped them.
    Categorical valuesSpelling variants, mixed Arabic and English labels, trailing spaces."Dubai", "dubai ", "دبي" and "DXB" as four cities.

    Much of it is a few lines of SQL:

    -- grain and duplicates
SELECT COUNT(*) AS n_rows,
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS n_customers,
       COUNT(*) - COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS extra_rows
FROM   customers;

-- the most frequent values in a column: sentinels show up here first
SELECT salary, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM   customers
GROUP  BY salary
ORDER  BY n DESC
LIMIT  10;

    Repeat the checks over time. A column 2% null last year and 40% null this quarter is a pipeline change, and it will hurt a model trained on the earlier period. Compare against what the business expects too: if product says two cards per customer and the table says eleven, the table is usually wrong. Turn the checks into automated tests (data validation) so the next load fails loudly.

    Sentinels, impossible values and unit mix-ups

    Old systems had no way to say "unknown", so people invented values. These sentinel values sit inside real columns and look like data.

    • Numbers: 0, −1, 99, 999, 9999, 999999. 9999 is also a plausible salary, so don't filter blindly: check how often it repeats.
    • Dates: 1900-01-01 and 1970-01-01 (clock zeros), 9999-12-31 and 2099-12-31 meaning "no end date". An age from 1900-01-01 is a 126-year-old customer.
    • Text: "N/A", "NULL", "-", "UNKNOWN", "TBD", a single space. Each is a spelling of missing, and none is a null.

    Impossible values are the cousin: a negative age, a transaction before the account opened, a closing date before the opening date. Write these rules down. A domain expert can state them without seeing data, so they are the cheapest validation you have.

    Unit and currency mix-ups are nastiest because every value looks reasonable. A kilometre column with some rows in miles is the same trap. Gulf cases:

    • Major versus minor units: dirhams and fils (1 AED = 100 fils), riyals and halalas.
    • Mixed currencies in one amount column, with the code elsewhere. AED, USD (pegged at about 3.6725) and others get summed, so totals and fraud thresholds are meaningless.
    • Thousands versus units after a spreadsheet export; decimal conventions ("1.250,00" against "1,250.00").
    • Time zones: a "daily" total cut at midnight UTC is not a Gulf day (UTC+4 UAE, UTC+3 Saudi Arabia), so month-end stops reconciling.
    • Arabic digits and names: the same number in 0–9 or ٠–٩, and different name transliterations. Exact-string joins and duplicate checks silently miss these.

    To spot a unit problem, plot the log-axis distribution. A ×100 error doesn't fatten the tail, it makes a second hump two decades along (Simulation 1). Or check a ratio between related columns (salary to credit limit): where it jumps by 100, you've found the feed.

    Watch outThe median barely moved when you added sentinels in Simulation 1. That makes it comforting and dangerous: a model still sees the junk while the median hides it on your dashboard. Fix the data; don't pick a statistic that hides the damage.

    Duplicates and joins that quietly change the count

    Three kinds of duplicate need three cures.

    • Exact duplicates: the same row twice, often a retry after a timeout. SELECT DISTINCT removes them, but ask why they exist: retries that duplicate rows may duplicate payments.
    • Key duplicates: one customer ID, two rows (two address versions). You need a rule for which wins (latest, verified), and "latest" needs a reliable timestamp.
    • Entity duplicates: one person under two IDs, such as a 2015 and a 2022 account with a slightly different spelling. This is record linkage: fuzzy matching on name (including Arabic-to-English), date of birth and national ID. It matters for exposure limits, KYC and fraud rings, where one risk is counted as two.

    The most common wrong number in banking analytics is a fan-out: join a coarse table to a finer one, then sum a column from the coarse side. Like counting a family's one bank balance once per child.

    customer_idaccount_balance (AED)card_id
    1049240,000C1
    1049240,000C2
    1049240,000C3

    One customer, one AED 40,000 account, three cards. After the join, SUM(account_balance) gives AED 120,000. The query ran and the number looks plausible, but deposits are overstated threefold. Defences: state each table's grain before joining; check row counts after (a one-to-one join shouldn't add rows); aggregate to the target grain before joining; reconcile to a control total.

    The mirror error is the dropped join: an inner join deletes every row without a match. If 8% of customers have no bureau record, your population silently becomes "customers with a bureau file". That is a selection effect (F2.6) made by one keyword.

    -- a one-to-one join should not change the row count
SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers)                      AS before_join,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM customers c
        JOIN bureau b ON b.customer_id = c.customer_id)      AS after_join;

    Missing data: why it's missing matters more than how much

    A null is a measurement that didn't happen, and the right fix depends on why. Rubin's 1976 classification is still the standard vocabulary. Think of an exam where some students skip questions: skipping at random is harmless, skipping the hard ones is not.

    MechanismMeaningBank exampleIs the complete-case average biased?
    MCAR
    missing completely at random    		Missingness is unrelated to anything, observed or not.A batch job drops a random 30% of income fields.No. You just have less data.
    MAR
    missing at random    		Missingness depends only on things you've observed.Affluent-segment customers skip the income question more often, and segment is recorded.Yes, overall; but fixable by modelling or weighting on the observed variable.
    MNAR
    missing not at random    		Missingness depends on the missing value itself.Higher earners decline to state income, even within a segment.Yes, and the data can't tell you how much.

    Formally, with R = 1 when a value is observed:

    P(R=1∣Yobs,Ymis)=P(R=1∣Yobs)(MAR)

    The chance a value is recorded may depend on what you can see (Yobs) but not on the missing value (Ymis). MCAR depends on neither; MNAR depends on Ymis, which you don't have, so MAR versus MNAR cannot be tested from the data. You defend it with knowledge of the process. This is selection bias (F2.6) with the rows present but the cells empty.

    The mechanism decides the fix, and each shortcut has a cost:

    • Drop incomplete rows. Fine under MCAR. Otherwise it changes who is in your data.
    • Fill with the mean or median. Keeps the mean, destroys the variance: intervals shrink and correlations weaken (Simulation 3).
    • Add a missing-indicator feature. Often best for prediction, since missingness can itself be a signal (F3.9). It learns the process, not the person.
    • Model-based or multiple imputation. Predict the value from other columns and repeat with noise so uncertainty carries through. Sound under MAR if the model is right.
    • Get the value elsewhere. For MNAR, an outside source (salary-transfer or bureau data) beats clever statistics.

    Distributions, not summaries: Anscombe and the outlier question

    Two football teams can both average 1.5 goals a game, one scoring 1-2 every week and one alternating 0 and 5. The average can't tell them apart. Anscombe (1973) made this precise with four datasets of eleven (x, y) pairs sharing the same means, variances, correlation (0.816) and fitted line (y ≈ 3.0 + 0.5x). Plotted: a noisy line, a perfect curve, a perfect line spoiled by one outlier, and eleven points in a column with one far-right point deciding the whole slope. Lesson: plot first, because summaries invite you to imagine a dataset that may not exist.

    The 2017 Datasaurus Dozen (Matejka and Fitzmaurice) went further: a dinosaur, a star, a circle and others, all with identical means, standard deviations and correlation to two decimals.

    Banking versions: two branches with the same average resolution time, one steady at 3 days and one split between same-day and 6 weeks; two fraud models with the same average loss, one dominated by three huge misses. The mean locates the middle and says nothing about the tail, where banks lose money.

    What do you do with an outlier? It depends on what it is.

    • An error (sentinel, ×100 feed, typo): fix or remove it, and fix the source.
    • Real and rare (a corporate account in a retail table, a genuine AED 4 million purchase): keep it, ask whether it belongs in this population, and summarise robustly (median, quantiles).
    • The thing you're looking for. In fraud, AML and credit the extreme rows are often the signal. Winsorising or deleting them to make a model "stable" can delete the target.

    Money is heavy-tailed (F1.5), so a few values dominate totals. Report medians and percentiles beside the mean, use a log axis, and ask what share the top 1% contributes. Check whether ten customers hold half the value before describing a "typical" one.

    Choosing a chart by the question

    Start from the question, then pick the encoding the eye reads best. Cleveland and McGill (1984) ranked them, best to worst: position on a common scale, position on separate scales, length, angle and slope, area, then volume and colour shade. So dots or lines often beat pies, and bars must start at zero (their length is the value).

If the chart needs a note to be understood, it isn't finished.

    Charts that mislead, and how to catch them

    Most misleading charts aren't forged. The numbers are real; the choices of axis, aggregation, window and scale were made, often unthinkingly, to favour a conclusion.

    • Truncated axes. Bars starting at 90 make 92 to 95 look like a 150% jump. For bars this is a lie, since length encodes value. Lines may start off zero when the question is change, but show the scale.
    • Dual axes. Choosing the axis ends makes two lines overlap, cross or diverge. Correlation never changes under rescaling, only the picture. Two trending series look related anyway.
    • Averages hiding spread. A bank-wide average can be flat while some branches improve and others collapse. Show the range or quantiles.
    • Aggregation and window. Monthly looks noisy, yearly smooth. A window starting at a seasonal peak shows decline. A cumulative chart always rises.
    • Log scales. Honest for growth rates and wide ranges, but exponential growth looks like a straight line and 10× can look small. Label them, never use bars on one.
    • Area and volume. Scale a circle's width and height by the value and area grows with the square: double the radius, four times the picture.
    • Missing context. No denominator or comparison period. "Fraud cases up 40%" while transactions rose 60%.

    Tufte's lie factor measures distortion:

    Lie factor=size of effect shown in the graphicsize of effect in the data,where effect size=b−aa

    Effect size is the change from first value (a) to last (b) as a share of the first, measured on the drawn heights or areas and on the data. 1 is honest; well above 1 exaggerates; below 1 hides change. Bars for 92 and 95 on an axis from 90 are drawn 2 and 5 tall: graphic +150%, data +3.3%, lie factor about 46.

    Same arithmetic for pictures. Both right-hand circles mean "twice as much"; the faded one was scaled the common, wrong way:

    The simulation applies these tricks to one honest dataset: 24 months of complaints per 10,000 accounts across eight branches, with a gentle real upward drift. Watch the lie factor.

    Treat these as choices to declare, not tricks to avoid. If you truncate a line chart, say so on the axis. If you aggregate, show spread. If you pick a window, show why. Reader trust is the asset you spend.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes in bank analyses and AI-generated ones, each with the quickest check.

    • Summarising before looking

      A dashboard says "average salary AED 115,000" and nobody plotted the column.

      Spot itAsk for the log-axis histogram and the ten most frequent values. Without them, the number is unverified.
    • Sentinels treated as values

      1900-01-01 turned into an age, 999 into a tenure, 0 into a balance.

      Spot itThe most common value of any date or amount column should make business sense. Compare the max to what is physically possible.
    • Mixed units and currencies

      Fils with dirhams, AED summed with USD, UTC days used as Gulf days.

      Spot itA second hump on a log axis, a ×100 jump in a column ratio, month-end totals missing the ledger by a few percent.
    • Fan-out joins

      A total that triples after joining to a finer table.

      Spot itCompare row counts before and after each join, and the total to a control. State every table's grain.
    • An inner join as a hidden filter

      Customers with no bureau record vanish and nobody mentions the population changed.

      Spot itAsk how many rows each join drops, and who they are. Prefer a left join and inspect the unmatched.
    • Mean-filling missing values

      Blanks replaced by the average, then a tight interval and strong correlations reported.

      Spot itThe imputed column has a tall spike at one value and a standard deviation below the observed data's.
    • Assuming missing means random

      "We dropped the 30% with no income" when most were affluent.

      Spot itCompare observed attributes of missing and complete rows. Differences mean not MCAR. For MNAR, seek an outside source or a sensitivity analysis.
    • Deleting outliers that are the signal

      "We removed anything above the 99th percentile" from fraud or AML data.

      Spot itAsk what share of known positives was among the removed rows.
    • Truncated bars, or averages without spread

      A small gain drawn as a leap; a branch league table of means with no range.

      Spot itCheck where the axis starts and whether bar ratios match number ratios. Ask for spread, count and an interval.
    • Dual axis implying a link

      Complaints and card spend seem to track each other.

      Spot itAsk for both axis ranges and the correlation after removing trend. Rescaling can produce any picture.
    • AI analysis that skips the looking

      An LLM writes confident group-average-plot code and never checks duplicates, units or sentinels. Its charts may default to truncated axes or 3D.

      Spot itNo row counts, no missingness table, every column assumed clean. Ask what profiling was done and to see the code.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Of 1,000 salary rows, 960 average AED 15,000 and 40 hold the placeholder 999,999. What is the mean, and what happens to the median?

    Mean = (960 × 15,000 + 40 × 999,999) ÷ 1,000 = 14,400 + 40,000 ≈ AED 54,400, over 3.5 times the truth. The median stays near AED 15,000 because 4% of rows can't move it far. A mean-to-median ratio of 3.6 is the alarm.

    2. A customer has one AED 40,000 account and three cards. After joining customer to cards, what does SUM(balance) give? What would you do instead?

    AED 120,000: the balance repeats on three card rows (a fan-out). Aggregate cards to one row per customer, or sum balances before the join, and check against a control total.

    3. Of 100 transactions, 95 are in AED averaging 400 and 5 are in fils with the same real average. What is the column mean, and how many times too high?

    The fils rows average 40,000. Mean = (95 × 400 + 5 × 40,000) ÷ 100 = 2,380, 5.95 times the true 400. Five bad rows nearly sextupled it. A log histogram shows a second hump two decades right.

    4. Income is blank for 30% of customers. How do you decide between MCAR, MAR and MNAR, and what can't you test?

    Compare observed variables (segment, age, product, channel) between blank and non-blank rows. Differences rule out MCAR. If those variables predict the blanks well, MAR is plausible, and reweighting or imputing helps. MAR versus MNAR can't be tested, because it depends on the incomes you don't have. Argue it from how the form works, run sensitivity checks, or bring in an outside source such as salary-transfer records.

    5. A bar chart axis starts at 90 and shows 92 then 95. What is the lie factor?

    Drawn bars are 2 and 5 tall: +150%. Data: +3.26%. Lie factor ≈ 150 ÷ 3.26 ≈ 46. Redraw from zero (lie factor 1) or use a line chart with the scale marked.

    6. An infographic shows a figure twice as big by doubling a circle's width and height. How misleading is it?

    Doubling the radius quadruples area: +300% shown for +100%, lie factor 3. Scale area instead (radius × √2 ≈ 1.41), or better, use bars.

    7. A dual-axis chart shows complaints and card spend moving in lockstep. Your manager says "clearly related". What do you check?

    The axis ranges (which can make any pair overlap); the correlation (which ignores axis choice); and whether both simply trend upward (any two trending series correlate). Compare changes or detrended values, side by side. If it matters, test properly (F2) and ask about confounders (F2.6).
