    Arabic Documents, Arabic Benchmarks and Islamic Finance

    Why reading an Arabic scan is harder than reading an English one, why a leaderboard score in English tells you little about your call-centre transcripts, and where AI can and cannot help with Sharia-compliant products.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceArabic work in a Gulf bank fails at the seams, where right-to-left text meets left-to-right numbers, where formal Arabic meets dialect, and where a model's answer meets a Sharia board's ruling, so you check each seam with field-level validation, a test set built from your own traffic, and a human authority that owns the final word.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take one line from a scanned transfer instruction in a Dubai branch:

    تحويل مبلغ 125,000.00 درهم إلى الحساب AE07 0331 2345 6789 0123 456 باسم شركة الخليج للتجارة

    A reader sees one sentence. A machine sees three problems stacked together. The Arabic letters run right to left and change shape depending on their neighbours, so the same letter looks different at the start, middle and end of a word. The amount and the IBAN are written left to right inside the right-to-left line. And the digits might be 0-9 or the Arabic-Indic forms ٠-٩, sometimes both on the same page. A stamp may sit across the IBAN. The customer's signature is handwritten.

    That is the first half of this lesson: document AI for Arabic and mixed Arabic-English paper. The second half is about two other places the same bank meets the same trouble. When you ask whether an Arabic language model is any good, the usual answer comes from English benchmarks or from English tests translated into Arabic, and it tells you little about how the model handles a Gulf-dialect complaint typed with English product names. And when the product is an Islamic financing contract, "is this compliant?" has a legal answer that belongs to a Sharia board, not a model, so the useful AI work sits around that decision: screening, retrieval of the right standard, and checking documents against it.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Character error rate is a research metric, not a business metric. The business cares whether the amount and the account number are exactly right. One wrong character in 23 is a payment to the wrong beneficiary.
    • The fix is not a better model alone, it is a pipeline with checks. An IBAN has a check digit; a date has a calendar; an amount in figures should match the amount in words. Validation turns silent errors into queued exceptions.
    • "Arabic" is several tasks. Printed formal text, handwritten forms, mixed-script tables, dialect speech transcripts and religious-legal text each behave differently, so one headline score hides most of the story.
    Hold onto thisFor anything touching money, think in fields and consequences, not characters and averages. Ask: what is the exact-match rate per field, which errors can a rule catch, and who sees the rest? The same habit carries over to benchmarks (test on your traffic) and to Islamic finance (the model prepares, the authority decides).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Arabic OCR and document AI

    A document pipeline has stages, and Arabic strains several of them. Seeing the stages tells you where a given error came from. The general pipeline, including layout and tables, is in parsing messy PDFs and scans and its trade-finance application in document AI for trade finance. Here is what is specific to Arabic.

    1. Cleaning and layout. Deskew, remove noise, find text blocks, tables, stamps and signatures, and decide the reading order. Arabic pages read right to left, so a table's first column is on the right. A layout model trained on English pages that assumes left-to-right order will scramble the columns of an Arabic statement without any single character being wrong.
    2. Text-line recognition. Classic systems, such as Tesseract, split a line into characters and classify each. That fails for Arabic because the script is cursive: letters in a word connect, there are no clean gaps between them, and each letter has up to four forms (isolated, initial, medial, final). Modern recognisers read the whole line image and output a character sequence directly, trained with a loss that aligns image columns to characters without needing the boundaries (CTC), or with an attention-based decoder.
    3. Dots and diacritics. Many Arabic letters differ only by the number and position of dots: ب ت ث ن share a shape. A speck of noise or a faint scan can move a dot and change the letter. Short vowel marks (tashkeel) sit above and below the line; most business text omits them, while Quranic and some formal or legal text includes them. The same word can therefore appear with or without marks.
    4. Direction and mixing. The Unicode bidirectional algorithm decides display order for mixed text. Numbers and Latin words stay left to right inside the right-to-left run. Text can be stored in logical order and displayed in visual order; confusing the two produces reversed or shuffled strings in your database. This is a data-engineering bug, not a model bug, and a classic one.
    5. Digits. Three digit systems occur: Western (0-9), Arabic-Indic (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and Eastern Arabic-Indic or Persian forms (۰۱۲۳۴۵۶۷۸۹), which share some shapes but differ for 4, 5 and 6. A bank in the Gulf sees all of them, often on the same document and often from customers whose paperwork originates elsewhere. Normalise early and keep the original.
    6. Key information extraction (KIE). Turn recognised text into fields: payer, amount, IBAN, trade licence number. Rules, layout-aware models, or a vision-language model prompted to return JSON do this.
    7. Validation and routing. Checksums, formats, cross-field consistency, confidence thresholds, then a human queue. This stage turned Simulation 1's silent errors into visible ones.

    Two architectures compete. The modular pipeline above gives you an inspectable output at each stage and a place to put rules. The newer end-to-end route feeds the page image to a vision-language model and asks for the text or the fields (see search over document images for the retrieval side of the same shift). Arabic-specific fine-tunes exist, such as the open Qari-OCR models built on a Qwen vision-language base. The attraction is that one model handles layout, language and context. The risk is specific to generative models: when the image is unclear, the model may write a plausible word rather than flag uncertainty. Classic OCR makes visible garbage; a vision-language model can make fluent errors, which is worse for a validator and for the next paragraph on metrics.

    Measuring it honestly

    Character error rate (CER) is the edit distance between the output and the truth, divided by the length of the truth. Word error rate (WER) does the same for words. Arabic raises three evaluation traps.

    • Normalisation changes the score. Do you treat the different alef forms (ا أ إ آ) as equal? The final ي and ى? Remove the tatweel stretching character, or the diacritics? Two papers can report different CER on the same model purely from these choices. Insist on a stated normalisation, and for banking use cases score without forgiving the distinctions that matter, such as the digits in an IBAN.
    • Edit distance can exceed 100%. A model that hallucinates a long extra passage can post a CER above 100%. One 2026 preprint on historical Arabic manuscripts reports exactly this for an OCR fine-tune applied to a domain it was not trained on (Part 4).
    • Averages hide the fields that matter. Score exact match per field type, report separately for printed and handwritten, for clean scans and phone photos, and look at the tail: the worst 5% of pages decide your exception queue.

    Handwriting deserves its own sentence. It is far harder than print: the variability of Arabic handwriting is large, training data is scarcer, and customer-written forms contain names and numbers where no language model can guess from context. The sensible bank design treats handwritten amounts and signatures as human-verified or cross-checked (amount in words against amount in figures) unless the measured error on your own samples is low enough for the risk.

    P(field right)=(1−c)n

    c is the per-character error rate and n the number of characters in the field. At c = 1%, a 23-character IBAN is right 79% of the time (0.9923), and all four fields in Simulation 1 together are right about 55% of the time. Real errors are not independent (a smudge hits neighbouring characters), so use this as a feel for scale, then measure exact match on real fields.

    Arabic evaluation benchmarks

    "Model X scores 78 on MMLU" says something about English multiple-choice knowledge. It says much less about whether the model can summarise a Gulf-dialect complaint call. The reasons are mechanical.

    • Diglossia. Written Arabic is mostly Modern Standard Arabic (MSA). Spoken Arabic is dialect: Gulf, Levantine, Egyptian, Maghrebi and more, differing in vocabulary and grammar. Customers write WhatsApp messages and speak to call centres in dialect, often mixing English or writing Arabic in Latin letters ("Arabizi"). Most benchmarks test MSA. A model strong at MSA can still stumble on dialect, and speech-to-text for dialect is a separate error source.
    • Translated benchmarks inherit the wrong world. Translating an English test keeps the English question's culture, names and assumptions, and adds translation artefacts. A question about Thanksgiving or a US tax form measures nothing you need. Native benchmarks, written or curated by Arabic speakers, avoid this; the community has moved in that direction (Part 4).
    • Multiple choice is not conversation. Choosing A, B, C or D rewards recognition. Your use cases are generation: draft a reply, summarise a case, extract fields. Generative evaluation needs a judge, human or model, and an LLM judge has its own language bias and may score Arabic fluency poorly (human evaluation and annotator agreement).
    • Tokenisation. Models trained mostly on English split Arabic words into more tokens, so the same text costs more and fits less in the context window, and quality at a fixed budget can suffer. This is partly why Arabic-centric models rebuild their tokenisers (tokenisation, Jais, ALLaM, Falcon).
    • Contamination and gaming. A public test set eventually leaks into training data. Some leaderboards respond by rotating hidden test sets or judging fresh generated answers.
    • Small samples. A per-dialect score based on 150 questions has an interval of several points. Rankings between close models are often noise (statistics for evals).

    The remedy is dull and effective: build a small test set from your own traffic. Two hundred to a thousand real, anonymised examples per task (with the consent and privacy controls of privacy basics), labelled by native speakers who know the bank's vocabulary, stratified by MSA, Gulf dialect, mixed language, and by the channel. Use public benchmarks to shortlist; use your own set to decide. A good public Arabic benchmark is still useful for what it is: a stable, comparable yardstick of general ability.

    AI for Islamic finance

    Islamic finance is not conventional finance with a different label. The contracts are structured around prohibitions (interest, or riba; excessive uncertainty, gharar; certain businesses) and around real assets and risk-sharing. The usual forms: murabaha (the bank buys the asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, paid later), ijara (lease, with ownership possibly transferring at the end), mudaraba and musharaka (profit-sharing partnerships), and sukuk (certificates of ownership in assets, often called Islamic bonds, though their legal structure differs). Takaful is the cooperative insurance alternative.

    Who decides what is compliant? Each Islamic bank has a Sharia supervisory board whose rulings (fatwas) bind the institution. Standard-setters publish reference standards: AAOIFI, the Bahrain-based Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions, issues accounting, auditing, governance and Sharia standards. National authorities add their own layers. I am confident that the UAE has a central Higher Sharia Authority at the central bank and that Saudi banks operate under SAMA supervision with their own Sharia boards; the exact current rules on how bank boards, central authorities and standards interact differ by jurisdiction and change, so check the current text rather than relying on this lesson (see CBUAE, SAMA and SDAIA expectations).

    Screening, and why the denominator matters

    Equity screening checks whether a listed company is acceptable for a Sharia-compliant fund. Typical tests: (1) the business is not in prohibited activity such as alcohol, gambling or conventional banking; (2) interest-bearing debt is below a limit relative to a base; (3) interest-bearing cash and deposits are below a limit; (4) income from non-compliant sources is below a small share of total income (often cited as about 5%). Commonly quoted thresholds are 30% for the two balance-sheet ratios and 5% for the income test, as in AAOIFI's standard on financial paper, but index providers differ: some use a 33% limit, and some divide by total assets rather than market capitalisation. Confirm the exact standard, edition and your board's adopted method before you encode anything.

    The base is the sting. Market capitalisation moves every day, total assets only each quarter. So a company can pass one methodology and fail another, and pass today and fail after a 25% share-price fall with nothing changing in its business. Compliance teams therefore need a rule for how often to re-screen, a tolerance band, and a purification step: when the company's income includes a small non-compliant portion, investors give away that portion of any dividend to charity.

    Retrieval over standards is where the rest of this curriculum applies directly. Standards are long, structured, bilingual documents full of cross-references, definitions and scope clauses. Chunk by clause and keep the clause number and standard edition in the metadata (chunking strategies), use hybrid search because exact Arabic terms such as مرابحة matter as much as meaning (hybrid search), return the source text next to any summary, and check groundedness (hallucination detection). Source files are often scanned or bilingual PDFs, so Part 2's OCR problems come back at the start of the pipeline. Evaluate against questions from the Sharia and compliance teams, with answers they sign off, and treat "I could not find a basis for this" as a good answer.

    Profit-rate modelling is the least exotic part. An Islamic bank prices fixed-markup contracts, funds itself in part with investment accounts whose returns are shared rather than guaranteed, and has to manage the gap between what depositors expect and what assets earn. Practitioners discuss concepts such as a profit equalisation reserve to smooth returns, and "displaced commercial risk", the pressure to pay out more than earned to keep deposits. Models for deposit pass-through, liquidity and stress are standard time-series and survival work (liquidity forecasting and ALM), applied with the product's rules in mind. The mistake is to import a conventional interest-rate model without asking how the contract actually pays.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Operations. Transfer instructions, cheques, trade documents, powers of attorney and account-opening packs. Cost saved depends on the straight-through rate after validation, not on headline accuracy.
    • Contact centre and chat. Dialect speech and typed messages with code-switching. Benchmarks must match, and the speech-to-text step needs its own evaluation.
    • Islamic banking and asset management. Screening of equity universes, product-document review, policy question-answering for branch staff, and Sharia audit support.
    • Compliance. Adverse media in Arabic, name matching across scripts, and regulatory circulars in both languages.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Progress is real and uneven. General vision-language models now read clean printed Arabic well. Handwriting, historical material and multi-column bilingual layouts remain weak spots, benchmarks for Arabic are maturing, and Islamic finance has only just acquired its first dedicated LLM benchmarks.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Quoting character error rate as the business case

      "98.5% accurate" on characters becomes a minority of documents with every field right.

      Spot itAsk for exact-match rate per field type and the straight-through rate after validation, on the bank's own scans.
    • Fluent hallucination from a vision-language model

      An unreadable name is replaced by a plausible one, or a digit is invented. It looks cleaner than OCR garbage and passes casual inspection.

      Spot itTest with deliberately degraded images and check whether the model flags uncertainty or confidently outputs text. Cross-check against a second reader or a checksum.
    • Scrambled order, columns or digits

      Right-to-left tables read left to right, bidirectional text stored in visual order, or a digit system silently converted.

      Spot itRound-trip a few documents end to end and compare the stored string with the page. Look at tables and at numbers inside Arabic sentences.
    • Over-normalising so errors disappear

      Folding alef variants and dropping diacritics is fine for search, wrong for scoring a name or number.

      Spot itAsk what normalisation the metric applied, and rescore the critical fields without it.
    • Choosing a model on an English or translated benchmark

      The winner on the leaderboard loses on dialect or mixed-language messages.

      Spot itAsk for results by segment (MSA, Gulf dialect, mixed, English) on data resembling production, with intervals.
    • An LLM judge that prefers English or longer answers

      Generative evaluation scored by a model that is itself weaker in Arabic.

      Spot itCompare judge scores with native-speaker ratings on a sample and report agreement.
    • Sharia screening on a single method and a stale price

      A stock quietly crosses a limit after a market fall and stays in a compliant fund.

      Spot itAsk which methodology the board approved, which base is used, how often re-screening runs and what the tolerance rule is.
    • Paraphrasing a standard instead of quoting it

      The summary shifts a condition (for example, from "must" to "may"), or cites a superseded edition.

      Spot itRequire clause number, edition and exact text beside every answer, and check a sample against the source.
    • Letting the model rule on compliance

      A confident "this structure is compliant" with no scholar behind it.

      Spot itLook for the approval line in the process. If the output feeds a customer product without a named Sharia authority signing, stop.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Reading Arabic paper is harder than English: the letters connect, the numbers run the other way, and handwriting is messy. A model that gets 99 characters in 100 right still gets about half of our transfer forms wrong somewhere. So the value comes from checks, not from the reader alone: we validate the account number, route doubtful pages to staff, and measure on our own scans. On Islamic finance, AI can screen shares and find the right standard fast, but the ruling on compliance stays with our Sharia board.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd evaluate at field level, exact match by field type, split by printed, handwritten and phone-photo, with the normalisation stated. Validation rules turn silent errors into exceptions, and I'd test vision-language extractors with degraded images because they fail by fluent invention. For model selection I'd build a few hundred anonymised examples from our traffic, stratified by MSA, Gulf dialect and code-switching, labelled by native speakers, with intervals, and use public Arabic benchmarks only to shortlist. For Sharia work, retrieval with clause-level citations and the board's approved screening method, reviewed by compliance.”
    “Which Arabic OCR should we buy?”

    I can't say without a bake-off on our documents. Public benchmarks such as KITAB-Bench are a shortlist: they show that general vision-language models are strong on clean print and weaker on handwriting. We'd score each candidate on field-level exact match and straight-through rate after validation, on a few hundred of our scans including the bad ones, and cost it per page including the human exception queue.

    “Why not just use a frontier model for everything Arabic?”

    Often we can, and strong general models are good at MSA. The questions are dialect, mixed-language messages, cost and token count, data residency, and whether it fails safely. I'd test it against an Arabic-focused model on our own set; the answer may differ by task, and a small specialist can win on one segment while a large model wins on another.

    “The model scored 85 on the Arabic benchmark. Is that good enough?”

    Good enough for what? Check what the benchmark measures (multiple choice or generation), whether it is native or translated, what dialect it covers, how many questions each segment has, and whether the test set was public. Then I'd run our own examples. A benchmark score is a general signal; our decision needs a local one.

    “Can AI tell us if a product is Sharia-compliant?”

    It can help prepare the decision: extract terms from the contract, compare them with a checklist the board has approved, retrieve the relevant standards and earlier rulings with citations. The ruling itself is a religious-legal judgement that our Sharia board and the regulator's framework own. I wouldn't put a model's answer in front of a customer as a compliance statement.

    “Why did the screen pass this stock last quarter and fail it now?”

    Most likely the denominator moved. If the method divides debt by market capitalisation, a share-price fall raises the ratio without any change in the company. I'd show the ratio history, state the board's methodology, and apply the agreed tolerance and re-screening rules, rather than treating the flip as a model error.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. An OCR system has a 0.5% character error rate. A 23-character IBAN is read. What is the chance it is entirely right, and what does a checksum add?

    0.99523 ≈ 0.891, so about 89% right and 11% wrong. A checksum cannot fix a wrong IBAN but flags most of the 11% (about 99% in the typical case), so only roughly 0.1% stay silently wrong instead of 11%.

    2. Why can the same Arabic letter look different in different places in a word, and why does that break character-by-character OCR?

    Arabic is cursive; letters join to neighbours and take initial, medial, final or isolated forms. There are no clean gaps to cut between letters, so segmentation-first OCR guesses boundaries badly. Modern systems read the whole line and predict the sequence directly.

    3. Name two reasons a score from an English benchmark translated into Arabic is a weak guide for your call-centre use case.

    It is mostly formal Arabic, while callers use Gulf dialect with English mixed in; and translation carries English-world content and artefacts. Also it is usually multiple choice, while the task is generation. (Others: tokenisation cost, contamination, small samples.)

    4. Model A beats B by 3.6 points on a public benchmark that is 60% English and 40% MSA. Your traffic is 10% English, 5% MSA, 55% Gulf dialect, 30% mixed. With accuracies A: 92/80/58/62 and B: 84/83/74/71, who wins on your traffic?

    A: 0.1×92 + 0.05×80 + 0.55×58 + 0.30×62 = 9.2 + 4 + 31.9 + 18.6 = 63.7. B: 8.4 + 4.15 + 40.7 + 21.3 = 74.55. B wins by about 10.9 points. The segment mix, not the models, flipped the ranking.

    5. A company has AED 3bn interest-bearing debt, market cap AED 12bn, total assets AED 14bn. Under the 30% market-cap test and the 33% total-assets test, what happens, and what if the share price falls 25%?

    Market cap method: 3 ÷ 12 = 25%, pass. Total assets: 3 ÷ 14 ≈ 21%, pass. After a 25% fall, market cap is 9bn: 3 ÷ 9 = 33%, now fails the 30% test, while the total-assets ratio is unchanged at about 21%. Same company, different verdict; the base matters. (Thresholds as commonly quoted; use your board's method.)

    6. Why is a vision-language model's OCR error sometimes more dangerous than a classic engine's?

    Classic engines tend to output visible garbage or low confidence on bad regions. A generative model can produce fluent, plausible text, a believable name or digits, that passes review. Mitigate with checksums, a second reader and degraded-image tests.

    7. What is the role of an AAOIFI-style standard versus the bank's Sharia board, and what does that imply for an AI assistant?

    Standards are reference documents; the board issues the binding rulings for the institution, within the regulator's framework. An assistant should retrieve and quote standards and rulings with citations and prepare checks, but never be the authority. Any compliance conclusion needs a named human sign-off.
