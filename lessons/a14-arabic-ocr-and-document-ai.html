    Arabic Documents, Arabic Benchmarks and Islamic Finance

    Why reading an Arabic scan is harder than reading an English one, why a leaderboard score in English tells you little about your call-centre transcripts, and where AI can and cannot help with Sharia-compliant products.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceArabic work in a Gulf bank fails at the seams, where right-to-left text meets left-to-right numbers, where formal Arabic meets dialect, and where a model's answer meets a Sharia board's ruling, so you check each seam with field-level validation, a test set built from your own traffic, and a human authority that owns the final word.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take one line from a scanned transfer instruction in a Dubai branch:

    تحويل مبلغ 125,000.00 درهم إلى الحساب AE07 0331 2345 6789 0123 456 باسم شركة الخليج للتجارة

    A reader sees one sentence; a machine sees stacked problems, like reading a sentence where the letters change shape depending on neighbours and a number is spelled backwards inside it.

    • Arabic runs right to left, and letters change shape with their neighbours (start, middle, end of word).
    • The amount and IBAN run left to right inside the right-to-left line.
    • Digits may be 0-9 or Arabic-Indic ٠-٩, sometimes both on one page.
    • A stamp may cover the IBAN, and the signature is handwritten.

    That is document AI for Arabic and mixed paper, the first half of the lesson. The second half covers two more places with the same trouble. First, Arabic model quality: usual answers come from English benchmarks or translations, which say little about a Gulf-dialect complaint typed with English product names. Second, Islamic financing: "is this compliant?" has a legal answer owned by a Sharia board, so useful AI work sits around that decision (screening, retrieving the right standard, checking documents).

    Three takeaways.

    • Character error rate is a research metric, not a business metric. The business cares whether the amount and account number are exact. One wrong character in 23 pays the wrong beneficiary.
    • The fix is a pipeline with checks, not a better model alone. An IBAN has a check digit, a date has a calendar, and the amount in figures should match the words. Validation turns silent errors into queued exceptions.
    • "Arabic" is several tasks. Printed formal text, handwritten forms, mixed-script tables, dialect speech and religious-legal text behave differently, so one headline score hides most of the story.
    Hold onto thisFor anything touching money, think in fields and consequences, not characters and averages. Ask: what is the exact-match rate per field, which errors can a rule catch, and who sees the rest? The same habit carries over to benchmarks (test on your traffic) and to Islamic finance (the model prepares, the authority decides).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Arabic OCR and document AI

    A document pipeline has stages, and Arabic strains several. Knowing them tells you where an error came from. General layout and tables: parsing messy PDFs and scans and document AI for trade finance. Arabic-specific points:

    1. Cleaning and layout. Deskew, denoise, find blocks, tables, stamps and signatures, set the reading order. Arabic tables start on the right. A left-to-right layout model scrambles the columns without a single wrong character.
    2. Text-line recognition. Classic OCR such as Tesseract splits a line into characters. That fails because Arabic is cursive: letters connect, with no clean gaps, and each has up to four forms (isolated, initial, medial, final). Modern recognisers read the whole line and output the sequence, using a loss that aligns image columns to characters without boundaries (CTC) or an attention decoder.
    3. Dots and diacritics. Letters like ب ت ث ن differ only by dots, so a speck of noise can change the letter. Short vowel marks (tashkeel) are mostly omitted in business text but present in Quranic and some legal text, so one word can appear with or without them.
    4. Direction and mixing. The Unicode bidirectional algorithm orders mixed text; numbers and Latin words stay left to right inside the right-to-left run. Text is stored in logical order and displayed in visual order. Confusing them gives reversed strings in your database: a data-engineering bug, not a model bug.
    5. Digits. Western (0-9), Arabic-Indic (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and Eastern/Persian (۰۱۲۳۴۵۶۷۸۹, which differ for 4, 5 and 6). Gulf banks see all three, often on one document. Normalise early and keep the original.
    6. Key information extraction (KIE). Turn text into fields (payer, amount, IBAN, licence number) with rules, layout-aware models, or a vision-language model prompted to return JSON.
    7. Validation and routing. Checksums, formats, cross-field consistency, confidence thresholds, then a human queue. This turned Simulation 1's silent errors into visible ones.

    Two architectures compete. The modular pipeline gives an inspectable output per stage and a place for rules. The end-to-end route feeds the page image to a vision-language model and asks for text or fields (see search over document images). Arabic fine-tunes exist, such as the open Qari-OCR models on a Qwen vision-language base. One model handles layout, language and context, but when the image is unclear it may write a plausible word instead of flagging doubt. Classic OCR makes visible garbage; a vision-language model makes fluent errors, which is worse for a validator and for metrics.

    Measuring it honestly

    Character error rate (CER) is edit distance to the truth divided by the truth's length: the share of characters you would have to fix. 1% means one fix per 100 characters. Word error rate (WER) is the same for words. Arabic adds three traps.

    • Normalisation changes the score. Are alef forms (ا أ إ آ) equal? Final ي and ى? Is tatweel or diacritics removed? Two papers can report different CER for the same model on these choices alone. Insist on a stated normalisation, and for banking do not forgive distinctions that matter, such as IBAN digits.
    • CER can exceed 100%. A model that hallucinates a long extra passage can score above 100%. A 2026 preprint on historical Arabic manuscripts reports 167% for a fine-tune applied to a domain it was not trained on.
    • Averages hide the fields that matter. Score exact match per field type, split printed versus handwritten and clean scans versus phone photos, and look at the tail: the worst 5% of pages decide your exception queue.

    Handwriting is far harder: more variability, scarcer training data, and names and numbers that no language model can guess from context. Treat handwritten amounts and signatures as human-verified or cross-checked (amount in words against figures) unless measured error on your own samples is low enough for the risk.

    P(field right)=(1−c)n

    c is the per-character error rate, n the characters in the field. At c = 1%, a 23-character IBAN is right 79% of the time (0.9923); all four fields in Simulation 1 are right about 55% of the time. Real errors cluster (a smudge hits neighbours), so use this for scale, then measure exact match on real fields.

    Arabic evaluation benchmarks

    "Model X scores 78 on MMLU" is like a driving-theory pass mark: it says little about driving in Dubai traffic. It measures English multiple-choice knowledge, not summarising a Gulf-dialect complaint call. Six reasons:

    • Diglossia. Written Arabic is mostly Modern Standard Arabic (MSA); speech is dialect (Gulf, Levantine, Egyptian, Maghrebi), differing in vocabulary and grammar. Customers use dialect on WhatsApp and calls, often mixing English or typing Arabic in Latin letters ("Arabizi"). Most benchmarks test MSA, and dialect speech-to-text is a separate error source.
    • Translated benchmarks inherit the wrong world. A translated question about Thanksgiving or a US tax form measures nothing you need. Native benchmarks avoid this.
    • Multiple choice is not conversation. It rewards recognition; your tasks are generation. That needs a judge, human or model, and an LLM judge can have language bias (human evaluation and annotator agreement).
    • Tokenisation. English-trained models split Arabic into more tokens, so the same text costs more and fits less context. This is why Arabic-centric models rebuild tokenisers (tokenisation, Jais, ALLaM, Falcon).
    • Contamination. Public test sets leak into training data. Some leaderboards rotate hidden test sets.
    • Small samples. A dialect score on 150 questions has an interval of several points, so close rankings are often noise (statistics for evals).

    The remedy is dull and effective: build a small test set from your own traffic, like trying shoes on rather than reading the size chart.

    • 200 to 1,000 real, anonymised examples per task, within the consent and privacy controls of privacy basics.
    • Labelled by native speakers who know the bank's vocabulary.
    • Stratified by MSA, Gulf dialect, mixed language and channel.

    Use public benchmarks to shortlist and your own set to decide. A good public benchmark remains a stable yardstick of general ability.

    AI for Islamic finance

    Islamic finance is built around prohibitions (interest, or riba; excessive uncertainty, gharar; certain businesses) plus real assets and risk-sharing. Main contracts:

    • Murabaha: the bank buys the asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, paid later.
    • Ijara: a lease, with ownership possibly transferring at the end.
    • Mudaraba and musharaka: profit-sharing partnerships.
    • Sukuk: certificates of asset ownership, often called Islamic bonds though the legal structure differs.
    • Takaful: cooperative insurance.

    Each Islamic bank has a Sharia supervisory board whose rulings (fatwas) bind the institution. AAOIFI (the Bahrain-based Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions) publishes reference standards. The UAE has a Higher Sharia Authority at the central bank, and Saudi banks have their own Sharia boards under SAMA supervision. Rules on how boards, authorities and standards interact differ by jurisdiction and change, so check the current text (CBUAE, SAMA and SDAIA expectations).

    Screening, and why the denominator matters

    Equity screening asks whether a listed company is acceptable for a Sharia-compliant fund. Four typical tests:

    • The business is not in prohibited activity (alcohol, gambling, conventional banking).
    • Interest-bearing debt is below a limit relative to a base.
    • Interest-bearing cash and deposits are below a limit.
    • Non-compliant income is a small share of total income (often about 5%).

    Commonly quoted limits are 30% for the two balance-sheet ratios and 5% for income, as in AAOIFI's standard on financial paper. Index providers differ: some use 33%, and some divide by total assets instead of market capitalisation. Confirm the standard, edition and your board's adopted method before encoding anything.

    The base is the sting, like grading on a curve that moves daily. Market cap changes every day; total assets change quarterly. A company can pass one method and fail another, or pass today and fail after a 25% share-price fall with its business unchanged. Teams need a re-screening frequency, a tolerance band, and purification: investors give the non-compliant share of any dividend to charity.

    Retrieval over standards reuses the rest of this curriculum. Standards are long, bilingual and cross-referenced.

    • Chunk by clause; keep clause number and edition in metadata (chunking strategies).
    • Use hybrid search, because exact terms such as مرابحة matter as much as meaning (hybrid search).
    • Return the source text beside any summary and check groundedness (hallucination detection).
    • Expect scanned or bilingual PDFs, so the OCR problems above return at the start of the pipeline.
    • Evaluate on questions from the Sharia and compliance teams with answers they sign off, and treat "I could not find a basis for this" as a good answer.

    Profit-rate modelling is the least exotic part. Think of a bank that sells at a fixed mark-up but funds itself with investment accounts whose returns are shared, not guaranteed. The gap between what depositors expect and what assets earn needs managing. Two terms: a profit equalisation reserve (smooths returns) and "displaced commercial risk" (pressure to pay out more than earned to keep deposits). The models are standard time-series and survival work (liquidity forecasting and ALM). The mistake is importing a conventional interest-rate model without asking how the contract actually pays.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting character error rate as the business case

      "98.5% accurate" on characters becomes a minority of documents with every field right.

      Spot itAsk for exact-match rate per field type and straight-through rate after validation, on the bank's own scans.
    • Fluent hallucination from a vision-language model

      An unreadable name becomes a plausible one, or a digit is invented. It looks cleaner than OCR garbage.

      Spot itTest with degraded images: does the model flag uncertainty or confidently output text? Cross-check with a second reader or a checksum.
    • Scrambled order, columns or digits

      Right-to-left tables read left to right, bidirectional text stored in visual order, or a digit system silently converted.

      Spot itRound-trip a few documents and compare the stored string with the page. Check tables and numbers inside Arabic sentences.
    • Over-normalising so errors disappear

      Folding alef variants and dropping diacritics is fine for search, wrong for scoring a name or number.

      Spot itAsk what normalisation the metric applied, and rescore critical fields without it.
    • Choosing a model on an English or translated benchmark

      The leaderboard winner loses on dialect or mixed-language messages.

      Spot itAsk for results by segment (MSA, Gulf dialect, mixed, English) on production-like data, with intervals.
    • An LLM judge that prefers English or longer answers

      Generative evaluation scored by a model that is itself weaker in Arabic.

      Spot itCompare judge scores with native-speaker ratings on a sample and report agreement.
    • Sharia screening on a single method and a stale price

      A stock quietly crosses a limit after a market fall and stays in a compliant fund.

      Spot itAsk which methodology the board approved, which base is used, how often re-screening runs and the tolerance rule.
    • Paraphrasing a standard instead of quoting it

      The summary shifts a condition (from "must" to "may"), or cites a superseded edition.

      Spot itRequire clause number, edition and exact text beside every answer; check a sample against the source.
    • Letting the model rule on compliance

      A confident "this structure is compliant" with no scholar behind it.

      Spot itFind the approval line in the process. If output reaches a customer product without a named Sharia authority signing, stop.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. An OCR system has a 0.5% character error rate. A 23-character IBAN is read. What is the chance it is entirely right, and what does a checksum add?

    0.99523 ≈ 0.891: 89% right, 11% wrong. A checksum cannot fix an IBAN but flags about 99% of the wrong ones, so roughly 0.1% stay silently wrong instead of 11%.

    2. Why can the same Arabic letter look different in different places in a word, and why does that break character-by-character OCR?

    Arabic is cursive. Letters join and take initial, medial, final or isolated forms, so there are no clean gaps to cut on. Segmentation-first OCR guesses boundaries badly; modern systems read the whole line and predict the sequence.

    3. Name two reasons a score from an English benchmark translated into Arabic is a weak guide for your call-centre use case.

    It is mostly formal Arabic, while callers use Gulf dialect mixed with English. Translation also carries English-world content, and the format is usually multiple choice while the task is generation. (Others: tokenisation cost, contamination, small samples.)

    4. Model A beats B by 3.6 points on a public benchmark that is 60% English and 40% MSA. Your traffic is 10% English, 5% MSA, 55% Gulf dialect, 30% mixed. With accuracies A: 92/80/58/62 and B: 84/83/74/71, who wins on your traffic?

    A: 9.2 + 4 + 31.9 + 18.6 = 63.7. B: 8.4 + 4.15 + 40.7 + 21.3 = 74.55. B wins by about 10.9 points. The segment mix flipped the ranking, not the models.

    5. A company has AED 3bn interest-bearing debt, market cap AED 12bn, total assets AED 14bn. Under the 30% market-cap test and the 33% total-assets test, what happens, and what if the share price falls 25%?

    Market cap: 3 ÷ 12 = 25%, pass. Total assets: 3 ÷ 14 ≈ 21%, pass. After a 25% fall, market cap is 9bn: 3 ÷ 9 = 33%, which fails the 30% test, while total assets stay at about 21%. Same company, different verdict: the base matters. (Thresholds as commonly quoted; use your board's method.)

    6. Why is a vision-language model's OCR error sometimes more dangerous than a classic engine's?

    Classic engines output visible garbage or low confidence on bad regions. A generative model writes fluent, plausible text that passes review. Mitigate with checksums, a second reader and degraded-image tests.

    7. What is the role of an AAOIFI-style standard versus the bank's Sharia board, and what does that imply for an AI assistant?

    Standards are reference documents; the board issues the binding rulings, within the regulator's framework. The assistant retrieves and quotes standards and rulings with citations and prepares checks, but is never the authority. Any compliance conclusion needs a named human sign-off.
