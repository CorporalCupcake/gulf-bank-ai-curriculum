    Scaling Laws, Then and Now, and the Data That Feeds Them

    Why a language model's loss falls on a smooth curve as you add parameters and tokens, how Chinchilla changed the recipe, why labs now train far past it, what happens when the web runs short, and why the quality of the tokens now matters as much as the quantity.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLoss falls predictably with model size and training tokens, so an AI budget is an allocation problem between the two; the "optimal" split moves once you count the cost of serving the model and the limited supply of good text, and that is why data curation, not just size, has become the main lever.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Training a model is like training an analyst: make her cleverer (more parameters) or have her read more (more tokens). Both help, both have diminishing returns, both cost money. A brilliant analyst who has read ten cases is poor. An average one who has read a million is better, but slow and expensive to keep on staff.

    The remarkable part is that the returns are smooth and predictable. Plot error against model size on log-log axes and small models fall on a straight line. Extend it and you forecast a model a thousand times bigger before paying for it. That is a scaling law. Three consequences drive the lesson.

    • A budget is a split. For fixed compute there is a best model size and amount of text. Too big a model on too little text wastes parameters; too small on too much wastes data. Chinchilla (2022) showed the field was making the first mistake.
    • "Best" depends on what you count. A model answering billions of queries is cheaper overall if it is smaller and trained for longer, even though training costs more. Most models you will meet at a bank are built this way.
    • Text is the constraint. Good web text is finite. The question has moved from "how big?" to "what do we train on, how many times, and how well was it filtered?"
    Hold onto thisA scaling law is an empirical curve, not physics. Its coefficients depend on the data, architecture and fitting method. Use it to reason about trade-offs, never as a promise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The shape: a power law plus a floor

    Loss is held-out cross-entropy: the average surprise per token (F1.7). Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found it falls as a power law in parameters N, training tokens D and compute. Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022) wrote the combined form still used today:

    L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ

    E is a floor: the randomness of text no model can predict. The A/Nα term is the penalty for a small model; the B/Dβ term is the penalty for too little text. The exponents are small (about 0.3 to 0.4), so returns diminish: halving a penalty means multiplying its input by roughly seven. A straight log-log line simply means each doubling of data removes the same percentage of reducible loss, which is why small runs forecast big ones.

    Cost needs two rules of thumb. Training compute is C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs (2 per parameter per token forward, 4 back). Serving costs about 2·N per token.

    Chinchilla: splitting a fixed budget

    Fix C; each model size N then fixes D = C / 6N. Plotting loss along that budget gives a valley: small models are size-limited, big ones are starved of tokens, and the bottom is the compute-optimal model. DeepMind trained over 400 models to find it: grow parameters and tokens together, about 20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B Chinchilla (1.4T tokens) beat the 280B Gopher (300B tokens) on the same compute. GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) sat far on the oversized side.

    Try it. The default is GPT-3's compute and size.

    Two caveats. The valley is flat near the bottom: within a factor of two of the optimal size costs little, so 20:1 is a guide, not a cliff. And the fit is delicate: a 2024 Epoch AI replication found errors in the published parametric fit, and other work traced the Kaplan-versus-Chinchilla disagreement partly to details like learning-rate schedules. The broad picture is robust; the third decimal place is not.

    Inference-aware scaling: why everyone "overtrains"

    Think of buying a delivery van: a cheaper-to-run van costs more upfront but wins if it drives every day. Chinchilla minimised training cost only. A production model is queried billions of times at 2·N FLOPs per token, so lifetime cost is 6·N·D + 2·N·T, where T is tokens served. The cheapest route to a target loss is a smaller model trained on more tokens than Chinchilla recommends. Sardana and Frankle (2023) showed the optimal ratio rises with demand. Llama 3 8B saw about 15T tokens, roughly 1,900 per parameter (nearly 100 times the Chinchilla ratio), and its loss was still falling.

    Two refinements. Chinchilla was fitted near the optimum, so labs fit new laws to overtrained runs (Gadre et al., 2024, found loss and downstream accuracy stay predictable there). And newer preprints (2025 to 2026) add model shape (width versus depth, which changes latency) and test-time compute (reasoning models spending many tokens per answer), the latter pushing the optimum further towards overtraining. Read these as direction, not settled fact.

    Running short of data: epochs, repeats and the wall

    The law assumes every token is fresh, but good text is finite. Epoch AI (2022, updated 2024) estimated public human-written text would be fully used around 2028 (median forecast, wide range). Repeating data is the obvious fix. Like re-reading a textbook, the first re-read helps, the tenth barely does. Muennighoff et al. (2023) found that up to about four epochs, repetition is nearly as good as fresh data, and beyond about sixteen it is close to worthless. With U unique tokens and R repeats (epochs minus one):

    Deff=U+UR*(1−e−R/R*)

    R* ≈ 15 is where repeats have lost most of their value. The first pass counts fully, each extra pass counts less, and total gain from repeating saturates near 15 extra passes. Other options: more code, translation, curated sources, or generated text (see synthetic data and model collapse).

    That is the "data wall": the curve does not stop, but more compute needs repeats, synthetic data or better filtering of what exists. Filtering is the rest of the lesson.

    Turning the web into a training set

    Raw material is usually Common Crawl: petabytes of archived pages. Think of a sieve with several meshes. Stages are broadly the same everywhere; the details differ.

    StageWhat it doesWhy it matters
    Extract and language-IDPull main text from HTML, detect language.Extraction quality alone changes results. Arabic right-to-left, diacritics and mixed-language pages break easily.
    Heuristic filtersDrop pages by simple rules: too short, too many symbols, repeated lines, spam.Cheap, removes the worst junk. English-tuned rules can wrongly delete good text in other languages.
    DeduplicationRemove repeated documents or spans (MinHash similarity hashing, suffix arrays).Duplicates waste compute and increase memorisation. Lee et al. (2021) showed dedup improves models.
    Quality classifierA small model scores pages; keep the top slice.The biggest lever; see next section.
    DecontaminationRemove text matching benchmark questions or answers.Otherwise a score measures memory, not skill (see benchmark measurement).
    MixingSet shares of web, code, maths, books, multilingual text, and sometimes order (cleaner data last).The mixture decides what the model is good at; Arabic capability is won or lost here.

    Quality over quantity: FineWeb, FineWeb-Edu and DCLM

    If good tokens are scarce, a filter that finds them beats a bigger crawl, like picking the best fruit rather than buying more crates. Three public efforts released data and recipes.

    • FineWeb (Hugging Face, 2024): about 15T tokens from 96 Common Crawl snapshots, built by small ablations (train a small model per pipeline variant, keep what improves benchmarks). Deduplicating everything together hurt; per-snapshot dedup did better.
    • FineWeb-Edu: Llama 3 70B scored a few hundred thousand pages for educational value, a small classifier learned to imitate those scores, and pages above a threshold were kept (about 1.3T tokens at the stricter setting). Discarding 90% paid off on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks.
    • DCLM (2024): a competition on a fixed 240T-token pool with fixed training recipes. The winner used a simple fastText classifier trained to separate instruction-style and high-quality forum text (OpenHermes, ELI5) from random pages, keeping the top 10%. A 7B model on 2.6T tokens reached about 64% MMLU, beating comparable open models trained with more compute.

    The pattern: a small, cheap model deciding what a big, expensive one sees acts as a multiplier on every token. The toy below shows the trade. Filtering raises usefulness per token but shrinks the pool, so there is a best cut, and it depends on how long you train.

    The toy matches the literature: filtering out about 90% suits shorter runs, but very long runs run short of unique tokens. NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC (2024) combined several classifiers and rewrote low-quality pages with a language model to keep more data. Test any recipe at the scale and token count you plan.

    Caution: "quality" means whatever the classifier was trained to like, usually educational English, not colloquial Gulf Arabic or business correspondence. Filters win on different benchmarks (SmolLM2: FineWeb-Edu stronger on MMLU and ARC, DCLM on commonsense, a mixture best), and a filter tuned to a benchmark can look better than it is.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Treating "20 tokens per parameter" as a law of nature

      It minimises training compute, under one fit, for a model trained once. For a heavily served model it is the wrong target.

      Spot itAsk: optimal for what objective, and who pays for inference? Calling a 15T-token 8B model "inefficient" uses the wrong cost.
    • Extrapolating far beyond the fitted range

      A line through five small models is projected 1000 times out. Exponent errors grow with distance and the regime may bend.

      Spot itAsk how far the forecast extends past the largest model trained, and whether exponents have intervals.
    • Equating lower loss with a better product

      Loss is smooth; tasks are not. A model can gain 0.02 nats with no business change, or a capability can appear suddenly.

      Spot itAsk for evaluation on your own tasks. Web-text loss says little about Arabic contract extraction.
    • Comparing token and parameter counts across designs

      Mixture-of-experts models have total and active parameters; tokenizers differ; "tokens" may include repeated epochs.

      Spot itAsk: active or total? unique or total tokens seen? which tokenizer?
    • Benchmark contamination

      Web-scale data contains benchmark questions, so a high score may be recall of the test.

      Spot itAsk what decontamination was done; look for a held-out or newly written test set.
    • Filtering that optimises a benchmark, not a use

      A classifier that likes educational English raises MMLU and may quietly remove dialect, colloquial and non-English text.

      Spot itCheck by language and register, not only the average. Ask what the classifier's positive examples were.
    • Assuming repeated data is as good as fresh forever

      Four epochs are nearly free; thirty are not. Training loss keeps falling while held-out loss stalls and memorisation rises.

      Spot itCompare held-out loss, not training loss, and count epochs per source.
    • Trusting a data-quality gain tested only at small scale

      A filter that helps a 400M model on 8B tokens may not help a 7B model on 5T tokens (see Simulation 4).

      Spot itAsk at what model size and token count it was tested, and whether it was re-checked at target scale.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only C ≈ 6ND and the Part 2 formulas.

    1. You have 6×1022 FLOPs and follow the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule. What model size and token count do you train?

    D = 20N, so C = 120N². N = √(5×1020) ≈ 2.2×1010, about 22B parameters, and D ≈ 450B tokens. Check: 6 × 2.2×1010 × 4.5×1011 ≈ 6×1022.

    2. An 8B model is trained on 15T tokens. How many tokens per parameter is that, and how many served tokens would cost as much as the training?

    15×1012 ÷ 8×109 ≈ 1,900, about 94 times Chinchilla. Training is 6ND; serving is 2N per token, so equality needs T = 3D = 45 trillion tokens. A widely used model passes that, so paying more for training can be cheaper overall.

    3. You have 1 trillion unique tokens and train for 4 epochs (4T tokens in total). How much is that worth in fresh tokens?

    R = 3. Deff = 1 + 15.4 × (1 − e−3/15.4) = 1 + 15.4 × 0.177 ≈ 3.73T, about 93% of the nominal 4T. Four epochs are nearly free.

    4. A filter keeps the top 10% of a 100T pool and you train on 15T tokens. How many epochs, and what is the repeat penalty?

    The pool is 10T, so 1.5 epochs, R = 0.5. Deff = 10 + 10 × 15.4 × (1 − e−0.5/15.4) ≈ 14.9T. The penalty is about 0.5%, so the gain from better tokens is almost undiluted. At 100T of training it would be ten epochs and a heavy penalty.

    5. Why can a smaller model that reaches the same loss as a larger one be the better buy, even though it took more compute to train?

    Lifetime cost is training plus serving. Serving costs 2 FLOPs per parameter per token, and a production model may process more tokens than it was trained on. The smaller model is cheaper per token forever, so the extra training spend is repaid once enough tokens are served. Break-even depends on demand.

    6. A paper reports that its new filter raised average benchmark score by 2 points on a 1B model trained on 30B tokens. What do you ask before you believe it will help a 7B model trained on 3T tokens?

    Whether it was tested at target scale, since the best filter depends on model size and token count (a harsh filter may run out of unique text in a long run). Also: is the gain across benchmarks or a few, how many seeds (2 points can be noise), was contamination checked, and how does it do by language.

    7. Why might a quality classifier hurt an Arabic-English bank assistant even while it improves the English leaderboard score?

    It prefers the style of its positive examples, usually educational English. Arabic, dialect and business-style text score lower and are discarded disproportionately, and averaged benchmarks hide this. Evaluate by language and register, check the classifier's training examples, and consider per-language thresholds.
