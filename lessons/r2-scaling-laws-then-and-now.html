    Scaling Laws, Then and Now, and the Data That Feeds Them

    Why a language model's loss falls on a smooth curve as you add parameters and tokens, how Chinchilla changed the recipe, why labs now train far past it, what happens when the web runs short, and why the quality of the tokens now matters as much as the quantity.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLoss falls predictably with model size and training tokens, so an AI budget is an allocation problem between the two; the "optimal" split moves once you count the cost of serving the model and the limited supply of good text, and that is why data curation, not just size, has become the main lever.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine training an analyst. You can make her cleverer (a bigger brain) or you can make her read more (more cases). Both help, both have diminishing returns, and both cost money. The interesting question is the mix. A brilliant analyst who has read ten cases is poor. An average analyst who has read a million is better, but only up to a point, and she is slow to pay each month.

    Language models behave the same way, with one remarkable difference: the returns are smooth and predictable. Train small models on small data, plot error against size, and the points fall on a straight line when both axes are logarithmic. Extend the line and it forecasts the error of a model a thousand times bigger, before anyone has paid for it. That is a scaling law.

    Three consequences drive everything in this lesson.

    • A budget is a split. For a fixed amount of compute, there is a best size of model and amount of text. Too big a model on too little text wastes parameters. Too small a model on too much text wastes data. The 2022 Chinchilla result said the field had been making the first mistake.
    • "Best" depends on what you count. If the model will answer billions of questions, a smaller model trained for longer is cheaper overall, even though it costs more to train. Almost every model you will meet in a bank was trained this way, well beyond the Chinchilla balance.
    • The text is the constraint. Good web text is finite and the good part is smaller still. So the question has shifted from "how big?" to "what do we train on, how many times, and how well was it filtered?"
    Hold onto thisA scaling law is an empirical curve, not a physical law. It is a forecasting tool for allocating compute, and its coefficients depend on the data, the architecture and the way you fit it. Use it to reason about trade-offs, never as a promise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The shape: a power law plus a floor

    The loss here is the cross-entropy on held-out text, the average surprise per token (F1.7). Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI (2020) found that it fell as a power law in parameters N, in training tokens D, and in compute, over many orders of magnitude. Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind (2022) wrote the combined form that is still used as the working model:

    L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ

    Read it in words. E is a floor: the irreducible randomness of text that no model can predict (what word the author happened to choose). The second term is the penalty for having a model that is too small, and it shrinks as N grows. The third is the penalty for having seen too little text, and it shrinks as D grows. The exponents α and β are small, around 0.3 to 0.4, which is why returns diminish: to halve a penalty term you must multiply its input by roughly seven.

    The second ingredient is cost. A transformer's training compute is close to C ≈ 6·N·D floating-point operations: about 2 per parameter per token to go forward and 4 to go back. Serving the model costs about 2·N per generated or read token. These two rules of thumb are all the accounting you need.

    "Log-log straight line" deserves one sentence of intuition. If doubling the data always shaves the same percentage off the reducible loss, then the loss against data is a power law, and a power law is a straight line when you plot logs. The straightness is what lets small experiments forecast big ones.

    Chinchilla: splitting a fixed budget

    Fix C. Every choice of N fixes D = C / 6N, so you can trace loss against model size along that budget (an "iso-FLOP" curve). It is a valley: too small a model is limited by its size, too big a model is starved of tokens, and the bottom is the compute-optimal model. DeepMind trained over 400 models to locate that bottom and concluded that parameters and tokens should grow in roughly equal proportion, around 20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B model, Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat the 280B Gopher that used the same compute but only 300 billion tokens. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) had also been far on the large-model side of the valley.

    Try it. The default setting is GPT-3's compute with GPT-3's size.

    Two honest caveats. First, the valley is flat near the bottom: being within a factor of two of the optimal size costs very little loss, which is why the "20 tokens per parameter" rule is a guide and not a cliff. Second, the fit itself is delicate. The original paper's three methods gave slightly different answers, and a 2024 replication by Epoch AI found that the published parametric fit had errors and that corrected coefficients gave the 20-tokens-per-parameter answer the other methods did. Other researchers traced the earlier Kaplan-versus-Chinchilla disagreement partly to experimental details such as how the learning-rate schedule was set. The takeaway for a meeting: the broad picture is robust, the third decimal place is not.

    Inference-aware scaling: why everyone "overtrains"

    Chinchilla minimised the cost of training for a target loss. But a model in production is queried, perhaps billions of times, and each query costs 2·N FLOPs per token. A smaller model is cheaper every time it is used. So if you add serving to the bill, the cheapest way to reach a given quality is a smaller model trained on more tokens than Chinchilla recommends. You pay more up front and save every day afterwards.

    The total lifetime cost for a target loss is 6·N·D plus 2·N·T, where T is the number of tokens the model will process in service. Sardana and Frankle (2023) made exactly this argument and showed the optimal ratio rises with expected demand. The industry took the lesson: Meta's Llama 3 8B was trained on about 15 trillion tokens, roughly 1,900 tokens per parameter, nearly a hundred times the Chinchilla ratio, and its loss was still falling.

    Two refinements keep this honest. First, the Chinchilla law was fitted on models trained near the optimum; heavily overtrained models sit outside the data it was fitted on, so labs fit new laws to overtrained runs (Gadre et al., 2024, showed loss and downstream accuracy remain predictable there). Second, 2025 and 2026 work adds more to the accounting: a 2025 paper adds the model's shape (width versus depth) to the law because it changes latency, and a 2026 preprint folds in test-time compute (reasoning models that spend many tokens per answer), concluding this pushes the optimum further towards overtraining. Both are preprints and should be read as direction, not settled fact.

    Running short of data: epochs, repeats and the wall

    The scaling law quietly assumed every token is fresh. In practice the supply of good text is finite. Epoch AI's 2022 analysis, updated in 2024, estimated that the stock of public human-written text would be fully used by around 2028 under current trends (a median forecast with a wide range). Even before then, labs are limited by how much of that stock is good.

    What happens if you run out and repeat the data? Muennighoff and colleagues (2023) trained hundreds of models with repeated data and found that up to about four epochs, repetition is nearly as good as fresh data, and after about sixteen epochs the extra passes are close to worthless. They fitted a form where the value of each repeat decays exponentially: the effective data from D tokens drawn from a pool of U unique tokens is

    Deff=U+UR*(1−e−R/R*)

    where R is the number of repeats (epochs minus one) and R* ≈ 15 is the point where repeats have lost most of their value. In words: the first pass counts fully, each extra pass counts a little less, and the total gain from repeating saturates at about 15 extra passes' worth. Other options exist: add code, translate, mix in more curated sources, or use model-generated text (R2.3 covers the risks of that last one in synthetic data and model collapse).

    This is the sense in which the field "hit a data wall": not that the curve stops, but that more compute needs a recipe with repeats, synthetic data or better filtering of what exists. The last of these is the subject of the rest of the lesson.

    Turning the web into a training set

    The raw material is usually Common Crawl: petabytes of archived web pages gathered every month or two. A pretraining dataset is what is left after a pipeline. The stages are broadly the same everywhere, and the differences between datasets are in the details.

    StageWhat it doesWhy it matters
    Extract and language-IDPull the main text out of HTML (boilerplate, menus and ads removed), then detect the language.Extraction quality alone changes results. For Arabic, right-to-left text, diacritics and mixed Arabic-English pages are easy to break.
    Heuristic filtersDrop pages by simple rules: too short, too many symbols, repeated lines, adult or spam markers, low ratio of words that end in punctuation.Cheap and removes the worst junk. Rules written for English can wrongly delete good text in other languages.
    DeduplicationRemove repeated documents or long repeated spans, usually with MinHash (similar-set hashing) or suffix arrays, within and across crawl snapshots.Duplicates waste compute, increase memorisation and distort what the model treats as common. Lee et al. (2021) showed it improves models.
    Quality classifierA small model scores each page; keep the top slice.The biggest lever, and the one the next section is about.
    DecontaminationRemove text that matches benchmark questions or answers.Otherwise a benchmark score measures memory, not skill (see benchmark measurement).
    MixingChoose the share of web, code, maths, books, multilingual text and so on, and sometimes the order (cleaner data last).The mixture decides what the model is good at; this is where Arabic capability is won or lost.

    Quality over quantity: FineWeb, FineWeb-Edu and DCLM

    If good tokens are the scarce thing, then a filter that picks them out is worth more than a bigger crawl. Three public efforts made this concrete, with the datasets and recipes released so others could check.

    • FineWeb (Hugging Face, 2024) is about 15 trillion tokens from 96 Common Crawl snapshots, built after many small ablations: train a small model on each variant of the pipeline and keep whatever improves a basket of benchmarks. One lesson was that deduplicating everything together can hurt; per-snapshot deduplication did better.
    • FineWeb-Edu keeps the pages that look educational. A large language model (Llama 3 70B) scored a few hundred thousand pages for educational value, a small classifier was trained to imitate those scores, and pages above a threshold were kept: roughly 1.3 trillion tokens at the stricter setting. The 90% that is discarded is a price worth paying for knowledge and reasoning benchmarks.
    • DCLM (DataComp for Language Models, 2024) turned data selection into a competition: a fixed 240-trillion-token pool, fixed training recipes at several scales, and everyone submits a filtering method. The winner, DCLM-Baseline, used a simple fastText classifier trained to separate instruction-style and high-quality forum text (OpenHermes and ELI5-type content) from random web pages, keeping the top 10%. A 7B model trained on it with 2.6T tokens reached about 64% on MMLU, better than comparable open models trained with more compute.

    The pattern is that a small, cheap model deciding what a big, expensive model sees gives a large gain. Cleaner data is, in effect, a multiplier on every token. The simulation below makes that trade explicit with a toy: filtering raises the usefulness of each token but shrinks the pool, so there is a best cut, and it depends on how many tokens you plan to train for.

    The toy reproduces a finding that is now stated in the literature: aggressive filtering that discards about 90% of the web suits shorter training runs, but for the very long runs labs now use it runs short of unique tokens. NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC (2024) responded by using several classifiers together, rewriting low-quality pages with a language model to rescue their information, and so keeping more data for long-horizon training. The practical rule is the same as everywhere else in this lesson: the best recipe depends on the whole budget, so test it at the scale and token count you actually plan.

    One caution on method. "Quality" here means whatever the classifier was trained to like, which means educational and encyclopaedic English far more than colloquial Gulf Arabic or business correspondence. Quality classifiers can therefore narrow the model's world. Different filters win on different benchmarks (a SmolLM2 study found FineWeb-Edu stronger on knowledge exams such as MMLU and ARC, DCLM stronger on commonsense tasks, and a mixture best), and a filter tuned to a benchmark can look better than it is.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Scaling is the budgeting layer under the whole language-model stack. These are the places it touches the rest of the curriculum.

    F1.6 – F1.7 · Likelihood and entropyWhat the loss in the law actually is

    The law predicts cross-entropy per token. The floor E is the entropy of language that no model can remove.

    F4.1 · Bias, variance, generalisationTwo ways to be wrong

    The N term and the D term are, loosely, capacity-limited error and data-limited error, the same trade in different clothes.

    F5.4 · Self-supervised learningWhy there is so much to scale on

    Predicting the next token needs no labels, so the supply of training signal is the whole text of the web, not a labelled set.

    F6 · OverfittingRepeats are mild overfitting

    Repeating data is why a model's training loss can fall while held-out loss stalls, and why data augmentation and dropout come back at scale.

    M1.1 · M1.2 · Pretraining and tokensWhere this budget is spent

    Pretraining is the phase these laws govern. Post-training (M1) is a small fraction of compute, with its own scaling behaviour.

    M1 · TokenisationTokens are not a fixed unit

    "Trained on 15 trillion tokens" depends on the tokenizer. Arabic usually takes more tokens per word, so equal token counts mean less Arabic text seen.

    M2 · Cost and latencyThe serving side of the bill

    The 2·N per token rule is why model choice at the bank is a cost and latency decision, and why small, long-trained models are attractive.

    P3 · Quantisation, pruning, distillationOther ways to shrink the serving bill

    Distillation and quantisation cut inference cost after training; overtraining cuts it by design. They stack.

    R1.1 · Mixture of expertsParameters you do not pay for

    With sparse models, "N" splits into total and active parameters, and the law's fit changes. Serving cost follows the active count.

    R2.3 · Synthetic dataThe escape from the data wall, and its risks

    If fresh human text is the limit, generated text is the obvious supply. When it helps and when it degrades the model is the next lesson.

    R3 · Post-training and reasoningA second scaling axis

    Reinforcement learning and test-time compute give a different return on spend, which is why inference-aware laws now include reasoning tokens.

    R6 · Double descent and grokkingWhere smooth curves break

    Scaling laws describe average behaviour; some capabilities and some behaviours at small scale do not follow a smooth line.

    R7 · Measuring capabilityFrom loss to what a model can do

    Loss is smooth; usefulness on tasks is not. Linking the two is a measurement problem, covered with task horizons and benchmark theory.

    G1 · Data poisoningThe web is an untrusted supply chain

    Curating a crawl is also a security step. Anything an attacker can get onto the open web may enter pretraining.

    R11 · Memorisation and privacyDuplicates drive memorisation

    Deduplication is one of the cheapest mitigations against models reproducing training text, including personal data.

    Where it shows up at the bank, even if you never train a foundation model:

    • Buy, adapt or build decisions. You will choose between a hosted frontier model, an open model run on-premises (hybrid and on-premises deployment) and a small model fine-tuned on bank text. Scaling reasoning tells you why a well-trained 8B open model can match last year's much larger one, and why a vendor's parameter count tells you little.
    • Reading model cards. Parameters, tokens trained, and data mix are the three numbers that predict quality and cost. Tokens-per-parameter and the stated data sources are quick tests of how a model was built.
    • Arabic and regional models. Falcon (built by the UAE's Technology Innovation Institute) was trained on RefinedWeb, a filtered Common Crawl set, and the same group's papers are a primary source on web curation. For Arabic, the limiting factor is the pool of high-quality Arabic text, so curation and the tokenizer matter more than they do for English.
    • Fine-tuning your own data. A bank's internal corpus (policies, contracts, call transcripts) is small. The repeat-decay result says a few passes help and many passes mostly memorise, which matters for confidentiality as well as accuracy.
    • Forecasting spend. Training FLOPs, GPU-hours and serving tokens are computed the same way here as in your capacity plan for an internal assistant.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basic picture is accepted. The open arguments are how reliably the laws extrapolate, what to do when data runs short, and how to account for reasoning at inference time. Quality filtering has moved from folklore to published, reproducible recipes.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with two light searches; older items are cited from the papers as I remember them and are marked in the Sources list. The 2026 items are preprints.

    1. 2020
      Scaling laws for neural language models

      Kaplan et al. showed smooth power laws in size, data and compute, and concluded that bigger models were the best use of extra compute. The field followed, producing very large models on modest token counts.

      Kaplan et al., arXiv, 2020
    2. 2022
      Chinchilla

      Hoffmann et al. re-fitted the problem, found that parameters and tokens should scale together (around 20 tokens per parameter), and showed a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beating a 280B model at the same compute.

      Hoffmann et al., NeurIPS, 2022
    3. 2023
      Data-constrained scaling, and inference-aware scaling

      Muennighoff et al. quantified the value of repeated data (about four epochs are nearly free). Sardana and Frankle put inference demand into the optimum and showed that smaller, longer-trained models are cheaper to run for large volumes.

      Muennighoff et al., NeurIPS, 2023; Sardana & Frankle, ICML, 2024
    4. 2023–24
      Web curation becomes a published craft

      Falcon's RefinedWeb showed filtered web data alone can match curated corpora. FineWeb and FineWeb-Edu published full pipelines and ablations; DCLM set up a controlled benchmark for data selection and released its pool, code and winning baseline.

      Penedo et al., 2023 and 2024; Li et al., NeurIPS, 2024
    5. 2024
      Replication and reconciliation

      Epoch AI's replication found problems in the Chinchilla parametric fit and corrected them; other work explained the Kaplan versus Chinchilla discrepancy through methodological details. Gadre et al. showed overtrained models still follow predictable laws. Epoch also updated its estimate of when the stock of public text runs out.

      Besiroglu et al., 2024; Porian et al., 2024; Gadre et al., 2024; Villalobos et al., 2024
    6. 2024–25
      Quality at long horizons

      Nemotron-CC argued that aggressive single-classifier filtering limits long training runs, and recovered volume by ensembling classifiers and rewriting low-quality text. SmolLM2 showed a tuned mix of FineWeb-Edu, DCLM and others beat either alone for a small model.

      Su et al., 2024; Allal et al., 2025
    7. 2025
      Architecture-aware scaling

      A paper on inference-efficient models added model shape (hidden size, depth) to the scaling law and reported better loss prediction than the Chinchilla form, including for overtrained models.

      Bian et al., arXiv 2501.18107
    8. 2026
      Test-time compute and data-constrained laws, revisited

      One preprint jointly optimises model size, training tokens and inference compute, and finds the optimum is overtrained when you account for repeated sampling at test time. Another argues the standard laws were calibrated for data-rich single-epoch training and adds an overfitting term for the data-limited case. A third reports a pretraining-data curation method evolved automatically that edges out DCLM by about 1.7 points on its average, a self-reported result at one model size. Treat all three as preprints awaiting independent replication.

      arXiv 2604.01411; arXiv 2605.09189; arXiv 2603.14420

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor slides, press coverage and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Treating "20 tokens per parameter" as a law of nature

      It is the optimum for minimising training compute, under one fit, for models trained once. For a model that will be heavily served it is the wrong target.

      Spot itAsk: optimal for what objective, and who pays for inference? If someone calls a 15T-token 8B model "inefficient", they are using the wrong cost.
    • Extrapolating far beyond the fitted range

      A line through five small models is projected 1000 times out. Errors in the exponents grow with distance, and the unobserved regime may bend.

      Spot itAsk how far the forecast extends past the largest model actually trained, and whether the paper shows an interval on the exponents.
    • Equating lower loss with a better product

      Loss is smooth; tasks are not. A model can gain 0.02 nats and a business metric not move, or a capability appear suddenly.

      Spot itAsk for the task-level evaluation on your own tasks. Loss on web text says little about Arabic contract extraction.
    • Comparing token counts and parameter counts across different designs

      Mixture-of-experts models have total and active parameters; tokenizers differ; "tokens" may include repeated epochs.

      Spot itAsk: active or total? unique tokens or total tokens seen? which tokenizer?
    • Benchmark contamination

      Web-scale data contains benchmark questions, so a high score may reflect recall of the test.

      Spot itAsk what decontamination was done, and look for a held-out or newly written test set.
    • Filtering that optimises a benchmark, not a use

      A quality classifier trained to like educational English raises MMLU and may quietly remove dialect, colloquial and non-English text.

      Spot itCheck performance by language and register, not only on the headline average. Ask what the classifier's positive examples were.
    • Assuming repeated data is as good as fresh data forever

      Four epochs are nearly free; thirty are not. Training loss keeps falling while held-out loss stalls and memorisation rises.

      Spot itCompare held-out loss, not training loss, and count epochs over each source.
    • Believing a data-quality gain that was tested only at small scale

      A filter that helps a 400M model on 8B tokens may not help a 7B model on 5T tokens, as the toy in Simulation 4 suggests.

      Spot itAsk at what model size and token count the filter was tested, and whether it was re-checked at the target scale.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Model quality improves in a very predictable way as you spend more on size and training text, which is why labs can plan multi-million-dollar training runs in advance. Two things have changed in the last few years. First, the cheapest model to run for a bank is often a smaller one trained for much longer, because we pay for every question it answers. Second, the limit is no longer compute but good text, so what counts is how carefully the training data was cleaned. For us that means a vendor's parameter count tells us little. I'd ask what it was trained on, how much of that was Arabic, and test it on our own documents.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd frame it as a constrained optimisation: loss as E plus a size term plus a data term, compute roughly 6ND, serving roughly 2N per token. Chinchilla optimises training cost alone and gives about 20 tokens per parameter, though the original parametric fit was corrected by a replication. Add expected lifetime inference and the optimum moves to smaller, longer-trained models, which is why recent open models run at hundreds or thousands of tokens per parameter. Once unique text binds, repeats beyond roughly four epochs decay, so curation and mixture choice become the lever. I'd treat any quality filter as a hypothesis to test at the target scale, and check the result by language, not just on the average.”
    “Why didn't the vendor just build the biggest model they could?”

    Because size is only one of two knobs and the cost does not stop at training. A bigger model costs more on every query, and without enough text it does not even reach the quality its size suggests. For a heavily used model the cheapest route to a quality target is usually a smaller model trained on far more tokens.

    “Are scaling laws still holding, or has progress hit a wall?”

    The pretraining curves still hold where they have been tested; what has tightened is the supply of good unique text. The field has responded with better filtering, multi-epoch training on the best sources, synthetic data and, separately, spending more compute at inference time. So returns from simply scaling web pretraining are diminishing in cost terms, while other axes have opened. I would say that with some humility: the long-range forecasts have large error bars.

    “Does an open 8B model really compete with a model ten times bigger?”

    On many tasks, closely, because it was trained on perhaps a hundred times more tokens per parameter than the Chinchilla rule, on better-curated data. It will usually lose on breadth of knowledge and hard multi-step reasoning, where size still buys capacity. The answer for the bank is to evaluate on our tasks, since the right model is set by accuracy, latency and cost together.

    “Why does data quality matter so much if the model is so big?”

    Because a token of clean, informative text teaches more than a token of spam or a duplicate, and good tokens are finite. In DCLM's controlled comparison, selecting the right 10% of the web beat using more of it. But the best filter depends on how long you train: harsh filtering suits short runs, and long runs run out of unique text.

    “Can we use this to predict how good a model we fine-tune will be?”

    Only roughly. Scaling laws are reliable for pretraining loss on a fixed data mix. Fine-tuning on a small bank-specific corpus follows different behaviour, so I'd run a small learning-curve experiment on our own data: train on 10%, 25%, 50% and 100% and extrapolate with an interval, treating the forecast as a guide.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only C ≈ 6ND and the formulas in Part 2.

    1. You have 6×1022 FLOPs and follow the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule. What model size and token count do you train?

    D = 20N, so C = 6·N·20N = 120N². N = √(6×1022 ÷ 120) = √(5×1020) ≈ 2.2×1010, about 22B parameters, and D ≈ 4.5×1011, about 450B tokens. Check: 6 × 2.2×1010 × 4.5×1011 ≈ 6×1022.

    2. An 8B model is trained on 15T tokens. How many tokens per parameter is that, and how many tokens of serving would cost as much as the training?

    15×1012 ÷ 8×109 ≈ 1,900, about 94 times the Chinchilla ratio. Training costs 6ND = 7.2×1023. Serving costs 2N per token, so equality needs T = 6ND ÷ 2N = 3D = 45 trillion tokens served. A widely used model passes that, which is why paying more for training can be the cheaper choice.

    3. You have 1 trillion unique tokens and train for 4 epochs (4T tokens in total). How much is that worth in fresh tokens?

    Epochs minus one gives R = 3. Deff = 1 + 15.4 × (1 − e−3/15.4) = 1 + 15.4 × (1 − 0.823) ≈ 1 + 2.73 = 3.73T, about 93% of the nominal 4T. Four epochs are nearly free.

    4. A filter keeps the top 10% of a 100T pool and you train on 15T tokens. How many epochs, and what is the repeat penalty?

    The pool is 10T, so 15T is 1.5 epochs and R = 0.5. Deff = 10 + 10 × 15.4 × (1 − e−0.5/15.4) ≈ 10 + 4.92 = 14.9T. The repeat penalty is about 0.5%, so the gain from the better tokens is almost undiluted. At 100T of training, the same filter would mean ten epochs and a heavy penalty.

    5. Why can a smaller model that reaches the same loss as a larger one be the better buy, even though it took more compute to train?

    Lifetime cost is training plus serving. Serving costs 2 FLOPs per parameter per token processed, and a model in production may process more tokens than it was trained on. A smaller model has a lower per-token cost forever, so the extra training spend is repaid once enough tokens have been served. The break-even depends on demand.

    6. A paper reports that its new filter raised average benchmark score by 2 points on a 1B model trained on 30B tokens. What do you ask before you believe it will help a 7B model trained on 3T tokens?

    Whether it was tested at the target scale or only at small scale, because the best filter depends on model size and token count (a harsh filter may run short of unique text in a long run). Also: whether the gain holds on each benchmark or comes from a few, how many seeds were run (a 2-point gap can be noise), whether contamination was checked, and how the filter does by language.

    7. Why might a quality classifier hurt an Arabic-English bank assistant even while it improves the English leaderboard score?

    The classifier learns to prefer the style of its positive examples, usually educational English. Arabic pages, dialect text and business-style writing score lower and are discarded disproportionately, so the model sees less of what the bank needs. Averaged benchmarks hide this. Evaluate by language and register, check the classifier's training examples, and consider a separate threshold per language.
