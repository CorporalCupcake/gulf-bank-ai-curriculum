    Scaling Laws

    Part 1

    What a scaling law is

    In one sentenceA scaling law is a smooth, predictable curve showing how a model's error falls as you add parameters (size), text (tokens) or compute.

    Think of training an analyst. You can make her cleverer (more parameters) or have her read more (more tokens). Both help, both have diminishing returns, and both cost money.

    The surprise is that the returns are so regular. Plot small models on log-log axes (each step is a multiplication, not an addition) and they fall on a straight line. So you can train five cheap models and forecast a much bigger one before paying for it.

    The line is an observation, not physics. It works as a way to reason about trade-offs, never as a promise.

    How it differs from other ideas

    • Learning curve: on your own small dataset, how score changes with more rows. A scaling law is the same idea for pretraining giant models.
    Part 2

    How it works

    Loss here means the average surprise per token on text the model hasn't seen. Lower is better.

    1. Train several small models of different sizes on different amounts of text.
    2. Record each one's loss.
    3. Fit a curve: a fixed floor (the randomness of text itself) plus a penalty for small size plus a penalty for too little text.
    4. Both penalties shrink as a power law, with small exponents of about 0.3 to 0.4. Halving a penalty means multiplying its input by roughly seven.
    5. Extrapolate to the big model you actually want to build.

    Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found the power-law shape; Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022) the combined form.

    Chinchilla: how to split a fixed budget

    Say you have a fixed compute budget. A big model sees too little text; a small one has too little capacity. The loss across model sizes forms a valley, and the bottom is the best split.

    DeepMind (2022) found the bottom by training over 400 models: grow parameters and tokens together, at about 20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B Chinchilla (1.4T tokens) beat the 280B Gopher (300B tokens) on the same compute. GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) sat on the oversized side. The valley is flat near the bottom, so being a factor of two off costs little.

    Why everyone now "overtrains"

    Chinchilla counted only the cost of training. But a model answers billions of queries, and serving costs too. Like a delivery van that costs more upfront but is cheaper to run, a smaller model trained on far more text can win overall. Sardana and Frankle (2023) showed the best tokens-to-parameters ratio rises with expected demand.

    Llama 3 8B saw about 15T tokens, roughly 1,900 per parameter and nearly 100 times the Chinchilla ratio.

    Running out of text

    The law assumes every token is fresh, but good text is finite. Epoch AI estimates public human-written text runs out around 2028, with a wide range. Repeating data is like re-reading a textbook: up to about four passes is nearly as good as fresh text, and beyond about sixteen it is close to worthless (Muennighoff et al., 2023). The sign is training loss still falling while held-out loss stalls.

    Quality matters too: filtering a crawl with a small classifier gave better models from less data in FineWeb-Edu and DCLM (both 2024).

    Part 3

    How to read it

    A model with 7B parameters and 140B tokens
    That is 20 tokens per parameter, the Chinchilla baseline: best for training cost alone.
    A forecast 1,000 times beyond your largest run
    Treat it as a guess. Stay within about ten times your largest model.
    Cost rule of thumb
    Training costs about 6 FLOPs (basic arithmetic operations) per parameter per token. Serving costs about 2 per parameter per token.
    Part 4

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    ApproachPick it whenAvoid when
    Compute-optimal (about 20 tokens per parameter)You train once, serve little, and training cost dominates.The model will serve billions of queries.
    Overtrained small modelHeavy serving: assistants, search, high-volume classification.Demand is uncertain or training budget is the tight limit.
    Fine-tune an open modelAlmost every bank: you need behaviour and domain, not new foundations.The base model lacks your language or domain.
    Learning curve on your own dataClassical ML on tabular data, deciding whether more labels would help.You are choosing a pretraining budget.

    Strong at:

    • Forecasting big runs from small ones before spending the real budget.
    • Splitting a budget between size and text.
    • Checking vendor claims: a quoted size, token count and loss can be sanity-checked against the curve.

    Weak at:

    • Predicting skills: loss is smooth, tasks are not. Web-text loss says little about Arabic contract extraction.
    • Precision: a 2024 Epoch AI replication found errors in the published fit. The broad picture is robust; the third decimal place is not.
    Show the formula

    Loss = E + A ÷ N0.35 + B ÷ D0.37, roughly. N is parameters, D is tokens, and E is the floor. The two fractions are the size penalty and the text penalty, and each shrinks as its input grows.

    Compute C ≈ 6 × N × D. For a fixed C, a bigger N means a smaller D, which is what makes the valley.

    In banking, almost no bank pretrains, so these ideas are used to choose among open models, estimate serving cost, and ask suppliers sharp questions about training data and Arabic coverage.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Extrapolating far beyond the fitted range

      A line through five small models projected a thousand times out is a guess. Ask how far past the largest model the forecast goes.

    • Treating 20 tokens per parameter as a law

      It minimises training cost only. For a heavily served model it is the wrong target. Ask who pays for serving.

    • Equating lower loss with a better product

      Loss is smooth, tasks are not. Evaluate on your own tasks.

    • Reusing coefficients from another setup

      Refit when the architecture, tokenizer, data mix or schedule changes.

    1. What does a scaling law let you do?

    Train a few small models, fit a curve, and forecast the loss of a much bigger one before paying for it.

    2. What is the Chinchilla result?

    For a fixed compute budget, grow model size and tokens together, at about 20 tokens per parameter.

    3. Why is Llama 3 8B trained on about 1,900 tokens per parameter?

    It will be served to huge numbers of users, so a smaller model trained on much more text is cheaper over its lifetime.

    4. What is the data wall?

    Fresh high-quality text is finite, and repeating it helps less each pass: nearly as good as fresh up to about four passes, close to worthless beyond about sixteen.

    5. You have a fraud model on 200,000 rows. Do you need a scaling law?

    No. Draw a learning curve: score on held-out data with 10%, 20%, 50% and 100% of the rows and see whether it is still rising.
