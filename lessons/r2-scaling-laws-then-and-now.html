    Scaling Laws, Then and Now, and the Data That Feeds Them

    Why model loss falls on a smooth curve, how Chinchilla changed the recipe, why labs now train far past it, and why data quality has become the main lever.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLoss falls predictably with model size and training tokens, so an AI budget is a split between the two, and because good text is finite, data curation has become the main lever.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Training a model is like training an analyst: make her cleverer (more parameters) or have her read more (more tokens). Both help, both have diminishing returns, both cost money.

    The returns are smooth and predictable. Small models fall on a straight line on log-log axes, so you can forecast a much bigger model before paying for it. That is a scaling law. It means a budget is a split between size and text, the best split depends on what you count, and good text is the real constraint.

    Hold onto thisA scaling law is an empirical curve, not physics. Use it to reason about trade-offs, never as a promise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The shape: a power law plus a floor

    Loss is the average surprise per token on held-out text. It falls with more parameters and more tokens, but with diminishing returns, and never below a floor: the randomness of text itself. Each doubling removes the same percentage of the avoidable loss, which is why small runs forecast big ones.

    The law has three parts. A floor is the randomness no model can predict. A size penalty shrinks as parameters grow. A data penalty shrinks as tokens grow. The exponents are small, about 0.3 to 0.4, so halving a penalty means multiplying its input by roughly seven. Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found the power-law shape; Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022) fitted the combined form used today. Two rules of thumb turn it into money. Training costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token (2 forward, 4 backward). Serving costs about 2 per parameter per token.

    Chinchilla: splitting a fixed budget

    For a fixed compute budget, loss along the choice of model size forms a valley. Small models are size-limited, big ones are starved of tokens. DeepMind (2022) found the bottom: grow parameters and tokens together, about 20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B Chinchilla (1.4T tokens) beat the 280B Gopher (300B tokens) on the same compute. GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) sat on the oversized side. They trained over 400 models to find this, so the answer is empirical. Try it below.

    The valley is flat near the bottom, so 20:1 is a guide, not a cliff: being a factor of two off costs little. The fit is also delicate. A 2024 Epoch AI replication found errors in the published parametric fit, and other work traced part of the Kaplan-versus-Chinchilla disagreement to details such as learning-rate schedules. The broad picture is robust; the third decimal place is not.

    Inference-aware scaling: why everyone "overtrains"

    Think of buying a delivery van: a cheaper-to-run van costs more upfront but wins if it drives every day. Chinchilla counted only training cost. A model that answers billions of queries is cheaper overall if it is smaller and trained on far more tokens than Chinchilla suggests. Lifetime cost is training (6 per parameter per token trained) plus serving (2 per parameter per token served). Sardana and Frankle (2023) showed the best ratio of tokens to parameters rises with expected demand. Llama 3 8B saw about 15T tokens, roughly 1,900 per parameter and nearly 100 times the Chinchilla ratio, and its loss was still falling.

    Two refinements. Chinchilla was fitted near the optimum, so labs now fit laws to overtrained runs too; Gadre et al. (2024) found loss and downstream accuracy stay predictable there. And reasoning models, which spend many tokens per answer, push further towards overtraining. Newer preprints on model shape and test-time compute are direction, not settled fact.

    Running short of data: epochs, repeats and the wall

    The law assumes every token is fresh, but good text is finite. Epoch AI estimates public human-written text runs out around 2028, with a wide range. Repeating data helps like re-reading a textbook: the first re-read is useful, the tenth is not. Up to about four passes, repeats are nearly as good as fresh text; beyond about sixteen they are close to worthless. The picture comes from Muennighoff et al. (2023). The mechanism is simple: a repeated token teaches less than a new one, and a big model starts to memorise rather than generalise. Training loss keeps falling while held-out loss stalls, which is how you spot it. Other options are more code, translation, curated sources, or synthetic data.

    That is the "data wall": the curve does not stop, but more compute needs repeats, synthetic data or better filtering of what exists.

    Turning the web into a training set

    Raw material is usually Common Crawl, petabytes of archived pages. Think of a sieve with several meshes, applied in order. The stages are broadly shared; the details differ and matter.

    StageWhy it matters
    Extract and filterPull text from HTML, detect language, drop spam and junk by simple rules. Extraction and English-tuned rules can break or delete good Arabic (right-to-left, diacritics) and mixed-language text.
    DeduplicateRemove repeated documents or spans, often with MinHash (a fast similarity hash). Duplicates waste compute and increase memorisation; Lee et al. (2021) showed dedup improves models.
    Quality classifierA small model scores pages; keep the top slice. The biggest lever, covered next.
    Decontaminate and mixRemove benchmark text (benchmark measurement). The mixture of web, code, maths, books and languages decides what the model is good at, Arabic included. Cleaner data is often placed last in training.

    Quality over quantity: FineWeb, FineWeb-Edu and DCLM

    If good tokens are scarce, a filter that finds them beats a bigger crawl, like picking the best fruit rather than buying more crates. Three public efforts released data and recipes.

    • FineWeb (Hugging Face, 2024): about 15T tokens from Common Crawl, built by small ablations: train a small model per pipeline variant and keep what helps. Deduplicating everything together hurt; per-snapshot dedup did better.
    • FineWeb-Edu: Llama 3 70B scored pages for educational value, a small classifier learned to imitate those scores, and only high scorers were kept (about 1.3T tokens). Discarding most of the data paid off on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks.
    • DCLM (2024): a competition on a fixed pool with fixed training recipes. The winner used a simple fastText classifier (a cheap linear text model) trained to prefer instruction-style, high-quality text, and kept the top 10%. A 7B model on 2.6T tokens reached about 64% MMLU, beating comparable open models trained with more compute.

    The pattern: a small, cheap model deciding what a big one sees is a multiplier on every token. Filtering raises usefulness per token but shrinks the pool, so there is a best cut, and it depends on how long you train.

    "Quality" means whatever the classifier was trained to like, usually educational English, not colloquial Gulf Arabic or business correspondence. Filters win on different benchmarks (in SmolLM2, FineWeb-Edu was stronger on MMLU and ARC, DCLM on commonsense, and a mixture was best), so a filter tuned to a benchmark can look better than it is. NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC (2024) combined several classifiers and rewrote low-quality pages with a language model to keep more data. Test any recipe at the scale you plan.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at:

    • Forecasting big runs from small ones. Loss is smooth in size and tokens, so five or six cheap runs fix the curve and you extrapolate before spending the real budget.
    • Splitting a budget. The valley tells you where the next pound is better spent: on parameters or on tokens.
    • Pricing the lifetime bill. Adding serving cost turns "what is the best model?" into "what is the cheapest model that is good enough at our demand?"
    • Comparing data recipes cheaply. Small ablations rank filters and mixtures, which is how FineWeb and DCLM were built.
    • Checking claims. A vendor quoting a model size, token count and loss can be sanity-checked against the curve.

    Weak at, or don't use when:

    • Predicting skills. Loss is smooth, tasks are not. A capability can appear suddenly or never, and web-text loss says little about Arabic contract extraction.
    • Extrapolating far. Exponent errors grow with distance. A line through small models projected a thousand times out is a guess.
    • Transferring across setups. Coefficients belong to one architecture, tokenizer, data mix and schedule. Refit when any of these changes.
    • Post-training and reasoning. Fine-tuning, preference tuning and test-time compute follow different curves.
    • Small tabular problems. For fraud or credit models, draw a learning curve on your own data instead: score on held-out data at 10%, 20%, 50% and 100% of the training set and see whether it is still rising.
    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Compute-optimal (about 20 tokens per parameter)You train once, serve little, and training cost dominates, as in research.The model will serve billions of queries.
    Overtrained small modelHeavy serving: assistants, search, high-volume classification.Demand is uncertain, or the training budget is the tight limit.
    Larger model with repeated or synthetic dataCompute is spare but fresh text has run out.You would need more than about four epochs of the same text.
    Fine-tune an open modelAlmost every bank: you need behaviour and domain, not new foundations.The base model lacks your language or domain knowledge.
    Learning curve on your own dataClassical ML, small data, deciding whether more labels will help.You are choosing a pretraining budget.

    In practice: start with 20 tokens per parameter as a baseline, then move to 100 or more if the model will be served widely. Keep repeats to about four epochs. To fit a law yourself, train five to eight models across a range of sizes, at least one or two orders of magnitude below your target, and extrapolate no more than about ten times past the largest. Scale matters: a 7B model on 2T tokens costs roughly 8×1022 FLOPs, about 60,000 GPU-hours at the rate assumed in Simulation 2. Banks almost never pretrain. They use these ideas to choose among open models, to estimate serving cost, to ask suppliers sharp questions about training data and Arabic coverage, and to decide when fine-tuning on their own documents is enough.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Extrapolating far beyond the fitted range

      A line through five small models is projected a thousand times out. Exponent errors grow with distance and the regime may bend.

      Spot itAsk how far the forecast goes past the largest model trained, and whether the exponents have error bars.
    • Treating "20 tokens per parameter" as a law

      It minimises training cost only. For a heavily served model it is the wrong target.

      Spot itAsk: optimal for what objective, and who pays for inference?
    • Equating lower loss with a better product

      Loss is smooth; tasks are not. Web-text loss says little about Arabic contract extraction.

      Spot itAsk for evaluation on your own tasks.
    • Filtering that optimises a benchmark, not a use

      A classifier that likes educational English raises MMLU and may quietly remove dialect and non-English text.

      Spot itCheck by language and register, not only the average.
    • Trusting a data gain tested only at small scale

      A filter that helps a small model on few tokens may not help a large one trained for long. Repeated data also stops helping.

      Spot itAsk at what model size and token count it was tested.
    • Assuming repeated data is as good as fresh forever

      Four epochs are nearly free; thirty are not. Training loss keeps falling while held-out loss stalls and memorisation rises.

      Spot itCompare held-out loss, not training loss, and count epochs per source.
    • Comparing token and parameter counts across designs

      Mixture-of-experts models have total and active parameters, tokenizers differ, and "tokens" may include repeats.

      Spot itAsk: active or total? unique or total tokens? which tokenizer?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model has 10B parameters. Roughly how many tokens does the 20:1 guide suggest, and what does training cost in FLOPs?

    About 200B tokens. Cost is about 6 × 10B × 200B = 1.2×1022 FLOPs.

    2. Why can a smaller model that reaches the same loss be the better buy, even if it took more compute to train?

    Lifetime cost is training plus serving. The smaller model is cheaper per token forever, so the extra training spend is repaid once enough tokens are served.

    3. Why is "20 tokens per parameter" a guide, not a rule?

    It comes from one fit and ignores serving cost. The valley is also flat near the bottom, so being a factor of two off costs little.

    4. A paper says its filter raised benchmark scores by 2 points on a 1B model. What do you ask?

    Whether it was tested at target scale, across several benchmarks and seeds, and whether contamination was checked.

    5. Why might a quality classifier hurt an Arabic-English bank assistant while improving the English leaderboard?

    It prefers the style of its positive examples, usually educational English, so Arabic and business-style text is discarded disproportionately. Evaluate by language and register.

    6. A bank wants an internal assistant used by 20,000 staff. Would you choose a Chinchilla-optimal 70B model or an overtrained 8B model, and why?

    The 8B model, usually. Serving dominates lifetime cost with that many users, and a smaller model is cheaper and faster per query. Check it on your own Arabic and English tasks first; if it falls short, a larger model or fine-tuning may be justified.

    7. You can keep the top 5% or the top 30% of a web pool. Which do you choose for a short run, and which for a very long run?

    Harsh for the short run: the small pool is enough and quality is the lever. Gentler for the long run: a tiny pool would be repeated too often. Test at the planned scale, and check language coverage either way.
