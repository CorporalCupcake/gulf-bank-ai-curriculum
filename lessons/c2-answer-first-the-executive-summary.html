    Answer First, and Say How Sure You Are

    How to write the page a senior reader acts on: the decision and the number first, the evidence after, and uncertainty given as ranges and counts that people can use instead of false precision or a shrug.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOpen with what you want decided and the number that supports it, then give the honest range around that number in words and counts a non-specialist can act on, because executives read the top of the page and decide with whatever they found there.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Scientists are trained to write in the order they worked: background, method, results, discussion, and the conclusion last. It mirrors how the work felt. It is the wrong order for a reader who has four minutes and a decision to make. A head of fraud, a chief risk officer or a credit committee chair is not reading to follow your journey. They are reading to find out what you want from them, how much it matters, and what could go wrong.

    So write the way a newspaper does. The first lines carry the decision. Everything after is there for the reader who wants to check, and each layer is optional. If the reader stops after three sentences, they should still have the decision, the number and the ask.

    The second skill is harder. Every number you produce comes with doubt: a recall measured on 60 cases, a default rate forecast for next year, a saving that depends on fraudsters not changing tactics. Two instincts go wrong. One is false precision ("recall is 62.4%"), which sounds confident and is not true. The other is hedging everything ("results may vary"), which is safe for you and useless for the reader. The craft sits between: a best estimate, a range, and what would change the decision.

    Hold onto thisWrite the first three sentences last, and test them alone. If a busy reader saw only those, would they know what to decide, why it is worth it, and what it costs or risks? If not, the memo is in the wrong order.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C2.1 The executive summary: answer first

    The structure has a name: the pyramid principle, from Barbara Minto's work at McKinsey. State the governing answer at the top. Support it with a small number of reasons (three is a good ceiling). Each reason is supported by evidence below it. The reader can descend as far as their doubt requires and no further.

    A working executive summary has five parts, in this order. It fits on half a page.

    PartWhat goes in itExample, fraud model
    1. The askThe decision, by whom, by when. A verb."Approve a six-week shadow run of the new card-fraud model from 1 March, with a go or no-go decision at the end."
    2. The numberThe one result that justifies the ask, with its range and its comparison."It catches about 6 in 10 frauds by value at 400 alerts a day, against 4 in 10 for today's rules."
    3. The costMoney, people, time, and what stops if you say yes."AED 180,000 and two fraud-operations analysts for six weeks."
    4. The riskThe biggest thing that could make part 2 wrong, and what you are doing about it."Recent labels are incomplete, so the 6 in 10 may be a few points high. The shadow run measures it on mature labels."
    5. What happens nextWho does what, and the date of the next decision point."Results to this committee on 15 April."

    Notice what is missing: the algorithm, the features, the history of the project. They belong below, for the people who ask. A useful test is the "so what" chain. Read each sentence aloud and ask so what? If the answer is already in the sentence, keep it. If the answer is in the next paragraph, the next paragraph should come first.

    Three habits that make the page work:

    • One number per claim, with a comparison. "62%" means nothing alone. "62% against 41% for current rules, at the same 400 alerts a day" is a result. Always say what it is compared to and at what operating point (see F7.1 to F7.4).
    • Translate the metric into the unit the reader manages. Not "AUC 0.93" but "of 100 confirmed frauds, the model would have stopped 62 before the money left". Or AED, customers, analyst hours.
    • Name the recommendation, not the options. Offer alternatives only if the reader must choose between them, and say which you prefer. "Here are three options" without a view hands your job to the reader.

    Format matters less than order, but a few rules save readers time: a descriptive title that states the finding ("New fraud model catches more fraud at the same alert volume") rather than the topic ("Fraud model update"); short paragraphs; the key number in bold once, not everywhere; and one chart at most in the summary, with a title that says what to see. Arabic-speaking readers and mixed English/Arabic documents add one practical point: if a summary will be translated or read in a second language, keep sentences short and avoid idiom, and do not rely on formatting such as colour to carry meaning.

    Where answer-first is not enoughSome audiences need the reasoning before they will accept the answer, for example a sceptical committee that rejected a similar proposal last year. Answer first still works: lead with the answer, then put the objection they have in the second paragraph ("You will ask why this differs from the 2024 pilot. Three things changed."). Do not hide the answer to build suspense.

    C2.2 Explaining uncertainty to non-technical people

    The aim is that the reader makes the same decision they would have made if they understood the statistics. That is different from teaching them statistics. Three tools do most of the work.

    Tool 1: a range, with a size that matches the evidence. A measured proportion has a standard error that depends on how many cases it rests on. For recall measured on n frauds:

    95% range≈r±1.96r(1−r)n

    r is the measured recall and n the number of frauds in the test set. This is the normal approximation from F2.1; it is rough when n is small or r is near 0 or 100%, where a bootstrap or exact interval is better. With r = 62% on 100 frauds: 1.96 × √(0.62 × 0.38 ÷ 100) = 1.96 × 0.0485 = 9.5 points, so about 52% to 71%. On 1,000 frauds it is ±3 points. The test set size, not the model, set the width.

    Tool 2: round to what you actually know. If the range is ±9 points, "62.4%" claims a precision you do not have and invites someone to ask why last month said 61.8%. Say "about 6 in 10". Rounding is honest when the width says the second digit is noise.

    Tool 3: counts of people instead of probabilities. Research by Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) showed that people, including trained doctors, reason far better about "23 out of 1,000" than about "2.3%" or "0.023". These natural frequencies also keep the base rate visible. "A 2% probability of default" is abstract. "Out of 1,000 customers like this one, about 20 will default in the next year, and we are not sure whether it is 15 or 30" is something a credit committee can picture.

    What a range does and does not mean. A 95% confidence interval is produced by a procedure that captures the true value in 95% of repeated studies. People hear it as "there is a 95% chance the truth is in here", and for communication that reading is close enough if you flag that the model assumptions might be wrong too. The bigger honesty gap is elsewhere: the interval covers sampling noise only. It does not cover drift, label delay, or a test set that does not look like next year. Say which doubts the range includes and which it does not.

    Words need numbers. "Likely", "unlikely" and "significant risk" mean different things to different people. Sherman Kent found in the 1960s that intelligence analysts used "probable" for anything from 20% to 80%; later studies of IPCC verbal scales found the same spread among readers (Budescu et al., 2009). If you use a word, pin it to a number once: "likely (roughly 2 in 3 or better)". Better, just give the number. Also say which direction the doubt leans: "if anything this overstates the benefit, because recent labels are incomplete" tells the reader what to do with the range.

    Two more practical points from the risk-communication literature. First, show the range alongside the number, not in a footnote. Van der Bles and colleagues (2019, 2020) tested this with real audiences: stating the uncertainty as a numeric range lowered trust in the number a little, but not trust in the person who gave it, and verbal hedges did little either way. Honest ranges are cheap. Second, give a decision rule, not just a range. "If recall on mature labels is below 50% we stop; above 55% we go; in between we extend by four weeks" turns uncertainty into a plan, and the committee will remember it.

    Where this shows up at the bank:

    • Model go-live papers to a model risk or credit committee: one page on top, the technical annex underneath.
    • Monthly fraud and AML performance packs, where movements of a point or two are routinely over-read. Add the range, and say when a change is within noise.
    • IFRS 9 provisioning discussions, where the forecast is a scenario-weighted range and the finance team needs the sensitivity, not just the central figure.
    • Regulator and audit conversations, where clear statements of what is known and not known build more credibility than confident ones that later move. I have not seen a specific CBUAE or SAMA template for this; check your bank's own model-governance standard for the required format.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    This is a mature, mostly settled area, so there are few recent breakthroughs. The live questions are about what works in practice and how AI writing tools change the job.

    Not web-checked for this lesson: the research below is established literature cited from memory. Verify details before quoting them to a regulator or in print.

    1. 1964
      Words of estimative probability

      Sherman Kent at the CIA showed that the same phrase, such as "serious possibility", was read as anything from a small to a large chance. The lesson survives: attach numbers to words.

      Kent, Studies in Intelligence, 1964
    2. 1995
      Natural frequencies

      Gigerenzer and Hoffrage showed that Bayesian reasoning problems become much easier when probabilities are stated as counts of cases. Many later studies reproduce the effect, including with clinicians.

      Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, Psychological Review, 1995
    3. 1996
      Fan charts at central banks

      The Bank of England began publishing inflation forecasts as fan charts, which show a widening range of outcomes instead of a single line. Other central banks adopted the form. It is the best-known institutional example of showing uncertainty rather than hiding it.

      Bank of England Inflation Report
    4. 2009
      Verbal scales are read inconsistently

      Budescu and colleagues tested how people interpret IPCC confidence language, such as "very likely", and found wide variation and a pull towards 50%. Giving both words and numbers worked better.

      Budescu et al., Psychological Science, 2009
    5. 2017
      Review of risk and uncertainty communication

      Spiegelhalter summarised what works: natural frequencies, absolute not only relative risks, visual aids, and plain statements of what is unknown.

      Spiegelhalter, Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application, 2017
    6. 2019–20
      Does admitting uncertainty cost trust?

      Experiments with thousands of participants found that numeric ranges reduced trust in the number slightly and left trust in the source largely unchanged. Verbal hedges had little measured effect. The common worry that ranges make you look weak is not well supported.

      van der Bles et al., Royal Society Open Science, 2019; PNAS, 2020
    7. 2023–26
      Language models write the first draft

      Staff now routinely ask an LLM to summarise a technical paper or draft a committee note. These tools are good at structure and tone. They also tend to smooth uncertainty away, add confident wording the evidence does not support, and can misstate numbers. Banks usually treat the human author as accountable for every figure. I have not found a standard industry practice; expect it to vary by institution and to be set by model and communications policy.

      Practitioner observation; no single source

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Review your own draft against this list before you send it. Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • The buried answer

      Three paragraphs of background before the recommendation. The reader decides on a guess, or sends it back.

      Spot itCover everything after the first three sentences. Can you still say what is being asked? If not, move the ask to the top.
    • The number with no comparison

      "The model achieves 94% accuracy." Compared with what, at what threshold, on how many cases?

      Spot itFor each headline figure, find its baseline and sample size in the same sentence. If they are absent, add them.
    • False precision

      "Expected savings are AED 4,137,200." Nobody believes it, and then they doubt the rest.

      Spot itCount significant digits. Anything beyond two for an estimate is probably noise; give "AED 3 to 5 million".
    • The blanket hedge

      "Subject to uncertainty and further validation" on every claim. The reader cannot tell the shaky claims from the solid ones.

      Spot itAsk of each caveat: what would the reader do differently because of it? If nothing, cut it. If something, say what.
    • Range shown, direction hidden

      "±5 points" with no word on whether errors are more likely upward or downward.

      Spot itAsk: if I am wrong, which way am I wrong? Say it. Incomplete recent labels, for example, make fraud recall look worse than the truth, not better.
    • Interval covers the wrong doubt

      A tight sampling interval that ignores drift, a changed fraud pattern or selection bias in the labels. It looks rigorous and is overconfident.

      Spot itWrite down what the interval does not cover. If the list is longer than what it does, say so.
    • Unanchored verbal probabilities

      "Likely" read by one director as 90% and by another as 55%. Both approve.

      Spot itSearch your draft for likely, unlikely, significant, material, high risk. Attach a number or a count to each.
    • Relative change without the base

      "Fraud attempts up 40%." From 10 a month to 14, or 5,000 to 7,000?

      Spot itAlways give the absolute figures next to a percentage change.
    • Jargon as authority

      "Recall at the 0.7 threshold, PR-AUC 0.41." The audience nods and understands nothing. Later they feel misled.

      Spot itRead it to someone outside the team. Anywhere they ask "meaning what?" needs translating into customers, AED or analyst hours.
    • Polished text over unchecked numbers

      An AI-drafted summary reads smoothly and quietly changes "may be a few points high" into "performs strongly". Or rounds 0.62 into "nearly two-thirds".

      Spot itCheck every number and every hedge word in the summary against the source analysis, one by one, before it leaves your hands.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    Opening a committee paper or call · 20 seconds
    “I'm asking for approval of a six-week shadow run of the new fraud model. In testing it catches about six in ten frauds by value at our current alert volume, against four in ten for today's rules. That's from 140 confirmed cases, so call it five to seven in ten. The shadow run costs AED 180,000 and two analysts. The main risk is that recent labels are incomplete, which would make the real figure better, not worse.”
    When you are not sure · 15 seconds
    “My best estimate is about two percent, so around twenty in a thousand customers. I'd plan for anywhere from fifteen to thirty. I wouldn't change the provision until we have another quarter of data, and here's the number that would make me change my mind.”
    “Just give me one number.”

    Give it, then the range in the same breath: “Best estimate six in ten. I'd plan on five to seven.” A single number with no range invites the question of how much to trust it, and you will be held to it. Refusing to give a number looks evasive. Giving only the number looks overconfident.

    “So is it 60% or isn't it?”

    “It's our best estimate. With 140 cases the honest range is about five to seven in ten. More cases would narrow it, and the shadow run gives us those. For the decision today, anything in that range clears the 50% hurdle except the bottom edge, so I'd proceed.” Tie the range to the decision.

    “Why does the figure keep changing month to month?”

    “Each month rests on about 40 confirmed frauds, which moves the figure by roughly ten points either way through chance alone. I'd rather show you a three-month rolling figure with its range than react to each month.” Back it with the interval from F2.1.

    “Isn't it a weakness to show all this doubt?”

    The evidence says no: experiments found that numeric ranges had little effect on trust in the person presenting, and a confident figure that later moves costs more credibility than a range that holds. Present doubt as a plan: here is what we know, what we do not, and what would settle it.

    “Can you put it in a sentence for the board?”

    Yes, and prepare it in advance: decision, number with comparison, cost. For example: “We recommend a low-cost six-week trial of a model that finds about six in ten frauds against four in ten today; we'll know within the quarter whether to roll it out.” Avoid the algorithm's name.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. What are the five parts of an answer-first executive summary, in order?

    The ask (decision, by whom, by when), the number (with its comparison and range), the cost, the main risk, and what happens next. Everything else, including method, history and detail, sits below.

    2. Recall is 70% on 50 confirmed frauds. Roughly what is the 95% range, and how would you say it?

    1.96 × √(0.7 × 0.3 ÷ 50) = 1.96 × 0.0648 ≈ 12.7 points, so about 57% to 83%. Say: “about seven in ten, plausibly between six and eight in ten, on 50 cases.” The normal approximation is rough at n = 50; a bootstrap would be a little asymmetric.

    3. How many frauds do you need to halve that range?

    The width shrinks with √n, so halving it needs four times as many cases: 200. This is why a bigger test period or pooling quarters often helps more than a better model when you are arguing about a few points.

    4. Turn “PD of 1.5%” on a 2,000-loan segment, loans of AED 80,000 and LGD 45%, into a sentence with an AED figure.

    Expected defaults 2,000 × 0.015 = 30 loans. Expected loss 30 × 80,000 × 0.45 = AED 1.08 million. “Of 2,000 loans like these, we expect about 30 to default over the year, roughly AED 1.1 million of loss before any recoveries beyond the LGD assumption.” Add the range if you have one.

    5. Why can a tight confidence interval still mislead?

    It captures sampling noise only. If labels are incomplete, the population has drifted, or the test set is unrepresentative, the true error can be far outside the interval. State what the range does not cover.

    6. A draft says “fraud attempts rose 40% and the new model is likely to be effective.” Fix two problems.

    Give the absolute numbers behind the 40% (say, from 500 to 700 a month), and replace “likely to be effective” with a measured result and comparison, such as “caught 62% of fraud by value against 41% for current rules, range 52 to 71%”. Both replace a vague claim with something checkable.

    7. When should you not lead with the answer?

    Rarely. Even for a hostile or sceptical audience, lead with the answer and then address their objection at once. The exception is delivering bad personal news or a very sensitive finding in person, where a brief lead-in is normal, but a written paper should still put the conclusion first.
