    Answer First, and Say How Sure You Are

    How to write the page a senior reader acts on: the decision and the number first, the evidence after, and uncertainty given as ranges and counts that people can use instead of false precision or a shrug.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOpen with what you want decided and the number that supports it, then give the honest range around that number in words and counts a non-specialist can act on, because executives read the top of the page and decide with whatever they found there.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Write like a newspaper: the headline first, the detail below for whoever wants to check. Scientists are trained to write in the order they worked (background, method, results, conclusion last). That is the wrong order for a head of fraud or a credit committee chair with four minutes. They want to know what you want from them, how much it matters, and what could go wrong. If they stop after three sentences, they should still have the decision, the number and the ask.

    The second skill is handling doubt. Every number comes with some: a recall measured on 60 cases, next year's default rate, a saving that assumes fraudsters don't change tactics. Two instincts go wrong. False precision ("recall is 62.4%") sounds confident and isn't true. Blanket hedging ("results may vary") is safe for you and useless for the reader. The craft sits between: a best estimate, a range, and what would change the decision.

    Hold onto thisWrite the first three sentences last, and test them alone. A busy reader seeing only those should know what to decide, why it is worth it, and what it costs or risks.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C2.1 The executive summary: answer first

    The structure is the pyramid principle (Barbara Minto, McKinsey): the answer at the top, up to three reasons under it, evidence under each reason. The reader descends only as far as their doubt requires. A working executive summary has five parts, in this order, on half a page.

    PartWhat goes in itExample, fraud model
    1. The askThe decision, by whom, by when. A verb."Approve a six-week shadow run of the new card-fraud model from 1 March, with a go or no-go decision at the end."
    2. The numberThe one result that justifies the ask, with its range and its comparison."It catches about 6 in 10 frauds by value at 400 alerts a day, against 4 in 10 for today's rules."
    3. The costMoney, people, time, and what stops if you say yes."AED 180,000 and two fraud-operations analysts for six weeks."
    4. The riskThe biggest thing that could make part 2 wrong, and what you are doing about it."Recent labels are incomplete, so the 6 in 10 may be a few points high. The shadow run measures it on mature labels."
    5. What happens nextWho does what, and the date of the next decision point."Results to this committee on 15 April."

    The algorithm, features and project history are missing on purpose. They go below, for people who ask. Test: read each sentence aloud and ask so what? If the answer is in the next paragraph, that paragraph should come first.

    Three habits:

    • One number per claim, with a comparison. "62%" alone means nothing, like "I ran 10 km" with no time. "62% against 41% for current rules, at the same 400 alerts a day" is a result. Say what it is compared to and at what operating point (see F7.1 to F7.4).
    • Use the reader's unit. Not "AUC 0.93" but "of 100 confirmed frauds, the model would have stopped 62 before the money left". Or AED, customers, analyst hours.
    • Name the recommendation, not the options. Give alternatives only if the reader must choose, and say which you prefer. "Here are three options" with no view hands your job to the reader.

    Formatting: a title that states the finding ("New fraud model catches more fraud at the same alert volume", not "Fraud model update"); short paragraphs; key number in bold once; at most one chart. For summaries read in a second language (English and Arabic), use short sentences, no idiom, and don't let colour carry meaning.

    Sceptical audience?Still lead with the answer, then answer their objection in paragraph two ("You will ask why this differs from the 2024 pilot. Three things changed."). Never hide the answer to build suspense.

    C2.2 Explaining uncertainty to non-technical people

    The aim is that the reader decides as they would if they understood the statistics, without being taught them. Three tools do most of the work.

    Tool 1: a range sized to the evidence. A coin flipped 10 times showing 6 heads tells you little; 1,000 flips showing 600 tells you a lot. Same for recall: the fewer frauds it rests on, the wider the range. For recall measured on n frauds:

    95% range≈r±1.96r(1−r)n

    r is the measured recall and n the number of frauds in the test set. Normal approximation from F2.1; rough when n is small or r is near 0 or 100% (use a bootstrap or exact interval). With r = 62% on 100 frauds: 1.96 × √(0.62 × 0.38 ÷ 100) = 9.5 points, so about 52% to 71%. On 1,000 frauds it is ±3 points. The test set size, not the model, set the width.

    Tool 2: round to what you know. A bathroom scale that wobbles by a kilo shouldn't report your weight to the gram. If the range is ±9 points, "62.4%" invites the question of why last month said 61.8%. Say "about 6 in 10".

    Tool 3: counts of people, not probabilities. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) showed that people, including trained doctors, reason far better about "23 out of 1,000" than "2.3%". These natural frequencies also keep the base rate visible. "2% probability of default" is abstract. "Out of 1,000 customers like this one, about 20 will default next year, maybe 15, maybe 30" is something a credit committee can picture.

    What a range does and does not mean. A 95% interval comes from a procedure that captures the truth in 95% of repeated studies. Hearing it as "95% chance the truth is in here" is close enough for communication. The real gap: the interval covers sampling noise only, not drift, label delay, or a test set unlike next year. Say which doubts the range includes and which it does not.

    Words need numbers. "Likely" means 90% to one director and 55% to another. Sherman Kent found intelligence analysts used "probable" for anything from 20% to 80%; IPCC scales show the same spread (Budescu et al., 2009). Pin a word to a number once ("likely: roughly 2 in 3 or better"), or just give the number. Also say which way the doubt leans: "if anything this overstates the benefit, because recent labels are incomplete."

    Two more practices. Show the range next to the number, not in a footnote. Van der Bles and colleagues (2019, 2020) tested real audiences: a numeric range lowered trust in the number a little, not trust in the person; verbal hedges did little either way. Give a decision rule, not just a range. "Below 50% recall on mature labels we stop; above 55% we go; in between we extend four weeks" turns uncertainty into a plan.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The buried answer

      Three paragraphs of background before the recommendation. The reader guesses, or sends it back.

      Spot itCover everything after the first three sentences. Can you still say what is asked? If not, move the ask up.
    • The number with no comparison

      "94% accuracy." Against what, at what threshold, on how many cases?

      Spot itEach headline figure needs its baseline and sample size in the same sentence.
    • False precision

      "Expected savings are AED 4,137,200." Nobody believes it, then they doubt the rest.

      Spot itMore than two significant digits on an estimate is probably noise. Say "AED 3 to 5 million".
    • The blanket hedge

      "Subject to uncertainty and further validation" on every claim. Shaky and solid claims look the same.

      Spot itFor each caveat ask what the reader would do differently. Nothing: cut it. Something: say what.
    • Range shown, direction hidden

      "±5 points" with no word on which way errors lean.

      Spot itAsk which way you are wrong, and say it. Incomplete recent labels make fraud recall look worse than the truth, not better.
    • Interval covers the wrong doubt

      A tight sampling interval that ignores drift, a changed fraud pattern or label selection bias. It looks rigorous and is overconfident.

      Spot itWrite down what the interval does not cover. If that list is longer, say so.
    • Unanchored verbal probabilities

      "Likely" read by one director as 90% and by another as 55%. Both approve.

      Spot itSearch for likely, unlikely, significant, material, high risk. Attach a number or count to each.
    • Relative change without the base

      "Fraud attempts up 40%": from 10 a month to 14, or 5,000 to 7,000?

      Spot itPut absolute figures next to every percentage change.
    • Jargon as authority

      "Recall at the 0.7 threshold, PR-AUC 0.41." The audience nods, understands nothing, and later feels misled.

      Spot itRead it to someone outside the team. Wherever they ask "meaning what?", translate to customers, AED or analyst hours.
    • Polished text over unchecked numbers

      An AI-drafted summary turns "may be a few points high" into "performs strongly", or 0.62 into "nearly two-thirds".

      Spot itCheck every number and hedge word against the source analysis before it leaves your hands.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What are the five parts of an answer-first executive summary, in order?

    The ask, the number (with comparison and range), the cost, the main risk, and what happens next. Method and history sit below.

    2. Recall is 70% on 50 confirmed frauds. Roughly what is the 95% range, and how would you say it?

    1.96 × √(0.7 × 0.3 ÷ 50) ≈ 12.7 points, so about 57% to 83%. Say: “about seven in ten, plausibly six to eight in ten, on 50 cases.” The approximation is rough at n = 50; a bootstrap would be a little asymmetric.

    3. How many frauds do you need to halve that range?

    Width shrinks with √n, so four times the cases: 200. A longer test period or pooled quarters often helps more than a better model when the argument is about a few points.

    4. Turn “PD of 1.5%” on a 2,000-loan segment, loans of AED 80,000 and LGD 45%, into a sentence with an AED figure.

    2,000 × 0.015 = 30 defaults; 30 × 80,000 × 0.45 = AED 1.08 million. “Of 2,000 loans like these, about 30 will default over the year, roughly AED 1.1 million of loss.” Add the range if you have one.

    5. Why can a tight confidence interval still mislead?

    It covers sampling noise only. Incomplete labels, drift or an unrepresentative test set can put the truth well outside it. State what it does not cover.

    6. A draft says “fraud attempts rose 40% and the new model is likely to be effective.” Fix two problems.

    Add the absolute numbers behind the 40% (say, 500 to 700 a month). Replace “likely to be effective” with a measured result and comparison: “caught 62% of fraud by value against 41% for current rules, range 52 to 71%”.

    7. When should you not lead with the answer?

    Rarely. Even for a sceptical audience, lead with the answer and address the objection at once. The exception is sensitive personal news delivered in person; a written paper still puts the conclusion first.
