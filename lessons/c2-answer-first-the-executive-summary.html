    Answer First, and Say How Sure You Are

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Write like a newspaper: headline first, detail below for whoever wants to check. Scientists write in the order they worked, conclusion last. A head of fraud with four minutes wants what you want, how much it matters, and what could go wrong.

    The second skill is handling doubt. False precision ("recall is 62.4%") sounds confident and isn't true. Blanket hedging ("results may vary") is safe for you and useless to the reader. The craft sits between: a best estimate, a range, and what would change the decision.

    Hold onto thisWrite the first three sentences last, and test them alone. A busy reader should know what to decide, why, and what it costs.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C2.1 The executive summary: answer first

    The structure is the pyramid principle (Barbara Minto): the answer at the top, up to three reasons under it, evidence under each. A working executive summary has five parts, in this order, on half a page.

    PartWhat goes in itExample, fraud model
    1. The askThe decision, by whom, by when."Approve a six-week shadow run of the new card-fraud model from 1 March, with a go or no-go decision at the end."
    2. The numberThe result that justifies the ask, with range and comparison."It catches about 6 in 10 frauds by value at 400 alerts a day, against 4 in 10 for today's rules."
    3. The costMoney, people, time."AED 180,000 and two fraud-operations analysts for six weeks."
    4. The riskWhat could make part 2 wrong, and your response."Recent labels are incomplete, so the 6 in 10 may be a few points high. The shadow run measures it on mature labels."
    5. What happens nextWho does what, and when."Results to this committee on 15 April."

    The algorithm and project history are missing on purpose; they go below, for people who ask. Test: ask so what? of each sentence. If the answer is in the next paragraph, that paragraph comes first.

    • One number per claim, with a comparison. "62%" alone is like "I ran 10 km" with no time. Say what it beats and at what operating point (see F7.1 to F7.4).
    • Use the reader's unit. Not "AUC 0.93" but "of 100 confirmed frauds, the model would have stopped 62 before the money left". Or AED, customers, analyst hours.
    • Name the recommendation, not the options. If you list options, say which you prefer.

    Use a title that states the finding ("New fraud model catches more fraud at the same alert volume"), short paragraphs, and at most one chart.

    Sceptical audience?Still lead with the answer, then address their objection in paragraph two. Never hide the answer to build suspense.

    C2.2 Explaining uncertainty to non-technical people

    The aim is that the reader decides as they would if they understood the statistics, without being taught them.

    Tool 1: a range sized to the evidence. Ten coin flips showing 6 heads tell you little; 1,000 flips showing 600 tell you a lot. Recall works the same way: the fewer frauds, the wider the range. Four times the cases halves the width, so test set size, not the model, sets it (see F2.1). Recall of 62% on 100 frauds is roughly 52% to 71%; on 1,000 it is within about 3 points.

    Tool 2: round to what you know. A wobbly bathroom scale shouldn't report your weight to the gram. If the range is ±9 points, "62.4%" invites why last month said 61.8%. Say "about 6 in 10".

    Tool 3: counts of people, not probabilities. People, even doctors, reason far better about "23 out of 1,000" than "2.3%" (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage, 1995). "Out of 1,000 customers like this one, about 20 will default next year, maybe 15, maybe 30" is something a credit committee can picture.

    What a range leaves out. It covers sampling noise only, not drift, label delay, or a test set unlike next year. Say which doubts it includes and which it does not.

    Words need numbers. "Likely" means 90% to one director and 55% to another (Budescu et al., 2009). Give the number, and say which way the doubt leans: "if anything this overstates the benefit, because recent labels are incomplete."

    Two more practices. Put the range next to the number, not in a footnote. A numeric range lowers trust in the number a little, not in the person (Van der Bles et al., 2019, 2020). Give a decision rule. "Below 50% recall on mature labels we stop; above 55% we go; in between we extend four weeks" turns uncertainty into a plan.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The buried answer

      Three paragraphs of background first. The reader guesses, or sends it back.

      Spot itCover everything after the first three sentences. Can you still say what is asked?
    • Numbers with no comparison or base

      "94% accuracy", or "fraud attempts up 40%". Against what, on how many cases, from 10 a month to 14 or from 5,000 to 7,000?

      Spot itEvery headline figure needs its baseline, sample size and absolute numbers in the same sentence. Check numbers against the source, especially after an AI redraft.
    • False precision

      "Expected savings are AED 4,137,200." Nobody believes it.

      Spot itMore than two significant digits on an estimate is probably noise. Say "AED 3 to 5 million".
    • The blanket hedge

      "Subject to uncertainty and further validation" on every claim. Shaky and solid claims look the same.

      Spot itAsk what the reader would do differently. Nothing: cut it.
    • Vague words and jargon

      "Likely" is 90% to one director and 55% to another, and both approve. Jargon leaves the room nodding and none the wiser.

      Spot itSearch for likely, significant, material, high risk and attach a number. Translate wherever an outsider asks "meaning what?".
    • An interval that covers the wrong doubt

      A tight sampling range that ignores drift or label bias, and says nothing about which way errors lean.

      Spot itWrite down what the range does not cover, and which way you are wrong.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What are the five parts of an answer-first executive summary, in order?

    The ask, the number (with comparison and range), the cost, the main risk, and what happens next. Method and history sit below.

    2. Recall is 70% on 50 confirmed frauds. How would you say it to a committee?

    “About seven in ten, plausibly six to eight in ten, on 50 cases.” Fifty cases is few, so the range is wide, roughly 12 points either side.

    3. How many frauds do you need to halve that range?

    Four times the cases, so about 200. A longer test period often helps more than a better model.

    4. How would you reword “PD of 1.5%” for a credit committee, for 2,000 loans of AED 80,000 with 45% loss given default?

    “Of 2,000 loans like these, about 30 will default over the year, roughly AED 1.1 million of loss.”

    5. Why can a tight confidence interval still mislead?

    It covers sampling noise only. Incomplete labels, drift or an unrepresentative test set can put the truth well outside it. State what it does not cover.

    6. A draft says “fraud attempts rose 40% and the new model is likely to be effective.” Fix two problems.

    Add the absolute numbers behind the 40% (say, 500 to 700 a month). Replace “likely to be effective” with a measured result and comparison: “caught 62% of fraud by value against 41% for current rules, range 52 to 71%”.

    7. When should you not lead with the answer?

    Rarely. Even for a sceptical audience, lead with the answer and address the objection at once. The exception is sensitive personal news in person.
