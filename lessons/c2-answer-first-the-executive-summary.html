    W1.3 Day one ★ Priority About 8 minutes

    Answer First: Writing for Decision-Makers

    Part 1

    What answer first means

    In one sentenceWriting answer first means putting the decision you want, and the one number that backs it, in the first three sentences, with the method and history below for anyone who wants to check.

    How it differs from a report or a paper

    The running example is a memo asking a head of fraud to approve a new card-fraud model.

    • A paper follows the order you worked in, with the conclusion last. A memo reverses that.
    • A technical report proves the result. An executive summary only has to let the reader decide, and points to the proof.
    Part 2

    How to write the summary

    Use five parts, in this order, on half a page. Here they are for the fraud model.

    1. The ask: the decision, by whom, by when. "Approve a six-week shadow run from 1 March, with a go or no-go decision at the end." (A shadow run means the model scores live cases but takes no action.)
    2. The number: the result that justifies the ask, with a range and a comparison. "It catches about 6 in 10 frauds by value at 400 alerts a day, against 4 in 10 for today's rules."
    3. The cost: money, people, time. "AED 180,000 and two analysts for six weeks."
    4. The risk: what could make the number wrong, and your response. "Recent labels are incomplete, so 6 in 10 may be a few points high. The shadow run measures it properly."
    5. What happens next: who does what, and when.

    The test: cover everything after the first three sentences. Can you still say what is being asked? If not, the answer is buried.

    Part 3

    How to say how sure you are

    The aim is that the reader decides as they would if they understood the statistics, without being taught them. Three habits do most of the work.

    • Give a range sized to the evidence. Ten coin flips with 6 heads tell you little. A thousand flips with 600 heads tell you a lot. Recall measured on 100 frauds is roughly 52% to 71% if it came out at 62%. On 1,000 frauds it is within about 3 points.
    • Round to what you know. "62.4%" invites the question why last month said 61.8%. If the range is nine points either side, say "about 6 in 10".
    • Use counts of people, not percentages. "Of 1,000 customers like this one, about 20 will default next year, maybe 15, maybe 30" is something a credit committee can picture. "2.3%" is not.

    Worked readings

    • "94% accuracy" says nothing. Against what, on how many cases, at what alert volume?
    • "Fraud attempts up 40%" needs the base. 10 to 14 a month is noise. 5,000 to 7,000 is a trend.
    • "AED 4,137,200 saved" is false precision. Say "AED 3 to 5 million".

    Put the range next to the number, not in a footnote. Say which way the doubt leans: "if anything this overstates the benefit, because recent labels are incomplete". A range covers sampling noise only, so name what it does not cover, such as drift or a test set unlike next year.

    Finally, give a decision rule: "Below 50% recall we stop, above 55% we go, in between we extend four weeks." That turns uncertainty into a plan.

    Part 4

    Which style to use

    StyleWhat it doesStrong atWeak at
    Answer firstAsk and number on topFast decisions by busy readersNeeds the work done before you start writing
    ChronologicalTells the story of the workA record for colleagues who ask laterThe ask is buried or never read
    Blanket hedge"Subject to further validation"Nothing, for the readerShaky and solid claims look the same

    Answer first is strong at: getting a decision and making your claims checkable. In banking, committees read many papers in a sitting, so the ask and number must survive a skim.

    It is weak at: hiding a weak result. Even with a sceptical audience, lead with the answer and address their objection in paragraph two. Never hide it to build suspense.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • The buried answer

      Three paragraphs of background first, so the reader guesses or sends it back. Use the cover test from Part 2.

    • Numbers with no comparison

      "94% accuracy" or "up 40%" with no base. Put the baseline, sample size and absolute numbers in the same sentence.

    • False precision or blanket hedging

      Too many digits, or "subject to uncertainty" on every claim. Round, give a range, and cut any caveat that would not change what the reader does.

    • Jargon and vague words

      Search for "likely", "significant", "material" and attach a number. Translate anything an outsider would answer with "meaning what?".

    1. What are the five parts of an answer-first summary, in order?

    The ask, the number (with comparison and range), the cost, the main risk, and what happens next.

    2. Recall is 70% on 50 confirmed frauds. How do you say it?

    "About seven in ten, plausibly six to eight in ten, on 50 cases." Fifty cases is few, so the range is wide, roughly 12 points either side.

    3. How many frauds do you need to halve that range?

    Four times as many, so about 200. A longer test period often helps more than a better model.

    4. Reword "PD of 1.5%" for 2,000 loans of AED 80,000 with 45% loss given default. (PD is probability of default.)

    "Of 2,000 loans like these, about 30 will default over the year, roughly AED 1.1 million of loss." That is 30 loans times AED 80,000 times 0.45.

    5. Why can a tight range still mislead?

    It covers sampling noise only. Incomplete labels, drift or an unrepresentative test set can put the truth well outside it, so say what it leaves out.
