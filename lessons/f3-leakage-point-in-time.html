    F3.1 Day one ★ Priority About 40 minutes

    Point-in-Time Correctness and Leakage

    Why a bank model can score 0.98 in testing and 0.60 in production, how to know what the bank actually knew on the day of a decision, and the checks that catch a result that is too good to be true.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model may only use what the bank knew at the moment it had to decide, so every feature, every join and every split has to be rebuilt as of that moment, or the test score measures how well you can read the answer key.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A risk team builds a model to predict which new personal-finance applicants will be 90 days past due within three months. Test AUC is 0.98. After a quarter live, it is 0.60.

    The culprit was one input, average daily balance over the last 30 days. The training table was built in September, so "last 30 days" meant the 30 days before the extract, not before each application. For a customer who applied in May and defaulted in July, the feature included the weeks when their balance collapsed. The model learned that a collapsing balance predicts default. True, and useless: at decision time the balance hadn't collapsed yet.

    Think of sitting an exam with the answer key in your bag. Your mark is real, but it measures nothing about what you know. That is leakage: information in the training or test data that wasn't available when the real decision was made. It is a question of when, not what.

    • The leaky value wasn't "wrong data". It was a correct balance for the wrong dates. Leakage is about the link between a value and a decision time, so staring at the value won't reveal it.
    • Test score up, live score down. The more the model leans on the leak, the better it looks and the worse it is. A validation number means something only if its pipeline matches the live one.
    • Stale costs little; peeking costs everything. A month-old feature gave away a fifth of one honest signal. A feature a month ahead gave away the answer. When unsure, be conservative about the date.
    Hold onto thisFor every number the model sees, ask: when did the bank first know this, and was that before the decision? If you can't answer, don't use it.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Three clocks: event time, knowledge time and decision time

    Most leakage comes from confusing three times attached to every warehouse row. Think of a letter: written on one day, posted on another, read on a third.

    • Event time: when it happened in the world. The raise took effect 1 March.
    • Knowledge time (recorded or system time): when the bank's systems first held the fact. The HR letter was uploaded 22 April. Card purchases settle two days late. The bureau file refreshes monthly.
    • Decision time: when the bank had to act, such as the application submission or the card authorisation.

    The rule: a value may feed a decision only if its knowledge time is on or before the decision time. Not its event time. A fact that happened before the decision but reached the bank after it is still the future to the model.

    The label has its own clock. "Default" means 90 days past due within a performance window after the decision, say 12 months. The observation window is the history before the decision. They must not overlap. Draw features left of the decision line and label right of it before writing code. Simulation 1 is built on this picture.

    Availability lag is leakage's quieter cousin. A legitimate feature may not be ready at decision time: transactions settle at T+2, salary files post on the 28th. Train on yesterday's closing balance, deploy on a feed two days old, and train and live disagree. Define each feature by when it is available, not when it is true.

    Why is the damage so large? Say honest features separate defaulters from non-defaulters by d0 standard deviations (the gap between group averages, in units of within-group spread), and a leaky one by dL. For bell-shaped scores, AUC for separation d is:

    AUC=Φ(d2)

    Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution (F1.5). AUC is the chance a random defaulter outscores a random non-defaulter (F7). d = 1 gives 0.76, d = 2 gives 0.92, d = 3 gives 0.98. A feature containing the outcome reaches high d easily.

    Fit the best linear score on history, where both features exist. Weights are proportional to each separation. Live, the leaky feature is noise:

    dvalidation=d02+dL2dlive=d02d02+dL2

    With d0 = 1 and dL = 2.6 (about 30 days of post-decision data in Simulation 1), validation has d = 2.8, AUC 0.975. Live has d = 1 / 2.8 = 0.36, AUC 0.60, worse than the honest model alone (0.76), because most weight sits on a dead feature. A leaky model is not just over-optimistic. It is worse than the clean one, and the test says the opposite.

    As-of joins and restated data

    The as-of join (point-in-time join) enforces the rule. Like asking "what did the bank's file say on 15 April?", it matches each decision row to the latest value the bank knew at that row's timestamp, not to "their" current value.

    A naive join hits the table as it stands today. If that table is overwritten in place ("type 1"), every historical row inherits today's segment, rating and corrected score. An as-of join needs history: a table that keeps every version with dates ("type 2" or bitemporal), or a snapshot at each decision.

    -- Leaky: attaches today's score to a past application
SELECT a.app_id, a.decision_ts, b.score
FROM applications a
JOIN bureau_current b USING (customer_id);

-- Point-in-time: the score the bank held at decision time
SELECT a.app_id, a.decision_ts, b.score
FROM applications a
LEFT JOIN LATERAL (
    SELECT h.score
    FROM bureau_history h
    WHERE h.customer_id = a.customer_id
      AND h.recorded_at <= a.decision_ts     -- knowledge time
    ORDER BY h.as_of_date DESC, h.recorded_at DESC
    LIMIT 1
) b ON TRUE;

    Syntax varies: DuckDB and Snowflake have ASOF JOIN, pandas has merge_asof, and feature stores such as Feast do it for you. Feast by default filters only on the feature's event timestamp, not creation time, so a later-corrected value can still be returned for an earlier date unless you also filter on creation time. That is the trap in Simulation 2. An as-of join is only as correct as the timestamp you tell it to respect.

    Three banking cases need care.

    • Restatements. Bureau scores, ratings and exposures get corrected. Keep the original value and its recording date alongside the correction.
    • Late or backdated records. Salary letters, KYC refreshes and reclassified transactions carry effective dates earlier than recording dates. Filter on recording date.
    • Backfills. A feature recomputed for history uses today's logic and data. Ask what the recompute read.

    Two rules. Snapshot what the model sees at decision time and keep it (feature vector, model version, timestamp). It can never be wrongly reconstructed, and validators will ask for it. And treat any table overwritten in place as unusable for training until someone shows its history.

    Splits: random, temporal, grouped and embargoed

    How you split decides what your score means. Think of revising from last year's papers. A random split is like testing yourself on shuffled questions from those same papers. A temporal (out-of-time) split trains on an earlier period and tests on a later one, like sitting this year's paper. That is how the bank uses the model, on customers who haven't happened yet, after fraud tactics, rates and products change. Credit-risk validation treats out-of-time testing as standard.

    Simulation 3 shows the shape whenever a signal slowly changes. It is not a claim about any real model.

    • Group the splits. If a customer, device or fraud ring sits on both sides, the model memorises the entity. Split by the unit that is "new" in production: the customer for an application model, the account and counterparties for a mule model.
    • Embargo near the boundary. If labels take 90 days to mature and training ends 31 March, a 15 March row's label uses outcomes through mid-June, overlapping the test period. Leave a gap at least as long as the label window (purging and embargo, López de Prado, 2018).
    • Mind label maturity. A 12-month default flag on loans made last month is nearly all zeros, so the cohort looks wonderful. This is censoring: use complete cohorts, or survival methods (F2.8).
    • Prefer walk-forward validation. Train on months 1-12, test 13-15; train 1-15, test 16-18; and so on. The trend in those scores is the decay curve you will monitor live (F7.7).
    • Report two numbers. The out-of-time score is the estimate. The drop from random to out-of-time shows how perishable the pattern is.

    Preprocessing, selection and tuning on the whole dataset

    The subtlest leak involves no time at all. Any step that learns from data must learn from training rows only, then apply unchanged to validation rows. Learning steps include scaling, imputing, feature selection, target-encoding employer or merchant, resampling, binning and hyperparameter choice.

    Do any on the full dataset and test labels shape the inputs. Usually small, but large when the step uses the label. Classic case: pick the 20 features most correlated with default out of 2,000, using every row, then cross-validate on those 20. It is like picking the winning horses after the race. The 20 were chosen because they lined up with the labels, test rows included, so the score looks good even if every candidate is noise (Ambroise & McLachlan, 2002; Hastie et al., The Elements of Statistical Learning, §7.10.2).

    A qualification, since the folklore is exaggerated. A 2026 study of 2,047 tabular datasets found that fitting a scaler or similar unsupervised transform on all rows moved AUC by 0.005 or less. What ruins results is label leakage: selection, peeking at test scores while tuning, duplicated rows and time-boundary problems random CV can't see. Still, enforce it with a pipeline object that fits every step inside each fold (F11), because inspection won't catch it.

    Tuning is selection too. Try 200 settings, report the best test AUC, and you have picked the test set's luckiest noise (forking paths, F2.4). Tune on validation folds, open a final test set once, and say so if you open it twice.

    The "too good to be true" AUC: a detection routine

    Treat a too-good number like a too-good deal. Credit application scorecards typically reach AUC 0.70 to 0.80. Fraud models do better but fraudsters adapt. A result far above what the problem allows is not a triumph until attacked. This routine takes an hour and finds most problems.

    1. Feature dictionary. Record each input's source, event time, knowledge time and refresh rate. A feature with no owner who can state when it becomes known is a suspect.
    2. Top features. If the leader is a status, flag, stage or "reason" field, ask who sets it and when.
    3. Score each feature alone. A single feature with AUC 0.9 on a hard problem is a leak, a label proxy or a join mistake.
    4. Time-shifted test. Train early, test late, compare to the random split. A large gap means temporal leakage or fast drift.
    5. Truncate features. Rebuild with a cut-off a month before the decision. If the score collapses, check the last weeks were legal.
    6. Weak baseline. Compare with bureau score alone or last month's behaviour. An unreasonable margin means look for what the complex model sees that the baseline can't.
    7. Run it forward. A shadow run on real incoming cases, scored at real decision time on real feeds, is the only fully convincing test. Keep the snapshot.

    Heuristic: ask what a data engineer would have to have done for the result to be false. If you think of it in ten seconds, check it first.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Leaks you will meet in bank and AI-generated analyses, each with its quickest check.

    • Windows anchored to the extract date

      "Last 30 days" computed once on build day, attached to applications from months earlier.

      Spot itSearch the SQL for CURRENT_DATE, NOW() or one parameter. Anchor every window on a decision-row column.
    • Status and outcome fields used as inputs

      Collections stage, block reason, "SAR filed". Set because the outcome occurred.

      Spot itFor a categorical field high in importance, ask who sets it. If a downstream team does, it is a consequence.
    • Today's dimension tables joined to history

      Segment, branch, KYC rating, employer category, overwritten in place.

      Spot itAsk if the table keeps history. If not, old rows inherit today's values, including changes caused by the outcome.
    • Restated or backfilled data treated as original

      A bureau score corrected later, or a feature recomputed with today's logic.

      Spot itCompare "updated at" with decision dates. Count training rows recorded after the decision.
    • Random split on time-ordered or grouped data

      Same customer or card on both sides, or months shuffled across train and test.

      Spot itCount test customers or devices also in training. Re-run split by time and entity.
    • Overlapping label windows at the boundary

      Training rows near the cut-off have labels that use outcomes from the test period.

      Spot itGap between last training decision and first test date should be at least the label window.
    • Immature labels in the newest cohort

      Last month's loans look excellent because none have had time to default.

      Spot itPlot default rate by origination month. A sharp recent fall is immaturity, not improvement.
    • Preprocessing or selection fitted on everything

      Selection, target encoding, SMOTE or imputation before splitting. Notebooks hide it.

      Spot itLook for fit or fit_transform on the full frame, or oversampling before the split. A pipeline fixes the order.
    • Tuning against the test set

      Choosing by the same test AUC you then report.

      Spot itAsk how many models were tried and which data picked the winner. "Many" and "the test set" means optimism.
    • Text that contains the outcome

      Complaints, call notes or analyst comments written after resolution.

      Spot itRead the top words. "After investigation", "refunded", "closed" and "تم إغلاق" are the outcome speaking.
    • AI-generated pipelines and AI-generated data

      Generated code scales and encodes before splitting, and joins on keys without timestamps. A language model used as forecaster may already know the outcome from training.

      Spot itMake the assistant use a pipeline and state each join's timestamp. For an LLM, test on events after its training cutoff.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Numeric questions need only the Part 2 AUC formula: Φ(0.71) ≈ 0.76, Φ(1.41) ≈ 0.92, Φ(2.12) ≈ 0.98.

    1. A single feature separates defaulters from non-defaulters by 2 standard deviations. What is its AUC alone? Is that plausible for a feature a bank has at application time?

    AUC = Φ(2 / √2) = Φ(1.41) ≈ 0.92. Implausibly strong for one raw application feature; the best bureau variables are far weaker. Treat it as a leak until its knowledge time is verified.

    2. An honest feature has d = 1 and a leaky one has d = 2. A linear model is fitted on history with both. What are the validation and live AUCs, if live the leaky one is noise?

    Validation: d = √(1 + 4) = 2.24, AUC = Φ(1.58) ≈ 0.94. Live: d = 1 / 2.24 = 0.45, AUC = Φ(0.32) ≈ 0.62. Worse than the honest feature alone (0.76), because the model puts four times the weight on the dead feature.

    3. An application is decided on 15 April. A salary certificate raising the customer's salary from AED 18,000 to AED 24,000, effective 1 March, was uploaded on 22 April. Which salary does the training row use?

    AED 18,000. The raise preceded the decision (event time), but the bank knew it on 22 April (knowledge time), after it. Filter on recording date, not effective date.

    4. A fraud model is validated by randomly splitting card transactions 80/20. Explain why this can overstate performance even without any time leakage.

    Fraud comes in bursts on one compromised card or device. A random split puts part of a burst in training and part in test, so the model recognises the entity, not fraud. Split by card or device, and by time.

    5. Default is "90 days past due within 12 months of disbursal". You validate on loans disbursed last month and see a 0.2% default rate and an AUC of 0.95. What is going on?

    The window isn't complete, so almost all loans are labelled good; the few defaults are early fraud or obvious cases. Use complete cohorts or censoring (F2.8), and plot default rate by origination month.

    6. You pick the 30 best features from 1,500 using the full dataset, then run 5-fold cross-validation and get an AUC of 0.74. The real signal in the data is weak. Is 0.74 trustworthy?

    No. Selection used labels of rows that later served as validation folds (Simulation 4). Redo it inside each training fold via a pipeline. The honest number may be 0.5 to 0.6. Trying many setups compounds this (F2.4).

    7. The top feature in a churn model's importance chart is "days since last call to the contact centre about account closure". What do you check?

    Whether the call precedes the decision, and what triggers the log. If logged when the customer asks to close, it is the outcome and unusable. A fees call weeks earlier may be a legitimate early signal. Check the timestamp and whether the call is only logged once an account is flagged for closure.

    8. In Simulation 3 the random split says 0.89, the three-month holdout says 0.87, and the first live year averages 0.79. Why is even the temporal holdout optimistic?

    The holdout sits right after training, so it sees only the first months of drift. Live runs a year while the model ages. Use walk-forward folds over a longer period, report the trend, and set a live monitoring trigger (F7.7).
