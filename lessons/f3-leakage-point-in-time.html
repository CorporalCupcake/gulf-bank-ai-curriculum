    F3.1 Day one ★ Priority About 40 minutes

    Point-in-Time Correctness and Leakage

    Why a bank model can score 0.98 in testing and 0.60 in production, how to know what the bank actually knew on the day of a decision, and the checks that catch a result that is too good to be true.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model may only use what the bank knew at the moment it had to decide, so every feature, every join and every split has to be rebuilt as of that moment, or the test score measures how well you can read the answer key.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A risk team builds a model to predict which new applicants will be 90 days past due within three months. Test AUC is 0.98. After a quarter live, it is 0.60.

    The culprit was one input, average daily balance over the last 30 days. The training table was built in September, so "last 30 days" meant the 30 days before the extract, not before each application. For a customer who applied in May and defaulted in July, it included the weeks when the balance collapsed. The model learned that a collapsing balance predicts default: true, and useless at decision time.

    It is like sitting an exam with the answer key in your bag. The mark is real but measures nothing. That is leakage: information in the training or test data that wasn't available when the real decision was made. It is about when, not what.

    • The leaky value wasn't wrong data. It was a correct balance for the wrong dates, so staring at the value won't reveal it.
    • Test score up, live score down. The more the model leans on the leak, the better it looks and the worse it is. Live, that weight sits on a feature that is just noise, so the model can end up worse than a clean one.
    • Stale costs little; peeking costs everything. A month-old feature loses a little signal. A feature a month ahead gives away the answer. When unsure, be conservative.
    Hold onto thisFor every number the model sees, ask: when did the bank first know this, and was that before the decision? If you can't answer, don't use it.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Three clocks: event, knowledge and decision time

    Most leakage comes from confusing three times attached to every warehouse row. Think of a letter: written, posted, read.

    • Event time: when it happened in the world. The raise took effect 1 March.
    • Knowledge time: when the bank's systems first held the fact. The HR letter was uploaded 22 April.
    • Decision time: when the bank had to act, such as the application or the card authorisation.

    The rule: a value may feed a decision only if its knowledge time is on or before the decision time. Not its event time.

    The label has its own clock: default within a performance window after the decision. Features sit left of the decision line, the label right, with no overlap.

    Availability lag is the quieter cousin. Train on yesterday's closing balance, deploy on a feed two days old, and train and live disagree. Define each feature by when it is available, not when it is true.

    Why is the damage so large? AUC is the chance that a random defaulter scores above a random non-defaulter; 0.5 is a coin flip. A leaky feature looks far stronger than any honest one, so the model puts most of its weight there. Live, that weight is wasted. A leaky model is not just over-optimistic: it can be worse than a clean one while its test score says the opposite. In Simulation 1, a month of future data lifts validation to about 0.98 and drops live to 0.60, below the honest model's 0.76.

    As-of joins and restated data

    The as-of join enforces the rule. It asks "what did the bank's file say on 15 April?" and matches each decision row to the latest value the bank knew at that timestamp, not the current one.

    A naive join hits today's table. If that table is overwritten in place, every historical row inherits today's segment, rating and corrected score. An as-of join needs history: a table that keeps every version with dates, or a snapshot at each decision.

    -- Leaky: attaches today's score to a past application
SELECT a.app_id, a.decision_ts, b.score
FROM applications a
JOIN bureau_current b USING (customer_id);

-- Point-in-time: the score the bank held at decision time
SELECT a.app_id, a.decision_ts, b.score
FROM applications a
LEFT JOIN LATERAL (
    SELECT h.score
    FROM bureau_history h
    WHERE h.customer_id = a.customer_id
      AND h.recorded_at <= a.decision_ts     -- knowledge time
    ORDER BY h.as_of_date DESC, h.recorded_at DESC
    LIMIT 1
) b ON TRUE;

    Tools differ (ASOF JOIN in DuckDB and Snowflake, merge_asof in pandas, feature stores such as Feast), but all are only as correct as the timestamp you tell them to respect: when the bank recorded the value, not when it took effect.

    • Restatements. Bureau scores and ratings get corrected. Keep the original value and its recording date.
    • Late or backdated records. Salary letters and KYC refreshes carry effective dates earlier than recording dates.

    Snapshot what the model sees at decision time and keep it. And treat any table overwritten in place as unusable for training until someone shows its history.

    Splits: random, temporal, grouped and embargoed

    How you split decides what your score means. A random split is like testing yourself on shuffled questions from last year's papers. A temporal (out-of-time) split trains on an earlier period and tests on a later one, like sitting this year's paper. That is how the bank uses the model: on customers who haven't happened yet.

    • Group the splits. If a customer, device or fraud ring sits on both sides, the model memorises the entity. Split by the unit that is "new" in production.
    • Embargo near the boundary. If labels take 90 days to mature, leave a gap at least that long between training and test, or training labels will use outcomes from the test period.
    • Walk forward, report two numbers. Train on months 1-12, test 13-15; then train 1-15, test 16-18. The out-of-time score is the estimate; the drop from random to out-of-time shows how perishable the pattern is.

    Preprocessing, selection and tuning on the whole dataset

    The subtlest leak involves no time at all. Any step that learns from data (scaling, imputing, feature selection, target encoding, resampling, tuning) must learn from training rows only, then apply unchanged to validation rows.

    Do it on the full dataset and test labels shape the inputs. Classic case: pick the 20 features most correlated with default out of 2,000, using every row, then cross-validate on those 20. It is like picking the winning horses after the race. It scores well even if every candidate is noise.

    Fitting a plain scaler on all rows usually matters little; label-based steps ruin results. Enforce it with a pipeline object that fits every step inside each fold (F11).

    Tuning is selection too. Try 200 settings and report the best test AUC, and you have picked the test set's luckiest noise (F2.4). Open a final test set once.

    The "too good to be true" AUC: a detection routine

    Treat a too-good number like a too-good deal. Application scorecards typically reach AUC 0.70 to 0.80. Attack a result far above that before anyone celebrates.

    1. Feature dictionary. Record each input's source and knowledge time. If the top feature is a status, flag or "reason" field, ask who sets it and when.
    2. Score each feature alone. One feature with AUC 0.9 on a hard problem is a leak, a label proxy or a join mistake.
    3. Time-shifted test. Train early, test late, compare to the random split. A large gap means temporal leakage or fast drift.
    4. Truncate features. Rebuild with a cut-off a month before the decision. If the score collapses, check the last weeks were legal.
    5. Run it forward. A shadow run on real incoming cases is the only fully convincing test.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Leaks you will meet in bank and AI-generated analyses, each with its quickest check.

    • Windows anchored to the extract date

      "Last 30 days" computed once on build day, attached to applications from months earlier.

      Spot itSearch the SQL for CURRENT_DATE, NOW() or one parameter. Anchor every window on a decision-row column.
    • Outcome fields and outcome text used as inputs

      Collections stage, block reason, "SAR filed", or notes written after resolution.

      Spot itAsk who sets a high-importance field. If a downstream team does, it is a consequence. In text, "refunded" or "تم إغلاق" is the outcome speaking.
    • Overwritten, restated or backfilled data treated as original

      Segment, KYC rating or bureau score overwritten in place, or recomputed with today's logic.

      Spot itAsk if the table keeps history. Count training rows recorded after the decision.
    • Random split on time-ordered or grouped data

      Same customer on both sides, months shuffled, or training labels that use test-period outcomes.

      Spot itCount test customers also in training. Re-split by time and entity, with a gap at least the label window.
    • Immature labels in the newest cohort

      Last month's loans look excellent because none have had time to default.

      Spot itPlot default rate by origination month. A sharp recent fall is immaturity, not improvement.
    • Preprocessing, selection or tuning on everything

      Selection, target encoding, SMOTE or imputation before splitting, or picking a model by the test AUC you report. AI-generated code often does this.

      Spot itLook for fit on the full frame. Ask how many models were tried and which data picked the winner.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A single feature available at application time gives an AUC of 0.92 for default on its own. Is that plausible?

    No. Whole scorecards reach 0.70 to 0.80. Treat it as a leak until its knowledge time is verified.

    2. A model is fitted on history with one honest feature and one strong leaky one, and scores 0.94 in validation. Live, the leaky feature is noise. Why can live be worse than a model using the honest feature alone?

    In training the model leans on the leaky feature because it looks far stronger. Live, that weight goes to noise. In Simulation 1 the result is 0.60 live against 0.76 for the clean model.

    3. An application is decided on 15 April. A salary certificate raising the customer's salary from AED 18,000 to AED 24,000, effective 1 March, was uploaded on 22 April. Which salary does the training row use?

    AED 18,000. The raise preceded the decision (event time), but the bank knew it only on 22 April (knowledge time). Filter on recording date.

    4. A fraud model is validated by randomly splitting card transactions 80/20. Why can this overstate performance even without any time leakage?

    Fraud comes in bursts on one compromised card. A random split puts part of a burst on each side, so the model recognises the card, not fraud. Split by card and by time.

    5. Default is "90 days past due within 12 months of disbursal". You validate on loans disbursed last month and see a 0.2% default rate and an AUC of 0.95. What is going on?

    The window isn't complete, so almost all loans are labelled good. Use complete cohorts or survival methods (F2.8).

    6. You pick the 30 best features from 1,500 using the full dataset, then run 5-fold cross-validation and get an AUC of 0.74. The real signal is weak. Is 0.74 trustworthy?

    No. Selection used the labels of rows that later served as validation folds (Simulation 4). Redo it inside each fold; the honest number may be 0.5 to 0.6.

    7. The top feature in a churn model's importance chart is "days since last call to the contact centre about account closure". What do you check?

    Whether the call precedes the decision, and what triggers the log. If it is logged when the customer asks to close, it is the outcome. A fees call weeks earlier may be a legitimate signal.

    8. In Simulation 3 the random split says 0.89, the three-month holdout says 0.87, and the first live year averages 0.79. Why is even the temporal holdout optimistic?

    The holdout sits right after training, so it sees only the first months of drift. Use walk-forward folds over a longer period and set a live monitoring trigger (F7.7).
