    Learning Without Labels, and Generating the Data That Isn't There

    How a model learns what "normal" looks like from data nobody has labelled, why contrastive learning works and where it quietly misleads, and how VAEs, GANs and diffusion models create images, text-like data and synthetic records, with a clear view of what each is good for in a bank.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLabels are scarce and expensive but the data itself is abundant, so you invent a task whose answer is hidden inside the data (fill in the gap, tell two views of the same thing from different things) and the representation learned along the way does most of the work for the real task; generative models go one step further and learn the whole distribution well enough to sample from it, which is useful for synthetic data and dangerous for identity.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank holds eight years of card transactions for several million customers: billions of rows. It holds a few thousand confirmed fraud cases, a few hundred confirmed money-laundering cases, and a few tens of thousands of complaints a human has tagged by topic. The data is huge. The labels are tiny, slow and expensive, because a person with a fraud-investigator's salary had to look at each one.

    Self-supervised learning starts from one observation: the raw data already contains a lot of supervision, if you hide part of it. Cover a word in a sentence and ask the model to guess it. Hide the amount of a transaction and ask for it back from the merchant, time and the customer's last twenty purchases. Show a model two crops of one cheque image and ask it to say they came from the same cheque. Nobody labelled anything, yet to do these tasks well the model must learn what spending looks like, what Arabic grammar looks like, what a cheque looks like. That knowledge is the representation, and it is what you keep. The made-up task (the pretext task) is scaffolding you throw away.

    Then you attach the real, label-hungry task (this is fraud, this is a salary credit) to the representation. It now needs only a handful of labels, because most of the learning is already done. The simulation shows the effect in the smallest possible setting.

    Three ideas to hold, then we turn to the second half of the lesson.

    • The representation is the product. A good one puts similar things close together and lets a simple model on top do the final job. This is the vector geometry of F1.1, learned instead of designed.
    • The pretext task decides what the representation ignores. If the task is "same cheque, different crop", the model learns to ignore crop position and keep ink and layout. If the task is "same customer, different month", it learns habits and ignores which month it is. Choosing the task is choosing the invariances.
    • Generative models are the ambitious version. Instead of learning features that separate things, they learn the distribution well enough to produce new samples: new images, new synthetic customers, new noise-free versions of a corrupted input. That brings power (data that does not exist yet) and risk (data that looks real and isn't, or that leaks something real).
    Hold onto thisSelf-supervised learning moves the cost from labelling to designing the pretext task and checking the representation. You still need some labels, to name things and to evaluate. And a representation can only be as good as the structure that the unlabelled data actually carries about your task, which Simulation 1 shows going wrong when the groups overlap.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Pretext tasks: predict the hidden part

    There are three families of pretext task, and nearly everything in the field is a variation on one of them.

    FamilyThe hidden answerExamplesBanking version
    Predict the next itemThe next token, step or frame.GPT-style language models, next-token training (M1.2).Predict a customer's next transaction (merchant category, amount band, time gap) from the last few hundred.
    Fill in the masked partWords or patches that were blanked out.BERT (15% of tokens masked); masked autoencoders for images (75% of patches masked).Mask a few fields of a transaction or a whole week and reconstruct them. Mask words of a complaint in Arabic or English.
    Tell views apartWhich pieces came from the same source.SimCLR, MoCo, CLIP (image with its caption), DINO.Two windows of the same customer's behaviour; a scanned invoice and its extracted text; a call recording and its transcript.

    The first two are generative pretext tasks: the model has to reproduce the missing data itself, pixel for pixel or token for token. The third is discriminative: the model only has to decide, never to reproduce. That difference is the subject of the next section, and it comes back in the generative half of the lesson.

    Why does predicting a masked word teach anything useful? Because to guess a missing word well you need grammar, facts and context. To guess a missing transaction amount you need to know that this customer pays rent on the 1st, that Thursday evenings mean groceries, and that a 5,000 AED electronics purchase abroad is out of character. The cheapest way for a network to get the answer right across billions of examples is to build an internal model of the world in which the data lives. Deep networks (F5.1) have the capacity to do so; the pretext task supplies the pressure.

    The practical recipe has two stages:

    • Pretrain on all the unlabelled data you can get. This is the expensive step and is done once.
    • Adapt to the real task. Either freeze the representation and train a small model on top with your labels (a linear probe: the cheapest honest test of representation quality), or fine-tune the whole thing at a low learning rate. With a good representation, a few hundred labels can match what thousands used to need.

    This is exactly the "pretraining then post-training" pattern of large language models (M1.2), seen from the angle of why it works. The label-efficiency curve (accuracy against number of labels, with and without pretraining) is the chart to ask for whenever anyone claims a self-supervised win.

    Contrastive learning and the InfoNCE loss

    Take an anchor (one view of something), a positive (another view of the same thing) and many negatives (views of other things). Train the encoder so that anchor and positive land close together in embedding space and the negatives land far away. "Close" is measured by cosine similarity (F1.1). The standard loss, called InfoNCE or the NT-Xent loss in SimCLR, is a softmax over similarities:

    ℒ=−logexp(s(a,p)/τ)exp(s(a,p)/τ)+∑jexp(s(a,nj)/τ)

    s is the cosine similarity between two embeddings, τ (tau) the temperature, nj the negatives. Read it as a classification problem: "out of the positive and all the negatives, which one is the true partner of the anchor?", scored with cross-entropy (F1.6). The loss is small when the positive's similarity towers over every negative's. If the model knows nothing, all similarities are equal and the loss is ln(N + 1) for N negatives.

    Two knobs shape everything, and the simulation lets you turn both.

    • Temperature τ. Dividing similarities by a small τ sharpens the softmax. With a small τ, a negative that sits almost as close as the positive gets nearly all the repulsive push; easy negatives are ignored. With a large τ the push is spread across all negatives equally. Small τ means a harder, more discriminating, but also more fragile objective.
    • Number of negatives N. More negatives make the task harder and the representation finer. This is why SimCLR used very large batches, and why MoCo kept a queue of past embeddings as a cheap way to have thousands of negatives. The cost is memory.

    A third element is silent but decisive: augmentation. The positive pair is made by transforming one input twice (crop, colour jitter, blur for images; token dropping or paraphrase for text; two different time windows for a customer). Whatever the augmentations leave unchanged is what the model learns to keep; whatever they change is what it learns to ignore (F6.2 covers augmentation in general). Choose augmentations that destroy irrelevant information and preserve what the downstream task needs.

    Notice the hidden issue. The loss assumes everything in the batch except the positive is a true negative. In banking data, that is often false: two accounts of one customer, two complaints about the same incident, two transactions at the same merchant branch, a mule account's near-identical siblings. The loss pushes them apart anyway, and the harder they look the harder it pushes. This is the false-negative problem, and the standard fixes are to construct batches so that known relatives never appear as negatives, to use a larger τ, or to use methods with no negatives at all (next section).

    Without negatives: collapse, and the tricks that avoid it

    Why do we need negatives? Because without them there is a trivial solution: output the same vector for every input. Then every positive pair is perfectly "close" and the loss is zero. This is collapse, and preventing it is the central design problem of the field. Different methods prevent it in different ways:

    MethodHow it avoids collapse
    SimCLR, MoCo, CLIPNegatives in the loss. Everything else must be different.
    BYOL, DINO, DINOv2/v3A student network predicts the output of a slowly updated teacher copy of itself (an exponential moving average). Stop-gradients, centring and sharpening keep the outputs from becoming constant. No explicit negatives.
    Barlow Twins, VICRegAn explicit penalty: keep each embedding dimension's variance above a floor, and decorrelate dimensions, so the vector cannot be constant or redundant.
    Masked autoencoders, BERTReconstruction. The target is the data itself, so there is nothing to collapse to.
    JEPA family (I-JEPA, V-JEPA)Predict the embedding of the hidden region rather than its pixels, with a teacher copy to supply the target. The aim is to predict what matters about the hidden part and skip unpredictable detail.

    The JEPA idea is worth a sentence of intuition, because it is the live debate. If you ask a model to reproduce missing pixels, it spends effort on detail that is unpredictable (the exact texture of a carpet). If you ask it to predict an abstract embedding of the missing region, it can keep what is predictable and drop the rest. Advocates (notably Yann LeCun) argue this is the route to models that understand rather than reproduce. Sceptics point out that reconstruction methods scale well and are simple. As of late 2026, both camps ship strong models (Part 4).

    Monitoring for collapse is not academic. If you train an embedding model on your own transactions and it quietly collapses to a small subspace, the loss may look fine while every customer looks alike. Check the effective rank or the spread of embeddings, and a linear-probe score on a few labels, during training, not only at the end.

    What it looks like on bank data

    Most of the published progress is on images and text, but the pattern transfers to the data a bank actually has.

    • Transaction sequences. Treat each customer's history as a sentence whose tokens are (merchant category, amount band, time gap, channel). Pretrain with masking or next-event prediction, then fine-tune for fraud, churn or credit. The idea is simple; whether it beats a well-engineered gradient-boosted model with hand-built features (F3.4) is an empirical question, and the answer is "sometimes, mainly when labels are scarce or you have many tasks to serve with one model". The topic has its own lesson: foundation models for transaction data.
    • Text, in two languages. Masked-language-model encoders and contrastive sentence embedders power search over policies, complaint triage and screening. Arabic adds dialects, morphology and transliteration, and a representation trained mostly on English underperforms there (see Arabic LLMs).
    • Documents and images. Image-text contrastive models (CLIP-style) let you search scans by description and cluster document types with no labels, the base of trade-finance document AI.
    • Behavioural signals. Two sessions by the same user form a positive pair; sessions by different users, negatives. The embedding then represents "how this person types and swipes", the base of behavioural biometrics.

    Three cautions specific to a bank:

    • Leakage hides in the pretext task. If you pretrain on all of 2018–2026 and test fraud on 2025, the representation has already seen 2025's patterns. Pretraining data obeys the same point-in-time rule as features (F3.1): only data available before the evaluation period.
    • The representation inherits the data's population. If pretraining mostly contains salaried residents, customers with different patterns (expatriate workers sending remittances, small merchants, new-to-bank customers) are represented worse. Check the probe's performance by segment, not only overall (G2).
    • Embeddings can leak. A representation trained on customer data may let someone infer facts about individuals. Treat the embedding store with the same controls as the source data (G2.4).

    Generative models: one question, four answers

    A generative model learns the distribution p(x) of the data, so that you can draw new samples from it. The difficulty is that real data (an image, a transaction history) lives in a space of thousands of dimensions and the distribution is intricate. Each family makes a different bet on how to represent it. Language models are already generative (they model the distribution of the next token); this half of the lesson is about images, tabular records and other continuous data, and about the three ideas that got there: the VAE, the GAN and diffusion.

    FamilyThe ideaStrengthWeakness
    VAE (variational autoencoder)Compress to a small, well-behaved latent code, then decode. Sample the code to generate.Stable to train; a smooth latent space; gives a likelihood bound; good for anomaly scoring.Samples tend to be blurry or generic; the latent code can be ignored by a strong decoder.
    GAN (generative adversarial network)A generator forges, a discriminator catches forgeries. They improve each other.Sharp samples in one forward pass; fast to generate.Unstable training; mode collapse (producing only a few kinds of sample); no likelihood.
    Diffusion / flow matchingLearn to remove a little noise at a time; generate by starting from pure noise and denoising repeatedly.Stable training; wide coverage of the data; the current best for images, audio, video.Slow sampling (many steps), though much reduced by distillation and better solvers.
    AutoregressivePredict one piece at a time given the previous ones.Exact likelihood; the engine of language models.Sequential and slow for long outputs; awkward for continuous data.

    VAEs: compress, regularise, decode

    An ordinary autoencoder squeezes an input into a small code and reconstructs it (F4.4 uses this for anomaly detection). The trouble for generation: the codes land wherever the network likes, with gaps between them. Pick a random code and the decoder produces garbage.

    A variational autoencoder fixes this by making the encoder output a distribution, a mean and a spread, for each input, and by adding a penalty that pulls all those distributions towards a standard bell curve. The training objective, the evidence lower bound (ELBO), has two terms:

    ELBO=𝔼q(z|x)[logp(x|z)]−KL(q(z|x)‖p(z))

    The first term rewards faithful reconstruction. The second (KL divergence, F1.7) penalises the code distribution for straying from a standard bell curve p(z), which is what makes random codes decodable. The two pull against each other. Too much weight on the KL term and the codes carry no information (the decoder ignores them and produces an average sample); too little and you have a plain autoencoder with gaps. Training works because of the reparameterisation trick: write the sampled code as mean + spread × noise, so gradients (F1.3) can flow through the sampling.

    VAEs get blurry samples because the reconstruction term averages over everything the code could mean. Ask for "a face" and you get the average face. That averaging property is not a defect when you want a score: the reconstruction error and the KL term together say how typical an input is, which is why VAEs remain a fixture for anomaly detection (F4.4). Their second life is as a compressor inside larger systems: Stable Diffusion runs its diffusion in the compact latent space of an autoencoder, not on raw pixels.

    GANs: the forger and the inspector

    Two networks play a game. The generator turns random noise into a candidate sample. The discriminator receives real and generated samples and tries to tell them apart. Each improves by beating the other: the discriminator gets better at spotting fakes, which forces the generator to produce fakes with fewer tells. If it works, the generator ends up producing samples the discriminator cannot distinguish from real data, and the generator has implicitly learned the distribution.

    The mathematics is a minimax game, and the practical story is a catalogue of difficulties. Training can oscillate rather than converge. The generator can find a few outputs that fool the discriminator and produce only those (mode collapse): a fraud-data GAN that produces ten kinds of fraud when there are two hundred. And there is no likelihood to monitor, so you cannot tell from a loss curve whether it is working.

    GANs dominated image generation from 2014 to about 2020 and still matter in two places a bank cares about. They are fast, so they suit real-time uses such as face synthesis and super-resolution; and they underlie the deepfakes that attack eKYC and voice authentication (A3.5). Tabular variants (CTGAN) were the first widely used synthetic-data generators for spreadsheets of mixed numbers and categories.

    Diffusion: learn to remove noise, then run it backwards

    Diffusion rests on an asymmetry. Destroying structure is easy: add a little random noise to an image, again and again, and after enough steps nothing but static remains. Rebuilding structure is hard, but each small step is nearly trivial: given a slightly noisy image, guess the slightly less noisy one. Train one network to do that, for every noise level, and you can generate: start from pure noise and apply the network repeatedly, and out comes a plausible sample.

    The forward (noising) step has a closed form, which makes training cheap. You can jump to any noise level directly:

    xt=α¯tx0+1−α¯tε

    x0 is a clean example, ε standard random noise, and ᾱt a number that falls from 1 (no noise) to nearly 0 (all noise) as t grows. At ᾱ = 0.25 the signal keeps half its amplitude (√0.25) and the noise has standard deviation √0.75 ≈ 0.87. Training: pick a random clean example, a random t and noise, form xt, and ask the network to predict the noise ε (or equivalently the clean x0). The loss is an ordinary squared error (F6.1). That's it.

    What the network learns, by predicting the noise, is the score: the direction in which the data becomes more probable. Sampling then follows that direction downhill in noise, a little at a time. This unifies diffusion with score-based models (Song et al., 2021), and the deterministic sampler, DDIM, lets you take far fewer steps. In the simulation the denoiser is not learned; because the "customers" are drawn from a known mixture of three groups, its optimal denoiser has a closed form, which lets you see the mechanism without a trained network.

    Four developments turned this idea into the engine behind modern image and video generators.

    • Latent diffusion. Run the process in the compact latent space of an autoencoder rather than on pixels (Rombach et al., 2022). Far cheaper, hence Stable Diffusion.
    • Conditioning and guidance. Feed the network a text prompt or a class label. Classifier-free guidance pushes samples harder towards the condition by extrapolating between the conditioned and unconditioned predictions, trading variety for fidelity.
    • Transformers as the denoiser. Replacing the original U-Net with a transformer (F5.3) in DiT, and in Stable Diffusion 3, made diffusion scale the way language models do.
    • Flow matching and rectified flow. Instead of the noise-prediction recipe, learn a velocity field that carries noise straight to data along near-straight paths. Same family, simpler training, fewer sampling steps; now the default recipe in many leading image models.

    The same denoising idea has been applied to discrete text, replacing left-to-right generation with iterative refinement of all tokens at once. See diffusion language models.

    Using generated data in a bank: fidelity, utility, privacy

    The most direct use of generative models in banking is synthetic data: records that look statistically like customers or transactions but are not any real customer. Common motives: to give developers and vendors a realistic test environment without exposing real data; to share data across teams or borders where policy forbids moving real rows; to augment a rare class. (On the last, the evidence is in F3.5: gains for strong models are weak and calibration is damaged.)

    Judge synthetic data on three axes, and demand all three:

    AxisQuestionTypical check
    FidelityDoes it look like the real data?Marginal distributions, correlations, a classifier trained to tell real from synthetic (accuracy near 50% is good).
    UtilityDoes it support the task?"Train on synthetic, test on real" (TSTR): a model trained on the synthetic data should perform close to one trained on real data, when both are tested on real held-out data.
    PrivacyDoes it leak real records or reveal who was in the training set?Distance of each synthetic row to its nearest real row; membership-inference attacks; formal guarantees through differentially private training.

    These axes conflict. A generator that reproduces the training rows exactly has perfect fidelity and utility and no privacy. One that adds a lot of noise is private and useless. Large generative models are known to memorise rare training examples, and outliers are exactly the unusual customers whom a bank is most obliged to protect (R11, A11.2). "Synthetic" is not a legal synonym for "anonymous". I am not aware of a rule that makes synthetic data automatically outside data-protection scope, and the position depends on the jurisdiction and on how it was generated; take it to your privacy and legal teams and keep the evidence of the privacy tests.

    • Fraud and AML with few labels. Pretrain on all transactions, probe with the few confirmed cases. Also, a bridge between supervised models and pure anomaly detection.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Self-supervised pretraining won: it is how nearly every strong text, image and speech model starts. The live arguments are about which objective scales best, whether to predict pixels or abstract embeddings, and how well the recipe carries over to structured data like transactions.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026. Searches were light; the 2025 and 2026 items below were confirmed in search results, older items are cited from memory of the literature.

    1. 2013–14
      VAE and GAN

      Kingma and Welling introduced the variational autoencoder and the reparameterisation trick; Goodfellow and colleagues introduced adversarial training. Two answers to "how do I sample from a complicated distribution?".

      Kingma & Welling, ICLR 2014; Goodfellow et al., NeurIPS 2014
    2. 2018–20
      Masked language models, then contrastive vision

      BERT showed masked prediction gives general-purpose text representations. SimCLR and MoCo showed that contrastive learning on images, with strong augmentation and many negatives, closes much of the gap to supervised pretraining. BYOL showed negatives are not essential.

      Devlin et al., 2018; Chen et al., ICML 2020; He et al., CVPR 2020; Grill et al., NeurIPS 2020

      Diffusion becomes practical; CLIP links images and text

      DDPM made denoising diffusion train stably and produce high-quality images. The score-based view unified it with SDE sampling. CLIP trained on hundreds of millions of image-caption pairs contrastively and became the standard way to connect pictures and language.

      Ho et al., NeurIPS 2020; Song et al., ICLR 2021; Radford et al., ICML 2021

      Self-distillation and masked images at scale

      DINO and DINOv2 produced frozen features that work across many vision tasks without fine-tuning. Masked autoencoders (75% of patches hidden) made image pretraining efficient. VICReg avoided collapse with explicit variance terms. I-JEPA predicted embeddings of masked regions rather than pixels.

      Caron et al., ICCV 2021; He et al., CVPR 2022; Bardes et al., ICLR 2022; Oquab et al., 2023; Assran et al., CVPR 2023

      Latent diffusion made image generation affordable; DiT put a transformer inside; flow matching and rectified flow simplified the training objective and cut the number of steps.       Rombach et al., CVPR 2022; Peebles & Xie, ICCV 2023; Lipman et al., ICLR 2023; Esser et al., ICML 2024; Kotelnikov et al., ICML 2023; Zhang et al., ICLR 2024

      Self-supervision at 7 billion parameters

      DINOv3 trained a 7-billion-parameter vision model on about 1.7 billion images without labels.       Flow matching alongside diffusion as the default recipe

      MIT's 2026 course notes on generative AI describe flow matching and denoising diffusion as the core of leading image, audio and video generators.     Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor claims, project proposals and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Pretraining that sees the test period

      Embeddings pretrained on all history, then evaluated on a "held-out" later month. The pretext task has already seen it.

      Spot itAsk for the date range of the pretraining data against the evaluation period. Pretraining must end before the first evaluation date.
    • Collapse that the loss hides

      The loss falls, every customer maps to nearly the same vector, and the downstream model gains nothing.

      Spot itLook at the spread (effective rank) of the embeddings and a linear-probe score on a few hundred labels at every checkpoint.
    • False negatives repelling real neighbours

      Accounts of the same customer, duplicated complaints, or near-identical merchants are treated as different and pushed apart.

      Spot itAsk how batches are built and whether known relatives can appear as negatives. Check nearest neighbours of a few embeddings by eye.
    • Augmentations that erase the signal

      Cropping away the stamp that distinguishes a forged cheque; shuffling the time order that carries the fraud pattern.

      Spot itAsk what each augmentation preserves and whether the downstream task depends on it. Compare a probe with and without it.
    • "Pretrained, therefore good" with no label-efficiency curve

      A claim of success on the full labelled set says nothing about the benefit of pretraining.

      Spot itAsk for accuracy against number of labels, with and without pretraining, against a tuned gradient-boosted baseline.
    • Blurry or collapsed generators judged by eye

      A sample of ten synthetic records looks fine. The generator has covered only the common customers.

      Spot itCompare rare segments and tails, not only averages. Check the share of each category in synthetic against real, and run train-on-synthetic, test-on-real.
    • Synthetic data that memorises

      Some synthetic rows are near copies of real ones, so releasing the data releases customers.

      Spot itCompute each synthetic row's distance to the nearest training row against the typical real-to-real distance; run a membership-inference test.
    • Mode collapse and fashionable fidelity metrics

      A GAN gives sharp, plausible samples of a few kinds. A single average score hides it.

      Spot itAsk for coverage (how much of the real data's variety is represented), not only realism. Count distinct clusters in samples against real data.
    • Retraining on one's own generated output

      Synthetic data feeds the next model, and the tails thin generation by generation.

      Spot itTrack the share of generated data in each training set and keep a fresh slice of real data as the anchor (R2).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We have billions of transactions but only a few hundred confirmed cases of the thing we want to catch. Self-supervised learning lets a model study all the unlabelled history first, learning what normal customer behaviour looks like, and then learn from the confirmed cases much faster. The test is simple: does it match our current model with far fewer labels, and does it hold up on next quarter's data? Synthetic data is a separate, useful tool for test environments, but we treat it as sensitive until the privacy checks pass.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd pretrain with masked or next-event prediction on transaction sequences ending before the evaluation window, check for collapse using embedding spread and a linear probe at each checkpoint, then compare label-efficiency curves against a tuned gradient-boosted baseline, by segment. For contrastive objectives I'd care about how negatives are sampled, because relatives of an anchor become false negatives. For synthetic data I'd report fidelity, train-on-synthetic-test-on-real utility and nearest-neighbour and membership-inference privacy tests, and I would not call it anonymous without legal review.”
    “What is the difference between contrastive learning and masked prediction?”

    Masked prediction is generative: the model has to reproduce the hidden part, so the target is the data itself and nothing can collapse. Contrastive learning is discriminative: the model decides which views belong together, so it needs negatives (or another trick) to prevent the trivial answer of mapping everything to one point. Contrastive representations tend to be good for similarity and retrieval; masked ones often need fine-tuning to shine but scale easily.

    “Why does a small temperature help, and what is the catch?”

    A small temperature makes the loss focus on the hardest negatives, which sharpens the embedding. The catch is that hard negatives are exactly where false negatives live: the near-duplicate that is really the same customer. The more you focus on them, the more damage mislabelled negatives do.

    “Is a GAN or a diffusion model better for generating synthetic transactions?”

    Neither wins by default. GANs are fast but unstable and prone to mode collapse, which means missing the rare customers. Diffusion-based tabular generators have reported better fidelity but need more compute at generation time. I'd run both on our data and compare fidelity, utility on real hold-out data and privacy distances, since all three matter and they trade off.

    “Can we treat synthetic data as non-personal and share it freely?”

    Not on the strength of the word "synthetic". A generator can memorise or reveal real records, particularly unusual ones. We would run nearest-neighbour and membership-inference tests, consider differentially private training if sharing externally, and get privacy and legal sign-off on the specific use. I'd not assert a regulatory position without them.

    “We have 8 years of data. Should we build our own foundation model?”

    First prove the value on a small version: pretrain a modest sequence model, and test the label-efficiency curve against our best feature-engineered model on one task. If the gain is real for several tasks, a shared representation pays for itself; if only one task benefits, better features may be cheaper. Also consider a vendor or open model for text and document work, where our data adds little at pretraining scale.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does masking part of a transaction history and asking the model to restore it teach anything useful for fraud detection, when it never saw a fraud label?

    To restore the missing part, the model has to learn regularities of normal behaviour: habits, timing, typical amounts, merchant patterns. That knowledge is what a fraud model needs as background, since fraud is a departure from it. Fraud labels then only need to teach the last step: which departures matter.

    2. An anchor has a positive with cosine similarity 0.8, and three negatives each with 0.2. With τ = 0.1, what is the InfoNCE loss, and what would it be if the model knew nothing (all similarities equal)?

    Logits are 8 and 2, 2, 2. The positive's softmax probability is e8 ÷ (e8 + 3e2) = 2981 ÷ 3003 ≈ 0.993, so the loss is −ln(0.993) ≈ 0.0074. With no knowledge, every one of the four candidates has probability 1/4, so the loss is ln 4 ≈ 1.39.

    3. You halve the temperature. What changes in how the loss treats easy and hard negatives?

    Dividing by a smaller τ stretches the differences between logits, so the softmax is sharper. The hardest negatives take an even larger share of the gradient, while easy negatives are effectively ignored. You get a finer embedding and more sensitivity to false negatives and noisy pairs.

    4. What is collapse, and name two different ways methods prevent it.

    Collapse is the model outputting the same (or nearly the same) embedding for every input, which makes any positive-pair loss zero. Prevented by: negatives in the loss (SimCLR, MoCo, CLIP); a teacher-student pair with stop-gradient and centring (BYOL, DINO); explicit variance and decorrelation penalties (VICReg, Barlow Twins); reconstruction targets (masked autoencoders).

    5. In diffusion with ᾱ = 0.64, a clean value x0 = 1.5 is noised. What is the mean and standard deviation of the noised value?

    The mean is √0.64 × 1.5 = 0.8 × 1.5 = 1.2. The standard deviation of the added noise is √(1 − 0.64) = √0.36 = 0.6.

    6. Why are VAE samples blurry, and why is the same property useful for anomaly detection?

    The VAE decoder is trained to give the best average reconstruction over everything that the code could stand for, so its output is a smoothed, typical version. For anomaly detection, a smoothed typical version is the point: inputs that depart from the typical pattern reconstruct badly, and the error is the score.

    7. A synthetic transaction table passes every distribution test, and a model trained on it scores AUC 0.91 on real hold-out data against 0.92 for one trained on real data. Is it safe to release outside the bank?

    Not yet. Fidelity and utility are shown; privacy is not. Check nearest real record distances, membership inference, and the treatment of outliers and rare customers, then get privacy and legal approval for the specific use.

    8. In Simulation 1, why does the benefit of unlabelled data vanish when the two groups are close together?

    The unlabelled data reveals clusters only if there is a gap between them. When the groups overlap, clustering splits the data somewhere that does not follow the real class boundary, so it gives no help (or misleads). The lesson: structure in unlabelled data only helps if it lines up with the label you care about.
