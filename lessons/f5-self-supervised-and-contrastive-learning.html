    Learning Without Labels, and Generating the Data That Isn't There

    How a model learns what "normal" looks like from data nobody has labelled, why contrastive learning works and where it quietly misleads, and how VAEs, GANs and diffusion models create images, text-like data and synthetic records, with a clear view of what each is good for in a bank.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceLabels are scarce but data is abundant, so you invent a task whose answer is hidden in the data (fill the gap, match two views of the same thing) and keep the representation it learns; generative models go further and learn the whole distribution so you can sample from it, which helps with synthetic data and is risky for privacy.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank holds billions of card transactions but only a few thousand confirmed fraud cases, because each label needed an investigator's time. Data is cheap; labels are not.

    Self-supervised learning hides part of the data and asks the model to restore it. It is like learning a language by covering words in newspapers and guessing them. Hide a transaction's amount and predict it from merchant, time and recent purchases. To do this well, the model must learn what normal spending looks like. That knowledge is the representation, and it is what you keep. The made-up task (the pretext task) is scaffolding.

    Then you attach the real task (this is fraud, this is a salary credit). Most of the learning is done, so a handful of labels is enough. Simulation 1 shows this in the smallest setting.

    • The representation is the product. A good one puts similar things close together so a simple model on top can finish the job. It is the vector geometry of F1.1, learned instead of designed.
    • The pretext task decides what the representation ignores. "Same cheque, different crop" teaches it to ignore crop position and keep ink and layout.
    • Generative models are the ambitious version. They learn the data well enough to produce new samples. The risk is data that looks real and is not, or that leaks something real.
    Hold onto thisSelf-supervision moves the cost from labelling to designing the pretext task and checking the representation. You still need some labels to name things and to evaluate.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Pretext tasks: predict the hidden part

    Nearly every pretext task is one of three families.

    • Predict the next item (GPT-style models). Bank version: the next transaction's category and amount band.
    • Fill in the masked part (BERT). Bank version: mask fields of a transaction, or words of a complaint.
    • Tell views apart (SimCLR, CLIP). Bank version: two windows of one customer, or an invoice scan and its text.

    Why does guessing a masked value teach anything? To guess a missing amount you must know this customer pays rent on the 1st and that 5,000 AED of electronics abroad is out of character. The cheapest way to be right is to model that world.

    The recipe has two stages. Pretrain on all the unlabelled data (expensive, done once). Then adapt: freeze the representation and train a small model on top (a linear probe, the cheapest honest test of quality), or fine-tune everything gently. A few hundred labels can match what thousands needed. When anyone claims a self-supervised win, ask for the label-efficiency curve: accuracy against number of labels, with and without pretraining (M1.2).

    Contrastive learning and the InfoNCE loss

    Think of a game of "spot the twin". Take an anchor, a positive (another view of the same thing) and many negatives (other things). Train the encoder to put anchor and positive close in embedding space (cosine similarity, F1.1) and negatives far. The loss, InfoNCE, is a quiz: "which of these candidates is the anchor's true partner?" A loss near 0 means the partner stands out clearly. A falling loss alone does not prove good features, so check for collapse and run a probe too.

    Three things shape the result.

    • Temperature. A low value makes the loss strict: it pushes hardest on the negatives that look most like the positive and ignores easy ones. More discriminating, but more fragile.
    • Number of negatives. More negatives make the task harder and the representation finer, at the cost of memory.
    • Augmentation. The positive pair comes from transforming one input twice (crop an image, take two time windows of a customer). What the transformations leave unchanged is what the model keeps (F6.2).

    The loss assumes everything except the positive is a true negative. In banking that is often false: two accounts of one customer, two complaints about one incident. The loss pushes them apart anyway, and harder the more alike they look. This is the false-negative problem. Fixes: keep known relatives out of each other's negatives, use a higher temperature, or use methods without negatives.

    Without negatives: collapse, and the tricks that avoid it

    Without negatives there is a trivial cheat: output the same vector for every input. Every positive pair is then perfectly close and the loss is zero. This is collapse. Methods avoid it with negatives (SimCLR, CLIP), a slowly updated teacher copy that the student must match (BYOL, DINO), penalties that force dimensions to vary (VICReg), or by reconstructing the data itself (BERT, masked autoencoders), where there is nothing to collapse to.

    Watch for collapse in your own embeddings: the loss can look fine while every customer looks alike. Track the effective rank (how many dimensions are really used) and a linear-probe score during training, not only at the end.

    What it looks like on bank data

    • Transaction sequences. Treat a customer's history as a sentence of tokens, pretrain with masking or next-event prediction, then fine-tune for fraud, churn or credit. Whether this beats a well-built gradient-boosted model (F3.4) is empirical: sometimes, mainly when labels are scarce (foundation models for transaction data).
    • Text and documents. Contrastive embedders power policy search and complaint triage; CLIP-style models search scans by description (trade-finance document AI). An encoder trained mostly on English underperforms on Arabic dialects (Arabic LLMs).

    Three cautions:

    • Leakage hides in pretraining. Pretrain on data up to 2026 and test fraud on 2025, and the representation has already seen 2025 (F3.1).
    • The representation inherits its population. If pretraining is mostly salaried residents, expatriate remitters and new customers are represented worse. Check by segment (G2).
    • Embeddings can leak facts about individuals. Protect them like the source data (G2.4).

    Generative models: one question, four answers

    A generative model learns what the data looks like well enough to draw new samples, like a chef who has tasted thousands of dishes and can invent a new one. Each family bets differently. Language models already do this for tokens; this half covers images, tabular records and continuous data.

    FamilyThe ideaWatch out for
    VAECompress to a smooth code, decode. Sample the code to generate.Blurry, generic samples.
    GANA forger and an inspector improve each other.Unstable; covers only a few kinds of sample.
    DiffusionLearn to remove a little noise; generate by denoising pure noise.Slow sampling. Best for images, audio, video.
    AutoregressivePredict one piece given the previous ones.Slow for long outputs. Powers language models.

    VAEs: compress, regularise, decode

    An ordinary autoencoder squeezes an input into a small code and rebuilds it (F4.4). For generation it fails: codes land wherever they like, with gaps, so a random code decodes to garbage. A variational autoencoder adds a penalty that keeps all the codes in one smooth, bell-shaped region, like forcing every student to sit inside one hall so any random seat is a valid seat. The training balances two pulls: rebuild the input faithfully, and keep codes tidy. Too much tidiness and the codes carry no information; too little and you have a plain autoencoder with gaps.

    VAE samples are blurry because the decoder averages over everything the code could mean: ask for "a face", get the average face. That is a feature for scoring: how badly an input reconstructs says how unusual it is, so VAEs remain a fixture of anomaly detection (F4.4).

    GANs: the forger and the inspector

    A counterfeiter and a bank inspector. The generator turns noise into a candidate sample; the discriminator tries to tell real from generated. Each forces the other to improve.

    In practice training is touchy. The generator may find a few outputs that fool the inspector and produce only those (mode collapse): a fraud-data GAN producing ten kinds of fraud when there are two hundred. There is also no likelihood, so a loss curve cannot tell you if it works.

    GANs matter to a bank mainly because they underlie the deepfakes that attack eKYC and voice authentication (A3.5), and tabular variants (CTGAN) were early synthetic-data generators for spreadsheets.

    Diffusion: learn to remove noise, then run it backwards

    Destroying structure is easy; rebuilding it in small steps is learnable. Add a little random noise to an image again and again until only static remains. Then train one network to undo one small step at every noise level. Start from pure noise, apply it repeatedly, and a plausible sample emerges. It is like restoring a smudged painting one stroke at a time.

    Training is simple: take a clean example, add a random amount of noise, and have the network guess the noise that was added. In the simulation below the denoiser is not learned: the "customers" come from a known mixture of three groups, so the ideal denoiser can be written down directly.

    Diffusion is now the engine of modern image generators, thanks to three developments.

    • Latent diffusion. Run it in an autoencoder's compressed space, not on pixels. Far cheaper: Stable Diffusion.
    • Conditioning. Feed a prompt or class label, with a dial that trades variety for fidelity.
    • Transformer denoisers and flow matching. Transformers (F5.3) let it scale like language models, and flow matching needs fewer steps (see also diffusion language models).

    Using generated data in a bank: fidelity, utility, privacy

    The most direct banking use is synthetic data: records that look statistically like customers but are none. Uses: realistic test environments, sharing where real rows cannot move, and augmenting a rare class (weak for strong models, and it damages calibration: F3.5). Judge it on three axes and demand all three:

    AxisQuestionTypical check
    FidelityDoes it look like the real data?A classifier that cannot tell real from synthetic (near 50% is good).
    UtilityDoes it support the task?"Train on synthetic, test on real": AUC close to a model trained on real data.
    PrivacyDoes it leak real records?Distance of each synthetic row to its nearest real row; membership-inference attacks.

    The axes conflict. A generator that copies training rows has perfect fidelity and utility and no privacy; one that adds heavy noise is private and useless. Models memorise rare examples, and outliers are the customers a bank must protect most (R11). "Synthetic" is not a legal synonym for "anonymous": take it to privacy and legal teams and keep the test evidence.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Pretraining that sees the test period

      Embeddings pretrained on all history, then "tested" on a month they have already seen.

      Spot itPretraining must end before the evaluation period starts.
    • Collapse that the loss hides

      The loss falls while every customer maps to nearly the same vector.

      Spot itCheck effective rank and a probe at every checkpoint.
    • False negatives repelling real neighbours

      Same-customer accounts or duplicate complaints are pushed apart.

      Spot itAsk whether known relatives can be negatives in a batch. Eyeball nearest neighbours.
    • "Pretrained, therefore good" with no label-efficiency curve

      Success on the full labelled set says nothing about pretraining's benefit.

      Spot itAsk for accuracy against number of labels, with and without pretraining, versus a tuned gradient-boosted baseline.
    • Generators judged by eye

      Ten synthetic records look fine, but only common customers are covered (blurry or mode-collapsed output).

      Spot itCompare rare segments and tails against real data; run train-on-synthetic, test-on-real.
    • Synthetic data that memorises

      Some synthetic rows are near copies of real ones, so releasing them releases customers.

      Spot itCheck nearest-real-row distances; run a membership-inference test.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Why does restoring a masked transaction history help fraud detection when the model never saw a fraud label?

    To restore it the model learns normal behaviour: habits, timing, typical amounts. Fraud is a departure from that, so the labels only need to teach which departures matter.

    2. In the "spot the twin" loss, what would a loss near zero tell you, and what would a loss that never moves from its starting value tell you?

    Near zero means the true partner stands out from every negative. A loss stuck at its starting value means every candidate looks equally likely, so nothing useful has been learned.

    3. You lower the temperature. How does the loss treat easy and hard negatives?

    A lower temperature makes the loss stricter. Hard negatives take an even larger share of the push; easy ones are ignored. The embedding gets finer but more sensitive to false negatives and noisy pairs.

    4. What is collapse, and name two ways methods prevent it.

    Collapse is the same embedding for every input, which makes any positive-pair loss zero. Prevented by negatives, a teacher-student setup, variance penalties, or reconstruction targets.

    5. In a diffusion model, what does the network learn to do at each step, and why is generating in many small steps easier than in one jump?

    It learns to remove a small amount of noise. Each step is a small, easy correction; going from static to a finished sample in one jump means getting everything right at once.

    6. Why are VAE samples blurry, and why does that help anomaly detection?

    The decoder outputs a smoothed, typical version. Inputs that depart from typical reconstruct badly, and that error is the anomaly score.

    7. A synthetic table passes every distribution test, and a model trained on it scores AUC 0.91 on real hold-out data against 0.92 for one trained on real data. Safe to release outside the bank?

    Not yet. Fidelity and utility are shown; privacy is not. Check nearest-real-record distances and membership inference, then get privacy and legal approval.

    8. In Simulation 1, why does unlabelled data stop helping when the groups are close?

    Unlabelled data reveals clusters only if there is a gap. When groups overlap, clustering splits the data in the wrong place. Unlabelled structure helps only if it lines up with the label you care about.
