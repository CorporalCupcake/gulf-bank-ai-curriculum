    Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning

    Part 1

    What self-supervised learning is

    In one sentenceSelf-supervised learning hides part of the data and trains a model to fill it back in, so the data supplies its own answers and nobody has to label anything.

    Take a phone with a million photos, of which only 200 are labelled "cat" or "dog". Data is cheap. Labels are expensive, because a person has to write each one.

    Why it teaches anything: to guess the missing word, the model has to know that cats sit on mats, not on theorems. Hide a patch of a photo and it has to learn what fur and ears look like. That knowledge is called a representation: a useful summary of the input as a list of numbers. It is what you keep.

    How it differs from other learning

    • Supervised learning needs a human label for every example (this photo is a cat). Accurate, but labels limit how much data you can use.
    • Unsupervised learning (such as clustering) finds groups with no answers at all. Self-supervised learning looks similar, but it sets itself a quiz with a known answer, so it can use the same training machinery as supervised learning.
    • Transfer learning reuses a model trained elsewhere. Self-supervised learning is how that first model is often built.
    Part 2

    How it works

    The recipe has two stages. First you pretrain on a pile of unlabelled data. Then you adapt the result to your real task with a few labels.

    1. Choose a quiz (a "pretext task") that the data can grade itself.
    2. Pretrain the model on all the unlabelled data. This is the expensive part.
    3. Keep the representation, the numbers the model builds inside.
    4. Adapt: freeze it and train a small model on top, or fine-tune everything gently with a small learning rate.

    Three kinds of quiz

    • Predict the next item. "The cat sat on the ..." This is how GPT-style language models learn. It also fits a customer's next purchase.
    • Fill in a hidden part. Mask a word or an image patch and recover it (BERT, masked autoencoders).
    • Tell views apart. Decide which inputs belong together (SimCLR, CLIP). This is contrastive learning, covered next.
    Part 3

    Contrastive learning: spot the twin

    In one sentenceContrastive learning trains a model so that two views of the same thing land close together in embedding space and different things land far apart.

    Each training step uses three roles. The anchor is one photo, say a cat. The positive is another view of it, such as a different crop. The negatives are other photos in the batch. Think of it as a "spot the twin" quiz.

    The loss (called InfoNCE) is the quiz score: out of all candidates, which one is the anchor's true partner? A loss near zero means the partner clearly stands out.

    The three settings that matter

    • Temperature sets how strict the quiz is. A low value focuses the push on the hardest negatives. That gives finer embeddings, but it is more fragile.
    • Number of negatives. More negatives make the quiz harder and the representation finer, but cost memory. This is why SimCLR needs huge batches.
    • Augmentation builds the positive pair by changing one input twice (crop, recolour). The model keeps what the changes leave alone, so pick changes that preserve what your task cares about.

    How to read it

    • A falling loss is not proof of good features. Always train a quick linear probe (a simple model on frozen embeddings) and look at its accuracy.
    • Check for collapse. The model can cheat by giving every input the same vector, so every pair is perfectly "close". The loss can look fine while every input looks alike. Watch the probe score, and the effective rank (how many dimensions the embeddings really use). A falling rank is the early warning.
    • False negatives. Two photos of the same cat in a big batch are pushed apart as if they were different. Keep known relatives out of each other's negatives, or raise the temperature.

    Methods avoid collapse in four ways. Negatives (SimCLR, CLIP) force different inputs apart. A slowly updated teacher copy that the student must match (BYOL, DINO) breaks the trivial answer. Variance penalties (VICReg) force every dimension to vary. Reconstruction (BERT, masked autoencoders) has nothing to collapse to.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ApproachNeedsBest when
    Plain supervisedMany labelsLabels are plentiful and cheap
    Predict or mask (GPT, BERT)Lots of raw dataText, event sequences, anything with an order or parts to hide
    Contrastive (SimCLR, CLIP)Raw data plus a way to make positive pairsYou want embeddings for search, matching or clustering
    No-negative methods (BYOL, DINO)Careful design against collapseBig batches are not affordable
    • Strong at: making use of unlabelled data, and doing well when labels are scarce.
    • Weak at: the cost of pretraining, the work of designing augmentations, and loss curves that do not show quality.

    When anyone claims a win, ask for accuracy against number of labels, with and without pretraining. The gain is largest when labels are few and shrinks as they grow.

    In banking, a customer's history can be treated as a sentence of events and pretrained by masking, then fine-tuned for fraud or churn, and contrastive embedders power policy search and complaint triage.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting the loss alone

      Contrastive loss can fall while the embeddings collapse. Run a linear probe during training.

    • Wrong augmentations

      If a crop removes the very thing your task depends on, the model learns to ignore it. Choose changes that keep what matters.

    • Ignoring false negatives

      Photos of the same thing treated as different push good embeddings apart. Exclude known relatives from the negatives.

    • Leaking the test period

      Pretraining on data that includes the test period quietly inflates results. Split by time first.

    1. Why does hiding a word teach a model anything useful?

    To guess it, the model must learn how the words around it behave, which means learning how language works.

    2. What is a representation?

    A useful summary of the input as a list of numbers. It is the part of the pretrained model you keep and reuse.

    3. In contrastive learning, what are the anchor, positive and negatives?

    The anchor is one input, the positive is another view of it, and the negatives are other inputs. Anchor and positive are pulled together, negatives pushed away.

    4. What is collapse, and how do you spot it?

    The model outputs nearly the same vector for every input. The loss may look fine, so watch the linear-probe score and the effective rank of the embeddings.

    5. You have 500 labels and 2 million unlabelled records. What is the sensible plan?

    Pretrain on the unlabelled records, freeze the representation, and train a small model on the 500 labels. Freezing is safer when labels are few.
