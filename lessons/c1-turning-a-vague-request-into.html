    C1.1 · C1.2 Day one ★ About 40 minutes

    From Vague Request to Decision Problem

    "Can we predict churn?" is not yet a data science task. Find the decision that will change and who owns it, then fix the target, the horizon and the unit, the three small choices that decide whether the model is ever used.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is worth only what the decision it changes is worth, so write down who will do what differently on seeing a score, then choose the target, the horizon and the unit that make that action possible and measurable.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A manager says: "We're losing customers. Can AI predict who will leave?" You build a model, get a strong AUC, and nobody uses it. The request named a prediction. The business needs a decision. It's like a weather forecast: it is only worth something if someone decides to carry an umbrella.

    Between prediction and decision sit questions nobody asked. Who acts on the score, and how? What does the action cost and save? How far ahead must the warning come? Is the thing scored a customer, an account or a household?

    Turn every request into one sentence: "On [day/event], [person/system] decides whether to [action] for each [unit], using a score for [outcome] within [horizon]; we will know it worked if [measure]." A blank you cannot fill is the next question to ask.

    Try it: "Can AI predict which customers will default?" What is missing?

    Who decides, and about what? The same score could set a credit limit, trigger an early-warning call at month 4, prioritise collectors, or feed IFRS 9 provisioning. Each has its own horizon (12 months, 90 days, 30 days, lifetime), unit (applicant, account, segment) and cost of error. "Default" also needs a definition, such as 90 days past due. Four projects hide in one sentence.

    Try it: "We want an AI to catch money laundering." What is missing?

    The decision: rank alerts for investigators, replace a rule, or pick customers for enhanced due diligence? The unit could be a transaction, customer or network. The target is unclear too: a filed suspicious-activity report reflects what investigators chose to examine, not what is criminal. Team capacity (cases per day) sets the useful operating point.

    Try it: "Use GenAI to improve the call centre." What is missing?

    Which decision, and whose? Routing a call, drafting a reply for an agent, summarising after the call, or answering customers directly are different problems with different risks. "Improve" needs a measure: handling time, first-contact resolution, complaints or compliance breaches. Without one you cannot tell a pilot from a demo.

    A score is worth what the action attached to it is worth. A smoke alarm is useless if no one will run for the exit. The value depends on the offer cost, how often the action works, and what a saved customer is worth. Two teams with the same model can get opposite results. Three consequences:

    • Prediction is not persuasion. Someone likely to leave may not be talkable out of it. You need a measured "kept" rate, usually from an experiment (F3.6, F9.3).
    • Metrics come last. Once you know the action, its cost and the team's capacity, the metric picks itself: precision at the number of cases the team can handle, or expected net value per customer.
    • Sometimes the answer is no model. If economics are negative at every cut-off, say so in week one. C1.4 covers this.
    Hold onto thisStart with "what will somebody do differently, and what is that worth?", not "what can I predict?". Target, horizon and unit follow.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Turning a request into a decision problem (C1.1)

    Replace an adjective ("better", "smarter", "proactive") with a decision that has an owner, an action, a cost and a measure. Ask these in order, ideally with the requester present, and write the answers down (half a page).

    QuestionWhat a good answer sounds likeWhat a bad answer sounds like
    1. What decision changes?"Which of 4,000 weekly overdue accounts collections calls first.""We want more insight into customers."
    2. Who makes it, and when?"The collections team lead, at 8am each Sunday, from a ranked list.""The business."
    3. What are the options, and what does each cost?"Call (AED 25), SMS (AED 0.2), do nothing. Calls capped at 600 a week.""Reach out in some way."
    4. What happens today without the model?"Oldest balance first. 31% of calls reach a payment within a week.""Nothing, so anything is an improvement." (see C1.3)
    5. What would a win be, in AED or customers?"Raise payments per 100 calls from 31 to 38 with no more complaints.""Higher AUC."
    6. What is the data at decision time?"Balance, history, contact attempts as at Saturday night.""All of our data" (leakage waiting to happen, F3.1).
    7. What constraints bind?"Needs to be explainable to a customer; no use of nationality; model risk sign-off."Not asked until the end.

    Questions 1 to 3 define the decision. Question 4 gives the baseline. Question 5 gives the value, needed for budget and for deciding when to stop. Question 6 guards against leakage. Question 7 prevents late surprises from risk and compliance. If a CBUAE or SAMA rule or internal model-risk policy may apply, ask the owner early; don't assume the specifics.

    Two decision shapes recur:

    • Ranking under capacity. Analysts can review 300 alerts a day, so the decision is "which 300?". Value sits in the top of the list; headcount sets the threshold.
    • Act on each case. Approve or decline, block or allow, price. Costs are asymmetric and set the threshold: act when P(bad outcome) × loss avoided exceeds the cost of acting (cost-ratio logic, F3.5 and F7).

    Two traps. Solutionism: the request arrives pre-solved ("build a deep learning model for X"); reach back to the decision it serves. Proxy decision: people ask for what they can imagine measuring (a churn score) rather than what they need (an effective retention programme). Sometimes the better question is who responds to the offer (uplift, F9.4), not who leaves.

    Choosing the target, horizon and unit (C1.2)

    Three choices turn a decision into a supervised-learning table. Each looks like a detail and each can silently make the model useless.

    The target is the column the model predicts. Check three things:

    • Definition. "Default" at 30, 60 or 90 days past due gives different base rates, lead times and regulatory meaning.
    • What the label records. A suspicious-activity report is an investigator's decision, not a fact about the world, and alert-driven labels exist only where someone looked (F2.6).
    • Decision-aligned alternative. Sometimes predict an amount (expected loss, F4, B4) or the effect of the action, not a yes/no.

    The horizon is how far ahead the label looks. Think of ordering a taxi for a flight: a 10-minute window is useless if the taxi takes 30 minutes to arrive. It interacts with the lead time the action needs and the label delay before you know the answer.

    P(T≤h)=1−(1−p12)h/12

    If a share p12 of customers leave within 12 months at a steady rate, the share who leave within h months is this. With p12 = 12%, leaving within 3 months is 3.1%, within 6 months 6.2%. A longer horizon gives more positives (easier to learn from) but each positive is less imminent, and a customer who leaves in the first L months, before your action can take effect, cannot be saved at all. Simulation 2 shows both sides.

    Rule of thumb: horizon ≥ lead time, and as short as the action's effect allows. Then check you can live with the label delay. If a label needs 12 months and the business changes quarterly, use a shorter proxy label and test that it tracks the real one. Also fix the snapshot date: features computed as of the decision day, outcome window starting after it (F3.3).

    The unit of decision is the thing that gets one score and one action: transaction, card, account, customer, household, RM portfolio, device, merchant, counterparty network. It decides:

    • What one alert is. Fraud teams want one case per stolen card, not eight alerts.
    • What the headline metric means. A model catching 60% of fraudulent transactions may catch 94% of fraudulent cards, since fraudsters try several times.
    • How to split the data. If the decision is per customer, one customer's rows must not sit on both sides of a train-test split (F3.1).
    • Whether the action is possible. You can block a card or call a customer, not a transaction.
    • How labels aggregate. Is a customer "bad" if any account defaulted, or the latest? Joint accounts are another problem again.

    The unit also sets the grain of the table (card-day, customer-month, transaction), which must match the decision grain. Scoring monthly when the action is daily means stale information. If the grain is finer than the decision, aggregate so one entity gets one action.

    Last check: write the scoring-time story. On the day the model is used, what is known, what is the question, and what happens within the horizon? If it needs information from after the decision day, the framing leaks. If it needs an action nobody can take in the lead time, it is useless. If nobody receives the score, it is orphaned.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A model with no owner

      A score goes to "the business" and no process changes.

      Spot itAsk for a name and a calendar slot: who looks at the output, and when?
    • Predicting what is easy to label, not what matters

      The data holds "account closed", so the model predicts closure, though the bank cares about losing the relationship, which may continue on another product.

      Spot itWrite the target definition and ask if the decision-maker would call it "the thing we care about".
    • Horizon shorter than the lead time

      The score fires two weeks before leaving; the offer takes four weeks to produce.

      Spot itSubtract the lead time from the horizon. If nothing is left, widen the horizon or speed the action.
    • Horizon chosen by label convenience

      "Defaults within 90 days" because it matures quickly, while the business needs a 12-month view.

      Spot itAsk which horizon the decision-maker would pick with no data constraints, and how the proxy relates to it.
    • Wrong unit, so double counting

      Transaction-level metrics presented as customer outcomes; eight alerts for one stolen card.

      Spot itAsk what one row and one alert each represent. If they differ, define the mapping.
    • Customer split across train and test

      The model learns people, not patterns.

      Spot itCheck for overlap of the unit's ID across splits.
    • No baseline or value, only an AUC

      "We reached 0.83" with no statement of worth or of what happens without it.

      Spot itAsk for the AED value per 1,000 customers against the current process.
    • Prediction treated as persuasion

      Contacting the customers most likely to leave, many of whom cannot be kept or would have stayed.

      Spot itAsk whether a randomised holdout measures the action's effect.
    • Using the first answer as the final spec

      The first request reflects what they can imagine, not what they need.

      Spot itCount how often you've asked "and then what happens?". Fewer than three, keep going.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Rewrite "Use AI to reduce fraud" as a decision sentence. List the blanks you cannot fill yet.

    For example: "Every day at midnight, fraud operations decides which cards to review, for each card with transactions that day, using a score for stolen-card fraud within 24 hours; it worked if confirmed fraud losses per 100,000 cards fall without more than a set rise in customer contacts." Unknowns: capacity, action cost, baseline loss rate.

    2. An offer costs AED 100, keeps 20% of the would-be leavers contacted, and a kept customer is worth AED 1,500. A group has a 15% chance of leaving. Is contacting them worth it?

    0.15 × 0.20 × 1,500 − 100 = 45 − 100 = −AED 55. No. Break-even P(leave) = 100 ÷ (0.2 × 1,500) = 33%. So the cut-off is about 33%, not "anyone likely to leave" (simple model of Simulation 1).

    3. Using the formula, what share of customers leave within 6 months if 12% leave within 12 months at a steady rate?

    1 − 0.880.5 = 1 − 0.938 = about 6.2%, not 6%: leavers are removed from the base as time passes.

    4. Your retention offer takes 6 weeks to prepare and send. Your model predicts "leaves within 30 days". What is wrong?

    The horizon is shorter than the lead time: anyone leaving within 30 days is gone before the offer arrives. Speed up the action, lengthen the horizon to 90 days or more, or use the model for a faster action such as an agent prompt on an inbound call.

    5. A model flags 60% of fraudulent transactions. Each stolen card has 3 fraudulent attempts. What share of stolen cards is touched, under independence? What if you were told only the transaction figure?

    1 − 0.43 = 93.6%. Seeing only "60% recall" underestimates the card-level catch; comparing a card-level number with a transaction-level one overstates improvement. Always state the unit with the metric. Real flags are correlated, so the true card-level figure is lower.

    6. List three consequences of choosing "customer" over "transaction" as the unit.

    One alert per customer, not many; the split keeps each customer on one side; labels need an aggregation rule; the action (call, block, review) becomes possible; features aggregate to the decision day; metrics count customers caught, not transactions.

    7. When is "no model" the right conclusion of the framing exercise?

    When net value is negative at every cut-off (Simulation 1 at high offer cost), nobody owns the decision, a simple rule or process fix gets most of the benefit (C1.4), or the lead time exceeds any useful horizon. Reporting that early is a good result.
