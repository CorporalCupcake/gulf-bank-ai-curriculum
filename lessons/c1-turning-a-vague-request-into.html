    C1.1 · C1.2 Day one ★ About 40 minutes

    From Vague Request to Decision Problem

    "Can we predict churn?" is not yet a data science task. Find the decision that will change and who owns it, then fix the target, the horizon and the unit, the three small choices that decide whether the model is ever used.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is worth only what the decision it changes is worth, so write down who will do what differently on seeing a score, then choose the target, the horizon and the unit that make that action possible and measurable.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    In your first month a senior manager will say something like: "We're losing too many customers. Can AI predict who is going to leave?" The tempting reply is "yes" followed by three weeks of modelling. A strong AUC arrives and nobody uses it.

    What went wrong is not the model. The request named a prediction. The business needs a decision. Between the two sit questions nobody asked: who will act on the score, and what exactly will they do? What does it cost, and what does it save? How far ahead must the warning come for the action to work? Is the thing being scored a customer, an account or a household?

    A useful habit is to turn every request into one sentence of this shape: "On [day/event], [person/system] decides whether to [action] for each [unit], using a score for [outcome] within [horizon]; we will know it worked if [measure]." If you cannot fill in a blank, you have found the next question to ask, and you have found it before spending weeks on the wrong thing.

    Try it: "Can AI predict which customers will default?" What is missing?

    Who decides, and about what? The same score could set a credit limit at origination, trigger an early-warning call at month 4, prioritise collectors, or feed IFRS 9 provisioning. Each has a different horizon (12 months, 90 days, 30 days, lifetime), a different unit (applicant, account, portfolio segment) and a different cost of being wrong. "Default" itself needs a definition: 90 days past due, or something earlier. Four different projects hide in one sentence.

    Try it: "We want an AI to catch money laundering." What is missing?

    The decision. Is the model supposed to rank alerts for investigators, replace a rule, or decide which customers get enhanced due diligence? The unit could be a transaction, a customer, or a network of accounts. The target is also unclear: what the bank can observe is a suspicious-activity report filed, which reflects what investigators chose to examine, not what is criminal. And the capacity of the team (how many cases per day) sets the useful operating point.

    Try it: "Use GenAI to improve the call centre." What is missing?

    Which decision, and whose? Routing a call, drafting a reply for an agent to edit, summarising after the call, or answering customers directly are different problems with different risks. "Improve" needs a measure: handling time, first-contact resolution, complaints, or compliance breaches. Without one, you cannot tell a good pilot from a demo.

    The point of that simulation is not churn. It is that the value of a score is set by the action attached to it: the offer cost, how often the action works, and what a saved customer is worth. Two teams with the same model can get opposite results. Three more observations:

    • Prediction is not persuasion. A customer likely to leave is not necessarily a customer who can be talked out of leaving. The model above has to be paired with a "kept" rate that someone has measured, usually by experiment (F3.6, F9.3).
    • Accuracy metrics come last. Once you know the action, its costs and the team's capacity, the metric almost chooses itself: precision at the number of cases the team can handle, or expected net value per customer.
    • Sometimes the right answer is no model. If the economics are negative for every cut-off, saying so in week one is a good outcome. C1.4 covers that decision.
    Hold onto thisDo not start with "what can I predict?". Start with "what will somebody do differently, and what is that worth?". The target, horizon and unit all follow from that answer.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Turning a request into a decision problem (C1.1)

    The aim is to replace an adjective ("better", "smarter", "proactive") with a decision that has an owner, an action, a cost and a measure. Work through these questions in order, ideally with the person who made the request in the room. Write the answers down; a half-page is enough.

    QuestionWhat a good answer sounds likeWhat a bad answer sounds like
    1. What decision changes?"Which of 4,000 weekly overdue accounts collections calls first.""We want more insight into customers."
    2. Who makes it, and when?"The collections team lead, at 8am each Sunday, from a ranked list.""The business."
    3. What are the options, and what does each cost?"Call (AED 25), SMS (AED 0.2), do nothing. Calls capped at 600 a week.""Reach out in some way."
    4. What happens today without the model?"Oldest balance first. 31% of calls reach a payment within a week.""Nothing, so anything is an improvement." (see C1.3)
    5. What would a win be, in AED or customers?"Raise payments per 100 calls from 31 to 38 with no more complaints.""Higher AUC."
    6. What is the data at decision time?"Balance, history, contact attempts as at Saturday night.""All of our data" (leakage waiting to happen, F3.1).
    7. What constraints bind?"Needs to be explainable to a customer; no use of nationality; model risk sign-off."Not asked until the end.

    Questions 1 to 3 define the decision. Question 4 defines the baseline. Question 5 defines value, which you will need when asking for budget and when judging whether to stop. Question 6 protects the model from leakage. Question 7 stops late surprises from risk and compliance. Where a CBUAE or SAMA rule, or an internal model-risk policy, is likely to apply, ask the owner early; do not assume the specifics.

    Two kinds of decision recur and have different shapes:

    • Ranking under capacity. Analysts can review 300 alerts a day. The decision is "which 300?". Value comes from the quality of the top of the list, and the threshold is set by headcount.
    • Accept or act on each case. Approve or decline, block or allow, price. Here costs are asymmetric and the threshold comes from them. In expected-value terms, act when the probability of the bad outcome times the loss avoided exceeds the cost of acting. This is the cost-ratio logic from F3.5 and F7.

    Two traps appear at this stage. The first is solutionism: the request arrives pre-solved ("build a deep learning model for X") and you must reach back to the decision it serves. The second is the proxy decision: people ask for what they can imagine measuring (a churn score) rather than what they need (an effective retention programme). A good framing sometimes changes the question: the best use of the data might be to find who responds to the offer (uplift, F9.4) rather than who leaves.

    Choosing the target, horizon and unit (C1.2)

    Three choices turn a decision into a supervised-learning table. Each one looks like a detail. Each can silently make the model useless.

    The target is the column the model predicts. Think about three things. First, definition: "default" at 30, 60 or 90 days past due give different base rates, different lead times and different regulatory meaning. Second, what the label really records: a suspicious-activity report is a decision by an investigator, not a fact about the world, and an alert-driven label only exists where someone looked (F2.6). Third, the decision-aligned alternative: sometimes you should predict an amount (expected loss, F4, B4) or the effect of the action, not a yes/no event.

    The horizon is how far ahead the label looks, and it interacts with two other times: the lead time the action needs, and the label delay before you know the answer.

    P(T≤h)=1−(1−p12)h/12

    If a share p12 of customers leave within 12 months at a steady rate, the share who leave within h months is this. With p12 = 12%, leaving within 3 months is 3.1%, within 6 months 6.2%. A longer horizon gives more positives (easier to learn from) but each positive is less imminent, and a customer who leaves in the first L months, before your action can take effect, cannot be saved at all. Simulation 2 shows both sides.

    The rule of thumb that falls out: horizon ≥ lead time, and as short as the action's effect allows. Then check you can live with the label delay. If the label needs 12 months to mature and the business changes every quarter, consider a shorter proxy label, and test that it relates to the real one. A related trap is the snapshot date: features must be computed as of the decision day, with the outcome window starting after it (F3.3).

    The unit of decision is the thing that gets one score and one action. Candidates in a bank: transaction, card, account, customer, household or relationship manager portfolio, device, merchant, counterparty network. The unit decides:

    • What one alert is. A fraud team does not want eight alerts for one stolen card; it wants one case.
    • What the headline metric means. A model that finds 60% of fraudulent transactions may catch 94% of fraudulent cards, because a fraudster makes several attempts.
    • How to split the data. If the decision is per customer, then rows from one customer must not appear on both sides of a train-test split (F3.1).
    • Whether the action is possible. You can block a card or call a customer, but you cannot call a transaction.
    • How labels aggregate. A customer is "bad" if any account defaulted? The latest? A household in a joint account is a different problem again.

    Choosing the unit also means choosing the grain of the table: one row per card-day, customer-month, or transaction. The grain must match the decision grain. If you score monthly but the action happens daily, you are forecasting with stale information. If the grain is finer than the decision, aggregate so one entity gets one action.

    A last check on all three choices together is to write the scoring-time story: on the day the model is used, what is known, what is the question, and what happens within the horizon? If the story requires information from after the decision day, the framing is leaky; if it requires an action that nobody can take within the lead time, it is useless; if nobody can say who receives the score, it is orphaned.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Framing sits in front of nearly everything else in the curriculum. Mistakes made here cannot be repaired by better modelling later.

    C1.3 · BaselinesThe comparison every result needs

    The framing question "what happens today?" becomes the baseline you must beat. Without it, no number is good or bad.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeSnapshot dates and the decision day

    A clear decision moment is what makes point-in-time features possible. Vague framing is the main source of leakage.

    F3.5 · F7.1 – F7.4 · ThresholdsCosts set the cut-off

    Once the action and its costs are known, the threshold and metric follow. See also precision, recall and calibration.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisWhen the horizon is not fixed

    If timing matters, "leaves within 3 months" throws away information. Time-to-event models keep it.

    F9.3 – F9.4 · Causal inference and upliftPrediction is not persuasion

    When the decision is whether to intervene, the right target is the effect of the intervention, not the outcome alone.

    F3.6 · ExperimentsProving the decision improved

    The measure you wrote in question 5 is what the experiment tests. Define it before launch.

    F4 · F6 · Models and lossesLoss should reflect cost

    If errors cost different amounts, the training loss and evaluation should say so, not just default to accuracy.

    C2 · C3 · Communication and scopingSay it in one decision sentence

    The same sentence structure reappears in the project scope and the executive summary.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card and payment fraud (B1). Unit (transaction, card, device), horizon (real-time versus end-of-day), and the lead time before a card is blocked. Labels arrive late: B1.3.
    • Churn and retention (B6). Definition of "churned" for a customer with several products, a horizon matched to the retention team's lead time, and the question of whether to model risk or response.
    • Credit and provisioning (B4). Default definitions, 12-month versus lifetime horizons, and the fact that the same loan sits at the grain of both origination and monitoring decisions.
    • AML and KYC. Alert versus case versus customer, and a target that is an investigator's decision. Capacity sets the operating point.
    • Islamic finance products. Contract structures (murabaha, ijara) change what "default" and "early settlement" mean and what can be done about them. Frame with the product owner and Sharia governance in mind, and do not assume conventional definitions carry over.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Framing is a craft more than a research area, so the "frontier" is partly about evidence on why projects fail and partly about methods that build the decision into the model.

    Checked on 6 November 2026. One search was made (the RAND report); the other items are from memory of well-known work and should be verified before you quote them in a formal document.

    1. 1999
      CRISP-DM puts "business understanding" first

      The most widely used data mining process starts with business objectives and success criteria, then data. The idea of this lesson is more than twenty-five years old and still routinely skipped.

      Chapman et al., CRISP-DM 1.0, 1999
    2. 2012
      Uplift modelling formalised

      Methods that predict the difference an action makes for each customer, so that offers go to the persuadable rather than the likely-to-leave. Frames the decision directly.

      Rzepakowski & Jaroszewicz, Knowledge and Information Systems, 2012
    3. 2015
      Machine learning is a small part of the system

      Google engineers described the hidden costs of ML systems: boundary erosion, undeclared consumers of a model's output, and feedback loops. Unclear ownership of outputs is a framing failure.

      Sculley et al., NeurIPS 2015
    4. 2022
      "Smart predict, then optimise"

      Train predictive models using the loss of the downstream decision, not squared error on the prediction. A research thread (decision-focused learning) that makes the lesson's central claim precise: judge predictions by the decisions they lead to.

      Elmachtoub & Grigas, Management Science, 2022
    5. 2024
      RAND: miscommunicated problems lead the list of failure causes

      Interviews with 65 experienced data scientists and engineers found that stakeholders often misunderstand or miscommunicate what problem AI is meant to solve, alongside missing data, chasing new technology over real problems, weak infrastructure, and over-hard problems. RAND reports that by some estimates over 80% of AI projects fail, about twice the rate of non-AI IT projects. It is an interview study, not a measured failure rate.

      Ryseff, De Bruhl, Newberry, RAND RRA2680-1, August 2024

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These happen in real projects, and in AI-generated plans. Each has a quick check.

    • A model with no owner

      A score is delivered to "the business" and nobody's process changes.

      Spot itAsk for a name and a calendar slot: who looks at the output, and when?
    • Predicting what is easy to label, not what matters

      The data holds "account closed", so the model predicts closure, even though the bank cares about the loss of the customer's relationship, which may continue on another product.

      Spot itWrite the target definition and ask whether the decision-maker would call it "the thing we care about".
    • Horizon shorter than the lead time

      The score fires two weeks before leaving; the offer takes four weeks to produce.

      Spot itSubtract the action's lead time from the horizon. If nothing is left, widen the horizon or speed the action.
    • Horizon chosen by label convenience

      "Defaults within 90 days" because it matures quickly, while the business needs a 12-month view.

      Spot itAsk which horizon the decision-maker would choose with no data constraints, and how the proxy relates to it.
    • Wrong unit, so double counting

      Transaction-level metrics presented as customer outcomes; eight alerts for one stolen card.

      Spot itAsk "what does one row represent, and what does one alert represent?" If the answers differ, define the mapping.
    • Customer split across train and test

      The same customer appears on both sides; the model learns people, not patterns.

      Spot itCheck for overlap of the unit's ID across splits.
    • No baseline or value, only an AUC

      "We reached 0.83" with no statement of what it is worth or what happens without it.

      Spot itAsk for the AED value per 1,000 customers against the current process.
    • Prediction treated as persuasion

      Contact the customers most likely to leave, many of whom cannot be kept or would have stayed.

      Spot itAsk whether a randomised holdout exists to measure the action's effect.
    • Using the stakeholder's first answer as the final spec

      The first request reflects what they can imagine, not what they need.

      Spot itCount the number of times you have asked "and then what happens?". If less than three, keep going.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a business owner who asked for "a churn model" · 30 seconds
    “Happy to build it. Before I start I'd like to agree three things, so what I deliver is something your team can use. Who will receive the list and what will they do with it? How far ahead do they need the warning for that to work? And what would you count as a win, in customers kept or dirhams? With those, I can tell you whether a model is worth building and what it needs to achieve, and we can test it against what you do today.”
    To a technical lead or validator
    “I scope from the decision backwards. The unit is the entity that receives one action; the horizon is at least the action's lead time; the target is defined as of the decision date with a stated label delay. I record the baseline, the costs of each action and the capacity, and I choose the metric from those, for example precision at the top N or expected net value. I split by unit and by time, and I agree the success measure and the experiment design before the build.”
    “We just want to explore what AI can do with our data.”

    That is a legitimate goal if time-boxed and labelled as discovery. I'd propose a two-week exploration with a short list of candidate decisions to check against, and criteria for moving one into a real project: an owner, an action, a measurable value. Otherwise exploration tends to produce charts and no change.

    “Why are you asking so many questions? Just build it.”

    Because the three most common ways of wasting a quarter, a model nobody uses, a horizon that is too short to act on, and a metric that does not reflect value, are all decided in the first conversation. Twenty minutes now is cheaper than rebuilding later. I'll keep it to the questions that change what I build.

    “Which is better, predicting default within 12 months or 90 days?”

    It depends on the decision. For provisioning, the 12-month view matches the accounting horizon. For an early-warning call, 90 days may be what the team can act on, and it matures faster. If both are needed, they can be separate models or a survival model. The deciding factors are lead time, label delay and the number of positives available.

    “Our model has an AUC of 0.85. Is that good?”

    I can't say without the decision. AUC ignores base rate, costs and capacity. I'd want to know what the team does with the score, what happens today, and what precision or net value we get at the volume they can handle. A model with AUC 0.85 can lose money; one with 0.75 can be profitable if the action is cheap.

    “Can't we use an LLM to do all this instead?”

    For unstructured inputs, such as complaints or documents, possibly. The framing questions are the same: who acts, on what, by when, and how will we know it worked? An LLM does not remove the need for a baseline or an evaluation set, and its cost per decision and error modes need to be part of the value estimate.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Rewrite "Use AI to reduce fraud" as a decision sentence. List the blanks you cannot fill yet.

    For example: "Every day at midnight, the fraud operations team decides which cards to review for each card with transactions that day, using a score for stolen-card fraud within 24 hours; we will know it worked if confirmed fraud losses per 100,000 cards fall without more than a set rise in customer contacts." Unknowns typically include the capacity, the action's cost, and the baseline loss rate.

    2. An offer costs AED 100, keeps 20% of the would-be leavers contacted, and a kept customer is worth AED 1,500. A group of customers has a 15% chance of leaving. Is contacting them worth it?

    Expected value per contacted customer = 0.15 × 0.20 × 1,500 − 100 = 45 − 100 = −AED 55. No. Break-even needs P(leave) = 100 ÷ (0.2 × 1,500) = 33%. So the cut-off is about 33%, not "anyone likely to leave". (This uses the simple model in Simulation 1.)

    3. Using the formula, what share of customers leave within 6 months if 12% leave within 12 months at a steady rate?

    1 − 0.880.5 = 1 − 0.938 = about 6.2%. Not 6%: leavers are removed from the base as time passes.

    4. Your retention offer takes 6 weeks to prepare and send. You have a model predicting "leaves within 30 days". What is wrong?

    The horizon is shorter than the lead time, so any customer who leaves within 30 days has gone before the offer arrives. Either speed up the action, lengthen the horizon to 90 days or more, or use the model for a different, faster action such as an agent prompt in an inbound call.

    5. A model flags 60% of fraudulent transactions. Each stolen card has 3 fraudulent attempts. What share of stolen cards is touched, under independence? What if you were told only the transaction figure?

    1 − 0.43 = 93.6%. Someone seeing "60% recall" would underestimate the card-level catch; someone comparing a card-level number with a transaction-level number would overstate the improvement. Always state the unit with the metric. Real flags are correlated, so the true card-level figure would be lower.

    6. List three consequences of choosing "customer" over "transaction" as the unit.

    One alert per customer instead of many; the split must keep each customer on one side; labels need an aggregation rule; the action (call, block, review) becomes possible; features aggregate over history up to the decision day; and metrics report customers caught, not transactions.

    7. When is "no model" the right conclusion of the framing exercise?

    When the net value is negative at every cut-off (as in Simulation 1 at high offer cost), when nobody owns the decision, when a simple rule or process fix gets most of the benefit (C1.4), or when the lead time is longer than any useful horizon. Reporting that early is a good result.
