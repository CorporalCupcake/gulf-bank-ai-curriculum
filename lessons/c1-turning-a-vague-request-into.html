    W1.1 Day one ★ Priority About 10 minutes

    Turning a Vague Request into a Decision Problem

    Part 1

    What it means

    In one sentenceTurning a vague request into a decision problem means finding out who will do what differently because of your model, and how you will know it worked.

    How it differs from "build a model"

    • A prediction is a number about the future, such as "this customer has a 30% chance of leaving".
    • A decision is a choice someone makes using that number, such as "call this customer this week".
    • The request usually names the prediction. The business needs the decision. A weather forecast is only worth something if someone decides to carry an umbrella.

    A strong model nobody acts on is worth nothing. So start with "what will somebody do differently, and what is that worth?", not "what can I predict?".

    Part 2

    How to do it

    Ask these questions in order and write the answers down. Aim to fill in one sentence: "On [day], [person] decides whether to [action] for each [unit], using a score for [outcome] within [horizon]. It worked if [measure]." Any blank you cannot fill is your next question.

    1. Who makes the decision? Get a name and a calendar slot, not "the business".
    2. What are the options and what do they cost? For example: call (AED 25), text (AED 0.2), do nothing. 600 calls a week.
    3. What happens today, without the model? For example: oldest balance first, and 31% of calls reach a payment. This is your baseline (Lesson W1.2).
    4. What would a win look like? For example: payments per 100 calls rise from 31 to 38. Not "a higher AUC".
    5. What data exists on the day of the decision? Not "all our data". Anything only known afterwards leaks the answer.

    Two shapes come up again and again. In ranking under capacity, analysts can review 300 alerts a day, so headcount sets the cut-off. In act on each case (approve, block, price), the costs of each kind of error set the cut-off.

    Three choices that look like details

    Three choices turn a decision into a table a model can learn from. Each can quietly make the model useless.

    • Target: the column the model predicts. Check its definition. "Default" at 30, 60 or 90 days late gives different rates. Check what the label really records too: a suspicious-activity report shows what investigators chose to examine, not what is criminal.
    • Horizon: how far ahead the label looks, such as "leaves within 3 months". A longer horizon gives more positives, but each is less imminent and you wait longer for labels.
    • Unit: the thing that gets one score and one action: transaction, card or customer. It decides what one alert is, what the metric means, and how to split train from test.

    If an offer takes a month to arrive, anyone leaving in that first month is gone before it lands. Rule of thumb: horizon at least as long as the action's lead time, and as short as the action's effect allows.

    Unit matters for metrics. A model that flags 60% of fraudulent transactions may catch 94% of fraudulent cards, because a thief tries several times and one flag is enough. Always say which unit a number is about.

    Part 3

    How to read the economics

    Once you know the action, its cost and its payoff, you can say who is worth acting on. Suppose a retention offer costs AED 100, keeps a customer 20% of the time, and a kept customer is worth AED 1,500.

    • Each offer earns 20% × 1,500 = AED 300 if the customer really would have left.
    • A group with a 15% chance of leaving returns about 15% × 300 = AED 45 per offer. That costs AED 100, so you lose money.
    • The break-even is a 33% chance of leaving. Only customers above that pay back. The economics set the cut-off, not the word "likely".
    • If no cut-off makes money, the right answer is "no model" (Lesson W1.2). Say so in week one.

    Prediction is not persuasion. Someone likely to leave may not be talkable round. The "kept" rate has to be measured, usually with an experiment that holds some customers back.

    Show the formula

    Break-even chance of leaving = cost of the action ÷ (success rate × value of success). Here 100 ÷ (0.20 × 1,500) = 0.33. Act only on cases above it.

    Part 4

    Which framing to use

    FramingLooks likeProblem
    Vague request"Use AI to improve the call centre"No owner, no action, no measure. You cannot tell a pilot from a demo.
    Prediction only"Predict who will default"Same score could set limits, trigger calls or feed provisioning. Each needs a different horizon and unit.
    Decision problemThe one-sentence template aboveTakes a day or two to write. Everything else follows from it.
    • Strong at: exposing missing owners, costs and baselines before you build anything.
    • Weak at: nothing technical, but it needs people in the room who own the decision. Without them, push back and wait.

    In banking, "predict who will default" could mean a credit limit, an early-warning call or IFRS 9 provisioning (the accounting rules for expected loan losses), and each has its own horizon, unit and cost of error.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Predicting what is easy to label

      The data holds "account closed", so the model predicts closure, though the bank cares about losing the relationship. Ask if the decision-maker would call the target "the thing we care about".

    • Horizon shorter than lead time

      The score fires two weeks before leaving but the offer takes four weeks. Subtract the lead time from the horizon; if nothing is left, widen it.

    • Wrong unit

      Eight alerts for one stolen card, or one customer's rows on both sides of a train-test split. Ask what one row and one alert each represent.

    • Only an AUC, no value

      "We reached 0.83" says nothing about worth. Ask for the AED value per 1,000 customers against the current process.

    1. Rewrite "Use AI to reduce fraud" as a decision sentence. What can't you fill in yet?

    For example: "Each day, fraud operations decides which cards to review, using a score for stolen-card fraud within 24 hours. It worked if confirmed losses fall without a big rise in customer contacts." Unknown: capacity, action cost, current loss rate.

    2. An offer costs AED 100, works 20% of the time, and a kept customer is worth AED 1,500. Why is "contact everyone likely to leave" wrong?

    Each offer must earn back its cost. Only customers with more than about a 33% chance of leaving pay back.

    3. Your offer takes 6 weeks to prepare, and the model predicts "leaves within 30 days". What is wrong?

    The horizon is shorter than the lead time, so those customers are gone before the offer arrives. Lengthen the horizon or use a faster action.

    4. A model flags 60% of fraudulent transactions. Why might it catch more than 60% of stolen cards?

    A card counts as caught if any attempt is flagged, and several tries give several chances. Always state the unit.

    5. When is "no model" the right result of framing?

    When net value is negative at every cut-off, nobody owns the decision, or a simple rule gets most of the benefit. Reporting that early is a good result.
