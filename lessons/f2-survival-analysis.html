    Survival Analysis: Time to Default, Churn and Dormancy

    Why "when" carries more information than "whether", how to count unfinished loans, and how a hazard curve becomes lifetime PD.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost loans haven't finished their story yet. Survival analysis uses each loan for exactly as long as you've watched it, and gives you the risk at every horizon.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank launched a personal finance product two and a half years ago, with 36-month loans. The risk committee asks: what share will default within two years?

    Dividing defaults by loans booked looks safe, but most loans are young. Dropping young loans looks alarming. Survival analysis lines every loan up by months on book and asks, month by month: of the loans still watched at this age, what share defaulted?

    The two naive errors point in opposite directions, and more data only makes them more precisely wrong.

    Hold onto thisA loan you're still watching isn't a good loan. It's a loan whose ending you haven't seen yet.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why time matters: what a 12-month flag throws away

    A 12-month default flag throws away when the default happened and everything after month 12. A survival model's target is the time to the event, plus a flag for whether it was seen. One model then gives the 3-month, 12-month or lifetime PD that IFRS 9 needs.

    Censoring: the unfinished stories

    A loan is right-censored when you know the event hadn't happened by some month, but not what came after. A censored loan says only "survived to month c".

    The assumption that does the workCensoring must be non-informative: a loan that leaves view should have the same future risk as one still watched. If collections restructures struggling loans into new accounts, the riskiest vanish before defaulting and estimates look too good.

    The survival function and the hazard

    The survival curve is the share of loans still performing at month t; one minus it is the cumulative PD. The hazard is the share of loans alive at the start of a month that default during it. Hazard is the speedometer; survival is the distance covered.

    Don't add hazards up: 36 months of 1% is about 30%, not 36%. Personal finance has a hump, so a seasoned loan is safer than a new one.

    Shape matters: two books with the same lifetime PD can have very different 12-month PDs, so a rising-hazard book looks safe in Stage 1, then needs far more at Stage 2.

    Kaplan–Meier: multiply the chances of getting through each month

    Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimates survival from real data: each month, divide defaults by loans still watched, and chain the chances of getting through. Censored loans count while watched, then leave. Always show the number at risk: a curve ending on 9 loans deserves less trust than one ending on 9,000.

    Comparing two curves: the log-rank test

    The log-rank test asks whether two curves differ by more than noise. Its weak spot is crossing hazards: if B is riskier early and safer later, the differences cancel and it reports nothing.

    Cox proportional hazards: hazard ratios

    The Cox model is regression for survival: everyone shares a baseline hazard shape, scaled by their characteristics. A hazard ratio (HR) of 2 means that at any age, among surviving loans, B defaults at twice A's monthly rate. It is not a PD ratio, and "proportional" often fails as effects wear off.

    When hazards cross, Cox can report a ratio near 1 and log-rank can find nothing. Let the effect change with time.

    Competing risks: default, early settlement and everything else

    A loan can end by default, early settlement, buy-out or repayment, and once one happens the others can't. These are competing risks. Treating early settlement as censoring estimates default in a world where nobody settles early, so 1 − KM overstates defaults, by a third or more in murabaha books. For a provision, use the cumulative incidence function.

    Discrete-time hazard models

    The discrete-time hazard model is the credit workhorse. Make one row per loan per month while at risk, with target 1 if it defaulted that month. A censored loan simply stops. Fit an ordinary logistic regression with a baseline by month on book plus monthly features, including macro variables. Split by loan, and use only features known at the start of the month.

    The IFRS 9 link: lifetime PD and Stage 2

    Stage 1 loans carry a 12-month expected loss. After a significant rise in credit risk a loan moves to Stage 2 and carries a lifetime loss. A survival model supplies the PD for every future period.

    • Stage 2 needs conditional PDs: risk from today onward, using today's covariates.
    • Prepayment belongs in the number: use cumulative incidence, not 1 − KM.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each with a quick check.

    • Unfinished loans counted as good, or dropped

      A default rate on loans that are mostly months old.

      Spot itAsk for the denominator's median months on book. "Excluded" is wrong.
    • 1 − KM used as a provisioning PD

      Overstates losses wherever early settlement is common.

      Spot itAsk whether lifetime PD is a cumulative incidence.
    • Informative censoring

      Restructured or sold loans leave the data before defaulting.

      Spot itList every way a loan leaves the data and ask if those loans were riskier.
    • Hazard ratios misread

      One ratio for a score whose effect fades, read as "twice as likely to default".

      Spot itAsk for KM curves by score band and PD by segment.
    • Leakage and weak validation

      Month-end features predicting that month's default, loans split across train and test, or a good C-index that says nothing about year-3 PDs.

      Spot itAsk when each feature was known, and for predicted-versus-actual vintage curves.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. One analyst divides defaults by loans booked; another drops loans watched for under 24 months. Which way does each err?

    The first errs low, as young loans count as survivors. The second errs high, as it discards young paying loans.

    2. A monthly hazard is a constant 1%. Why is the 36-month PD below 36%?

    Each month's 1% applies only to loans still alive.

    3. Segment B has a hazard ratio of 2. Is its 36-month PD twice A's?

    No, somewhat below twice. Ask for PD by segment.

    4. Why does 1 − KM overstate defaults when many contracts settle early?

    A settled contract can't default. Use cumulative incidence.

    5. Cox and log-rank both find no effect for thin-file customers. What do you check?

    Whether the hazards cross and cancel. Let the effect change with time.
