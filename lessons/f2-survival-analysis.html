    Survival Analysis: Modelling Time Until Something Happens

    Part 1

    What survival analysis is

    In one sentenceSurvival analysis models how long until something happens, such as a customer cancelling, while correctly handling the cases where it hasn't happened yet.

    Our running example is a phone company with 36-month contracts. The manager asks: what share of customers will cancel within two years? (In banking the same story is loans and defaults.)

    The tricky part is that most contracts are young. A contract signed three months ago hasn't had time to be cancelled, but it isn't loyal either. We just haven't seen how it ends.

    A hollow dot is censored: we only know the contract survived up to that point. Survival analysis uses that information instead of throwing it away or guessing.

    How it differs from other approaches

    • Counting cancellations ÷ contracts signed is too low, because young contracts count as "no cancellation" when they simply aren't finished.
    • Dropping unfinished contracts is too high, because it keeps mostly the ones that ended early.
    • A "cancelled within 12 months" yes/no flag (logistic regression) loses when it happened and everything after month 12. A month-2 and a month-11 cancellation both become "1", and a month-13 one counts as loyal.
    • Survival analysis keeps the timing, uses every contract for exactly the months it was watched, and gives the 3-month, 12-month or lifetime chance from one model.

    Anything with a clock fits: churn, machine failure, time to a first insurance claim, time to loan default. Define the event precisely. Churn is rarely a formal closure, so pick a rule such as "no calls or data for two months".

    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Record two things per contract: how many months it was watched, and whether the event (cancellation) happened.
    2. Line everyone up by months since signing, not by calendar date.
    3. Month by month, take the contracts still being watched and ask what share cancelled that month.
    4. Chain the chances of getting through each month. Multiplying them gives the survival curve.
    5. Add features (plan type, app use) with a regression model to see what speeds up or slows down cancellation.

    Step 3 is the Kaplan–Meier method. A censored contract counts while it is watched, then leaves quietly. No assumption about the curve's shape is needed.

    How to read it

    • Survival curve: the share still running at each month. One minus it is the chance of having cancelled by then. In the picture, 13% have cancelled by month 12.
    • Hazard: of the contracts alive at the start of a month, the share that cancel during it. Hazard is the speedometer; survival is the distance covered.
    • Don't add hazards up. Thirty-six months at 1% is about 30%, not 36%.
    • Shape tells the story. A hump (peaks around months 6 to 18, then falls) is typical of new contracts: a seasoned contract is safer than a new one. A falling hazard means risk is early, such as shock at the first bill. A rising hazard means risk builds with age.
    • Check the number at risk. A curve ending on 9 contracts deserves far less trust than one ending on 9,000.

    The 12-month trap. Two books can have the same lifetime cancellation chance but very different 12-month chances. A book whose risk rises with age looks safe on a 12-month view, then turns out far riskier over its whole life.

    Comparing groups

    • Kaplan–Meier per group: draw one curve per segment and look at the gap.
    • Log-rank test: asks whether two curves differ by more than noise. It says whether they differ, not by how much. It misses curves that cross, because early and late differences cancel.
    • Cox model: regression for survival. Everyone shares a baseline hazard shape, scaled by their features. It gives a hazard ratio (HR).

    Reading a hazard ratio. Say customers who never opened the app (B) have HR 2 against those who did (A). At any age, among contracts still running, B cancels at twice A's monthly rate. HR 1 means no effect; below 1 is protective.

    • It is not a ratio of probabilities. HR 2 gives a three-year cancellation chance a little under twice A's. Ask for the cumulative chance by segment too.
    • "Proportional" is an assumption. If the effect wears off or reverses (a rough first bill predicts early cancellation more than late), one HR averages a changing truth. Check by drawing the curves per group.

    Two assumptions to respect

    • Censoring must be uninformative. A contract that leaves view should have the same future risk as one still watched. If a retention team moves unhappy customers to new accounts, the riskiest vanish before cancelling and the results look too good.
    • Competing risks. A contract can end by cancelling, upgrading early or running its full term, and only one can happen. Treating early upgrades as plain censoring overstates cancellations, by a third or more when upgrades are common. Use cumulative incidence to forecast the share that will actually cancel.

    The credit version

    Banks often use a discrete-time model. Make one row per contract per month while at risk, with target 1 if it cancelled that month. A contract cancelling in month 4 gives four rows: three zeros and a one. A censored contract simply stops. Then fit an ordinary logistic regression with month and monthly features. Split by contract, never by row, and use only features known at the start of the month.

    Show the formula

    Kaplan–Meier survival at month t = the product, over each month up to t, of (1 − cancellations that month ÷ contracts still watched that month). Each factor is the chance of getting through that month.

    Survival methods are strong at: unfinished data (young contracts), questions about timing, and several horizons from one model.

    They are weak at: cases where the unfinished ones differ in risk from the rest, and at hazard ratios taken as causal effects. To ask whether a retention programme worked, compare survival curves at fixed horizons.

    In banking, survival models give the probability of default (PD) for every future period. Under IFRS 9, the accounting standard for expected credit losses, Stage 1 loans carry a 12-month expected loss, while loans whose credit risk has risen significantly move to Stage 2 and carry a lifetime loss. Expected loss adds up PD × loss given default × exposure over those periods. More than 30 days past due is a rebuttable presumption of a significant rise.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating unfinished contracts as loyal

      A contract still running hasn't ended. Counting it as "never cancelled" understates risk.

    • Dropping unfinished contracts

      This keeps mostly the early leavers and overstates risk.

    • Trusting one hazard ratio blindly

      If the effect changes with age, one number can mislead. Draw the curves per group.

    • Splitting rows at random

      With one row per month, a random split puts the same contract on both sides. Split by contract, preferably by sign-up date.

    1. What does "censored" mean?

    We know the contract survived up to some month, but not what happened after. It carries information and should be used, not dropped.

    2. What is the difference between survival and hazard?

    Survival is the share still running at a given month. Hazard is the share of those alive at the start of a month that cancel during it.

    3. A Cox model gives HR = 0.5 for customers on a loyalty plan. What does it mean?

    At any age, among surviving contracts, loyalty-plan customers cancel at half the monthly rate of others.

    4. Why not just use a "cancelled within 12 months" flag?

    It loses when the cancellation happened and everything after month 12, and it mishandles contracts younger than 12 months.

    5. Many contracts end by early upgrade. What goes wrong if you treat that as censoring?

    You estimate cancellation in a world where nobody upgrades early, so you overstate the share that will cancel. Use cumulative incidence.
