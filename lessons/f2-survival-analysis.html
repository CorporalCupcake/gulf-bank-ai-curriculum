    Survival Analysis: Time to Default, Churn and Dormancy

    Why "when" carries more information than "whether", how to count loans that haven't finished yet, and how a hazard curve becomes the lifetime PD behind every Stage 2 provision.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost of a loan book hasn't finished its story yet. Survival analysis uses each loan for exactly as long as you've watched it, multiplies the month-by-month chances of getting through, and gives you the risk at every horizon at once.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank launched a personal finance product two and a half years ago. Loans run 36 months. The risk committee asks: what share will default within two years?

    • Analyst 1 divides defaults by loans booked. The number looks safe, but most loans are young. A loan booked four months ago has had four months to default, not 24. It is like judging a marathon at 10 km and calling everyone still running a finisher.
    • Analyst 2 drops every loan watched for under 24 months. Now the number looks alarming. The early defaults stay in, and the young loans that are quietly paying are thrown away. Dropping the unfinished throws away mostly good news.
    • Survival analysis lines every loan up by months on book and asks, month by month: of the loans still watched at this age, what share defaulted? A loan counts for every month it was observed and stops counting when we lose sight of it. Multiply the chances of getting through each month and you have the answer.

    What to take from it:

    • The two naive errors point in opposite directions. Counting unfinished loans as survivors biases the default rate down. Dropping them biases it up. More data doesn't help: 4,000 loans are just more precisely wrong.
    • Kaplan–Meier is noisy with 40 loans but centred on the truth. That is sampling error, and more loans fix it. Bias isn't fixed that way.
    • It gets less certain further out. Late in the curve only the oldest loans are still at risk, so each default moves the curve a lot. With no loan watched that long, it honestly says nothing.
    • A gap can remain even with no unfinished loans. Push the date to 60 months and defaults over all loans still sits below Kaplan–Meier. That gap is early settlers, who can't default any more. Whether you want it in your number depends on the question. Simulation 4 returns to this.
    Hold onto thisA loan you're still watching isn't a good loan. It's a loan whose ending you haven't seen yet. Use what you've seen, month by month, and never pretend to know the rest.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why time matters: what a 12-month flag throws away

    Most scorecards use a binary target: did the account reach 90 days past due within 12 months? Like a school report that only says "pass" or "fail", it throws away three things.

    • When it happened. A default in month 2 (often fraud) and one in month 11 (often a lost job) are both "1".
    • Everything after month 12. A month-13 default is labelled good. On a 48-month auto murabaha that is most of the contract.
    • The youngest loans. With under 12 months of history they can't be labelled, so the newest vintages, which reflect current policy, are excluded.

    A survival model's target is the time to the event, plus a flag for whether the event was seen or observation stopped first. One model gives the 3-month, 12-month or lifetime probability, and IFRS 9 needs both 12-month and lifetime numbers for the same loan.

    Anything with a clock fits: time to churn, time to dormancy (a formal account status with regulatory rules in the UAE and Saudi Arabia), time to first takaful claim, time to complaint resolution. Define the event precisely. Churn is rarely a closure, so pick a rule, such as salary not credited for two consecutive months, and stick to it.

    Censoring: the unfinished stories

    An observation is right-censored when you know the event hadn't happened by some time, but not what came after. A runner who is still going when the stopwatch is switched off isn't a non-finisher. You just don't have her time. Sources in a loan book:

    • Administrative censoring. The loan is still running on the analysis date. The big one in a growing book.
    • Early settlement or prepayment. Common in UAE and Saudi personal finance, where banks compete for salary transfers. Censoring for the default hazard, but really a competing event (below).
    • Other exits. Portfolio sales, transfers, death with takaful cover settling the balance.

    Both tempting fixes are wrong (Simulation 1). Treating censored loans as survivors biases PD down. Dropping them biases it up, and is selection on the outcome (F2.6).

    The correct treatment lives in the likelihood (see F1.6). A loan that defaulted at month t contributes "survived to t, then defaulted". A loan censored at month c contributes only "survived to c". Nobody invents an ending. Every estimator here is built on that rule.

    The assumption that does the workCensoring must be non-informative: a loan that leaves view has the same future risk as a similar loan still watched. It fails when leaving is tied to risk. If collections restructures struggling loans and books them as new accounts, the riskiest loans vanish just before defaulting and every estimate looks too good.

    Left truncation (delayed entry) is a cousin. If a 2022 warehouse migration kept only loans still open then, those loans are survivors by construction. They must enter the risk set at the age they had when the data starts, not at month 0, or the early months look safer than they were.

    The survival function and the hazard

    Let T be the month a loan defaults. Two functions describe it, with the same information.

    S(t)=P(T>t)

    The survival function: the probability a loan gets through month t without defaulting. It starts at 1 and only falls. Cumulative PD by month t is 1 − S(t). S(24) = 0.90 means 90 in 100 loans are still performing at month 24.

    h(t)=P(T=t∣T≥t)S(t)=∏u=1t(1−h(u))

    The hazard: of loans alive at the start of month t, the share that default during it. Survival is the product of the chances of getting through each month. In continuous time, S(t) = exp(−H(t)), where H is the hazard added up over time.

    Think of the hazard as a speedometer and survival as the distance already covered. Hazard answers "how dangerous is this month, for those still here?". A hazard of 1% means 1 in 100 survivors defaults this month. Going from 1% to 2% doubles that month's risk. The common misreading is to add hazards up as if they were the PD. Each month's 1% applies only to the loans still alive.

    The hazard's shape tells the story. A constant hazard means age doesn't matter (memoryless: geometric or exponential, F1.5). Real books rarely are:

    • Hump: unsecured personal finance and cards. Low at first, peaking around months 6 to 18, then falling as weak borrowers are gone.
    • Falling: risk concentrated early, such as first-payment fraud or thin-file customers.
    • Rising: risk builds with age, as in SME lines with ageing collateral.
    • Bathtub: high early, quiet in the middle, rising at the end, as with balloon structures.

    The hazard also gives the number every Stage 2 calculation needs: the risk from now, given survival so far.

    P(default in the next 12 months∣alive at montha)=1−S(a+12)S(a)

    A loan that survived to month a has escaped the hazard before a. With a hump, a seasoned loan is safer than a new one. With a rising hazard it is riskier.

    The yearly bars are a term structure of default: the chance a new loan defaults in year 1, year 2 and so on. They sum to the lifetime PD, and IFRS 9 multiplies them by LGD and exposure year by year.

    Shape matters more than people expect. Two books with the same lifetime PD can have very different 12-month PDs. A rising-hazard book looks safe in Stage 1, then needs a far larger provision at Stage 2. A falling-hazard book has already taken most losses in year 1.

    Kaplan–Meier: multiply the chances of getting through each month

    Kaplan–Meier (KM) is the hazard-product formula with each month's hazard replaced by what you observed: defaults that month divided by loans still at risk.

    S^(t)=∏ti≤t(1−dini)

    di is the number of defaults at month ti; ni is the number still watched just before it, excluding earlier defaults and censored loans.

    Worked example, ten loans. Month 3: one defaults, 10 at risk, survival × 9/10 = 0.900. Month 4: two are censored (one settles early, one reaches the analysis date) and just leave the risk set. Month 6: one more defaults with only 7 at risk, so survival = 0.900 × 6/7 = 0.771. PD by month 6 is 22.9%; the naive count, 2 of 10, says 20%. Censored loans weren't counted as survivors. They stopped contributing once we lost sight of them.

    Uncertainty grows as the risk set shrinks. Greenwood's formula gives the standard interval by adding d/(n(n − d)) over the months, so months with few loans dominate. Always show the number at risk under a KM chart: a curve ending on 9 loans shouldn't carry the weight of one ending on 9,000.

    A bank's vintage curve (cumulative default by months on book for each quarter's originations) is a survival curve per cohort. With no censoring beyond the calendar it equals 1 − KM. Once loans prepay or are sold they diverge, so know which one your chart shows.

    Comparing two curves: the log-rank test

    Are loans without a salary transfer really riskier, or is the gap noise? The log-rank test visits every month with a default, asks how many defaults group B would have if both groups had the same hazard (given each group's loans at risk), and sums "observed minus expected". It compares that total with its standard error.

    It is the standard first test, strongest when one hazard is a constant multiple of the other. Its weak spot is crossing hazards: if B is riskier early and safer later, the excess and deficit cancel and the test reports no difference between very different groups. Simulation 3 shows this. Weighted variants emphasise early or late months.

    Cox proportional hazards: hazard ratios

    KM describes one group. To use many characteristics at once (salary band, debt-service ratio, employer category, bureau score) you need a regression. The standard is the Cox model (David Cox, 1972).

    h(t∣x)=h0(t)·exp(β1x1+…+βpxp)

    A baseline hazard h0(t) of any shape, times a customer factor that doesn't depend on time. exp(β) is the hazard ratio (HR) for a one-unit rise in x.

    How to read it: HR 2 means that at any given age, among loans still alive, segment B defaults at twice A's monthly rate. HR 1 means no effect, below 1 is protective. A coefficient of 0.69 is HR 2, since exp(0.69) ≈ 2.

    Cox's trick is that the βs can be estimated without specifying the baseline. At each default, ask: of all loans at risk right now, how likely was it to be this one? That is the defaulter's exp(βx) over the sum for everyone at risk, and the baseline cancels. Multiplying across defaults gives the partial likelihood. Censored loans sit in the denominators while watched, which is how they contribute.

    Two things to say precisely in a meeting:

    • A hazard ratio is not a PD ratio. With HR 2, SB(t) = SA(t)2. If 15% of A default within 36 months, B's survival is 0.85² = 0.7225, a PD of 27.75%: 1.85 times A's, not 2. The gap widens as PDs grow.
    • "Proportional" is an assumption that often fails. The model says B's hazard is twice A's at month 1 and month 36. In credit, effects wear off: an application score predicts early default well and later default much less. A welcome offer cuts churn for three months, then does nothing.

    When it fails, Cox doesn't crash. It reports one HR that averages the true, changing ratio, weighted to the months with most defaults, so implied curves can be badly wrong at some horizons. Diagnose with scaled Schoenfeld residuals against time (flat means proportional; the Grambsch–Therneau test formalises it) or check that log(−log S) curves run parallel. Fixes: stratify on the variable, add a time interaction, or fit by age band.

    The bottom chart is what a Schoenfeld plot tries to show: the true log hazard ratio over time against the flat line the model assumed. In the crossing case Cox can report a ratio near 1 and log-rank can find nothing, while B's hazard is over double A's early and under half later. Don't drop the variable. Let its effect change with time.

    One more caution: even in a randomised trial, an HR isn't a clean causal effect, because later risk sets are selected by surviving earlier months. If B's risky borrowers default early, B's survivors are better than average (Hernán, "The hazards of hazard ratios"). To ask "did the restructuring programme reduce defaults?", compare survival curves or cumulative incidence at fixed horizons.

    Competing risks: default, early settlement and everything else

    A loan can end by default, early settlement, refinancing, buy-out or repayment at term. Once one happens the others can't. These are competing risks, and they change what "probability of default" means.

    KM for default, with early settlement as censoring, estimates default risk in a world where nobody settles early. It assumes settled contracts would have defaulted at the rate of those still running. But a contract settled in month 10 never defaults in month 11. To count contracts that will actually default, which a provision needs, use the cumulative incidence function.

    Fd(t)=∑u=1tS(u−1)·hd(u)

    Chance of still being alive (not defaulted, not settled) at the start of month u, times that month's default hazard, summed. S is survival from every exit, so more early settlement lowers S and the defaults. 1 − KM uses only the default hazard and ignores this.

    Murabaha makes this concrete: contracts are often settled early with a rebate (ibra') on unearned profit, or bought out by a competitor chasing the salary transfer. Where a quarter or more settle early, 1 − KM can overstate the defaulting share by a third or more.

    Two ways to add covariates, answering different questions:

    • Cause-specific hazards: one ordinary Cox or discrete-time model per exit, others as censoring. Best for "what drives default?" Coefficients mean what you'd expect.
    • Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray): model cumulative incidence directly. Best for "how many will default?" Coefficients are harder to read: a variable that only speeds up settlement will look like it "reduces default".

    For prediction, fit cause-specific models and combine them through the cumulative incidence formula. Most IFRS 9 implementations that handle prepayment do this, often in discrete time.

    Discrete-time hazard models: logistic regression on person-months

    Bank data is monthly: payments, delinquency, warehouse snapshots. So the discrete-time hazard model is the workhorse of credit survival modelling, and it is easier than it sounds.

    Make one row per loan per month on book while at risk. The target is 1 if the loan defaulted that month, else 0. A loan defaulting in month 4 gives four rows: three zeros and a one. A loan still running after 2 months gives two zeros and stops. That is all censoring means here. An early settlement stops too.

    ContractMonth on bookSalary credited last monthCard utilisation at month startUnemployment indexDefault this month
    M-2011Yes35%1000
    M-2012Yes48%1010
    M-2013No71%1030
    M-2014No94%1031 defaulted, no more rows
    M-2021Yes12%1020
    M-2022Yes10%1030 still running at the analysis date: censored
    M-2031Yes20%1000
    M-2032Yes18%1010 settled early in month 2: censored for default

    Then fit an ordinary logistic regression (log loss, nothing new) to these rows:

    logith(t∣xt)=αt+β⊤xt

    αt is the baseline hazard by month on book (a dummy per age band or a spline). xt can change monthly: behaviour, balances, macro variables. With the complementary log-log link instead of logit, the model is exactly Cox for monthly-grouped data, with the same hazard ratios.

    Why it works: a loan's history is "survived month 1, survived month 2, …, then defaulted (or was censored)". That is one Bernoulli term per row, which is exactly the logistic likelihood on the expanded data. Censoring is free: a censored loan has no rows after it leaves.

    Banks like it because it reuses their logistic tooling, scorecard governance, validation tests and sign checks. It handles time-varying covariates (how macro scenarios get in). It extends to competing risks with a three-way target (running, defaulted, settled) and multinomial logistic regression. Gradient-boosted trees can run on the same rows.

    Two traps. Rows aren't independent customers: 50,000 loans can become a million rows, and a random row split puts the same loan on both sides. Split by loan, preferably by time. Every feature must be known at the start of its month. A month-end balance leaks the outcome. This is point-in-time correctness (F3.1), and person-period data is where it breaks most.

    The IFRS 9 link: lifetime PD, term structures and Stage 2

    Stage 1 loans carry a 12-month expected credit loss (ECL). After a significant rise in credit risk since origination (over 30 days past due is a rebuttable presumption) a loan moves to Stage 2 and carries a lifetime ECL. Stage 3 is credit-impaired. A survival model produces the lifetime number because it gives the default probability in every future period.

    ECL=∑k=1K[S(k−1)−S(k)]⏟marginal PD·LGDk·EADk·DFk

    For each future period k: the chance of defaulting then (the yearly bars in Simulation 2), times loss rate, expected exposure and a discount factor. Stage 1 sums K = 12 months. Stage 2 sums the expected life.

    Four consequences:

    • Stage 2 needs conditional PDs. A loan moving to Stage 2 at month 20 needs 1 − S(a + k)/S(a) with today's, worse, covariates, not the origination curve.
    • Prepayment belongs in the number. IFRS 9 uses cash flows over the expected life, including prepayment options. A lifetime PD from 1 − KM with prepayment censored overstates losses (Simulation 4). Use cumulative incidence or combined cause-specific hazards.
    • Forward-looking means time-varying covariates. Feed each weighted scenario's path (oil price, non-oil GDP, unemployment) into future rows and read off a term structure per scenario.
    • Vintage curves are the backtest. Plot actual cumulative default by months on book per cohort against the prediction. Curves consistently below actual mean under-provisioning, however good the AUC.

    Alternatives exist: roll-rate or Markov matrices between delinquency buckets, or scaling up a 12-month PD. Survival methods earn their place for loan-level, time-varying, scenario-conditioned term structures, which is what Stage 2 asks for.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes you'll meet in real and AI-generated survival analyses, each with the quickest check.

    • Unfinished loans counted as good

      "Default rate on last year's bookings: 2.1%." Most of those loans are months old.

      Spot itAsk for the median months on book of the denominator. If well below the quoted horizon, the number is biased down.
    • Unfinished loans dropped

      "We only used loans with a full 24 months." That excludes the newest vintages and, if prepaid loans were also dropped, inflates the PD.

      Spot itAsk in one sentence how censored and prepaid loans were treated. "Excluded" is wrong for both.
    • 1 − KM used as a provisioning PD

      Prepayment treated as censoring, then used to count expected defaults. Overstates losses wherever settlement is common, including most murabaha books.

      Spot itAsk whether lifetime PD is a cumulative incidence that accounts for prepayment. Compare with the actual default share of a fully matured vintage.
    • Informative censoring

      Restructured, rescheduled or sold loans leave the data just before defaulting. Survival looks better than reality.

      Spot itList every way a loan can leave the data and ask whether those loans were riskier. Treat restructuring as an event or state, not an exit.
    • Left truncation ignored

      Loans entering after a system migration counted as at risk from month 0, though only survivors made it in.

      Spot itLook for origination dates before the data start. They need an entry time, not zero.
    • Proportional hazards assumed, never checked

      One hazard ratio for an application score whose effect fades after a year. Early PDs are understated and late PDs overstated, or the reverse.

      Spot itAsk for the Schoenfeld plot or Grambsch–Therneau test, and KM curves by score band. Converging or crossing curves give it away.
    • Hazard ratios read as PD ratios or as causes

      "No salary transfer means twice as likely to default." With HR 2 the 3-year PD ratio might be 1.8, and the ratio isn't causal.

      Spot itAsk for cumulative PD by segment at the horizon that matters.
    • Leakage in person-period rows

      A row uses that month's end balance, days past due or collections flag to predict that month's default. Accuracy looks superb.

      Spot itFor each feature ask "when was this known?" It must be before the month starts.
    • Splitting person-months at random

      Loan X's month 5 is in training and month 6 in test. Validation is optimistic.

      Spot itCheck the split key is the loan or customer, ideally the origination date.
    • Judging by the C-index alone

      The concordance index measures ranking, like AUC. It says nothing about whether year-3 PDs are right, and Harrell's version depends on the censoring pattern.

      Spot itAsk for time-dependent AUC and Brier score at key horizons, plus predicted-versus-actual vintage curves.
    • AI-generated survival code that doesn't add up

      Signs: event flag inverted (1 for censored), durations in days for some loans and months for others, a "survival" curve that rises, Cox with no assumption check, lifetime PD below 12-month PD.

      Spot itRecompute one KM point by hand from the at-risk table, as in Part 2. Check event counts match the raw defaults.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the Part 2 formulas.

    1. Twenty loans. In month 2, two default. In month 5, three are censored (two still running, one settled early). In month 7, three more default. What's the KM PD by month 7, and what do the two naive counts say?

    Month 2: survival × 18/20 = 0.900. Month 5: three leave the risk set. Month 7: 15 at risk, 3 default, × 12/15 = 0.800, so S(7) = 0.720 and the KM PD is 28.0%. Defaults over all loans: 5/20 = 25.0%, too low. Dropping censored loans: 5/17 = 29.4%, too high.

    2. A constant monthly default hazard of 1%. What are the 12-month and 36-month PDs?

    12 months: 1 − 0.9912 = 11.4%. 36 months: 1 − 0.9936 = 30.4%, not 36%. Each month's 1% applies only to loans still alive.

    3. Segment A's 36-month PD is 15%. Segment B has a constant hazard ratio of 2. What's B's 36-month PD?

    SB = 0.852 = 0.7225, so B's PD is 27.75%, about 1.85 times A's.

    4. An auto murabaha book: 0.5% of running contracts default each month and 2% settle early. Over 48 months, what's 1 − KM for default, and what share actually default?

    1 − KM = 1 − 0.99548 = 21.4%. Cumulative incidence = 0.005/0.025 × (1 − 0.97548) = 0.2 × 0.704 = 14.1%. 1 − KM overstates defaults by about half. Check both in Simulation 4.

    5. 50,000 loans with an average of 22 months observed. How many rows does the discrete-time dataset have, and how should you split it?

    About 1.1 million person-month rows. Split by loan, never by row. Better, split by origination date: train on older vintages, test on newer, as the model will be used.

    6. A personal finance loan with a hump-shaped hazard moves to Stage 2 at month 30. Why is the origination 36-month curve the wrong input for its lifetime ECL?

    First, it has survived the peak months, so the unconditional curve counts risk it has escaped: use 1 − S(30 + k)/S(30). Second, it moved to Stage 2 because its risk rose, so the hazard should use current covariates (missed salary credits, rising utilisation), not the application profile.

    7. A Cox model reports a hazard ratio of 1.1 (not significant) for thin-file customers, and the log-rank test agrees. A colleague says thin-file status doesn't matter. What do you check?

    Whether the hazards cross. Plot KM curves and scaled Schoenfeld residuals against time. If thin-file customers default far more early and less later, the effects cancel in both tests (Simulation 3, crossing case). Model the effect as changing with time, for example separate ratios for months 1–6 and later.
