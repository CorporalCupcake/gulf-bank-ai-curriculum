    Survival Analysis: Time to Default, Churn and Dormancy

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A phone company launched a 36-month contract two and a half years ago. The manager asks: what share of customers will cancel within two years?

    Dividing cancellations by contracts signed looks safe, but most contracts are young and have had little time to be cancelled. Dropping young contracts looks alarming. Survival analysis lines every contract up by months since signing and asks, month by month: of the contracts still watched at this age, what share cancelled? (In banking the same story appears with loans and defaults.)

    The two naive errors point in opposite directions, and more data only makes them more precisely wrong. Kaplan–Meier avoids both by counting each contract only for the months it was actually watched.

    Hold onto thisA contract you're still watching isn't a loyal customer. It's a contract whose ending you haven't seen yet.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why time matters: what a 12-month flag throws away

    A 12-month cancel flag throws away when the cancellation happened and everything after month 12. A survival model's target is the time to the event, plus a flag for whether it was seen. One model then gives the 3-month, 12-month or lifetime chance of cancelling. (In banking these are the PDs, probabilities of default, that IFRS 9 needs.)

    A cancellation in month 2 (often a shock first bill) and one in month 11 (often a better offer elsewhere) are both "1" in a flag, and a month-13 cancellation is labelled loyal. Anything with a clock fits: churn, machine failure, time to a first insurance claim, time to a loan default. Define the event precisely. Churn is rarely a formal closure, so pick a rule, such as no calls or data use for two months.

    Censoring: the unfinished stories

    A contract is right-censored when you know the event hadn't happened by some month, but not what came after. A censored contract says only "survived to month c". In a contract book this happens in two main ways: the contract is still running on the analysis date (the big one in a growing book), or it ended early through an upgrade, which is censored for the cancellation hazard but is really a competing event (below). Both tempting fixes, counting such contracts as loyal or dropping them, are wrong, as Simulation 1 shows.

    The assumption that does the workCensoring must be non-informative: a contract that leaves view should have the same future risk as one still watched. If a retention team moves unhappy customers onto new accounts, the riskiest vanish before cancelling and estimates look too good.

    Left truncation is the cousin to know. If a system migration kept only contracts open on a given date, those contracts are survivors by construction. They must enter the analysis at the age they had then, not at month 0, or early risk is understated.

    The survival function and the hazard

    The survival curve is the share of contracts still running at month t; one minus it is the cumulative chance of cancelling. The hazard is the share of contracts alive at the start of a month that cancel during it. Hazard is the speedometer; survival is the distance covered.

    Don't add hazards up: 36 months of 1% is about 30%, not 36%. Many contract books have a hump, so a seasoned contract is safer than a new one.

    The shape tells the story. A hump (new phone contracts, new jobs, unsecured personal loans) peaks around months 6 to 18, then falls as the unhappy ones leave. A falling hazard means risk is concentrated early, as with first-bill shock. A rising or bathtub hazard builds with age, or is high at the start and again at the end, as with a contract that has a big final payment. The yearly bars in Simulation 2 are a term structure of cancellation and sum to the lifetime chance.

    Shape matters: two books with the same lifetime cancellation chance can have very different 12-month chances, so a rising-hazard book looks safe on a 12-month view, then turns out far riskier over its whole life. In banking this is the jump in provisions between IFRS 9 Stage 1 and Stage 2.

    Kaplan–Meier: multiply the chances of getting through each month

    Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimates survival from real data: each month, divide cancellations by contracts still watched, and chain the chances of getting through. Censored contracts count while watched, then leave. Always show the number at risk: a curve ending on 9 loans deserves less trust than one ending on 9,000.

    KM needs no distribution assumption, which is its strength, and it describes one group at a time, which is its limit. A cohort curve (cumulative cancellations by months since signing for each quarter's sign-ups; banks call it a vintage curve) is a survival curve per cohort. Once contracts end early for other reasons it no longer equals 1 − KM, so know which one your chart shows.

    Comparing two curves: the log-rank test

    The log-rank test asks whether two curves differ by more than noise. Its weak spot is crossing hazards: if B is riskier early and safer later, the differences cancel and it reports nothing. It checks, month by month, whether one group has more cancellations than it would if both shared one hazard, and adds up the surplus. It is the standard first test, and it tells you whether the groups differ, not by how much or why.

    Cox proportional hazards: hazard ratios

    The Cox model is regression for survival: everyone shares a baseline hazard shape, scaled by their characteristics. A hazard ratio (HR) of 2 means that at any age, among surviving contracts, B cancels at twice A's monthly rate. HR 1 means no effect and below 1 is protective. Two points to say precisely in a meeting. An HR is not a ratio of probabilities: HR 2 gives a three-year cancellation probability a little under twice A's, and the gap widens as probabilities grow, so ask for cumulative probability by segment. And "proportional" is an assumption that often fails: a rough first-bill experience predicts early cancellation better than late.

    The model does not crash when this fails. It reports one HR that averages the true, changing ratio, so implied curves can be badly wrong at some horizons. Diagnose by plotting the effect against time (flat means proportional, a Schoenfeld plot) or by drawing KM curves per group. Features can also change monthly, which Cox handles with time-varying covariates. An HR is not a clean causal effect either: to ask whether a retention programme reduced cancellations, compare survival curves at fixed horizons.

    When hazards cross, Cox can report a ratio near 1 and log-rank can find nothing. Let the effect change with time.

    Competing risks: cancelling, upgrading early and everything else

    A contract can end by cancellation, early upgrade, transfer or running its full term, and once one happens the others can't. These are competing risks. Treating early upgrades as censoring estimates cancellation in a world where nobody upgrades early, so 1 − KM overstates cancellations, by a third or more when upgrades are common. For a forecast, use the cumulative incidence function: the chance of still being alive at each month, times that month's cancellation hazard, added up. In banking, murabaha contracts are often settled early with a rebate (ibra') or bought out by a competitor, which is why the gap there is so large.

    To add covariates, two options answer different questions. Cause-specific hazards fit one ordinary model per exit, treating the others as censoring, and suit "what drives cancellation?". Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray) model cumulative incidence directly and suit "how many will cancel?", but are harder to read: a variable that only speeds up upgrades will look as if it reduces cancellation.

    Discrete-time hazard models

    The discrete-time hazard model is the workhorse in credit. Make one row per contract per month while at risk, with target 1 if it cancelled that month. A censored contract simply stops. Fit an ordinary logistic regression with a baseline by month on book plus monthly features, including outside variables such as the economy. Split by contract, and use only features known at the start of the month.

    Example: a contract that cancels in month 4 gives four rows, three zeros and a one. A contract still running after two months gives two zeros and stops. That is all censoring means here. Banks like this form because it reuses their logistic tooling and validation, and because time-varying covariates are natural: each row carries that month's behaviour, balances and economic variables, which is how scenarios get in. Two traps. A random row split puts one contract on both sides, so split by contract, preferably by sign-up date. And a month-end balance leaks the month's outcome (see F3.1).

    In banking: lifetime PD and IFRS 9 Stage 2

    Banks are the heaviest users of this idea. Here PD (probability of default) is the chance a loan defaults, the same as the cancellation chance above. Under IFRS 9, Stage 1 loans carry a 12-month expected loss. After a significant rise in credit risk a loan moves to Stage 2 and carries a lifetime loss. A survival model supplies the PD for every future period, and ECL adds up PD × LGD × EAD over those periods: Stage 1 sums 12 months, Stage 2 the expected life. Over 30 days past due is a rebuttable presumption of a significant rise in risk.

    • Stage 2 needs conditional PDs: risk from today onward, using today's covariates.
    • Early exits belong in the number: use cumulative incidence, not 1 − KM.
    • Forward-looking means time-varying covariates: feed each weighted scenario's path (oil price, non-oil GDP, unemployment) into future rows.
    • Vintage curves are the backtest: plot actual cumulative default per cohort (vintage = loans signed in the same period) against the prediction. Curves consistently below actual mean under-provisioning, however good the AUC.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Using unfinished data honestly. Each contract counts for exactly the months it was watched, so young cohorts inform the estimate instead of biasing it. A fixed-window flag must discard or mislabel them.
    • One model, every horizon. The hazard gives 3-month, 12-month and lifetime risk, plus risk from today for a contract already seasoned. Banks need precisely that term structure for IFRS 9.
    • Showing timing. Hump, falling or bathtub shapes tell retention and fraud teams when to act, which a single score hides.
    • Handling several exits. Competing-risk versions separate cancelling from early upgrade, so forecasts are not inflated.
    • Interpretability. KM, Cox and discrete-time logistic models give curves and ratios a validator and regulator can read.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • Few events. Precision depends on the number of cancellations, not contracts. With a few dozen events, a Cox model with many variables overfits. A rough guide is at least 10 events per variable.
    • Informative censoring. If account moves or sales remove risky contracts, every curve is optimistic and no method repairs it.
    • Changing effects. Plain Cox assumes a fixed ratio. Strong time-varying effects need interactions with time or a discrete-time model.
    • Only a short fixed horizon matters. For a 12-month application score with fully mature outcomes, an ordinary classifier is simpler and often as good.
    • Heavy non-linearity. Many interacting features favour tree-based or neural survival models, at the cost of explainability.

    In practice. Start with KM by cohort and by key segment, with numbers at risk. Then fit a discrete-time logistic model with a baseline by months since signing and a few behavioural and economic features. Compare its implied curves to KM, and backtest on curves from newer cohorts. Lifelines or scikit-survival in Python, or the survival package in R, handle KM, Cox and competing risks. Compute is rarely a constraint: discrete-time models on tens of thousands of contracts give millions of rows, which a logistic fit handles easily.

    In banking you will see this in IFRS 9 lifetime PD, behavioural life of deposits, time to churn or dormancy, collections timing and takaful claims. Always report the cause-specific or cumulative-incidence version when early exits are common.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each with a quick check.

    • Unfinished contracts counted as good, or dropped

      A cancellation rate on contracts that are mostly months old.

      Spot itAsk for the denominator's median months since signing. "Excluded" is wrong.
    • 1 − KM used as a forecast

      Overstates cancellations wherever early exits are common (in banking, provisions).

      Spot itAsk whether the lifetime figure is a cumulative incidence.
    • Informative censoring

      Moved or sold contracts leave the data before cancelling.

      Spot itList every way a contract leaves the data and ask if those contracts were riskier.
    • Hazard ratios misread

      One ratio for a factor whose effect fades, read as "twice as likely to cancel".

      Spot itAsk for KM curves by band and cumulative probability by segment.
    • Leakage and weak validation

      Month-end features predicting that month's cancellation, contracts split across train and test, or a good C-index that says nothing about year-3 probabilities.

      Spot itAsk when each feature was known, and for predicted-versus-actual cohort curves. The C-index only measures ranking, like AUC.
    • Too many variables for too few events

      A Cox model with 30 features fitted on 60 cancellations. Coefficients swing between samples.

      Spot itCount events, not contracts, and ask how stable the coefficients are on a re-sample.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. One analyst divides cancellations by contracts signed; another drops contracts watched for under 24 months. Which way does each err?

    The first errs low, as young contracts count as survivors. The second errs high, as it discards young contracts that are still running.

    2. A monthly hazard is a constant 1%. Why is the 36-month cancellation chance below 36%?

    Each month's 1% applies only to contracts still alive.

    3. Segment B has a hazard ratio of 2. Is its 36-month cancellation chance twice A's?

    No, somewhat below twice. Ask for cumulative probability by segment.

    4. Why does 1 − KM overstate cancellations when many contracts are upgraded early?

    An upgraded contract can't cancel later. Use cumulative incidence.

    5. Cox and log-rank both find no effect for customers who skipped onboarding. What do you check?

    Whether the hazards cross and cancel. Let the effect change with time.

    6. A bank must produce lifetime PD for IFRS 9 Stage 2 with economic scenarios, on monthly data. Cox or discrete-time logistic, and why?

    Discrete-time logistic. It gives absolute PDs for every month directly, takes time-varying macro features naturally, and reuses the bank's validation tooling. Cox is better for ranking drivers.

    7. A product has 40 cancellations in 5,000 contracts. Would you fit a boosted survival model or KM with a few segments, and why?

    KM by a few segments, perhaps a small Cox model. Forty events cannot support a flexible model, and precision depends on events, not loans.
