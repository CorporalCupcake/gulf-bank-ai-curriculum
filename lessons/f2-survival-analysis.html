    Survival Analysis: Time to Default, Churn and Dormancy

    Why "when" carries more information than "whether", how to count loans that haven't finished yet, and how a hazard curve becomes the lifetime PD behind every Stage 2 provision.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost of a loan book hasn't finished its story yet. Survival analysis uses each loan for exactly as long as you've watched it, multiplies the month-by-month chances of getting through, and gives you the risk at every horizon at once.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank launched a personal finance product for new-to-country salaried customers two and a half years ago. Loans run for 36 months. The risk committee asks a simple question: what share of these loans will default within two years?

    An analyst pulls the book and divides defaults by loans booked. The number looks reassuring. But most of the book is young. A loan booked four months ago has had four months to default, not 24. Counting it as "didn't default" is like judging a marathon at the 10 km mark and declaring every runner still on the road a finisher.

    A second analyst spots the problem and throws out every loan that hasn't been watched for 24 months. Now the number looks alarming. The loans that defaulted early are all still in the data, but the young loans that are quietly paying have been removed. Throwing away the unfinished cases throws away mostly good news.

    Survival analysis takes a third route. It lines every loan up by months on book and asks, month by month: of the loans we were still watching at this age, what share defaulted? A loan contributes to every month it was observed, and stops contributing the moment we lose sight of it. Multiply the chances of getting through each month and you have the answer.

    Three things to take from it.

    • The two naive errors point in opposite directions. Counting unfinished loans as survivors biases the default rate down. Dropping them biases it up. Neither shrinks with more data: with 4,000 loans both stay wrong, just more precisely.
    • Kaplan–Meier is noisy with 40 loans but centred on the truth. Redraw the book a few times and it wanders either side. That's ordinary sampling error, which more loans fix. Bias isn't fixed by more loans.
    • The answer gets less certain the further out you look. Late in the curve only the oldest loans are still at risk, so each default moves the curve a lot. When no loan has been watched that long, Kaplan–Meier honestly says nothing.

    Push the analysis date to 60 months, so every loan has finished. The defaults-over-all-loans count still sits below Kaplan–Meier. That gap is no longer about unfinished loans. It's the loans that settled early, which genuinely can't default any more. Whether you want that gap in your number depends on the question you're answering. Hold onto that; it's the whole of Simulation 4.

    Hold onto thisA loan you're still watching isn't a good loan. It's a loan whose ending you haven't seen yet. Survival methods use what you have seen, month by month, and never pretend to know the rest.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why time matters: what a 12-month flag throws away

    Most bank scorecards are trained on a binary target: did the account reach 90 days past due within 12 months of the observation date? It works, and it's easy to validate. But the flag throws away three things.

    • When it happened. A default in month 2 and one in month 11 are the same "1". The first is often fraud or a first-payment default. The second is often a lost job. They have different causes and different fixes.
    • Everything after month 12. A loan that defaults in month 13 is labelled good. For a 48-month auto murabaha, that's most of the contract's life.
    • The youngest loans. Any loan with less than 12 months of history can't be labelled, so it's excluded. Those are the newest vintages, which reflect the current underwriting policy and the current economy. You train on the past because the present hasn't finished yet.

    A survival model keeps all three. Its target is the time to the event, plus a flag saying whether the event was seen or the observation stopped first. From one fitted model you can read off the 3-month, 12-month or lifetime probability, which matters because IFRS 9 needs both 12-month and lifetime numbers for the same loan.

    The same framing fits anything with a clock: time to churn (salary transfer moved to another bank), time to dormancy (no customer-initiated activity, which both UAE and Saudi regulators treat as a formal account status with rules attached), time to first claim on a takaful policy, time until a complaint is resolved. Defining the event precisely is half the work. "Churn" in a current account is rarely a closure. It's usually a slow drain, so you have to choose a rule, such as salary not credited for two consecutive months, and stick to it.

    Censoring: the unfinished stories

    An observation is right-censored when you know the event hadn't happened by some time, but not what happened after. In a loan book there are several sources:

    • Administrative censoring. The loan is still running on the analysis date. This is the big one in any growing book.
    • Early settlement or prepayment. The customer paid off early, refinanced, or another bank bought the loan out, which is common in UAE and Saudi personal finance where banks compete for salary transfers. Treated as censoring for the default hazard, but it's really a competing event (more below).
    • Exits for other reasons. Portfolio sales, transfers to another entity, a customer's death with takaful cover settling the balance.

    The two tempting fixes are both wrong, as Simulation 1 showed. Treating censored loans as survivors assumes they would never default, so it biases the PD down. Dropping them keeps the early defaults and discards the quiet payers, so it biases the PD up. Dropping them is also a form of selection bias (F2.6): the sample you keep was selected by the outcome.

    The correct treatment drops out of the likelihood. If you met maximum likelihood in F1.6, the idea is simple: a loan that defaulted at month t contributes "the probability of surviving to t and then defaulting". A loan censored at month c contributes only "the probability of surviving to c". Nobody invents an ending. Every estimator in this lesson is built on that rule.

    The assumption that does the workAll of this assumes non-informative censoring: a loan that drops out of view has the same future risk as a similar loan you're still watching. It fails when the reason for leaving is related to risk. If collections restructures struggling loans and the restructured contracts are booked as new accounts, the riskiest loans vanish just before they would have defaulted, and every survival estimate looks too good.

    A cousin of censoring catches people out too: left truncation, or delayed entry. Suppose the data warehouse was migrated in 2022 and older loans only appear if they were still open then. Those loans are survivors by construction. They must enter the risk set at the age they were when the data starts, not at month 0. Otherwise the early months look safer than they were.

    The survival function and the hazard

    Let T be the month in which a loan defaults. Two functions describe it, and they carry the same information.

    S(t)=P(T>t)

    The survival function: the probability a loan gets through month t without defaulting. It starts at 1 and only ever falls. The cumulative PD by month t is 1 − S(t).

    h(t)=P(T=t∣T≥t)S(t)=∏u=1t(1−h(u))

    The hazard: of the loans still alive at the start of month t, the share that default during it. Survival is the product of the chances of getting through each month. In continuous time the product becomes S(t) = exp(−H(t)), where H is the hazard added up over time.

    The hazard is the more useful of the two for thinking, because its shape tells a story. A constant hazard means age doesn't matter: the loan is "memoryless", and time to default follows the geometric or exponential distribution from F1.5. Real portfolios are rarely like that:

    • Hump-shaped: typical for unsecured personal finance and cards. Low in the first months (the customer has just passed underwriting and has cash), peaking around months 6 to 18, then falling as the weak borrowers are gone and the balance shrinks.
    • Falling: risk concentrated early, as with first-payment fraud or loans to thin-file customers whose risk resolves quickly.
    • Rising: risk building with age, as in some SME lines whose collateral ages or whose owners' circumstances drift.
    • Bathtub: high early, quiet in the middle, rising again at the end, as with balloon or bullet structures where a large final payment comes due.

    The hazard also gives you the number every Stage 2 calculation needs: the risk from now, given the loan has survived this far.

    P(default in the next 12 months∣alive at montha)=1−S(a+12)S(a)

    A loan that has already survived to month a has escaped the hazard before a. With a hump-shaped hazard, a seasoned loan is safer than a new one. With a rising hazard it's riskier.

    The yearly bars are a term structure of default: the unconditional chance that a newly booked loan defaults in year 1, year 2, and so on. They add up to the lifetime PD. This is exactly what an IFRS 9 lifetime ECL calculation multiplies by loss given default and exposure, year by year.

    The shape matters more than people expect. Two portfolios with the same lifetime PD can have very different 12-month PDs. A rising-hazard book looks safe in Stage 1 and then needs a far larger provision when its loans move to Stage 2. A falling-hazard book has already taken most of its losses in year 1.

    Kaplan–Meier: multiply the chances of getting through each month

    The Kaplan–Meier estimator is the hazard-product formula with each month's hazard replaced by what you observed: defaults that month divided by loans still at risk that month.

    S^(t)=∏ti≤t(1−dini)

    di is the number of defaults at month ti; ni is the number still being watched just before it, so it includes neither earlier defaults nor loans already censored.

    A worked example with ten loans. In month 3, one defaults: 10 at risk, so survival is multiplied by 9/10 and becomes 0.900. In month 4, two loans are censored: one settles early and one reaches the analysis date. They simply leave the risk set. In month 6, one more defaults, but now only 7 are at risk, so survival becomes 0.900 × 6/7 = 0.771. The PD by month 6 is 22.9%. The naive count, 2 out of 10, says 20%. The two censored loans weren't counted as survivors; they just stopped contributing once we lost sight of them.

    The uncertainty grows as the risk set shrinks. The standard interval comes from Greenwood's formula, which adds up d/(n(n − d)) over the months. Months with few loans at risk dominate it. Always show the number at risk under a Kaplan–Meier chart; a curve that ends on 9 loans shouldn't carry the same weight as one that ends on 9,000.

    Banks use this constantly without always calling it Kaplan–Meier. A vintage curve, the cumulative default rate by months on book for each quarter's originations, is a survival curve per cohort. When there's no censoring other than the calendar, the vintage curve and 1 − KM coincide. Once loans prepay or are sold, they diverge, and you need to know which one your chart is showing.

    Comparing two curves: the log-rank test

    Are loans to customers without a salary transfer really riskier, or is the gap between their curves noise? The log-rank test walks through every month in which a default happened. At each, it asks how many of the defaults you'd expect in group B if both groups had the same hazard, given how many loans each group had at risk. It adds up "observed minus expected" across all months and compares that total with its standard error.

    It's the standard first test, and it's most powerful when one group's hazard is a constant multiple of the other's. Its weak spot is crossing hazards. If B is riskier early and safer later, the early excess and the later deficit cancel, and the test can report no difference between two groups that behave completely differently. Simulation 3 shows this. Weighted variants that emphasise early or late months exist for exactly that case.

    Cox proportional hazards: hazard ratios

    Kaplan–Meier describes one group. To use many characteristics at once, such as salary band, debt-service ratio, nationality group, employer category or bureau score, you need a regression. The Cox model, published by David Cox in 1972 and among the most cited statistics papers ever written, is the standard one.

    h(t∣x)=h0(t)·exp(β1x1+…+βpxp)

    A baseline hazard h0(t) that can take any shape, multiplied by a factor that depends on the customer but not on time. exp(β) is the hazard ratio for a one-unit change in x.

    Cox's insight was that you can estimate the βs without ever specifying the baseline. At each moment a default happens, ask: of all the loans at risk right now, how likely was it to be this one? That probability is the defaulter's exp(βx) divided by the sum of everyone's at risk, and the baseline cancels out. Multiply those across all defaults and you have the partial likelihood. Censored loans appear in the denominators for as long as they were watched, which is how they contribute.

    Two things to say precisely in a meeting:

    • A hazard ratio is not a PD ratio. With hazard ratio 2, survival satisfies SB(t) = SA(t)2. If 15% of segment A default within 36 months, segment B's survival is 0.85² = 0.7225, a PD of 27.75%. That's 1.85 times A's PD, not 2. The gap widens as PDs grow.
    • "Proportional" is an assumption, and it fails often. The model says B's hazard is twice A's at month 1 and at month 36. In credit, effects commonly wear off: an application-time score predicts early default well and later default much less, because circumstances change. In marketing, a welcome offer reduces churn for three months and then does nothing.

    When the assumption fails, the Cox model doesn't crash. It reports a single hazard ratio that is a kind of weighted average of the true, changing ratio, weighted towards the months where most defaults happen. The survival curves it implies can then be badly wrong at some horizons. Diagnose it with scaled Schoenfeld residuals plotted against time (a flat line means proportional; the Grambsch–Therneau test formalises this) or by checking that log(−log S) curves for the groups run parallel. Fixes include stratifying on the offending variable, adding an interaction with time, or fitting a piecewise model by age band.

    The bottom chart is what a Schoenfeld residual plot is trying to show you from data: the true log hazard ratio over time against the flat line the model assumed. In the crossing case, the Cox model can report a ratio near 1 and a log-rank test can find nothing, while segment B's hazard is more than double A's early and well under half later. The right response isn't to drop the variable. It's to let its effect change with time.

    A last caution, well known in epidemiology. Even in a randomised trial, a hazard ratio isn't a clean causal effect, because the loans still at risk in later months were selected by surviving the earlier ones. If B's risky borrowers default early, B's survivors are a better-than-average group. Miguel Hernán's short paper "The hazards of hazard ratios" makes this point well. For questions such as "did the restructuring programme reduce defaults?", compare survival curves or cumulative incidence at fixed horizons, not hazard ratios.

    Competing risks: default, early settlement and everything else

    A loan can end in several ways: default, early settlement, refinancing, a buy-out by another bank, or repayment at term. Once one happens, the others can't. These are competing risks, and they change what "the probability of default" means.

    Kaplan–Meier for default, treating early settlement as censoring, estimates the default risk in a world where nobody settles early. It assumes the settled contracts would have gone on to default at the same rate as those still running. But a contract settled in month 10 will never default in month 11. If you want to know how many contracts will actually default, which is what a provision needs, you want the cumulative incidence function.

    Fd(t)=∑u=1tS(u−1)·hd(u)

    The chance of still being alive (not defaulted, not settled) at the start of month u, times the default hazard in that month, summed. S here is survival from every exit, so raising the early-settlement hazard lowers S and with it the number of defaults. 1 − KM uses only the default hazard and ignores this.

    Islamic finance makes this concrete. Murabaha contracts for cars and personal finance are often settled early, with the bank granting a rebate (ibra') on the unearned profit. Customers refinance or are bought out by a competitor offering a better rate for their salary transfer. In a book where a quarter or more of contracts settle early, 1 − KM can overstate the share that will default by a third or more.

    There are two ways to put covariates into a competing-risks model, and they answer different questions.

    • Cause-specific hazards: one ordinary Cox or discrete-time model per exit type, treating the others as censoring. Best for "what drives default?" A coefficient means what you'd expect: the effect on the default rate among contracts still running.
    • Subdistribution hazards (the Fine–Gray model): models the cumulative incidence directly. Best for "how many will default?" Its coefficients are harder to interpret, because a variable that only speeds up early settlement will show up as "reducing default".

    For prediction, you can also fit cause-specific models and combine them through the cumulative incidence formula above. That's what most IFRS 9 survival implementations that handle prepayment end up doing, often in the discrete-time form described next.

    Discrete-time hazard models: logistic regression on person-months

    Bank data is monthly. Payments are due monthly, delinquency is measured monthly, and the data warehouse snapshots monthly. That makes the discrete-time hazard model the workhorse of survival modelling in credit, and it's easier than it sounds.

    Expand the data so there's one row per loan per month on book while the loan is at risk. The target in each row is 1 if the loan defaulted in that month and 0 otherwise. A loan that defaulted in month 4 contributes four rows, three zeros and a one. A loan still running after 2 months contributes two zeros and then stops: that's all censoring means here. A contract settled early stops too.

    ContractMonth on bookSalary credited last monthCard utilisation at month startUnemployment indexDefault this month
    M-2011Yes35%1000
    M-2012Yes48%1010
    M-2013No71%1030
    M-2014No94%1031 defaulted, no more rows
    M-2021Yes12%1020
    M-2022Yes10%1030 still running at the analysis date: censored
    M-2031Yes20%1000
    M-2032Yes18%1010 settled early in month 2: censored for default

    Then fit an ordinary logistic regression (log loss, nothing new) to these rows:

    logith(t∣xt)=αt+β⊤xt

    αt is the baseline hazard by month on book, fitted as one dummy per age band or a smooth spline. xt can change every month: behaviour, balances, and macroeconomic variables. Swap the logit for the complementary log-log link and the model is exactly a Cox model for data grouped into months, with the same hazard ratios.

    Why does this work? A loan's whole history has probability "survived month 1, survived month 2, …, then defaulted (or was censored)". That's a product of one Bernoulli term per row, which is precisely the logistic-regression likelihood on the expanded data. Censoring is handled for free: a censored loan simply has no rows after it leaves.

    Banks like this because it reuses everything they already have: logistic-regression tooling, scorecard governance, familiar validation tests and coefficient sign checks. It handles time-varying covariates naturally, which is how macroeconomic scenarios get in, and it extends to competing risks by swapping the binary target for a three-way one (running, defaulted, settled early) with multinomial logistic regression. Gradient-boosted trees can be dropped in on the same rows.

    Two traps. First, rows aren't independent customers: 50,000 loans can become a million rows, and splitting train and test by row puts the same loan on both sides. Split by loan, and preferably by time. Second, every feature in a row must be known at the start of that month. A feature computed with the month-end balance leaks the outcome. That's point-in-time correctness (F3.1), and person-period data is where it most often breaks.

    The IFRS 9 link: lifetime PD, term structures and Stage 2

    IFRS 9 sorts loans into three stages. Stage 1 loans carry a 12-month expected credit loss. When credit risk has increased significantly since origination, the loan moves to Stage 2 and carries a lifetime expected credit loss. More than 30 days past due is a rebuttable presumption of a significant increase. Stage 3 is credit-impaired. A survival model is the natural way to produce the lifetime number, because it gives you the probability of default in every future period.

    ECL=∑k=1K[S(k−1)−S(k)]⏟marginal PD·LGDk·EADk·DFk

    For each future period k: the chance of defaulting in that period (the yearly bars in Simulation 2), times the loss rate, the exposure expected then, and a discount factor. For Stage 1, K covers 12 months. For Stage 2, it covers the expected life.

    Four practical consequences:

    • Stage 2 needs conditional PDs. A loan moving to Stage 2 at month 20 needs the term structure from month 20 onwards, given it has survived to month 20 and given its current, worse, state. That's 1 − S(a + k)/S(a) with today's covariates, not the origination curve.
    • Prepayment belongs in the number. IFRS 9 asks for cash flows over the expected life, considering contractual terms such as prepayment options. A lifetime PD from 1 − KM with prepayment censored overstates expected losses, as Simulation 4 showed. Use cumulative incidence, or cause-specific hazards combined properly.
    • Forward-looking means time-varying covariates. Provisions must reflect several weighted macroeconomic scenarios. In a discrete-time model you feed each scenario's path for oil prices, non-oil GDP or unemployment into the future rows and read off a term structure per scenario.
    • Vintage curves are the backtest. Plot actual cumulative default by months on book for each cohort against what the model predicted for it. A model whose curves sit consistently below the actual vintages is under-provisioning, however good its AUC.

    Survival models aren't the only route. Many banks build lifetime PDs from roll-rate or Markov transition matrices between delinquency buckets, or by extrapolating a 12-month PD with a scaling curve. Survival methods earn their place when you need loan-level, time-varying, scenario-conditioned term structures, which is what Stage 2 really asks for.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Survival analysis is where probability, likelihood, regression and credit risk meet. Expect to see these links in other lessons.

    F1.5 · DistributionsConstant hazard is the exponential

    A memoryless time to event is geometric in months and exponential in continuous time. Weibull adds a rising or falling hazard. Real books need more shape than either.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodCensoring lives in the likelihood

    Events contribute "survived, then failed"; censored cases contribute "survived". The Cox partial likelihood and the person-period log loss are both this idea.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsA hazard model is a GLM

    Discrete-time survival is a binomial GLM with a logit or complementary log-log link on person-month rows. Piecewise exponential models are Poisson GLMs with an exposure offset.

    F2.6 · Selection biasDropping the unfinished is selecting on the outcome

    Both naive fixes in Simulation 1 are selection bias. So is the shrinking at-risk group behind hazard ratios that drift over time.

    F3.1 · Point-in-time correctnessEvery row must be knowable at its start

    Time-varying covariates make survival models powerful and make leakage easy. Month-end balances in a row predicting that month's default are the classic bug.

    F4.3 · Gradient boostingTrees can fit hazards too

    XGBoost has Cox and accelerated failure time objectives. Boosted trees can also be trained on the person-period rows as a plain classifier.

    F4.5 · Temporal splitsValidate on later vintages

    Split by loan and by origination date. A random row split leaks the same loan into training and test.

    F7.4 · CalibrationA term structure must be calibrated at every horizon

    A model can rank well and still mis-state year-3 PDs. Backtest predicted against actual vintage curves, horizon by horizon.

    B4.1 and B4.3 · PD, LGD, EAD and IFRS 9Lifetime ECL is a sum over the term structure

    Marginal PDs from the survival curve multiply LGD, EAD and discount factors per period. Stage 2 uses the conditional curve from today.

    B4.4 · Stress testingScenarios enter as time-varying covariates

    Feed an oil-price shock or an unemployment path into future person-months and read off the stressed term structure.

    B6.1 · Churn and retentionTime to churn, not "will they churn"

    When a customer is likely to move their salary decides when a retention call is worth making. Event definition is half the battle.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceHazard ratios aren't causal effects

    The risk set is selected by survival. For "did the programme work?", compare cumulative incidence at fixed horizons.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Kaplan–Meier, Cox and discrete-time hazards are settled and decades old, and they're what regulators and validators expect to see. Three things are moving: machine-learning survival models as challengers, a push to make IFRS 9 term-structure modelling more standardised and transparent, and, since 2026, tabular foundation models that do survival analysis without being trained on your data.

    How we got here

    1. 1958
      Kaplan–Meier

      The product-limit estimator for survival with incomplete observations. Each event multiplies survival by one minus the share at risk that failed.

      Kaplan & Meier, JASA, 1958
    2. 1966–72
      The log-rank test

      Observed-minus-expected events summed across event times, developed by Mantel and named and formalised by Peto and Peto.

      Mantel, 1966; Peto & Peto, JRSS A, 1972
    3. 1972
      Cox proportional hazards

      Regression on the hazard with the baseline left unspecified, estimated by partial likelihood. Still the default survival regression half a century later.

      Cox, JRSS B, 1972
    4. 1982
      Discrete-time event history methods

      Showed that survival in discrete time can be fitted as logistic regression on person-period data, with time-varying covariates for free.

      Allison, Sociological Methodology, 1982
    5. 1994
      Testing proportional hazards

      Scaled Schoenfeld residuals plotted against time show how a coefficient changes, with a formal test for non-proportionality.

      Grambsch & Therneau, Biometrika, 1994
    6. 1999
      "Not if but when"

      Argued that credit scoring should model when borrowers default, not only whether. Survival analysis enters the credit-scoring mainstream.

      Banasik, Crook & Thomas, JORS, 1999
    7. 1999
      Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards

      A proportional-hazards model for the cumulative incidence of one cause in the presence of competing risks.

      Fine & Gray, JASA, 1999
    8. 2002
      Survival models for personal loans

      Applied Cox models to personal-loan data for both default and early repayment, and built survival-based scorecards.

      Stepanova & Thomas, Operations Research, 2002
    9. 2008
      Random survival forests

      Random forests adapted to right-censored data, with survival splitting rules and ensemble cumulative hazard estimates.

      Ishwaran, Kogalur, Blackstone & Lauer, Annals of Applied Statistics, 2008
    10. 2009
      Macroeconomics as time-varying covariates

      On a large UK credit card dataset, adding interest rates and unemployment to a survival model improved fit and gave a modest gain in out-of-sample default prediction. This is the template for stress testing and forward-looking ECL.

      Bellotti & Crook, JORS, 2009
    11. 2014
      IFRS 9 issued

      Expected-loss provisioning with 12-month and lifetime ECL, effective from January 2018. Lifetime PD term structures become a balance-sheet requirement.

      IASB, IFRS 9 Financial Instruments, 2014
    12. 2017
      A benchmark on real credit books

      Compared AFT, Cox, spline-based Cox and mixture cure models on ten credit datasets from Belgian and UK lenders. Splines and a cure model did well, and AUC alone failed to capture the benefit of survival models.

      Dirick, Claeskens & Baesens, JORS, 2017
    13. 2018–19
      Deep survival models

      DeepSurv replaced Cox's linear risk score with a neural network. DeepHit learned the distribution of event times directly, with competing risks, and dropped proportional hazards. Kvamme and colleagues showed scalable neural Cox models with and without proportionality (the pycox library).

      Katzman et al., BMC Med. Res. Methodol., 2018; Lee et al., AAAI 2018; Kvamme, Borgan & Scheel, JMLR, 2019
    14. 2020–22
      Survival objectives in gradient boosting

      XGBoost gained an accelerated failure time objective that handles right-, left- and interval-censored labels, alongside its Cox objective.

      Barnwal, Cho & Hocking, JCGS, 2022
    15. 2025
      A tutorial for IFRS 9 term structures

      A review of lifetime PD approaches with a worked, open-source tutorial on discrete-time survival models and diagnostics, aimed at standardising practice. A companion paper extends Cox models to recurrent defaults.

      Botha & Verster, arXiv 2507.15441, 2025; Botha et al., arXiv 2505.01044, 2025
    16. 2025–26
      Foundation models of banking event sequences

      Nubank's nuFormer and Revolut's PRAGMA learn representations of customers' transaction and event histories, fine-tuned for credit, fraud and recommendation tasks. They're not survival models, but their embeddings are natural inputs to one.

      Braithwaite et al., arXiv 2507.23267, 2025; Revolut, arXiv 2604.08649, 2026
    17. 2026
      Tabular foundation models do survival

      Recasting survival as a series of binary classifications at discretised times (the person-period trick again) let off-the-shelf tabular foundation models beat classical and deep baselines on average across 53 datasets, with no training. SurvPFN, pretrained on millions of synthetic censored datasets, was competitive on the same SurvSet collection.

      Kim, Lai & Zhang, arXiv 2601.22259, 2026; Böhm et al., SurvPFN, ICML 2026 workshop
    The current consensus, in one lineFor regulated credit models, discrete-time hazards and Cox, with competing risks handled properly and calibration checked against vintages, remain the standard. Survival forests, boosted and deep survival models are useful challengers. The 2026 foundation-model results are worth watching, and they validate an old idea: survival is classification, one period at a time.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you'll meet in real survival analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Unfinished loans counted as good

      "Default rate on last year's bookings: 2.1%." Most of those loans are months old.

      Spot itAsk for the median months on book of the denominator. If it's well below the horizon being quoted, the number is biased down.
    • Unfinished loans dropped

      "We only used loans with a full 24 months of performance." That excludes the newest vintages and, if prepaid loans were also dropped, inflates the PD.

      Spot itAsk how censored and prepaid loans were treated, in one sentence. "Excluded" is the wrong answer for both.
    • 1 − KM used as a provisioning PD

      Prepayment or early settlement treated as censoring, then the result used to count expected defaults. Overstates losses wherever settlement is common, which includes most murabaha books.

      Spot itAsk whether the lifetime PD is a cumulative incidence that accounts for prepayment. Compare it with the actual default share of a fully matured vintage.
    • Informative censoring

      Restructured, rescheduled or sold loans leave the data just before they would have defaulted. Survival looks better than reality.

      Spot itList every reason a loan can leave the dataset. For each one, ask whether those loans were riskier than the ones that stayed. Treat restructuring as an event or a state, not an exit.
    • Left truncation ignored

      Loans that entered the data after a system migration counted as at risk from month 0, although only survivors made it into the data.

      Spot itCheck for loans whose origination date is earlier than the start of the data. They need an entry time, not a start time of zero.
    • Proportional hazards assumed, never checked

      A single hazard ratio for an application score whose effect fades after a year. Early PDs are understated and late PDs overstated, or the reverse.

      Spot itAsk for the Schoenfeld residual plot or the Grambsch–Therneau test for the main variables, and for KM curves by score band. Curves that converge or cross are the giveaway.
    • Hazard ratios read as PD ratios or as causes

      "Customers without salary transfer are twice as likely to default." With HR 2, the 3-year PD ratio might be 1.8. And the ratio isn't a causal effect.

      Spot itAsk for the cumulative PD by segment at the horizon that matters, not the hazard ratio alone.
    • Leakage in person-period rows

      A month's row uses that month's end balance, days past due or a collections flag to predict default in the same month. Accuracy looks superb.

      Spot itFor each feature, ask "when was this value known?" It must be before the start of the row's month.
    • Splitting person-months at random

      The same loan's month 5 is in training and month 6 in test. Validation is optimistic.

      Spot itCheck that the split key is the loan or customer, and ideally the origination date.
    • Judging a survival model by the C-index alone

      The concordance index measures ranking, like AUC. It says nothing about whether year-3 PDs are right, and Harrell's version depends on the censoring pattern.

      Spot itAsk for time-dependent AUC and Brier score at the key horizons, plus predicted-versus-actual vintage curves.
    • AI-generated survival code that doesn't add up

      Typical signs: the event flag inverted (1 for censored), durations computed in days for some loans and months for others, a "survival" curve that rises, a Cox model fitted without any assumption check, or a lifetime PD lower than the 12-month PD.

      Spot itRecompute one point of the KM curve by hand from the at-risk table, as in Part 2. Check that event counts in the summary match the defaults in the raw data.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    "Most of this book is less than a year old, so a simple default rate understates the risk: those loans haven't had time to go bad. We measure month by month how many loans still on the book default, and build the curve from that. On that basis we expect about one in six of these contracts to default over their life, with the peak in the second year. That's the number driving the Stage 2 provision, and we check it every quarter against how each vintage actually performs."
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    "I'd treat this as a discrete-time hazard on person-months, split by loan and origination date, with the baseline by months on book as a spline and time-varying behavioural and macro covariates known at the start of each month. Early settlement is a competing risk, so I'd fit cause-specific hazards and combine them into a cumulative incidence for the lifetime PD rather than use 1 − KM. I'd check proportionality with Schoenfeld residuals if we use a Cox form, look for informative censoring from restructuring, and validate calibration horizon by horizon against vintage curves, not only with the C-index."
    "Why not just train a classifier on a 12-month default flag, like we always have?"

    For a 12-month score, that's fine and well understood. But it can't use loans with less than 12 months of history, it treats a default in month 13 as good, and it gives you one horizon only. IFRS 9 Stage 2 needs a lifetime term structure, and pricing needs expected losses over the whole term. A discrete-time hazard model is the same logistic regression on monthly rows, so it keeps the familiar tooling and adds every horizon at once.

    "The hazard ratio for this segment is 2. So they're twice as risky?"

    Their instantaneous default rate is twice as high at every age, if the proportional-hazards assumption holds. Their cumulative PD is less than double, because survival is raised to the power of the hazard ratio: 15% becomes about 28%, not 30%. And I'd check that the ratio really is constant. Application-time variables often matter a lot early and much less later.

    "We treat early settlements as censored. Is that a problem?"

    For understanding what drives default, it's fine: that's a cause-specific hazard. For counting how many contracts will default, it isn't. 1 − KM then estimates default risk in a world without early settlement, which overstates expected losses. In a murabaha book with heavy early settlement the overstatement can be a third or more. Use the cumulative incidence, which accounts for contracts leaving early.

    "Can we use XGBoost or a neural network instead of Cox?"

    Yes. XGBoost has Cox and AFT objectives, and you can train boosted trees directly on person-month rows as a classifier. Random survival forests and DeepHit-style models exist too. They're good challengers, and 2026 results show tabular foundation models doing well on public survival benchmarks. For a regulated IFRS 9 model, the bar is explainability, stability and calibration against vintages, so I'd usually keep a transparent discrete-time model as the champion and use the ML model to find what it's missing.

    "How do you know the lifetime PD is right if no loan has finished its term yet?"

    You don't know it for certain, and it's worth saying so. The late part of the curve rests on the oldest loans, so its uncertainty is wide; I'd show the number at risk and a confidence band. I'd backtest the parts we can observe, horizon by horizon, against each vintage, use older products with similar customers as a benchmark for the tail, and set a conservative overlay where the data is thin.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. Twenty loans. In month 2, two default. In month 5, three are censored (two still running, one settled early). In month 7, three more default. What's the Kaplan–Meier PD by month 7, and what do the two naive counts say?

    Month 2: 20 at risk, 2 defaults, survival × 18/20 = 0.900. Month 5: three leave the risk set. Month 7: 15 at risk, 3 defaults, survival × 12/15 = 0.800, so S(7) = 0.720 and the KM PD is 28.0%. Defaults over all loans: 5/20 = 25.0%, too low. Dropping the censored loans: 5/17 = 29.4%, too high.

    2. A constant monthly default hazard of 1%. What are the 12-month and 36-month PDs?

    12 months: 1 − 0.9912 = 11.4%. 36 months: 1 − 0.9936 = 30.4%, not 36%. Each month's 1% applies only to the loans still alive.

    3. Segment A's 36-month PD is 15%. Segment B has a constant hazard ratio of 2. What's B's 36-month PD?

    SB = SA2 = 0.852 = 0.7225, so B's PD is 27.75%, about 1.85 times A's.

    4. An auto murabaha book: 0.5% of running contracts default each month and 2% settle early. Over 48 months, what's 1 − KM for default, and what share actually default?

    1 − KM = 1 − 0.99548 = 21.4%. Cumulative incidence = 0.005/0.025 × (1 − 0.97548) = 0.2 × 0.704 = 14.1%. Using 1 − KM would overstate defaults by about half. You can check both in Simulation 4.

    5. 50,000 loans with an average of 22 months observed. How many rows does the discrete-time dataset have, and how should you split it for validation?

    About 1.1 million person-month rows. Split by loan, never by row, so no loan appears on both sides. Better still, split by origination date, training on older vintages and testing on newer ones, because that's how the model will be used.

    6. A personal finance loan with a hump-shaped hazard moves to Stage 2 at month 30. Why is the origination 36-month curve the wrong input for its lifetime ECL?

    Two reasons. It has already survived the peak months, so the unconditional curve counts risk it has escaped; you need 1 − S(30 + k)/S(30). And it moved to Stage 2 because its risk rose, so the conditional hazard should use its current covariates, such as missed salary credits or rising utilisation, not its application profile.

    7. A Cox model reports a hazard ratio of 1.1 (not significant) for thin-file customers, and the log-rank test agrees. A colleague says thin-file status doesn't matter. What do you check?

    Whether the hazards cross. Plot KM curves for both groups and the scaled Schoenfeld residuals against time. If thin-file customers default far more in the first months and less afterwards, the effects cancel in both the Cox ratio and the log-rank test. Simulation 3's crossing case shows exactly this. Model the effect as changing with time, for example with separate ratios for months 1–6 and later.
