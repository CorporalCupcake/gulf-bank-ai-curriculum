    Validating LLMs

    Part 1

    What validating an LLM means

    In one sentenceYou validate a large language model (LLM) like a person in a sensitive role: not by scanning its brain, but by collecting evidence about its behaviour and the controls around it.

    Running example: an LLM that drafts a one-paragraph summary of each customer complaint for the complaints team. The question is not "do we understand the mechanism?" but "is it fit for this task, within these limits, and will we notice if it stops being so?"

    How it differs from validating a scorecard

    • A scorecard has a few coefficients a validator can read and a challenger model to compare against.
    • An LLM has billions of weights and no readable meaning in any one of them.
    • The tempting mistake is to hand over an explanation: a heatmap or the model's own written reasons. These look like understanding but are not evidence. A written reason is just more generated text and can leave out what really drove the answer.
    Part 2

    What the validator asks for

    Independent validation follows the US supervisory guidance SR 11-7 (check your own policy and current CBUAE and SAMA texts). It asks four questions.

    1. Is the design sound? A defined use, a baseline to beat, and a reason a language model is the right tool.
    2. Does it perform well enough? Measured on realistic cases, with a margin of error, against a pass mark agreed in advance.
    3. Does it stay inside limits as things change? New wording, languages, document types or vendor model versions.
    4. Can people challenge and override it? Who approves outputs, what is logged, who is accountable.

    Question 4 is the cheapest to satisfy and cuts risk most: if a human approves every summary that matters, the accuracy needed for question 2 drops.

    Part 3

    How many tests make "good enough" defensible

    The commonest weakness is a small test set reported as one percentage. Five heads in five tosses does not prove a biased coin, and "50 cases, none failed" proves little. The rule of three gives a quick bound: with zero failures in n independent cases, the true failure rate could still be about 3 ÷ n.

    Clean test casesFailure rate could still be up to
    50about 6%
    300about 1%
    3,000about 0.1%

    How to read it

    • Report the bound, not just the rate. "0 failures in 50" means "below about 6%", not "0%".
    • Fix the pass mark first. If the summaries must fail under 1% of the time, you need roughly 300 clean cases to claim it.
    • Count independent cases. Twenty rewordings of each of 10 complaints give 200 rows but only about 10 real trials, since a model that fails a scenario tends to fail all its rewordings.
    • Use real samples. A tight bound is only as good as the test set is representative.
    Show the formula

    The rule of three is an approximation of the exact 95% upper bound when no failures are seen: the true rate p must satisfy (1 − p)n ≥ 0.05, which gives p ≈ 3 ÷ n. With some failures, use the exact (Clopper-Pearson) bound.

    Part 4

    Probes: a smoke detector inside the model

    As an LLM reads a prompt, each layer produces a list of numbers (activations). A probe is a small classifier, often logistic regression, trained on those numbers to predict a label such as "jailbreak attempt" or "ordinary request". Think of a smoke detector, not a fire report: it says something may be wrong, not why.

    • Strong at: near-zero cost, because the activations are already computed. A probe and a text monitor make different mistakes, so they work best together.
    • Weak at: it detects correlation with a label on its training data. A high score is a cue to look, not a verdict, and does not show the model "knows" or "intends" anything. One trained on English jailbreaks may behave differently on Gulf Arabic or 100-page PDFs, and it needs retraining for each new model version.
    • Steering pushes the model along a learned direction while it runs. Pushing along "refusal" makes it refuse more, and subtracting it can make it comply (Arditi et al., 2024). That is a dial for caution, and a warning that some safeguards are thin when someone has weight access. Hosted APIs do not expose activations, so this mainly concerns on-premises open-weight models.
    Part 5

    Which evidence to rely on

    EvidenceValidators accept it asWeakness
    Test set with boundCore proof of performanceOnly as good as the sample
    Human approval and logsStrong risk controlRubber-stamping
    Model's written reasonsNot evidenceCan omit the real driver
    ProbesExtra monitoring layerCorrelation only; shifts with new data

    In banking, no validator will accept "we steered it to be safe" or "the model explained itself" without behavioural test evidence behind it.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Offering an explanation as evidence

      Heatmaps and the model's own reasons are not proof. Offer test results and controls.

    • One percentage from a small set

      Report the bound. 50 clean cases only support 'below about 6%'.

    • Counting paraphrases as separate tests

      Rewordings of one scenario are not independent trials.

    • Treating a probe score as a verdict

      It is a cue to look. Test it on your languages and document types.

    1. What kind of evidence do validators accept for an LLM?

    Evidence that would change their mind if bad: failure rates with bounds, comparison with a simpler alternative, controls that limit damage, and monitoring with triggers.

    2. 50 test cases, no failures. What can you claim?

    Only that the failure rate is probably below about 6% (3 ÷ 50).

    3. Why does a human approval gate lower the testing burden?

    It bounds the damage a wrong output can do, so the accuracy needed is lower.

    4. What is a probe, and what is it not?

    A small classifier on the model's internal numbers that flags things like jailbreaks. It shows correlation with a label, not why or what the model intends.

    5. Why retrain probes and rerun tests after a model update?

    Hosted models change under you, so earlier evidence may no longer apply.
