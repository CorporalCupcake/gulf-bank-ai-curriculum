    Validating an LLM You Cannot Explain: Evidence Packages, Test Bounds and Probes

    A validator cannot read a language model's reasoning off its weights. What they can accept is behaviour measured with honest error bars, controls around the model, and monitoring that keeps watching, now including cheap probes that read internal state.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDo not make a language model explain itself to a validator; give them evidence about what it does (test sets with bounds, comparisons, sensitivity checks), what surrounds it (approval gates, change control) and how you will notice it going wrong (monitoring, with internal-state probes as a promising extra layer, not yet a proof).

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of how a bank approves a person for a sensitive role. Nobody scans their brain. It checks references, sets a realistic test, limits what they can sign alone, reviews their work and watches their results. That is an evidence package about behaviour and controls. The question is not "do we understand the mechanism?" but "is it fit for this purpose, within these limits, and will we notice if it stops being so?"

    An LLM works the same way. For a logistic-regression scorecard the validator reads coefficients and compares with a challenger. An LLM has billions of weights and nobody can say what one means. The tempting wrong move is to hand over an explanation: a saliency heatmap, the model's own written reasons. These look like understanding but are usually not evidence. A written reason is more generated text and can omit what really drove the answer.

    The newer piece is probes: small classifiers that read a model's internal activations for signals such as "this is a jailbreak attempt". Think of a smoke detector, not a fire report: it says something may be wrong, not why.

    Hold onto thisValidators accept evidence that would change their mind if it were bad: failure rates with bounds, comparison with a simpler alternative, controls that limit damage, and monitoring with triggers. They need no hole in the case, not an understanding of the weights.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The validator's four questions, and what answers them

    Independent validation asks four things, following SR 11-7 (check your own policy and current CBUAE and SAMA texts).

    1. Is the design sound for the purpose? A defined use, a baseline to beat, and a reason a language model is the right tool.
    2. Does it perform well enough? Measured on realistic cases, with an interval, against a threshold agreed in advance.
    3. Does it stay inside limits as things change? New wording, language, document type or vendor model version.
    4. Can people challenge and override it? Who approves outputs, what is logged, who is accountable.

    A boring item (a labelled test set with an interval) outweighs an impressive one (internals). Question 4 is the cheapest to satisfy and lowers risk most: if a human approves every output that matters, the performance needed for question 2 drops.

    How many tests make "good enough" defensible

    A small test set reported as one percentage is the commonest weakness. Five heads in five coin tosses does not prove a biased coin; likewise "50 cases, none failed" proves little. With zero failures in n independent cases, the true failure rate could still be about 3 ÷ n (the "rule of three"). 50 clean cases: up to about 6%. Below 1% needs about 300 clean cases; below 0.1%, about 3,000. With some failures the simulation uses the exact bound (F2.1, A4.1).

    Test cases are also rarely independent. 20 paraphrases of each of 10 scenarios gives 200 rows but about 10 real trials, because a model that fails a scenario tends to fail all its rewordings.

    Fix the pass bar before testing, report the bound not just the rate, and build the set from real production samples. A tight bound is only as good as the sample is representative (F2.6).

    What a probe is, and what it can and cannot tell you

    As a model reads a prompt, each layer produces a vector of numbers (activations) per token. A probe is a small classifier, often logistic regression (F4.2), trained to predict a label from those vectors, for example jailbreak versus ordinary request. Its score says how much the model's internal state resembles the jailbreak examples (R5).

    Why a bank cares: the activations are already computed, so a probe adds almost no cost, unlike a second LLM re-reading the text. A text monitor sees the words; a probe sees how the model represented them. They make different mistakes, so they work best together.

    What it is not: a probe detects correlation with a label on its training data. A high score is a cue to look, not a verdict, and does not show the model "knows" or "intends" anything. One trained on English jailbreaks may behave differently on Gulf Arabic or 100-page PDFs, and it needs retraining for each new model version.

    Steering: the other thing you can do with a direction

    If a direction matches a concept, you can also push on it while the model runs. Pushing along "refusal" makes a model refuse more; subtracting it can make it comply (Arditi et al., 2024).

    It cuts both ways. As a control it is a dial for more cautious behaviour without retraining. As a risk it shows how thin some safeguards are: anyone with weight access can remove them. Hosted APIs do not expose activations, so it matters mainly for on-premises open-weight models (A5.2, P4.2) and for judging vendor safety claims. No validator will accept "we steered it to be safe" without behavioural test evidence.

    Probes in production: the cascade

    Think of airport security: a quick scan for everyone, a manual search only for the ambiguous. In a cascade the probe scores every request at near-zero cost. Low scores pass, very high scores are blocked, and the uncertain middle goes to a costlier check such as an LLM judge (A4.2) or a human.

    Three lessons. The base rate rules (F1.4, F3.5): with rare violations, even a tiny false-alarm rate leaves precision well below 100%, so blocked requests need human review. Thresholds are tuned for a distribution: when inputs change, recall changes silently, so monitoring needs labelled samples of live traffic (F7.7). The cascade reduces cost, not risk: the judge is itself an unvalidated model, and its error rate belongs in the package. Probes can also be fooled by crafted prompts, so a probe is one layer of defence, never the only one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "Explainability" offered as validation

      A heatmap or the model's rationale presented as proof.

      Spot itAsk what test shows the explanation is true of the model. "Plausible" is illustration, not evidence.
    • Zero failures in a small test

      "We ran 40 cases, all passed."

      Spot itRule of three: 3 ÷ 40 is about 7.5%. Is that the failure rate they meant to show?
    • Paraphrase inflation

      A large row count made of rewordings of a few scenarios.

      Spot itAsk how many distinct scenarios there are.
    • Test set written by the builders

      Cases the developer imagined, in the style the prompt was tuned on.

      Spot itAsk where cases came from. Keep a held-out set nobody has seen.
    • Pass bar set after the results

      "85% is good enough" decided once 85% was observed.

      Spot itLook for a dated test plan that precedes the results.
    • English only

      Everything tested in English for customers who write Arabic, Arabizi and mixed text.

      Spot itCount rows per language and dialect; report bounds per language.
    • Rubber-stamp review counted as a control

      A human approves 99.9% of outputs in seconds.

      Spot itAsk for override rate and review time. Seed known-bad outputs.
    • A probe or judge treated as ground truth

      "The monitor found 0.02% violations" reported as the violation rate.

      Spot itAsk for the monitor's recall on a labelled sample.
    • Monitoring thresholds never revisited

      Tuned once on launch data; new languages or a model update shift them silently.

      Spot itAsk when the monitor was last checked against fresh labelled traffic.
    • A vendor model update with no re-test

      The provider changes the model behind the same name; the evidence goes stale.

      Spot itCheck the version is pinned and a regression run is logged per change.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why does a validator not accept "the model explained its reasoning" as evidence?

    The explanation is generated text and can omit what actually drove the answer. Evidence must look different if the model were bad: measured behaviour.

    2. You ran 100 independent test cases with no failures. What can you claim about the failure rate, and roughly how many clean cases would you need to claim below 0.5%?

    Only that it is probably below about 3% (the rule of three: 3 ÷ 100). To claim below 0.5% you need about 600 clean cases.

    3. Your 500 test rows are 25 scenarios each in 20 phrasings. Roughly how many trials do you really have?

    About 25. With zero failures the bound is near 3 ÷ 25 = 12%, not 3 ÷ 500 = 0.6%.

    4. Name the four validator questions and one cheap piece of evidence for each.

    Soundness: scope plus challenger. Performance: expert-labelled test set with a bound. Stability and monitoring: sensitivity tests, pinned versions, monitoring plan with triggers. Governance: approval gates and logs.

    5. Violations are rare (0.1% of requests) and the cascade catches 85% of them, with a very low false-alarm rate. Why can a large share of blocked requests still be genuine?

    Genuine requests outnumber violations a thousand to one, so even a tiny false-alarm rate produces a comparable number of wrong stops. In this case about one stop in three is genuine. The process for releasing blocked requests matters as much as the probe.

    6. What does a probe's high score tell you, and what does it not?

    The activations resemble the labelled examples it was trained on, so look closer. It does not say why, prove intent, or hold on unlike inputs (other languages, long documents).

    7. Why might a bank using a hosted API be unable to use probes or steering?

    Both need internal activations, which hosted APIs do not expose. They suit self-hosted open-weight models or a vendor's own safety stack. For hosted models, use behavioural evidence, logging and external monitors.

    8. Your monitor reports 0.02% violations among requests. What do you ask before believing it?

    Its recall and false-alarm rate on a labelled sample of current traffic. At 50% recall the true rate is about double, and if traffic has shifted (say, partly Arabic) recall may have changed.
