    Validating an LLM You Cannot Explain: Evidence Packages, Test Bounds and Probes

    A validator cannot read a language model's reasoning off its weights, and nobody can yet. What they can accept is behaviour measured with honest error bars, controls around the model, and monitoring that keeps watching, now including cheap probes that read the model's internal state.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDo not try to make a language model explain itself to a validator; give them evidence about what it does (test sets with bounds, comparisons, sensitivity checks), what surrounds it (approval gates, change control) and how you will notice it going wrong (monitoring, where internal-state probes are a promising extra layer but not yet a proof).

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit team wants to use an LLM to draft the first page of a credit memo from a client's financial statements. Model validation, the independent second line, receives the request. For a logistic-regression scorecard the validator's job is familiar: read the coefficients, check the signs make sense, test stability, compare with a challenger. For the LLM there are no coefficients to read. There are billions of weights and nobody, at any institution, can say what a given one means.

    So what can the validator accept? Think of how a bank approves a person for a sensitive role. Nobody scans their brain. The bank checks references, sets them a realistic test, limits what they can sign alone, has a manager review their work, and watches their results. That is an evidence package about behaviour and controls. It works because the question is not "do we understand the mechanism?" but "is it fit for this purpose, within these limits, and will we notice if it stops being so?"

    The tempting wrong move is to hand the validator an explanation instead: a saliency heatmap over the input, a model's own written reasons, a diagram of attention. These look like understanding. They usually are not evidence. A model's written reason is another piece of generated text, and studies show it can omit what really drove the answer (Part 4). Heatmaps can be unfaithful to the computation. A validator who has read the model-risk guidance will ask the sharp question: how do you know this explanation is true of the model?

    The newer part of the story is the second half of the title. Researchers can now read directions in a model's internal activations that correlate with concepts such as "this request is a jailbreak attempt" or "the model is unsure". A small classifier on those activations, a probe, is cheap enough to run on every request. Labs have deployed them to catch misuse. For a bank this opens a monitoring layer that sees something a text-only monitor cannot. It is a smoke detector, not a post-mortem report. It tells you something may be wrong, not why.

    Hold onto thisValidators accept evidence that would change their mind if it were bad: measured failure rates with bounds, comparisons with a simpler alternative, controls that limit damage, and monitoring with triggers. They do not need to understand the weights; they need to be unable to find a hole in the case.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The validator's four questions, and what answers them

    Strip away the paperwork and independent validation asks four things. The names differ across institutions; the content is stable and follows the logic of SR 11-7, which most Gulf banks' model-risk policies echo in spirit (check your own policy and the current CBUAE and SAMA texts; Part 3 says more).

    1. Is the design sound for the purpose? (conceptual soundness) Does the approach make sense for this task, and is the scope clear? For an LLM, this means a defined use, a baseline to beat, and an argument for why a language model is the right tool.
    2. Does it perform well enough? (outcome analysis) Measured on realistic cases, with an interval, against a threshold the business agreed in advance.
    3. Does it stay inside limits as things change? (ongoing monitoring and sensitivity) Different wording, language, document type, a new model version from the vendor.
    4. Can people challenge and override it? (governance and use controls) Who approves outputs, what is logged, who is accountable.

    Simulation 1 maps ten kinds of evidence onto these four. Two patterns to notice. First, a cheap, boring item (a labelled test set with an interval) carries more weight than an impressive-looking one (internals). Second, question 4 is often the cheapest to satisfy and the one that most lowers risk: if a human approves every output that matters, the required performance for question 2 drops.

    How many tests make "good enough" a claim you can defend

    The most common weakness in an LLM evidence package is a test set that is too small to say anything, reported as a single percentage. "We tested 50 cases and none failed" sounds reassuring. The statistics say otherwise. If you see zero failures in n independent cases, the upper 95% bound on the true failure rate is

    pupper=1−0.051/n≈3n

    The right-hand side is the "rule of three" (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983). With 50 clean cases the true failure rate could still be as high as about 6%. To show it is below 1% you need about 300 clean cases; below 0.1%, about 3,000. If there are some failures, use the exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) upper bound, which the simulation computes. This is the same logic as F2.1 intervals and A4.1 statistics for evals.

    A second trap is that LLM test cases are rarely independent. If you wrote 20 paraphrases of each of 10 scenarios, you have 200 rows but closer to 10 independent trials: the model that fails a scenario tends to fail all its paraphrases. The effective sample is smaller, and the interval wider, than the row count suggests. The simulation applies a rough correction (divide by the cluster size) so the effect is visible.

    Practical consequences for the package. State the pass bar before testing. Report the bound, not just the point rate. Grow the set from real production samples, since your invented cases will be easier than real ones. And note that a tight bound on a bad rate does not rescue a test set that does not look like the bank's inputs: a bound is only as good as the sample is representative. That is the same point as F2.6 on selection bias.

    What a probe is, and what it can and cannot tell you

    When a transformer reads a prompt, each layer produces a vector of numbers (activations) for each token. A probe is a small, simple classifier, often logistic regression (F4.2), trained to predict a label from those vectors. Train it on activations from prompts you know are jailbreak attempts versus ordinary requests, and it learns a direction in activation space along which the two groups differ. The probe's score for a new prompt is its position along that direction. The method goes back to linear classifier probes on neural-net layers (Alain and Bengio, 2016) and ties to the broader finding that many concepts are roughly linear directions in the model's internal space (A7's neighbour lesson, R5, covers this).

    Why a bank cares: a probe reads activations the model has already computed, so it adds almost nothing to the cost of the request, unlike a second LLM that must re-read the whole text. A text-only monitor sees the words; a probe sees a proxy for how the model has represented them. In research on production misuse filters the two make different mistakes, which is why they work best together.

    What a probe is not. It detects correlation with a label on the data you trained it on. It does not show the model "knows" or "intends" anything, and a high probe score is a cue to look, not a verdict. A probe trained on English jailbreaks may behave differently on Gulf Arabic or on 100-page PDFs; a published production study of probes for Gemini found long inputs a weak spot (Part 4). A probe that is also trained on the model it watches cannot be moved to a different model or even a new version without retraining.

    Steering: the other thing you can do with a direction

    If a direction in activation space corresponds to a concept, you can not only read it but push on it: add a scaled copy of the vector to the activations while the model runs. This is activation steering (Turner et al., 2023, and representation engineering, Zou et al., 2023). Pushing along a "refusal" direction makes a model refuse more; subtracting it can make it comply with requests it would normally decline (Arditi et al., 2024, found refusal is mediated by a single direction in many open models).

    For monitoring, the relevance is two-sided. As a control, steering is attractive: a dial that might nudge a model towards more cautious behaviour without retraining. As a risk, it shows how thin some safeguards are: anyone with weight access can remove a refusal direction. For a bank using a hosted API, steering is mostly not available (you do not get the activations). It matters for on-premises open-weight models (A5.2, P4.2), where you do control them, and for judging how much weight to put on a vendor's safety claims. Treat steering as a research tool today. Its effects are often blunt and can damage fluency; no validator will accept "we steered it to be safe" without behavioural test evidence of the effect.

    Probes in production: the cascade

    The deployment pattern that has emerged is a cascade. The probe scores every request at near-zero cost. Very low scores pass; very high scores are blocked or escalated; the uncertain middle band goes to a more expensive check, such as a prompted LLM judge (A4.2) or a human. You choose two thresholds and trade four things: recall, false alarms, cost, and speed. The simulation shows the trade-off with the arithmetic exposed.

    Three lessons in that simulation. The base rate rules (F1.4, F3.5): with rare violations, even a very low false-alarm rate gives a precision well below 100%, so the blocked set needs human review. Thresholds are tuned for a distribution: when inputs change, recall changes silently; that is why monitoring needs labelled samples of live traffic, not just the probe's own scores (F7.7). And the cascade reduces cost, not risk: the judge is itself an unvalidated model, and its error rate belongs in the package too.

    One more caution for validators. Probes can be attacked. A prompt crafted to move the model's internal state while keeping its output unchanged can fool a probe, as shown for latent-space defences (Bailey et al., 2024, from memory). So a probe is a layer of defence in depth, never the only one.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Two literatures are converging: model-risk practice, which was built for statistical models and is adapting to generative AI, and interpretability research, which has moved from curiosity to engineering but is not yet at the standard of proof a validator expects.

    1. 2011
      SR 11-7 sets the validation vocabulary

      The US supervisory guidance on model risk management set out conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring and outcomes analysis, and the idea of effective challenge. Its structure still shapes how many banks, including in the Gulf, frame validation.

      Federal Reserve and OCC, 2011
    2. 2016
      Linear probes as a diagnostic

      Alain and Bengio trained linear classifiers on each layer of a network to see where information becomes linearly available. The origin of the probing toolkit (from memory).

      Alain & Bengio, arXiv 1610.01644
    3. 2023
      Directions you can read and push

      Representation engineering and activation addition showed that concept directions in activations can both be read out and used to steer behaviour at inference time.

      Zou et al., arXiv 2310.01405; Turner et al., arXiv 2308.10248
    4. 2023–25
      Stated reasons are not a reliable window

      Experiments showed that chain-of-thought explanations can omit the real influence on an answer, such as a planted hint or bias. Later work on reasoning models found hints used but rarely acknowledged. This is why generated explanations are not accepted as evidence (from memory).

      Turpin et al., arXiv 2305.04388; Chen et al. (Anthropic), arXiv 2505.05410
    5. 2024–25
      Features and circuits in production-scale models

      Sparse autoencoders extracted millions of interpretable features from a production model, and circuit tracing then followed how some behaviours are computed. Impressive, partial, and costly; coverage of the model is incomplete (from memory).

      Templeton et al., 2024; Lindsey et al., 2025 (Anthropic, transformer-circuits.pub)
    6. 2025
      Probes for high-stakes interactions, and the cascade

      Probes were compared with LLM monitors for spotting high-stakes conversations. Probes were far cheaper, comparable in some settings, and a probe-plus-LLM cascade beat either alone at fixed compute. One reported limit: the best probe found fewer high-stakes cases than the best LLM baseline at a 1% false-positive rate.

      McKenzie et al., arXiv 2506.10805
    7. Jan 2026
      Production-ready probes at Google DeepMind

      A paper on probes for cyber-misuse and jailbreak detection in Gemini reports that probes complement black-box classifiers and informed deployed mitigations. Probes struggled to generalise to long-context inputs, a production distribution shift. The architecture routes the uncertain middle band to a prompted LLM, the cascade of Simulation 3. A secondary digest reports Anthropic describing a similar architecture; I did not read that paper.

      arXiv 2601.11516, 2026
    8. Apr 2026
      US model-risk guidance replaced, with generative AI out of scope

      The US banking agencies issued SR 26-2 and OCC Bulletin 2026-13 on 17 April 2026, superseding SR 11-7, and that the new text places generative and agentic AI outside its scope (checked on the Federal Reserve site, November 2026). Commentary adds that a request for information on AI is expected. The practical reading: the principles still guide validators, and expect frameworks such as NIST AI RMF to fill the gap.

      Federal Reserve SR 26-2; Fiddler AI and Fruggr summaries

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in evidence packages, human-written and AI-written. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "Explainability" offered as validation

      A heatmap, attention plot or the model's own rationale presented as proof the model is sound.

      Spot itAsk what test shows the explanation is true of the model. If the answer is "it looks plausible", it is illustration, not evidence.
    • Zero failures in a small test

      "We ran 40 cases, all passed."

      Spot itCompute the rule of three: 3 ÷ 40 is about 7.5%. Ask what failure rate that is supposed to demonstrate.
    • Paraphrase inflation

      A large row count built from rewordings of a few scenarios.

      Spot itAsk how many distinct underlying scenarios there are, and report the bound on that number.
    • Test set written by the builders

      Cases the developer imagined, in the style the prompt was tuned on. Performance is optimistic, and the prompt was tuned against them.

      Spot itAsk where the cases came from and whether any were used to tune the prompt. Keep a held-out set nobody has seen.
    • Pass bar set after the results

      "85% is good enough" decided once 85% was observed.

      Spot itLook for a dated test plan that precedes the results.
    • English only

      Everything tested in English for a bank whose customers write Arabic, Arabizi and mixed text.

      Spot itCount test rows per language and dialect. Report the bound per language, not pooled.
    • Rubber-stamp review counted as a control

      A human approves 99.9% of outputs in seconds, and the package counts it as risk reduction.

      Spot itAsk for override rate and review time. Seed known-bad outputs and see whether reviewers catch them.
    • A probe or judge treated as ground truth

      Reporting "the monitor found 0.02% violations" as the violation rate.

      Spot itAsk for the monitor's own recall on a labelled sample. The reported rate is the true rate times recall, plus false alarms.
    • Monitoring thresholds that never get revisited

      Probe or judge thresholds tuned once on launch data. New languages, longer inputs or a model update shift them silently.

      Spot itAsk when the monitor was last checked against fresh labelled traffic, and what triggers a recheck.
    • A vendor model update with no re-test

      The provider changes the model behind the same name and the evidence goes stale.

      Spot itCheck the model identifier is a pinned version and that a regression run is logged for each change.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a head of model risk · 30 seconds
    “We can't explain this model's internals, and I won't pretend a heatmap does. What I'll give you is evidence about behaviour: a test set drawn from real credit memos, labelled by two senior analysts, with a pass bar fixed in advance and a confidence bound on the failure rate. Plus a comparison with the manual process, sensitivity tests across wording and language, a human approval step with override logging, and a monitoring plan with a number that triggers a pause.”
    To a technical validator or interviewer
    “The package maps to the usual four questions. Soundness: scope, challenger, grounding. Outcomes: clustered test set with Clopper-Pearson bounds per language. Stability: prompt and version sensitivity with a frozen regression suite. Governance: gates, logs, change control. For monitoring on self-hosted models, we're piloting a probe on internal activations as a cheap first filter, with an LLM judge on the grey zone. But the probe's recall is measured on labelled live samples and rechecked after any distribution change. It supports the case; it isn't the case.”
    “Can't you just show us why it gave this answer?”

    Not reliably, and I'd rather say so than show a plausible story. For each output we can show the source passages it cites, so a reviewer can check the claim directly. For the model overall, we evidence behaviour: error rates with bounds, stability under rewording, and what happens when it's wrong. Explanations of internals are research tools today; I'd treat them as supporting colour, not as proof.

    “You tested 200 cases and had one failure. Is that good enough?”

    It depends on the bar and on how independent the cases are. One failure in 200 gives an upper 95% bound of about 2.4%. If those 200 are 20 scenarios in 10 phrasings each, the effective sample is nearer 20 and the bound is much wider. I'd tell you the bound, the number of distinct scenarios, and what we'd need to add to reach the agreed bar.

    “Why should we trust a probe that reads the model's internals?”

    You shouldn't trust it blindly. It's a classifier trained on labelled examples, so its worth is what we measure: recall and false alarms on fresh labelled traffic, in each language. Its advantage is cost and a different error pattern from a text judge. We use it as the first stage of a cascade and keep a human or judge behind it, and we recheck it when inputs or the model change.

    “Is this covered by the model risk rules?”

    Our policy treats LLM applications that influence decisions as in scope, and I'd apply the same four-question structure regardless of how a particular regulator words it. Rules for generative AI are still moving (the US replaced SR 11-7 in 2026 and reportedly carved generative AI out for now), so I'd confirm the current CBUAE or SAMA position with compliance rather than quote from memory.

    “What's the cheapest control that reduces risk the most?”

    Usually a well-designed human approval step, because it changes the consequence of an error, not only its probability. The caveat is that review must be real: measure override rates and review time, and seed known errors to check reviewers catch them.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a validator not accept "the model explained its reasoning" as evidence?

    The explanation is generated text. Experiments show it can leave out what actually drove the answer, so it may not be true of the model's computation. Evidence must be something that would look different if the model were bad: measured behaviour, not a story.

    2. You ran 100 independent test cases with no failures. What is the upper 95% bound on the failure rate? How many would you need to show it is below 0.5%?

    1 − 0.05^(1/100) ≈ 2.9%, close to 3 ÷ 100. For 0.5% you need about 3 ÷ 0.005 = 600 clean independent cases (exactly 598).

    3. Your 500 test rows are 25 scenarios each in 20 phrasings. Roughly how many trials do you really have?

    About 25. Rows that fail together are not independent. With zero failures the bound is near 3 ÷ 25 = 12%, not 3 ÷ 500 = 0.6%.

    4. Name the four validator questions and one cheap piece of evidence for each.

    Soundness: scope plus a challenger comparison. Performance: expert-labelled test set with a bound. Stability and monitoring: sensitivity tests, pinned versions, a monitoring plan with triggers. Governance: approval gates and logs.

    5. Of 100,000 daily requests, 100 are real violations (0.1%). A probe-first cascade catches 85 of them and wrongly stops 0.05% of the 99,900 genuine requests. Using these numbers, what share of stops are real?

    Genuine stops: 99,900 × 0.0005 ≈ 50. Real stops: 85. Precision = 85 ÷ 135 ≈ 63%. Roughly one in three stops needs human release, so the process behind the block matters as much as the probe.

    6. What does a probe's high score tell you, and what does it not?

    It says the activations resemble those of the labelled examples the probe was trained on, so the request deserves a closer look. It does not say why, does not prove intent, and may not hold on inputs unlike the training data (other languages, long documents).

    7. Why might a bank using a hosted API be unable to use probes or steering?

    Both need access to the model's internal activations, which hosted APIs normally do not expose. They apply to self-hosted open-weight models, or to the vendor's own safety stack. For hosted models, rely on behavioural evidence, logging and external monitors.

    8. Your monitor reports 0.02% violations among requests. What do you ask before believing it?

    The monitor's recall and false-alarm rate on a labelled sample of current traffic. If recall is 50%, the true rate is about twice what it reports; if it was tuned on English and traffic is now partly Arabic, recall may have changed.
