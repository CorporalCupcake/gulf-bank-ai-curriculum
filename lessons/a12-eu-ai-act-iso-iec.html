    AI Frameworks, Model Risk for LLMs and Agents, and AI Incidents

    Which global rulebooks actually reach a Gulf bank, what a validator can honestly test when the model is a vendor's API, and how to log, handle and learn from the day an AI system goes wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceYou cannot validate a model you do not own, so you validate the system you built around it, monitor it for silent vendor change, keep an inventory that says where every AI is, and rehearse what to do when one misbehaves, mapping all of it to whichever framework your regulator or partner asks about.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Classic model risk management was designed for a bank's own models: a credit scorecard built in-house, with known data, fixed coefficients and a document you can read line by line. A validator can rebuild it, challenge it and sign it off.

    Now picture a customer-service assistant. The bank wrote a system prompt and a retrieval pipeline. The reasoning engine is a vendor's model behind an API. Nobody at the bank has its weights, its training data or its changelog. The vendor can improve it next Tuesday without asking. It answers in Arabic and English, it is not deterministic, and in an agent setup it can also do things: send an email, open a case, move a limit.

    The old playbook does not fit, but the old purpose does: make sure someone independent has looked at the thing, knows its limits, and will notice when it changes. Three pieces of work follow.

    • Frameworks. The EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF are not Gulf laws. They matter because European customers, partners, vendors and group parents carry them to you, and because Gulf regulators and auditors borrow their vocabulary. You need a map, not a legal opinion.
    • Model risk for LLMs and agents. Validate what you control (the prompt, retrieval, tools, permissions, the evidence on your own task) and monitor what you do not (the vendor's model).
    • Incidents and inventories. You cannot manage an AI you did not know existed, and you cannot learn from a failure you did not record.
    Hold onto thisThe unit of governance has changed from the model to the system around a model: model, prompt, data, tools, permissions, humans and monitoring, all versioned together. Every question in this lesson is a version of "which part of that system do we control, and what is our evidence for it?"
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The three frameworks: law, standard, guide in brief

    They are different kinds of thing, and mixing them up is the commonest error in meetings. One is a law with fines. One is a standard you can be audited against. One is a voluntary playbook.

    EU AI ActISO/IEC 42001NIST AI RMF

    The EU AI Act in a paragraph. It bans a short list of practices (for example social scoring and emotion recognition in the workplace), regulates "high-risk" uses listed in an annex, and imposes light transparency duties on things like chatbots and synthetic media. For a bank, the headline high-risk use is evaluating the creditworthiness of natural persons or setting their credit score. The same annex excludes systems used to detect financial fraud from that credit entry. It also covers employment uses, such as CV screening. General-purpose model obligations fall mostly on the model providers, not on you, unless you fine-tune or rebrand a model so heavily that you become one.

    Timing moved. The high-risk obligations were due in August 2026. A "Digital Omnibus" agreed in 2026 defers the standalone high-risk categories (the ones that cover credit scoring) to 2 December 2027, and the product-embedded ones to August 2028. Prohibitions and basic AI-literacy duties were already running from 2025. Sources differ on details, so check the Official Journal text before quoting a date to a lawyer.

    ISO/IEC 42001 is the one to know if a European counterparty asks "are you certified?". It resembles ISO 27001 for information security: you show a functioning management system, not a perfect model. ISO/IEC 23894 gives companion guidance on AI risk. NIST AI RMF is most useful as a way to structure a risk conversation: Govern (who is accountable), Map (context and impact), Measure (tests and metrics), Manage (treat and monitor).

    A point validators like: these do not conflict. A sensible bank keeps one internal control set, anchored on its regulator's expectations (A12.1) and its own model risk policy (G3), and keeps a mapping table showing which control satisfies which clause of each framework. That is cheaper and clearer than three parallel compliance projects.

    Model risk rules applied to LLMs and agents

    Start with what model risk management (MRM) asks for, in the form that came from the US guidance known as SR 11-7 and is mirrored in most banks' policies, including Gulf ones (G3.2). Three pillars: development and use (sound design, documented, fit for purpose), validation (independent, with "effective challenge": someone competent and senior enough to push back) and governance (inventory, roles, policy, reporting). Validation traditionally checks conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring and outcomes analysis.

    Every one of those assumed you could open the model. With a vendor API you cannot. So ask the practical question: what is a validator actually able to examine? The answer is a table.

    ComponentCan the bank inspect it?What is validated instead
    Vendor model weights and training dataNoVendor documentation and attestations (model card, safety reports, change policy); contract terms on notice, retention and training-on-your-data; behavioural tests on your task. Conceptual soundness becomes "is this the right tool for this job, and what do we know about its failure modes?"
    System prompt and configurationYesTreated as code: versioned, reviewed, tested (P2.5). Small wording changes can move behaviour, so every change goes through the eval set.
    Retrieval and data sourcesYesRetrieval quality, freshness, access control, and groundedness of answers (A4.3). Often the biggest source of real error.
    Tools, permissions, approval gatesYesLeast-privilege review, action limits, human approval for money or customer-impacting steps (M4.5), logging of every call. This is where agents differ most from chatbots.
    Task-level performanceYes, by testingA fixed, representative, Arabic-and-English evaluation set with intervals (A4.1), slice results, adversarial and injection tests (G1.1).
    Ongoing behaviourOnly by monitoringA regression suite run on a schedule and on every vendor notice; sampled human review of live outputs; drift and complaint signals.

    Two things make LLMs genuinely harder than a scorecard, and both are worth saying out loud.

    • Outputs are open-ended and stochastic. There is no single AUC. You define success per task (right fee quoted, correct citation, refused when it should) and measure it with enough cases to see the difference you care about. Run the same prompt twice and you may get different answers, so test repeatedly and report a rate.
    • The thing underneath can change without you. If you call a floating alias such as "latest", the vendor can swap the model behind it. If you pin a dated snapshot, it will eventually be retired and you must migrate. Either way, the model you validated is not the model that runs in a year. Vendor model risk (G3.6) is therefore part of the job, not a separate topic.

    One regulatory point to hold with care. In the US, the model risk guidance was replaced in April 2026 (SR 26-2 and OCC Bulletin 2026-13, issued jointly with the FDIC on 17 April 2026, checked on the Federal Reserve site in November 2026). The new text puts generative and agentic AI outside its scope, says it sets no enforceable standards, and is aimed mainly at banks with over USD 30 billion in assets. The practical consequence for a Gulf bank is not "LLMs are exempt". It is that no regulator has handed you a finished recipe, so your own documented, proportionate approach is the standard you will be judged on. What CBUAE and SAMA specifically require is covered in A12.1.

    Proportionality: how much validation does this system need?

    Not every LLM use deserves the same effort. A tool that drafts meeting notes for staff and one that tells customers what they owe are not the same risk. Tier by impact if wrong, autonomy, data sensitivity and how exposed you are to vendor change. Then let the tier set the evidence.

    Catching a silent vendor change

    The least glamorous and most valuable control is a regression suite: a fixed set of cases with known good answers, run on a schedule against the live endpoint. If the vendor changes something, the pass rate shifts. But a pass rate measured on n cases is itself noisy (F2.1), so the real question is whether the suite is big enough to see the drop you care about before customers do. The weekly pass rate has standard error √(p(1−p)/n), and a common alarm line sits three of those below the baseline.

    alarm ifp^<p0−3p0(1−p0)n

    p0 is the baseline pass rate and n the cases run each week. At p0 = 92% and n = 200 the line sits at 86.2%. The chance a healthy week triggers it is about 0.13%. The chance a week after a real 6-point drop triggers it is only about 54%, because the drop is about the same size as the noise. At n = 1,000 that chance is above 99%.

    What this shows in a validation meeting: monitoring is a power problem, the same one as F2.7. A suite of 50 cases feels like diligence and cannot see a six-point drop. Decide the smallest drop you care about, then size the suite for it. Split by language, product and risk type, because a regression in Arabic refusals is invisible in an average dominated by English.

    AI incident management and GenAI inventories

    An AI incident, in the OECD's framing, is an event where an AI system's development, use or malfunction leads to actual harm: to people, to rights, to property or operations. A near miss (or hazard) is the same event caught before harm. Banks should record both, because near misses are cheap lessons. Typical bank examples: an assistant states a wrong fee; an agent acts outside its remit; a fraud model silently stops scoring; staff paste customer data into an unapproved tool (shadow AI); a prompt injection in an uploaded document makes an agent leak data.

    An incident process for AI looks like an IT incident process with three additions. Evidence is perishable (prompts, retrieved context and tool calls must be traced, or you cannot reproduce a stochastic failure). Harm can be diffuse and slow (wrong advice to a thousand customers). And causes are often outside your walls (a vendor update). The sequence:

    1. Detect. From monitoring, complaints, staff reports, or a vendor notice. Make reporting easy and blame-free.
    2. Triage. Severity from harm and spread: who was affected, is it still happening, is money or personal data involved.
    3. Contain. Stop further harm first: switch to a fallback, disable a tool, revoke a credential, route to humans. This is what kill switches are for.
    4. Preserve evidence. Freeze traces, prompts, model version, retrieved documents before retention rules delete them.
    5. Escalate and notify. Internal risk, compliance and data protection immediately; regulators, customers and partners where a rule or contract requires it. Notification clocks may be short, so know in advance whom to call.
    6. Fix the cause. Technical (prompt, data, permissions) or contractual (vendor).
    7. Record and learn. Add the failing case to the regression suite, update the inventory and risk rating, share the lesson.

    The EU AI Act separately requires providers of high-risk systems to report serious incidents to authorities on tight deadlines; the details sit in the text and are worth checking when a system is in scope. Whether CBUAE or SAMA require AI-specific incident reporting beyond their general operational-risk and cyber incident rules, I have not verified; ask compliance rather than assume.

    The inventory is the spine

    The incident process, the tiering and the framework mapping all hang off one thing: a register of every AI use in the bank, including GenAI. G3.1 covers the model inventory. A GenAI inventory needs more fields because the risk lives in the configuration, and because much AI is bought or used without a project ever being raised.

    FieldWhy it is there
    Use case, business owner, usersSomeone accountable by name; defines intended use and who can be affected.
    Model, provider, version or alias, hosting regionLets you act within hours when a vendor announces a change, retirement or breach.
    Data classes touched, retention of prompts and outputsDrives data-protection review and what a leak could expose.
    Tools and permissions, autonomy level, approval gatesFor agents, the real risk surface (see A1.1).
    Risk tier, validation date, eval set version, open findingsShows the evidence and its age.
    Framework mapping (e.g. EU high-risk? ISO control IDs)Answers a partner or auditor without a new project.
    Linked incidents and near missesCloses the learning loop.

    Two practical points. First, build the inventory from discovery too: gateway logs, expense and procurement records, browser-extension scans, and an amnesty for shadow AI, not only from self-declaration. Routing all model traffic through a central gateway (A15.1) gives you both logging and a kill switch. Second, an inventory that is updated once a year is a fiction; link it to the deployment pipeline so a new model alias cannot ship without a record.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson is the governance layer over most of the rest of the curriculum.

    • Gulf regulators first. A12.1 covers what CBUAE, SAMA and SDAIA expect locally. The frameworks here are the translation layer to partners and group parents.
    • The classic MRM lifecycle. G3.1 to G3.2 (inventory and lifecycle), G3.3 to G3.4 (independent validation and documentation) and G3.5 to G3.6 (monitoring and vendor risk) are the base. This lesson adapts each to LLMs.
    • Evidence. Honest evals with intervals (A4.1), groundedness checks (A4.3) and scripted agent simulations (A4.5) are what the validation plan actually asks for.
    • Controls in the system. Approval gates and guardrails (M4.5), threat modelling (G1.1) and tracing and versioning (P2.5) produce the logs and limits an incident process depends on.
    • Drift. The weekly regression suite is the LLM cousin of PSI and KS monitoring for tabular models (F7.5).
    • Bank use cases. Credit decisions (B4, A10) are where the EU "high-risk" label and fair-lending expectations meet. Fraud and AML (B1, B2) are typically outside the EU credit entry, but face the same MRM and incident discipline. KYC and call-centre assistants raise the transparency and data-protection questions.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Regulators are moving slowly and unevenly, supervisors are using existing risk rules rather than writing LLM-specific ones, and the live work is in practical governance: certifying management systems, building inventories, and inventing incident reporting for a technology that fails differently.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are patterns in bank AI governance, and in AI-generated policy documents. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Paper compliance

      A framework mapping, a policy and a certificate, but no tests run and no traces kept. The document says "monitored"; nothing is.

      Spot itAsk to see the last regression run, its date and its result, and the last incident record.
    • Validating "the model" when it is a vendor API

      The report reproduces the vendor's benchmark table and calls it validation.

      Spot itLook for results on the bank's own task, own data, in Arabic as well as English, with intervals.
    • Floating alias in production

      Pointing at "latest". Behaviour changes with no change record on the bank's side.

      Spot itCheck the inventory for the exact model version, and the contract for change-notice terms.
    • A tiny, unrepresentative eval suite

      Fifty hand-picked easy cases, all English. It passes forever.

      Spot itAsk the smallest drop the suite can detect and the share of cases per language and risk type.
    • Missing shadow AI

      The inventory lists approved projects only. Staff use public chatbots with customer data.

      Spot itCompare the inventory with gateway, proxy or procurement logs.
    • Evidence that expires

      Trace retention of 7 days when complaints take 3 weeks to arrive. The incident cannot be reproduced.

      Spot itCompare trace retention with the typical complaint and investigation timeline.
    • Classifying by technology instead of use

      "We use generative AI, so everything is high-risk" or "it is just a chatbot, so nothing is". Both ignore what the system decides.

      Spot itAsk what decision or action the output feeds, and who is affected.
    • Human oversight on paper only

      An approval step that reviewers click through at volume (automation bias).

      Spot itCheck approval rates and review time per item; near-100% approval in seconds is a warning.
    • Treating incidents as only security events

      A wrong-but-confident answer harms customers and never reaches the risk team because no system was breached.

      Spot itAsk whether complaints and quality failures feed the same register as breaches.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We can't open the vendor's model, so we test what we can control and watch what we can't. Each AI use gets a risk tier by customer impact and autonomy. Higher tiers get independent testing on our own Arabic and English cases, and every one sits in a register with an owner and a model version. If the vendor changes something, a weekly test should catch it before customers do. The European rules mostly reach us through partners, and we map our controls to them once rather than building three programmes.”
    To a validator or auditor
    “I'd treat the vendor model as a third-party component: documentation, contract terms on versioning and notice, and behavioural testing on our task. Validation scope is the prompt, retrieval, tools, permissions and approval gates, with a stratified eval set, repeated runs to estimate variance, adversarial and injection tests, and slice results by language and product. Ongoing monitoring is a scheduled regression suite sized to detect a defined drop, plus sampled human review. Incidents and near misses go in one register with trace retention long enough to reproduce them.”
    “Does the EU AI Act apply to us?”

    It depends on the use and the footprint. It reaches systems placed on the EU market or whose output is used in the EU, so EU branches, EU customers or EU group entities bring it in. Credit scoring of individuals is the main high-risk case, with obligations now deferred to December 2027 as I understand the 2026 amendment; fraud detection is carved out of that entry. For a Gulf-only business it is mostly indirect, through vendors and partners. Counsel should confirm the actual scope.

    “How can you validate a model you can't see inside?”

    The same way you assess any outsourced component: what can I examine, and what evidence compensates for what I can't? I examine the system I built, I test behaviour on our task with enough cases to see the difference that matters, I get contractual notice of changes, I pin versions, and I run a regression suite continuously. Conceptual soundness shifts from "are the equations right" to "is this the right tool, with known limits, for this job".

    “Do we need ISO 42001 certification?”

    Not because a law says so, as far as I know. It is useful if partners or tenders ask for it, and as an external check that the management system works. I'd decide on the commercial and assurance value, and make sure the same controls already satisfy the regulator's expectations, so certification is a by-product rather than a separate project.

    “The vendor says the new model is strictly better. Do we just upgrade?”

    No. Better on public benchmarks is not better on our task, and refusal, tone, and Arabic behaviour can move. Run the new version through the regression suite in a shadow or canary stage (P2.3), compare with intervals, check the slices, then switch with a rollback ready. For a higher-tier use, that goes through the change process like any other model change.

    “What do we do when the chatbot gives a customer wrong advice?”

    Contain first if it may be repeating: disable the feature or route to humans. Preserve the trace and model version. Escalate to risk, compliance and data protection, and let them decide on customer remediation and any regulatory notice. Then fix the cause, and add that case to the regression suite so a vendor update can't silently bring it back.

    “Isn't SR 11-7 enough for LLMs?”

    Its principles still hold: independence, effective challenge, inventory, monitoring. Its mechanics assume you can see the model, so they need adapting for vendor APIs, stochastic outputs and agents that act. I'd also note the US agencies reportedly replaced it in 2026 and excluded generative AI from scope, so the principles matter more than any specific text.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the kind of thing each is: EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF.

    EU AI Act: binding law with penalties, scaled by use-case risk. ISO/IEC 42001: certifiable standard for an AI management system. NIST AI RMF: voluntary framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage).

    2. Which of these is high-risk under the EU Act's credit entry: (a) personal-loan scoring, (b) card-fraud detection, (c) SME loan scoring?

    (a). The entry covers natural persons' creditworthiness or credit score. Fraud detection is expressly excluded. SME or corporate scoring is not caught by that entry, though other rules may apply to the people behind it. Confirm with counsel.

    3. List four things a validator can still examine when the model is a vendor API.

    Any four of: the system prompt and configuration, retrieval and data sources, tools and permissions with approval gates, task-level performance on a bank-built eval set, vendor documentation and contract terms, live monitoring and regression results.

    4. Baseline pass rate is 90% on 100 weekly cases. Where is the 3-standard-error alarm line?

    SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 100) = 0.03. Alarm line = 90% − 9 = 81%. A real drop to 86% would be unlikely to cross it in any given week, which shows how coarse a 100-case suite is.

    5. Why does a floating model alias break the validation logic, and what are the two ways to manage it?

    The model you validated can be replaced without a change request. Either pin a dated version and plan migrations before retirement, or accept the alias but run a regression suite on a schedule with contractual change notice, and treat any shift as a model change.

    6. An agent sends an internal summary to an external address after reading an injected PDF. What are the first two actions and why that order?

    Contain (revoke the send permission or disable the agent), then preserve evidence (traces, tool calls, the PDF, model version). Containing stops more leakage; evidence can be lost to retention if you wait. Escalation to security and data protection follows straight away.

    7. Why does a GenAI inventory need fields a classic model inventory does not?

    Because the risk sits in configuration and context: model version or alias, prompts, retrieval sources, tools, permissions, autonomy, data classes and prompt retention. These change without a new model being built, and much AI is acquired or used outside normal projects.
