    AI Frameworks, Model Risk for LLMs and Agents, and AI Incidents

    Which global rulebooks actually reach a Gulf bank, what a validator can honestly test when the model is a vendor's API, and how to log, handle and learn from the day an AI system goes wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceYou cannot validate a model you do not own, so you validate the system you built around it, monitor it for silent vendor change, keep an inventory that says where every AI is, and rehearse what to do when one misbehaves, mapping all of it to whichever framework your regulator or partner asks about.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Compare a car you built with one you rent. A bank's in-house credit scorecard is the built car: known parts, a manual, and a mechanic (the validator) who can take it apart and sign it off. An LLM assistant is the rental. The bank wrote the system prompt and retrieval pipeline, but the engine is a vendor's model behind an API. Nobody at the bank has its weights, training data or changelog, and the vendor can swap the engine next Tuesday. It is not deterministic, it answers in Arabic and English, and as an agent it can do things: send an email, open a case, move a limit.

    The old playbook does not fit, but its purpose does: someone independent has looked, knows the limits, and will notice change. Three jobs follow.

    • Frameworks. The EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 and NIST AI RMF are not Gulf laws. They reach you through European customers, partners, vendors and group parents, and Gulf regulators borrow their vocabulary. You need a map, not a legal opinion.
    • Model risk for LLMs and agents. Validate what you control (prompt, retrieval, tools, permissions, evidence on your own task) and monitor what you do not (the vendor's model).
    • Incidents and inventories. You cannot manage an AI you did not know existed, or learn from a failure you did not record.
    Hold onto thisThe unit of governance has moved from the model to the system around it: model, prompt, data, tools, permissions, humans and monitoring, versioned together. Every question here is "which part do we control, and what is our evidence?"
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The three frameworks: law, standard, guide in brief

    Mixing up their kinds is the commonest meeting error. One is a law with fines, one a standard you can be audited against, one a voluntary playbook.

    EU AI Act. It works like traffic lights on use cases. It bans a short list of practices (social scoring, emotion recognition at work), regulates "high-risk" uses listed in an annex, and sets light transparency duties for chatbots and synthetic media. The headline bank case is evaluating the creditworthiness of natural persons or setting their credit score. The same annex excludes financial-fraud detection from that entry, and also covers employment uses such as CV screening. General-purpose model duties fall mostly on model providers, unless you fine-tune or rebrand so heavily that you become one.

    Timing moved. High-risk obligations were due August 2026. A "Digital Omnibus" agreed in 2026 defers the standalone high-risk categories (including credit scoring) to 2 December 2027 and product-embedded ones to August 2028. Prohibitions and AI-literacy duties have run since 2025. Sources differ on details: check the Official Journal before quoting a date to a lawyer.

    ISO/IEC 42001 answers "are you certified?" from a European counterparty. Like ISO 27001 for security, it proves a functioning management system, not a perfect model. ISO/IEC 23894 gives companion risk guidance. NIST AI RMF structures a risk conversation: Govern (who is accountable), Map (context and impact), Measure (tests and metrics), Manage (treat and monitor).

    They do not conflict. Keep one internal control set, anchored on your regulator's expectations (A12.1) and model risk policy (G3), plus a mapping table of which control satisfies which clause of each framework. That beats three parallel compliance projects.

    Model risk rules applied to LLMs and agents

    Model risk management (MRM) comes from the US guidance SR 11-7 and is mirrored in most bank policies, Gulf ones included (G3.2). It has three pillars: development and use (sound, documented, fit for purpose), validation (independent, with "effective challenge": someone competent and senior enough to push back) and governance (inventory, roles, policy, reporting). Validation traditionally checks conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring and outcomes.

    All of that assumed you could open the model. With a vendor API you cannot, like inspecting a restaurant's food without seeing its kitchen: you test the dishes and audit what you do control. So ask what a validator can actually examine.

    ComponentCan the bank inspect it?What is validated instead
    Vendor model weights and training dataNoVendor documentation (model card, safety reports, change policy); contract terms on notice, retention and training-on-your-data; behavioural tests on your task. Conceptual soundness becomes "right tool for this job, with known failure modes?"
    System prompt and configurationYesTreated as code: versioned, reviewed, tested (P2.5). Small wording changes move behaviour, so every change goes through the eval set.
    Retrieval and data sourcesYesRetrieval quality, freshness, access control, groundedness (A4.3). Often the biggest source of real error.
    Tools, permissions, approval gatesYesLeast privilege, action limits, human approval for money or customer-impacting steps (M4.5), logging of every call. Where agents differ most from chatbots.
    Task-level performanceYes, by testingFixed, representative Arabic-and-English eval set with intervals (A4.1), slice results, adversarial and injection tests (G1.1).
    Ongoing behaviourOnly by monitoringScheduled regression suite, also run on every vendor notice; sampled human review of live outputs; drift and complaint signals.

    Two things make LLMs harder than a scorecard.

    • Outputs are open-ended and stochastic. There is no single AUC. Define success per task (right fee quoted, correct citation, refused when it should) and measure it with enough cases to see the difference you care about. The same prompt can give different answers, so repeat tests and report a rate.
    • The engine can change without you. A floating alias such as "latest" lets the vendor swap the model silently. A pinned dated snapshot is eventually retired, forcing migration. Either way, the model you validated is not the one running in a year. Vendor model risk (G3.6) is part of the job.

    US model risk guidance was replaced in April 2026 (SR 26-2 and OCC Bulletin 2026-13, issued jointly with the FDIC on 17 April 2026, checked on the Federal Reserve site in November 2026). It puts generative and agentic AI outside its scope, sets no enforceable standards, and targets mainly banks over USD 30 billion in assets. For a Gulf bank this does not mean "LLMs are exempt". It means no regulator has handed you a recipe, so your own documented, proportionate approach is the standard you will be judged on. CBUAE and SAMA specifics are in A12.1.

    Proportionality: how much validation does this system need?

    Meeting-notes drafting and telling customers what they owe are different risks, like a bike helmet versus a parachute. Tier by impact if wrong, autonomy, data sensitivity and exposure to vendor change. The tier sets the evidence.

    Catching a silent vendor change

    A regression suite is the cheapest control: a fixed set of cases with known good answers, run on a schedule against the live endpoint. It is a canary in a coal mine. If the vendor changes something, the pass rate shifts. But a pass rate on n cases is itself noisy (F2.1), so ask whether the suite is big enough to see the drop you care about before customers do. The weekly pass rate has standard error √(p(1−p)/n); a common alarm line sits three of those below baseline.

    alarm ifp^<p0−3p0(1−p0)n

    p0 is the baseline pass rate, n the cases run each week. At p0 = 92% and n = 200 the line is 86.2%. A healthy week triggers it about 0.13% of the time. A week after a real 6-point drop triggers it only about 54% of the time, since the drop is about the size of the noise. At n = 1,000 that chance is above 99%.

    Monitoring is a power problem, the same one as F2.7. Fifty cases feels like diligence and cannot see a six-point drop. Pick the smallest drop you care about, then size the suite for it. Split by language, product and risk type: an Arabic-refusal regression vanishes in an average dominated by English.

    AI incident management and GenAI inventories

    An AI incident (OECD framing) is an event where an AI system's development, use or malfunction causes actual harm to people, rights, property or operations. A near miss is the same event caught before harm. Record both: near misses are cheap lessons, like a smoke alarm that went off with no fire. Bank examples: an assistant states a wrong fee; an agent acts outside its remit; a fraud model silently stops scoring; staff paste customer data into an unapproved tool (shadow AI); a prompt injection in an uploaded document makes an agent leak data.

    An AI incident process is an IT incident process plus three twists. Evidence is perishable (without traced prompts, retrieved context and tool calls you cannot reproduce a stochastic failure). Harm can be diffuse and slow (wrong advice to a thousand customers). Causes are often outside your walls (a vendor update). The sequence:

    1. Detect. From monitoring, complaints, staff reports or a vendor notice. Make reporting easy and blame-free.
    2. Triage. Severity from harm and spread: who is affected, is it ongoing, is money or personal data involved.
    3. Contain. Stop further harm first: fall back, disable a tool, revoke a credential, route to humans. This is what kill switches are for.
    4. Preserve evidence. Freeze traces, prompts, model version and retrieved documents before retention rules delete them.
    5. Escalate and notify. Risk, compliance and data protection at once; regulators, customers and partners where a rule or contract requires. Clocks can be short, so know whom to call beforehand.
    6. Fix the cause. Technical (prompt, data, permissions) or contractual (vendor).
    7. Record and learn. Add the failing case to the regression suite, update the inventory and risk rating, share the lesson.

    The EU AI Act separately requires providers of high-risk systems to report serious incidents on tight deadlines; check the text if a system is in scope. Whether CBUAE or SAMA require AI-specific incident reporting beyond general operational-risk and cyber rules is unverified: ask compliance.

    The inventory is the spine

    Think of a fire register: you cannot evacuate a building if you do not know who is inside. The incident process, tiering and framework mapping all hang off one register of every AI use in the bank, GenAI included (the base model inventory is G3.1). A GenAI inventory needs extra fields because the risk lives in configuration, and because much AI is bought or used with no project ever raised.

    FieldWhy it is there
    Use case, business owner, usersA named accountable person; defines intended use and who can be affected.
    Model, provider, version or alias, hosting regionLets you act within hours when a vendor announces a change, retirement or breach.

    • Discover, do not only self-declare. Use gateway logs, expense and procurement records, browser-extension scans and an amnesty for shadow AI. Routing all model traffic through a central gateway (A15.1) gives you logging and a kill switch.
    • Wire it to deployment. A yearly-updated inventory is fiction. Link it to the pipeline so a new model alias cannot ship without a record.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each pattern comes with its quickest check.

    • Paper compliance

      A mapping, a policy and a certificate, but no tests run and no traces kept. The document says "monitored"; nothing is.

      Spot itAsk for the last regression run (date, result) and the last incident record.
    • Validating "the model" when it is a vendor API

      The report copies the vendor's benchmark table and calls it validation.

      Spot itLook for results on the bank's own task and data, in Arabic and English, with intervals.
    • Floating alias in production

      Pointing at "latest": behaviour changes with no change record at the bank.

      Spot itCheck the inventory for the exact model version and the contract for change-notice terms.
    • A tiny, unrepresentative eval suite

      Fifty easy, hand-picked, English-only cases. It passes forever.

      Spot itAsk the smallest drop it can detect, and the share of cases per language and risk type.
    • Missing shadow AI

      The inventory lists approved projects only; staff paste customer data into public chatbots.

      Spot itCompare the inventory with gateway, proxy or procurement logs.
    • Evidence that expires

      Traces kept 7 days, complaints arrive after 3 weeks. The incident cannot be reproduced.

      Spot itCompare trace retention with the typical complaint and investigation timeline.
    • Classifying by technology instead of use

      "It's generative AI, so high-risk" or "it's just a chatbot, so nothing". Both ignore what the system decides.

      Spot itAsk what decision or action the output feeds, and who is affected.
    • Human oversight on paper only

      Reviewers click through approvals at volume (automation bias).

      Spot itCheck approval rate and review time per item; near-100% approval in seconds is a warning.
    • Treating incidents as only security events

      A confident wrong answer harms customers but never reaches the risk team, because nothing was breached.

      Spot itAsk whether complaints and quality failures feed the same register as breaches.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the kind of thing each is: EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF.

    EU AI Act: binding law with penalties, scaled by use-case risk. ISO/IEC 42001: certifiable standard for an AI management system. NIST AI RMF: voluntary framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage).

    2. Which is high-risk under the EU Act's credit entry: (a) personal-loan scoring, (b) card-fraud detection, (c) SME loan scoring?

    (a). The entry covers natural persons' creditworthiness or credit score. Fraud detection is expressly excluded. SME or corporate scoring is not caught, though other rules may apply to the people behind it. Confirm with counsel.

    3. List four things a validator can still examine when the model is a vendor API.

    Any four of: system prompt and configuration, retrieval and data sources, tools and permissions with approval gates, task-level performance on a bank-built eval set, vendor documentation and contract terms, live monitoring and regression results.

    4. Baseline pass rate is 90% on 100 weekly cases. Where is the 3-standard-error alarm line?

    SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 100) = 0.03, so the line is 90% − 9 = 81%. A real drop to 86% would rarely cross it in a given week: a 100-case suite is coarse.

    5. Why does a floating model alias break the validation logic, and what are the two ways to manage it?

    The validated model can be replaced without a change request. Either pin a dated version and plan migration before retirement, or accept the alias but run a scheduled regression suite with contractual change notice, and treat any shift as a model change.

    6. An agent sends an internal summary to an external address after reading an injected PDF. What are the first two actions, and why in that order?

    Contain (revoke the send permission or disable the agent), then preserve evidence (traces, tool calls, the PDF, model version). Containing stops more leakage; evidence can be lost to retention if you wait. Escalation to security and data protection follows straight away.

    7. Why does a GenAI inventory need fields a classic model inventory does not?

    The risk sits in configuration and context: model version or alias, prompts, retrieval sources, tools, permissions, autonomy, data classes, prompt retention. These change without a new model being built, and much AI is acquired outside normal projects.
