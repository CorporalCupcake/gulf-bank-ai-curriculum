    AI Frameworks, Model Risk for LLMs and Agents, and AI Incidents

    Which global rulebooks actually reach a Gulf bank, what a validator can honestly test when the model is a vendor's API, and how to log, handle and learn from the day an AI system goes wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceYou cannot validate a model you do not own, so you validate the system you built around it, monitor it for silent vendor change, keep an inventory that says where every AI is, and rehearse what to do when one misbehaves, mapping all of it to whichever framework your regulator or partner asks about.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Compare a car you built with one you rent. An in-house scorecard is the built car: known parts, a manual, and a mechanic (the validator) who can take it apart. An LLM assistant is the rental. The bank wrote the prompt and retrieval, but the engine is a vendor's model behind an API, with no weights or changelog you can see. The vendor can swap it next Tuesday, and as an agent it can do things, like moving a limit.

    The old playbook's purpose still holds: someone independent has looked, knows the limits, and will notice change. Three jobs follow.

    • Frameworks. The EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 and NIST AI RMF are not Gulf laws, but they reach you through European customers and vendors. You need a map, not a legal opinion.
    • Model risk. Validate what you control (prompt, retrieval, tools, permissions, results on your task) and monitor what you do not (the vendor's model).
    • Incidents and inventories. You cannot manage an AI you did not know existed, or learn from a failure you did not record.
    Hold onto thisThe unit of governance has moved from the model to the system around it: model, prompt, data, tools, permissions, humans and monitoring, versioned together. Every question here is "which part do we control, and what is our evidence?"
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The three frameworks: law, standard, guide in brief

    Do not mix up their kinds: one is a law with fines, one a standard you can be audited against, one a voluntary playbook.

    EU AI ActISO/IEC 42001NIST AI RMF
    What it isEU regulation, risk-tiered, with penaltiesCertifiable standard for an AI management system (plan, do, check, improve)US voluntary framework: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage
    Who it reachesAnyone whose AI or its output is used in the EU, via branches, customers or vendorsNobody, unless a customer, regulator or the bank adopts itNobody; US supervisors reference it

    EU AI Act. Think of traffic lights on use cases: a short banned list (social scoring, emotion recognition at work), "high-risk" uses in an annex, and light transparency duties for chatbots and synthetic media. The headline bank case is credit scoring of natural persons; fraud detection is expressly excluded. A 2026 "Digital Omnibus" defers high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027, so check the Official Journal before quoting a date.

    ISO/IEC 42001 answers "are you certified?" from a European counterparty. NIST AI RMF structures a risk conversation: who is accountable, what is the context, what do tests show, how do we monitor.

    They do not conflict. Keep one internal control set, anchored on your regulator (A12.1) and model risk policy (G3), plus a table mapping each control to each framework. That beats three parallel projects.

    Model risk rules applied to LLMs and agents

    Model risk management (MRM), from US guidance SR 11-7, has three pillars: development and use, validation (independent, with "effective challenge" from someone senior enough to push back) and governance (inventory, roles, policy).

    That assumed you could open the model. With a vendor API you cannot, like judging a restaurant without seeing its kitchen: you test the dishes and audit what you control.

    ComponentCan the bank inspect it?What is validated instead
    Vendor weights and training dataNoVendor documents, contract terms (change notice, retention), and behavioural tests on your task (A4.1, G1.1).
    System prompt and configurationYesTreated as code: versioned, reviewed, re-tested on every change.
    Retrieval and data sourcesYesQuality, freshness, access control, groundedness (A4.3). Often the biggest source of real error.
    Tools, permissions, approval gatesYesLeast privilege, action limits, human approval for money or customer-impacting steps (M4.5). Where agents differ most from chatbots.
    Ongoing behaviourOnly by monitoringScheduled regression suite, sampled human review, drift and complaint signals.

    Two things make LLMs harder than a scorecard.

    • Outputs vary. There is no single AUC. Define success per task and report a rate over enough repeated cases.
    • The engine can change without you. A floating alias such as "latest" lets the vendor swap the model silently; a pinned snapshot is eventually retired (G3.6).

    US guidance was replaced in April 2026 (SR 26-2) and puts generative and agentic AI out of scope. That does not mean "LLMs are exempt". It means no regulator has handed you a recipe, so your own documented, proportionate approach is the standard. CBUAE and SAMA specifics are in A12.1.

    Proportionality: how much validation does this system need?

    Drafting notes and telling customers what they owe are different risks, like a helmet versus a parachute. Tier by impact if wrong, autonomy, data sensitivity and vendor exposure. The tier sets the evidence.

    Catching a silent vendor change

    A regression suite is the cheapest control: a fixed set of cases with known good answers, run on a schedule against the live endpoint. It is a canary in a coal mine. If the vendor changes something, the pass rate shifts. But a weekly pass rate is noisy, more so with fewer cases. A common alarm line sits a few "standard errors" (the typical size of that noise) below baseline, so only a real drop should cross it.

    Take a 92% baseline. With 200 cases a week, a real 6-point drop is about the size of the noise, so the alarm fires only about half the time. With 1,000 cases it fires almost every week.

    Pick the smallest drop you care about, size the suite for it, and split results by language: an Arabic regression vanishes in an average dominated by English.

    AI incident management and GenAI inventories

    An AI incident is an event where an AI system's use or malfunction causes actual harm; a near miss is the same event caught first. Record both: near misses are cheap lessons, like a smoke alarm with no fire. Examples: a wrong fee quoted; an agent acting outside its remit; staff pasting customer data into an unapproved tool (shadow AI).

    It is an IT incident process with one big twist: evidence is perishable, because without traced prompts and tool calls you cannot reproduce a failure. The sequence:

    1. Detect. From monitoring, complaints, staff or a vendor notice.
    2. Triage. Who is affected, and is it ongoing.
    3. Contain. Stop further harm first: fall back, disable a tool, revoke a credential, route to humans.
    4. Preserve evidence. Freeze traces, prompts, model version and retrieved documents before retention rules delete them.
    5. Escalate and notify. Risk, compliance and data protection at once; regulators and customers where required.
    6. Fix the cause. Technical or contractual.
    7. Record and learn. Add the failing case to the regression suite and update the inventory.

    The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to report serious incidents quickly. Whether CBUAE or SAMA require AI-specific reporting beyond general rules is unverified: ask compliance.

    The inventory is the spine

    Think of a fire register: you cannot evacuate a building if you do not know who is inside. Incidents, tiering and framework mapping all hang off one register of every AI use in the bank (base inventory: G3.1). GenAI needs extra fields because the risk lives in configuration, and much AI is used with no project ever raised.

    FieldWhy it is there
    Use case, business owner, usersA named accountable person; defines who can be affected.
    Model, provider, version or alias, regionLets you act within hours when a vendor announces a change or breach.
    Tools, permissions, autonomy, approval gatesFor agents, the real risk surface (see A1.1).
    Risk tier, validation date, framework mapping, linked incidentsShows the evidence and its age, answers an auditor, and closes the learning loop.
    • Discover, do not only self-declare. Use gateway logs and procurement records. A central gateway (A15.1) gives you logging and a kill switch.
    • Wire it to deployment. A yearly-updated inventory is fiction. Link it to the pipeline so a new model alias cannot ship without a record.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each pattern comes with its quickest check.

    • Paper compliance

      A policy and a certificate, but no tests run, no traces kept, and reviewers clicking through approvals. The document says "monitored"; nothing is.

      Spot itAsk for the last regression run and incident record. Near-100% approval in seconds per item is a warning.
    • Validating the vendor's benchmark, not your task

      The report copies the vendor's table, or rests on fifty easy English-only cases that pass forever.

      Spot itLook for results on your own task in Arabic and English, and ask the smallest drop the suite can detect.
    • Floating alias in production

      Pointing at "latest": behaviour changes with no change record at the bank.

      Spot itCheck the inventory for the exact model version and the contract for change-notice terms.
    • Missing shadow AI

      The inventory lists approved projects only; staff paste customer data into public chatbots.

      Spot itCompare the inventory with gateway, proxy or procurement logs.
    • Classifying by technology instead of use

      "It's generative AI, so high-risk" or "it's just a chatbot, so nothing". Both ignore what the system decides.

      Spot itAsk what decision or action the output feeds, and who is affected.
    • Incidents that vanish

      A confident wrong answer harms customers but never reaches risk because nothing was breached, or traces expire before the complaint arrives.

      Spot itAsk whether quality failures feed the same register as breaches, and compare trace retention with complaint timelines.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the kind of thing each is: EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF.

    EU AI Act: binding law with penalties, scaled by use-case risk. ISO/IEC 42001: certifiable standard for an AI management system. NIST AI RMF: voluntary framework.

    2. Which is high-risk under the EU Act's credit entry: (a) personal-loan scoring, (b) card-fraud detection, (c) SME loan scoring?

    (a). The entry covers natural persons' creditworthiness. Fraud detection is excluded and SME scoring is not caught. Confirm with counsel.

    3. List four things a validator can still examine when the model is a vendor API.

    Any four of: prompt and configuration, retrieval, tools and permissions, performance on a bank-built eval set, vendor documents and contract terms, live monitoring.

    4. Why can a 50-case weekly suite not reliably catch a six-point drop in pass rate, and what would you change?

    With so few cases, normal week-to-week noise is as large as the drop, so the alarm line is crossed only by luck. Run more cases, or split by slice and size the suite for the smallest drop you care about.

    5. Why does a floating model alias break the validation logic, and what are the two ways to manage it?

    The validated model can be replaced without a change request. Either pin a dated version and plan migration, or accept the alias but run a scheduled regression suite with contractual change notice.

    6. An agent sends an internal summary to an external address after reading an injected PDF. What are the first two actions, and why in that order?

    Contain (revoke the send permission), then preserve evidence (traces, tool calls, the PDF, model version). Containing stops more leakage; evidence can expire if you wait. Escalation follows at once.

    7. Why does a GenAI inventory need fields a classic model inventory does not?

    The risk sits in configuration: model version or alias, prompts, retrieval, tools, permissions, data classes. These change without a new model being built.
