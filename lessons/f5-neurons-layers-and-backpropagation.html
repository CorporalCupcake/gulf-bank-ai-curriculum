    Neural Networks from the Inside: Neurons, Backpropagation and Trainable Depth

    What a network actually computes, how one clean rule (the chain rule) trains millions of weights, and why depth only works because of three tricks: careful initialisation, normalisation and skip connections.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA neural network is a stack of weighted sums and bends that learns by measuring, for every weight, how much the error would change if it moved (backpropagation), and very deep stacks train only because initialisation, normalisation and residual connections keep that signal from vanishing or exploding on its way back.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a fraud analyst deciding whether a card payment looks wrong. She does not run one rule. She weighs several small signals at once: the amount is high for this customer, it is 3 a.m., the merchant is new, the device is unfamiliar. Each signal nudges her suspicion up or down by a different amount. If the total passes some level, she pauses the payment.

    That is one neuron: a weighted sum of inputs, plus a bias, passed through a bend. A layer is many such analysts, each weighting the same inputs differently, so each learns to notice a different pattern. The next layer does not see raw transactions at all. It sees the first layer's verdicts ("looks like card testing", "looks like travel") and combines those. Stack enough layers and the network builds its own features, ones nobody wrote down.

    Learning is the part that feels like magic and is not. Start with random weights. Show the network a transaction and the truth. It is wrong by some amount. Backpropagation asks, for every single weight, "if I nudged you up a tiny bit, would the error go up or down, and by how much?" Then every weight takes a small step in the direction that reduces the error. Repeat a few million times.

    Depth adds one more problem. The "how much would the error change" message has to travel backwards through every layer. At each layer it is multiplied by something. Multiply a hundred numbers a little smaller than one and the message dies. Multiply a hundred numbers a little bigger than one and it explodes. Most of the engineering history of deep learning, from 2010 to today, is the story of keeping that message alive.

    Hold onto thisThree ideas carry the whole lesson. (1) A neuron is a weighted sum followed by a non-linear bend; without the bend, stacking layers adds nothing. (2) Backpropagation is the chain rule applied efficiently: one backward sweep gives the gradient for every weight at once. (3) Depth is a signal-propagation problem, and initialisation, normalisation and skip connections are the three standard fixes.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    One neuron: a weighted vote and a bend

    A neuron takes a list of numbers x (the inputs), multiplies each by a weight, adds a bias, and applies an activation function f:

    z=w1x1+w2x2+ba=f(z)

    The weights say how much each input matters and in which direction. The bias sets how easily the neuron fires. z is the "pre-activation": a plain linear score. a is what the neuron passes on. With a logistic sigmoid for f and nothing else, this is exactly logistic regression (F4.2). A neural network is logistic regression's core repeated and stacked.

    Geometrically, the set of points where z = 0 is a straight line (a plane in more dimensions). The neuron answers one question: which side of this line is the input on, and how far? The weights are the direction of the line's normal; this is the dot product from F1.1 doing real work. Move the sliders below and watch the line and the output change.

    The slope in the third box matters later. Backpropagation multiplies by it at every neuron. A sigmoid is steepest at its centre (slope 0.25) and flat at both ends (slope near 0); that flatness is the root of the "vanishing gradient". ReLU has slope exactly 1 on its active side and 0 on the other, which is why it largely replaced sigmoid inside deep networks. Modern language models mostly use smooth relatives of ReLU such as GELU and SiLU/Swish. The choices differ in detail, not in spirit.

    Tanh(ez − e−z) / (ez + e−z)−1 to 1Recurrent cells; small tabular nets.

    Layers, and why the bend is not optional

    A layer of H neurons applied to a vector x is a matrix multiplication followed by the bend: a = f(Wx + b). A network is a chain of these. For a small fraud scorer with 40 input features, 64 hidden units and one output, the parameter count is 40×64 + 64 (first layer) plus 64×1 + 1 (second): 2,689 numbers. Large language models are the same arithmetic with billions of them.

    The bend is essential. If f were the identity, two layers would give W2(W1x), which is just another single matrix W2W1 applied to x. A hundred linear layers collapse into one linear layer, which cannot do more than logistic regression. The non-linearity is what lets each layer carve the space in a way the previous one could not.

    How much can a bent network represent? The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1989) says that a network with one hidden layer, wide enough, can approximate any reasonable continuous function. Read it correctly. It says such a network exists. It does not say gradient descent will find it, nor that it will be small, nor that it will generalise. In practice depth buys efficiency: some functions need exponentially many neurons in one layer but only a few in a stack, because each layer can reuse features built by the one before.

    The classic demonstration is XOR: the label is 1 when the two inputs have the same sign. No single line separates the classes, so a single neuron, or any stack of linear layers, tops out around 50–75%. Two bent neurons in a hidden layer solve it. Simulation 2 lets you see that for yourself, with a real network learning in your browser.

    Backpropagation: the chain rule, run backwards

    Training needs one quantity: the gradient of the loss L with respect to every weight (F1.3 covers gradients and descent; here we open up how the network produces them). Write the network as a chain: input → layer 1 → layer 2 → … → output → loss. The chain rule says the effect of a weight on the loss is the product of the local effects along the path from that weight to the loss.

    The efficient trick is to share work. Do a forward pass and store every layer's activations. Then do one backward pass, carrying a running quantity δ, "how much does the loss change if this neuron's pre-activation z changes?". For a neuron in layer l:

    δl=(Wl+1Tδl+1)⊙f′(zl)∂L∂Wl=δlal−1T

    Read it in words. The error signal at layer l is the next layer's error signal sent back through the same weights (that is the transpose WT), then scaled neuron by neuron by the slope f′ of the bend. The gradient for a layer's weights is that signal times the activations that fed in: a weight changes the loss in proportion to how active its input was and how much the downstream error cares. ⊙ means multiply element by element.

    At the very end there is a pleasant simplification. For a sigmoid output with cross-entropy loss (the standard for fraud or default), the starting signal is just δ = p − y: the predicted probability minus the true label (0 or 1). If the model says 0.9 for a legitimate transaction, δ = 0.9 and the network is pushed hard. If it says 0.01 for a legitimate one, δ = 0.01 and it barely moves. This is why cross-entropy is paired with sigmoid and softmax: the slope of the sigmoid cancels against the log and leaves a clean, never-vanishing error. F6 and F1.6 explain why that loss is the natural one.

    The cost of all this is the key engineering fact. One backward pass costs about the same as two forward passes, whatever the number of weights. The naive alternative, nudging each weight and re-measuring the loss, costs one forward pass per weight. For a model with 10 billion weights that is the difference between possible and absurd. Backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986; the general idea of reverse-mode automatic differentiation is older) made deep learning feasible.

    You will never write this by hand at work. Frameworks (PyTorch, JAX) record the forward computation and run the backward pass automatically. But you will read training logs, so know the shape of the loop:

    for batch in loader:                 # a few hundred examples at a time
    p = model(batch.x)               # forward: compute and store activations
    loss = bce(p, batch.y)           # one number: how wrong, on average
    optimiser.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()                  # backward: fills .grad for every weight
    optimiser.step()                 # w -= lr * (a function of .grad)

    The next simulation runs exactly this, on a tiny network in your browser, with no libraries. The data is the XOR pattern in two features: the label is 1 when both have the same sign, with a gap around the axes. The network has one hidden layer. You choose its width and activation.

    Three things to notice. First, the network is not given the idea of "same sign". Each hidden unit finds one slanted line; the output neuron combines them. Second, nothing happens for a while and then the loss collapses: early on the weights are small and symmetric, and learning has to break that symmetry. Third, the finite-difference check agrees with backpropagation to many digits. That check, called gradient checking, is exactly how people verify a hand-written backward pass. Autodiff frameworks make it unnecessary in daily work, but it shows there is no mystery in the number.

    One more practical detail. Real training does not use all the data each step. Mini-batch stochastic gradient descent uses a random few hundred examples, giving a noisy but cheap estimate of the gradient. Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) and its variant AdamW, the standard optimisers, rescale each weight's step by a running estimate of that weight's gradient size, so weights with tiny gradients still move. F1.3 covers learning-rate schedules and why they matter. For a transformer, the batch size, learning rate, warm-up and weight decay together decide whether training works at all.

    Initialisation: start the signal at the right size

    Before training, every weight needs a starting value. "Small random numbers" sounds harmless and is where deep nets used to fail. Consider a layer with n inputs. Each output is a sum of n terms, each a weight times an input. If the weights have variance σ² and the inputs have variance 1, the output has variance about nσ². Pick σ² too small and the signal shrinks layer after layer until the output is the same for every input. Pick it too large and it grows until activations saturate or overflow. Repeat over 50 layers and the factor compounds: 0.9 per layer becomes 0.005 after 50, and 1.1 becomes 117.

    The cure is to choose the weight scale so that variance stays near 1 from layer to layer. The values to remember:

    Xavier / Glorot:Var(w)=1ninHe / Kaiming:Var(w)=2nin

    Glorot and Bengio (2010) derived the first for tanh and sigmoid-like activations (their exact form averages fan-in and fan-out; for square layers it is the same). He et al. (2015) noticed that ReLU zeros out half the signal, so the weights need twice the variance to compensate. For a layer with 256 inputs: Xavier std = 1/16 = 0.0625, He std = √(2/256) ≈ 0.088. Biases usually start at zero. The frameworks apply these by default, which is why you rarely think about it; it matters when you build something custom or the loss refuses to move.

    One subtlety. Initialisation must also break symmetry. If every weight started at the same value, every hidden neuron would compute the same thing and receive the same gradient, and would stay identical forever. Random starts make the neurons different, so they can specialise.

    Normalisation: re-centre the signal at every layer

    Initialisation sets the right scale at step zero. During training, weights change and the scale can drift. Normalisation layers remove the drift by rescaling activations on the fly. The basic operation is to subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, then let the network learn a scale γ and shift β to undo it if it wants to:

    x^=x−μσ2+εy=γx^+β

    What differs between the variants is which numbers μ and σ are computed over. Batch normalisation (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015) uses all examples in the mini-batch, for each feature. Layer normalisation (Ba et al., 2016) uses all features of one example, so it does not depend on other examples. RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019) drops the mean subtraction and divides by the root-mean-square only; cheaper and about as good.

    VariantStatistics overBehaviour at inferenceTypical home
    BatchNormThe batch, per featureUses stored running averages, so train and test behave differently. Awkward with small or non-i.i.d. batches.Convolutional vision models, some tabular nets
    LayerNormThe features of one exampleIdentical to training. No batch dependence.Transformers, sequence models
    RMSNormThe features of one example, no meanIdentical to training.Most recent open large language models

    Why does it help? The original explanation, "reducing internal covariate shift", turned out to be weak. Santurkar et al. (2018) found that batch norm instead makes the loss surface smoother and the gradients more predictable, which lets you use larger learning rates. The practical effects are what matter: faster, more stable training, less sensitivity to initialisation, and a mild regularising effect from batch noise. In a transformer there is a second job covered next: normalisation placement decides whether very deep stacks train without careful warm-up.

    For tabular banking models, a related and simpler point: standardise your input features (F3.4). A network fed raw transaction amounts in AED (range 1 to 5,000,000) next to a binary flag will train badly for the same signal-size reason, and no normalisation layer fully rescues it.

    Residual connections: a highway for the gradient

    By 2015 researchers could initialise and normalise well, and still found that a 56-layer network trained worse than a 20-layer one, even on the training set. That was not overfitting; the deeper network was simply harder to optimise. He et al. (2016) fixed it with a small change. Instead of each block computing hl+1 = F(hl), it computes

    hl+1=hl+F(hl)∂hl+1∂hl=I+∂F∂hl

    The block only has to learn a correction to its input. The derivative has an identity matrix I added, so going backwards the gradient always has a direct path of multiplication by 1, whatever F does. Across 100 blocks the product no longer has to survive 100 shrinking factors; it travels along the skip path and picks up contributions from the blocks as it goes. A zero-output block is a perfectly valid "do nothing" layer, so adding depth cannot easily make things worse.

    The same idea runs through every transformer. A transformer block is h + Attention(Norm(h)), then h + MLP(Norm(h)). The running vector h is often called the residual stream, and each layer reads from it and writes small additions back. Interpretability work (R5) treats that stream as the model's working memory.

    Residual connections bring a side effect that normalisation then fixes. Each block adds to h, so with no care its size grows with depth: with a ReLU branch at He scale, the variance roughly doubles per block. The common arrangement is pre-norm: normalise the input to each branch and leave the stream itself alone. Xiong et al. (2020) showed that pre-norm transformers have well-behaved gradients at initialisation and need much less warm-up than the original post-norm design; most large models since use it.

    Simulation 3 puts all three tricks on one stage. It builds a random deep network, sends one input forward and a gradient backward, and shows what survives. Everything here is computed exactly in your browser.

    What the picture shows, in order. Sigmoid at depth 20: the slope is at most 0.25, so each layer multiplies the gradient by something of order 0.25 × (weights). After 20 layers the early layers receive about nothing; they cannot learn. That is the vanishing gradient problem (Hochreiter, 1991; Bengio et al., 1994), and it kept deep nets out of reach for twenty years. ReLU with He scale preserves size on average. Too-large weights, even with ReLU, multiply at every layer: the exploding gradient, usually met in practice as a loss that suddenly becomes NaN. A standard emergency measure is gradient clipping, which caps the gradient's norm.

    With skips and no normalisation, nothing vanishes, but at He scale each block adds a full-size contribution, so the forward signal and the gradient both grow with depth (shrink the weight scale and you can tame it, which is why residual branches are often initialised small). With layer norm before each block, both are held within a modest range. This pairing is no accident: it is the arrangement in essentially every transformer you will meet, and Part 4 covers where researchers are still tinkering with it.

    Where it shows up at a bank:

    • Card fraud on sequences. A network can read a customer's last 50 transactions as a sequence and learn behavioural features directly. Large processors report real gains over hand-built features, but at a Gulf retail bank with a few thousand confirmed cases, a tuned boosted-tree model with good features is a hard baseline to beat.
    • Tabular credit and churn models. Studies on benchmark tabular data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Part 4) find trees usually win at medium data sizes. Networks earn their place when you have embeddings of text, merchants or accounts, many tasks sharing one representation, or very large data.

    • Image and signature checks. Cheque images, ID cards and face match use convolutional and transformer nets. All the sizing here applies.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core of this lesson is settled and thirty-plus years old. The live research is mostly at the second topic: how to arrange normalisation and the residual stream so that models with hundreds of layers, trained at extreme scale, do not diverge, and how to make that arrangement more expressive.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures in practice are quiet: training runs and produces a number. Each item has the quickest check.

    • Loss does not move from about 0.69 (or the base-rate value)

      The network outputs the same probability for everyone. Causes: learning rate far too small, dead activations, unscaled inputs, a bug in the labels.

      Spot itCheck that the model can overfit a batch of 50 examples to near-zero loss. If not, the pipeline is broken, not the idea. Check the average prediction against the base rate.
    • Loss turns NaN or jumps

      Exploding gradients, a learning rate too high, or log(0) in a hand-written loss.

      Spot itLog the gradient norm each step. A sudden rise precedes the spike. Gradient clipping and lower learning rate are the first fixes.
    • Dead ReLUs

      A neuron whose pre-activation is negative for every input outputs zero, gets a zero gradient and never recovers. Large learning rates cause it.

      Spot itCount the share of hidden units that are zero on a whole batch. If it is above 30–50% something is off.
    • Unscaled inputs

      AED amounts next to binary flags. The big feature dominates every weighted sum.

      Spot itLook at each input's mean and standard deviation before training. Standardise, or log-transform heavy tails.
    • Different results on every run

      Random initialisation and batch order give a different model per seed. A single run's score may be luck.

      Spot itAsk for results over five or more seeds, with the spread. Differences smaller than the seed-to-seed spread are noise.
    • Train/eval mismatch from batch norm

      Excellent training metrics, poor live ones, because batch statistics differ from the stored averages at inference, or the model was left in training mode.

      Spot itConfirm evaluation uses eval mode, and compare metrics with and without batch statistics. Layer norm avoids the issue.
    • A network where a simpler model would do

      A deep net on 3,000 labelled rows and 30 columns, claimed to beat logistic regression by a point of AUC.

      Spot itAsk for the boosted-tree and logistic baselines on the same time-ordered split, with intervals. A one-point gain on small data is usually noise.
    • Believing the universal approximation theorem means it will work

      The theorem says a good network exists, not that training finds it or that it generalises.

      Spot itWhen a pitch cites it as evidence, ask for held-out, forward-in-time results instead.
    • Treating output scores as probabilities

      A sigmoid output is not automatically calibrated. Modern deep networks tend to be overconfident (Guo et al., 2017).

      Spot itPlot a reliability curve on held-out data (F7.4) and recalibrate before using the score in a threshold or an expected-loss figure.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A neural network is a long chain of simple scoring steps. It learns by seeing how wrong it was on each example and nudging millions of dials a tiny amount in the direction that reduces the error. That is a lot of arithmetic but not a mystery. For our structured customer data, simpler models often match it, so the real case for networks is text, documents, sequences of transactions and images, where they find patterns we cannot hand-code.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “A layer is an affine map followed by a non-linearity; backprop is the chain rule with cached activations, so the cost of a gradient is about two forward passes. Deep stacks train because of variance-preserving initialisation, He for ReLU, a norm before each block, and identity skip paths that give the gradient a direct route. I'd report results across seeds, compare to a boosted-tree baseline on a forward-in-time split, and recalibrate output scores before anything depends on them as probabilities.”
    “Why not just use a deep network for everything?”

    On medium-sized tabular data, trees are usually as accurate or better, train faster, need less tuning and are easier to explain. Networks earn their place with unstructured data (text, images, sequences), shared representations across many tasks, or very large data. I'd start with a boosted baseline and add a network when it clears that baseline with intervals.

    “What actually is backpropagation?”

    The chain rule organised to reuse work. A forward pass stores activations; a backward pass carries an error signal from the loss back through each layer, multiplying by the local slopes and weights, which yields the gradient for every weight in one sweep. It costs roughly two forward passes regardless of parameter count.

    “Why did deeper networks used to be harder to train?”

    The backward signal is multiplied by a factor at every layer. If those factors are typically below one, as with sigmoids, it vanishes; above one, it explodes. Fixes: variance-preserving initialisation, ReLU-type activations, normalisation, and above all residual connections, which add an identity path so the gradient is never forced through all those factors.

    “Batch norm or layer norm?”

    Batch norm depends on the other examples in the batch and behaves differently at inference, which is fine for large-batch image models and awkward otherwise. Layer norm works on one example at a time, so it is the default for transformers and sequences. Recent large language models mostly use RMSNorm, a cheaper variant, in pre-norm position.

    “Our network's AUC changes by 0.01 each time we retrain. Is that a problem?”

    It is normal seed variation, but it matters for governance. Measure it over several seeds and report the spread, treat gains smaller than it as noise, and fix seeds and data order for reproducible validation. If scores for individual customers swing a lot between retrains, consider averaging a few models.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A sigmoid neuron has weights (2, −1), bias −0.5, and receives inputs (1.5, 0.5). What does it output?

    z = 2×1.5 − 1×0.5 − 0.5 = 2.0. The sigmoid of 2 is 1 ÷ (1 + e−2) ≈ 0.881. Its slope there is 0.881 × 0.119 ≈ 0.105.

    2. How many parameters has a network with 100 inputs, a hidden layer of 64, a second hidden layer of 32, and one output?

    (100×64 + 64) + (64×32 + 32) + (32×1 + 1) = 6,464 + 2,080 + 33 = 8,577. Each layer has a weight matrix plus one bias per output unit.

    3. Why can't ten stacked linear layers do more than one?

    The product of matrices is a single matrix. W10…W1x equals Mx for M = W10…W1. Without a non-linearity between them, the layers add no expressive power. This is why Simulation 2 stalls well short of 100% with "Linear".

    4. The output is a sigmoid with cross-entropy loss. The model says 0.9 for a transaction that is legitimate (y = 0). What is the starting error signal, and what if it had said 0.01?

    δ = p − y = 0.9 − 0 = 0.9, a strong push. At 0.01 it is 0.01, nearly none. The error signal is simply "prediction minus truth".

    5. A 20-layer network uses sigmoids, whose slope is at most 0.25. Roughly how much can the gradient shrink from the slopes alone?

    0.2520 ≈ 9 × 10−13. Weights can partly offset this, but with sizes near one the early layers receive essentially no signal. That is the vanishing gradient.

    6. A layer takes 256 inputs and uses ReLU. What weight standard deviation does He initialisation use, and why not the Xavier value?

    √(2/256) ≈ 0.088, against Xavier's 1/16 = 0.0625. ReLU zeros out about half the pre-activations, which halves the variance of the signal, so the weights need twice the variance to compensate.

    7. Why does a residual connection help the gradient, in one sentence?

    The derivative of h + F(h) is I + ∂F/∂h, so the backward signal always has a direct path multiplied by one, however poorly the block F is behaving.

    8. A colleague says "more hidden units guarantees a better model because of universal approximation". What is wrong?

    The theorem is about existence of an approximating network, not about finding it by gradient descent or about generalising to new data. More units can fit noise and need more regularisation, more positives to support them and more compute.
