    Neural Networks from the Inside: Neurons, Backpropagation and Trainable Depth

    What a network computes, how the chain rule trains its weights, and why depth needs three tricks.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA network is a stack of weighted sums and bends that learns by backpropagation, and deep stacks train only because initialisation, normalisation and skip connections keep that signal alive.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a fraud analyst weighing small signals at once: high amount, 3 a.m., new merchant. That is one neuron: a weighted sum of inputs plus a bias, passed through a bend. A layer is many such analysts, and the next layer sees their verdicts, so the network builds features nobody wrote down.

    Learning is tuning dials. Backpropagation asks every weight: "if I nudged you up, would the error rise or fall?" Each takes a small step, repeated millions of times. Depth adds a problem: that message is multiplied at every layer, so it can vanish or explode. Much of deep learning's engineering keeps it alive.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    One neuron: a weighted vote and a bend

    A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias and passes the total through an activation function. A sigmoid alone is logistic regression (F4.2); a network is that core, stacked. Geometrically, it draws a line and asks which side an input is on.

    The slope in the third box matters because backpropagation multiplies by it at every neuron. Sigmoid is flat at both ends, the root of the vanishing gradient. ReLU has slope 1 on its active side, so it largely replaced sigmoid in hidden layers.

    Layers, and why the bend is not optional

    A layer is many neurons side by side, and a network chains layers. Without the bend, a hundred layers collapse into one straight-line model, no better than logistic regression.

    The universal approximation theorem says a wide-enough hidden layer can approximate any reasonable function. It says such a network exists, not that gradient descent finds it or that it generalises.

    Backpropagation: the chain rule, run backwards

    Training needs the gradient: how the loss changes if each weight moves (F1.3). A forward pass stores activations. One backward pass then carries an error signal back through the layers, scaled at each neuron by the bend's slope, giving every weight's gradient at once. It costs about two forward passes, however many weights there are.

    At the output, for a sigmoid with cross-entropy loss, the starting signal is just predicted probability minus true label. Simulation 2 trains a tiny network on XOR.

    Each hidden unit finds one slanted line and the output neuron combines them. Real training uses mini-batches, a noisy but cheap gradient, and optimisers such as Adam (F1.3).

    Initialisation: start the signal at the right size

    Small random starting weights sound harmless, but too small and the signal shrinks layer by layer, too large and it blows up. It compounds: 0.9 per layer becomes 0.005 after 50 layers.

    The cure is to scale starting weights so the signal stays the same size. Xavier suits tanh and sigmoid; He uses larger weights for ReLU. Random starts also stop hidden neurons being identical.

    Normalisation: re-centre the signal at every layer

    Normalisation layers rescale activations on the fly (centre them, set their spread to one) so the scale cannot drift as weights change. Batch norm uses the batch, so training and live behaviour can differ. Layer norm works per example and is standard in transformers. For tabular banking models, standardise inputs too (F3.4).

    Residual connections: a highway for the gradient

    Very deep nets once trained worse than shallower ones, even on training data. The fix is the residual connection: each block adds its output to its input instead of replacing it, like editing a draft rather than rewriting it. The gradient gets a direct path through, and a block that does nothing is harmless.

    Every transformer works this way: the running vector is the residual stream (R5). Simulation 3 puts all three tricks on one stage.

    Sigmoid at depth 20 shows the vanishing gradient: early layers receive almost nothing. Too-large weights give the exploding gradient, usually seen as a loss that turns NaN. With skips and layer norm together, both stay in range, which is the pairing in nearly every transformer.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Loss stuck near 0.69 (or the base-rate value)

      The network gives everyone the same probability: learning rate too small, dead ReLU units, unscaled inputs or a label bug.

      Spot itCheck the model can overfit 50 examples. If not, the pipeline is broken.
    • Loss turns NaN or jumps

      Exploding gradients or a learning rate too high.

      Spot itLog the gradient norm; a rise precedes the spike. Clip gradients and lower the rate.
    • Unscaled inputs

      AED amounts next to binary flags: the big feature dominates every weighted sum.

      Spot itCheck each input's mean and spread. Standardise, or log-transform heavy tails.
    • One lucky run, or a train/eval mismatch

      Random starts give a different model per seed. Batch norm can also look good in training and poor live if left in training mode.

      Spot itAsk for five or more seeds with the spread, and confirm eval mode.
    • A deep net where a simpler model would do

      A deep net on 3,000 rows claiming a point of AUC over logistic regression. The approximation theorem is no evidence it works. Its scores are also not automatically calibrated.

      Spot itAsk for boosted-tree and logistic baselines on the same time-ordered split, and a reliability curve (F7.4).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A neuron's output barely changes however you move one input. What could explain it?

    Either its weight on that input is near zero, or it sits on a flat part of its curve (saturated sigmoid, or ReLU on its zero side). The second is how learning stalls.

    2. Why can't ten stacked linear layers do more than one?

    Without a bend between them, the layers collapse into one linear step. Ten rulers stacked still draw a straight line.

    3. The model says 0.9 for a legitimate transaction. How strong is the starting error signal, compared with 0.01?

    The signal is prediction minus truth, so 0.9 is a strong push and 0.01 is almost none.

    4. Why do early layers of a deep sigmoid network struggle to learn?

    Sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so each layer shrinks the backward message. After many layers almost nothing reaches the start. That is the vanishing gradient.

    5. A colleague says "more hidden units guarantees a better model, because of universal approximation". What is wrong?

    The theorem says a good network exists, not that gradient descent finds it or that it generalises. More units can also fit noise.
