    Neural Networks and Backpropagation

    Part 1

    What a neural network is

    In one sentenceA neural network is many small weighted-vote units (neurons) stacked in layers, whose dials (weights) are tuned automatically to cut the error.

    Our running example: decide whether a day is good for a picnic. Two inputs: how sunny it is, and how windy. One neuron does three things.

    1. Multiply each input by a weight (how much it matters; negative means it counts against).
    2. Add them up, plus a bias (the neuron's built-in mood, how easily it says yes).
    3. Pass the total through a bend (an activation function), which turns it into the output.

    Common bends: sigmoid squashes any number into 0 to 1, like a probability. ReLU outputs zero for negative totals and the total itself for positive ones. Softmax turns several scores into probabilities that add to 1, and is the usual output for multi-class problems.

    How it differs from other models

    • Logistic regression is exactly one neuron with a sigmoid. It draws one straight dividing line.
    • A neural network stacks many neurons, so it can bend the dividing line into curves.
    • Tree models cut the data into boxes. Networks learn smooth shapes, and they learn their own features from raw inputs such as pixels or words.
    Part 2

    Layers: building features nobody wrote down

    A layer is several neurons side by side, all looking at the same inputs. The next layer sees their outputs. So the first layer might learn "nice weather overall", and the next might combine that with "weekend" into "good picnic day".

    Why the bend is not optional. Without it, a hundred layers collapse into one straight-line model, no better than logistic regression. The bend is what lets stacking add power.

    • Width is neurons per layer; depth is the number of layers. These are your main design choices.
    • Parameters (the weights and biases) grow with width: doubling a layer's width roughly quadruples its weights.
    • Hidden layers are everything between input and output. Their outputs are the features the network invents.
    Part 3

    How it learns: backpropagation

    In one sentenceBackpropagation is a fast way to ask every weight, all at once, "if I nudged you up, would the error rise or fall?"
    1. Forward pass. Feed in a day's weather, let it flow through the layers, get a prediction.
    2. Measure the error against the true answer (did the picnic go well?). This is the loss.
    3. Backward pass. Send the error back through the layers. At each neuron it is scaled by the slope of the bend. Every weight ends up with a number: which way, and how strongly, it should move.
    4. Nudge every weight a small step in the direction that cuts the error.
    5. Repeat on many batches of examples, millions of times.

    Why not just nudge each weight and re-measure? That costs one full pass per weight, which is hopeless for millions of weights. The backward pass costs about two forward passes in total, however many weights there are. The price is memory, since the forward values must be kept.

    How to read training

    • Loss falling on both training and held-out data means it is learning. For a yes/no output, a loss of about 0.69 is a coin flip.
    • Training loss falls, held-out loss rises means overfitting. Use early stopping: stop when held-out loss stops improving.
    • Loss jumps around or turns NaN means the learning rate is too high. Barely moves means too low.
    • Learning rate is the size of each nudge. Batch size is how many examples feed each nudge; bigger batches are smoother. Optimisers such as Adam scale each weight's step by how large its recent gradients have been.
    Part 4

    Making deep networks trainable

    Depth has a catch. The backward message is multiplied at every layer. If each step shrinks it a little, 0.9 per layer becomes about 0.005 after 50 layers: the vanishing gradient, where early layers learn almost nothing. If each step grows it, you get the exploding gradient, usually seen as loss turning NaN. Four tricks keep the signal healthy.

    • ReLU instead of sigmoid. Sigmoid is flat at both ends, so it passes back almost nothing. ReLU has slope 1 on its active side. Its one failure is a "dead" neuron stuck on the zero side. Variants such as leaky ReLU, GELU and SiLU keep a small slope there.
    • Sensible starting weights. Too small and the signal shrinks layer by layer; too large and it blows up. Xavier initialisation suits sigmoid and tanh, He initialisation suits ReLU. Random starts also stop neurons being identical copies.
    • Normalisation. Layers that re-centre and rescale the signal on the fly. Batch norm uses the batch; layer norm works per example and is standard in transformers.
    • Residual (skip) connections. Each block adds its output to its input instead of replacing it, like editing a draft rather than rewriting it. The gradient gets a direct path through, and a block that does nothing is harmless. Every transformer works this way.
    Part 5

    Which one to use

    ModelHow it thinksStrong atWeak at
    Neural networkStacked weighted votes with bendsImages, text, audio, huge dataNeeds lots of data and tuning; hard to explain
    Gradient boostingTrees fixing each other's errorsSpreadsheet-style dataRaw images, text, sound
    Logistic regressionOne weighted voteSmall data, easy to explainCurved boundaries

    Neural networks are strong at:

    • Learning features from raw inputs such as pixels, audio, words and long event sequences.
    • Scaling with data: with millions of examples, accuracy usually keeps improving where simpler models plateau.
    • Reuse: a pretrained network can be fine-tuned on a small labelled set.

    They are weak at:

    • Small tables of data, where boosting is usually as good or better with far less effort.
    • Explaining themselves. Millions of weights don't give a readable rule.
    • Cost: they need more data, compute and tuning than classical models.

    In practice: for table data, start with boosting. Reach for a neural network when the input is images, text, audio or sequences, or when you can fine-tune a pretrained one.

    Show the formula

    One neuron: output = bend(weight₁ × input₁ + weight₂ × input₂ + bias). With a sigmoid bend, that is logistic regression.

    Each weight's update: new weight = old weight − learning rate × (how much the loss changes per unit change in that weight). Backpropagation supplies that last part for every weight at once.

    In banking, neural networks power document reading, call-centre speech and text models, and sequence models on transaction histories, often alongside a simpler model for regulators.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Using a network on a small table

      Boosting usually matches it with less effort and is easier to explain.

    • Judging by training accuracy

      A big network can memorise its training data. Always watch held-out data and stop early.

    • Skipping the bend

      Stacked layers without activations are just one linear model, however deep.

    • Learning rate left at a guess

      Too high gives jumping or NaN loss; too low crawls. It is the first knob to tune.

    1. What does one neuron do?

    It multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias, and passes the total through a bend such as sigmoid or ReLU.

    2. Why does a network need the bend between layers?

    Without it, stacked layers collapse into a single straight-line model. The bend lets depth add power.

    3. What does backpropagation compute, and why is it fast?

    For every weight, which way and how strongly it should move to cut the error. One backward pass costs about two forward passes, however many weights there are.

    4. Early layers of a 50-layer sigmoid network barely learn. Why, and what helps?

    The vanishing gradient: the backward message shrinks at every layer. Use ReLU, sensible starting weights, normalisation and skip connections.

    5. Training loss keeps falling but held-out loss rises. What now?

    It is overfitting. Stop early, or add more data or regularisation.
