    Gradients, Backpropagation and Optimisation

    How a model finds out which way to adjust millions of numbers at once, why training sometimes stalls or explodes, and what optimisers such as Adam actually do about it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining is walking downhill on a loss surface in fog: backpropagation is the chain rule used to feel the slope in every direction at once, and an optimiser is the rule for how big a step to take, and in which direction.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take a credit model predicting whether a personal-loan customer will default. Its adjustable numbers are weights: how much to care about debt-to-income, missed payments, salary transfers. A small logistic model has 40. A fraud network might have 2 million. A fine-tuned open-weights LLM has billions.

    For any setting of the weights, one number says how wrong the model is on training data: the loss. Picture each weight setting as a position on a landscape, with loss as height. Training means finding a low point.

    You are in thick fog and can only feel the slope underfoot. That slope is the gradient. Training is a loop: feel the slope, step downhill, repeat thousands or millions of times. Step size is the learning rate, the setting that most often makes or breaks a run.

    • Step size alone means nothing; learning rate times curvature does. Below 1, you slide smoothly to the bottom. Between 1 and 2 you overshoot and zigzag inwards. Above 2 each overshoot grows and the loss explodes: the “loss went to NaN” every ML engineer has seen.
    • Too small is a quiet failure. Nothing breaks on screen. Training takes 20 times longer or stops early, and someone concludes the features are weak.
    • A real model has millions of directions, each with its own sharpness. One learning rate must be safe in the sharpest, so it is painfully slow in the flattest. That tension explains almost every optimiser since the 1960s.
    Hold onto thisEvery training run is one loop: measure the loss, compute its gradient with backpropagation, step against the gradient. Part 2 covers where the gradient comes from, how it vanishes or explodes, and how optimisers choose the step.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Derivatives: sensitivity of one number to another

    A derivative answers: if I nudge this input a little, how much does the output move? If default probability is 0.08 and its derivative with respect to debt-to-income is 0.30, raising the ratio by 0.01 raises the probability by about 0.003. It is an exchange rate between two quantities, valid for small changes, like a car's speedometer reading right now.

    f(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)·h

    For a small nudge h, the output moves by the derivative times the nudge. The approximation gets worse as h grows, because the curve bends. That bending is the curvature from Simulation 1, and it's why big steps go wrong.

    The gradient: every sensitivity at once

    A model has many weights, so the loss has one derivative per weight. Stack them and you get the gradient, ∇L. Each entry is a partial derivative: how the loss moves when you nudge that one weight and hold the others still. Think of a mixing desk with a thousand sliders, and the gradient as the effect of each slider on the sound.

    ∇L=(∂L∂w1,∂L∂w2,…,∂L∂wn) w←w−η∇L

    First line: the gradient is a vector with one entry per weight. Second line: gradient descent moves every weight a small amount η (the learning rate) against its own entry.

    For a small step Δw, the loss changes by about ∇L · Δw (the dot product, F1.1). Two facts follow:

    • The gradient points steepest uphill. Of all steps of a given length, the one aligned with it raises the loss most; its negative is steepest descent.
    • It is perpendicular to the contour lines. Moving along a contour leaves the loss unchanged, so that direction has zero dot product with the gradient. Plain descent therefore crosses contours at right angles, which is why it zigzags in a narrow valley (Simulation 4).

    Its size tells steepness: large means steep, near zero means flat. Flat could be the bottom, a plateau or a saddle, and the gradient alone can't tell you which.

    The chain rule and backpropagation

    A model is a chain of simple steps, like a recipe. For a one-neuron credit model: multiply each input by its weight, add a bias to get z, squash through a sigmoid to get a probability p, score p against what happened with log loss (F1.7). Each step is easy to differentiate. The chain rule: the sensitivity of the whole chain is the product of the sensitivities of its links.

    ∂L∂w1=∂L∂p·∂p∂z·∂z∂w1=(p−y)·x1

    If the loss moves 3 units per unit of p, and p moves 0.25 per unit of z, then the loss moves 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 per unit of z. Multiply the local exchange rates along the path. For log loss and a sigmoid, the first two factors collapse to the prediction error p − y.

    Backpropagation is the chain rule organised cleverly, like auditing a bill from the total back to each line item. Run the model forwards once, keeping every intermediate value. Then walk backwards from the loss: at each node, take the gradient arriving from above, multiply by the node's local derivative, and pass it to each input. Shared gradients, such as ∂L/∂z here, are computed once and reused by every weight below.

    One backward sweep gave the gradient for every weight, and every input. The naive alternative is to nudge each weight and re-run the model: for 7 billion weights, 7 billion forward passes per step. Backpropagation costs roughly two or three forward passes, whatever the weight count. This is reverse-mode automatic differentiation; PyTorch and JAX record the graph as your code runs and sweep backwards on loss.backward().

    The price is memory: forward values (p, x) are stored until the backward pass uses them. For large models, activation memory often runs out before weight memory. Gradient checkpointing trades it back for extra compute by recomputing some activations.

    Vanishing and exploding gradients

    The chain rule multiplies, like a rumour passed through 20 people who each repeat only a quarter of it. A deep network is a long chain, so the gradient reaching layer 1 is a product of dozens of local factors. Typically below 1 and it shrinks exponentially; above 1 and it grows exponentially. There is little room in between.

    A sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so 20 sigmoid layers can shrink the gradient by 0.2520, about 10−12. Early layers get updates a trillion times smaller than late ones and effectively never learn. That is why deep networks were nearly untrainable until about 2010–2015, and why recurrent networks forgot anything more than a few dozen steps back in a transaction sequence.

    The fixes are the standard deep-learning toolkit (F5.2):

    • ReLU activations: slope exactly 1 where active, so no shrinkage by themselves.
    • Careful initialisation (Glorot, He): random starting weights chosen so each layer neither shrinks nor grows the signal on average. The scale slider shows how narrow that window is.
    • Residual connections: compute h + f(h). The “+ h” is a direct path back with derivative 1, a motorway beside the winding road.
    • Normalisation layers: rescale activations at every layer so nothing drifts out of range.
    • Gradient clipping: a blunt safety net. If the gradient's overall size exceeds a limit, scale it down. Almost every LLM recipe uses it.

    Gradients as explanations

    Backpropagation also gives gradients with respect to the inputs. ∂p/∂x answers: which inputs, nudged slightly, move this prediction most? Shown as a heat map over an image, document or customer's features, it is a saliency map, the oldest neural explanation method.

    Weakness: it measures local sensitivity, not contribution. For the one-neuron model, ∂p/∂x1 = p(1 − p) × w1. At 99% default, p(1 − p) is about 0.01, so every input looks unimportant exactly when they push hardest. Like judging a full bucket by how much one more drop changes it.

    Integrated gradients fixes this by summing the gradient along a straight path from a baseline customer to this one. Attributions add up exactly to the change in prediction, the property that makes SHAP (F8.2) attractive; gradient-based SHAP variants work the same way. If a validator asks how a neural fraud model's reason codes are made, the answer often starts with backpropagation. Like any reason-code method, they explain the model, not the world.

    Loss landscapes: convex, non-convex, saddles

    Convex means bowl-shaped everywhere: one valley, so any converging solver finds the same answer. Logistic regression with log loss is convex, which is a quiet reason scorecards are regulator-friendly: same data, same coefficients, whoever fits them.

    Neural networks are non-convex: many low regions, plateaus and ridges, and where you land depends on the seed, data order and optimiser. Two networks trained on the same data with different seeds can have the same AUC and still disagree on thousands of customers, which matters when one is declined.

    The old worry was getting stuck in a bad local minimum. In high dimensions it rarely is. A minimum needs upward curvature in every direction; with a million directions, a flat point almost surely curves down in some. That is a saddle point, like a mountain pass. Gradients there are tiny, so plain descent creeps. Research since 2014 suggests most high-loss critical points in large networks are saddles, and most local minima are nearly as good as each other.

    Learning rate, noise and batch size

    Simulation 1's rule: on a bowl with curvature a, descent converges only if η < 2/a. In a real network the sharpest direction (largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, the curvature matrix) sets the limit. The edge of stability finding (2021): full-batch training pushes sharpness up until it sits at 2/η and hovers there. The network adapts its landscape to the step size you choose.

    Nobody computes the gradient on all the data at once. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses a random mini-batch, say 256 customers, like polling a sample instead of the whole electorate. The estimate is noisy, but cheap, and the noise jostles weights off saddles and away from very sharp minima, which often generalise worse.

    Batch size and learning rate are linked. A bigger batch is less noisy, so you can step further. The linear scaling rule: double the batch, double the rate, up to a point. Beyond the critical batch size, extra examples stop buying faster progress and just burn compute. It grows with the amount of training data far more than with model size.

    Momentum, RMSProp and Adam

    Plain gradient descent has two weaknesses: it zigzags across narrow valleys (the sharp direction caps the step while the flat direction starves), and it crawls near saddles where the gradient is tiny.

    Momentum steps along a running average of recent gradients, like a heavy ball rolling down the valley. Zigzag components alternate in sign and cancel; the consistent downhill part adds up. With the usual β = 0.9, a steady gradient is amplified up to 10 times, because 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10.

    v←βv+gw←w−ηv

    g is the current gradient and v is the velocity, a decaying sum of past gradients.

    Per-parameter step sizes fix the curvature mismatch directly, like giving each hiker a stride suited to their own terrain. RMSProp keeps a running average of each weight's squared gradient and divides the step by its square root: consistently large gradients get smaller steps, tiny ones get bigger steps. Adam combines this with momentum, plus a correction for the first steps when the averages are near zero.

    m←β1m+(1−β1)g s←β2s+(1−β2)g2 w←w−ηm^s^+ε

    m is the momentum and s the running average of squared gradients, both kept separately for every weight. Hats mean bias-corrected. Defaults: β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, often 0.95 for LLMs. The ratio is roughly ±1 per step whatever the gradient's raw size, so η becomes a step length in weight units.

    The stretched valley is common. Put an account balance in AED (millions) next to a ratio (0 to 1) and the loss is far more sensitive to one weight, so the valley is long and thin. Standardising features is the cheapest optimiser upgrade there is.

    Push the shared learning rate above 0.5 and SGD diverges. That is Simulation 1's rule: the sharp direction has curvature 4, and 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. Momentum and Adam survive well beyond it.

    The tilted valley shows Adam's limit. Its rescaling is diagonal: one number per weight, aligned with the weight axes. When sharp and flat directions mix several weights, as they mostly do in real networks, it can't fully straighten the valley. Newer optimisers (Shampoo, SOAP, Muon) work on whole weight matrices to close this gap. Careful benchmarks put their gain over a well-tuned AdamW at roughly 1.1–1.4×, shrinking as models grow, so AdamW stays the default for bank-sized models.

    AdamW applies weight decay (shrinking weights towards zero) separately from the adaptive step. The older L2 penalty went into the gradient, and Adam's division by √s then weakened the decay on exactly the weights that move most. AdamW is now the default almost everywhere.

    Warmup and schedules

    The best learning rate changes during training, like driving: slow out of the car park, fast on the motorway, slow again to park.

    • Warmup. Ramp up from near zero over the first hundreds or thousands of steps. Early on the weights are random, the landscape is at its sharpest and Adam's averages rest on few gradients, so full-size steps can throw the model somewhere it never recovers from.
    • Decay. Shrink the step towards the end so the weights settle instead of bouncing. Common shapes: linear, cosine, step drops.
    • Warmup-stable-decay (WSD). Hold the rate constant, then decay sharply at the end. You needn't fix the training length in advance: branch off and decay from any checkpoint.

    Mental picture, the river valley: the high constant rate makes fast progress along the valley floor while bouncing off the walls. The final decay stops the bouncing and reveals the progress already made, so loss drops sharply when decay begins.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Loss becomes NaN or shoots upwards

      The learning rate is above the stability limit for some sharp direction, often at the start or after an extreme batch, such as one AED 40 million corporate transfer in a retail dataset.

      Spot itLog the gradient norm every step. A spike just before the loss jumps means exploding gradients. Lower the learning rate, add warmup and clipping, check inputs for outliers.
    • Loss falls a little, then goes flat

      Causes: a learning rate that is too small, a saddle plateau, vanishing gradients in early layers, or “dead” ReLUs that output zero for every input.

      Spot itLook at per-layer gradient norms (Simulation 3). Early layers thousands of times smaller than late ones means an architecture or initialisation problem, not a data problem.
    • Unscaled features make a stretched valley

      Balances in AED next to ratios between 0 and 1 give the narrow valley from Simulation 4. Training is slow, unstable, or both.

      Spot itCheck inputs are standardised or log-transformed before a neural network sees them. Trees don't care; neural networks and regularised regressions do.
    • Fine-tuning learning rate copied from a from-scratch recipe

      A rate of 10−3 on a pretrained Arabic-English model can wipe out what it knew within a few hundred steps (catastrophic forgetting).

      Spot itFine-tuning rates are usually 10–100 times smaller than pretraining rates. Test on a general held-out set, not only the new task.
    • Adam with L2 when AdamW was meant

      In PyTorch, Adam(weight_decay=…) adds an L2 penalty to the gradient. It is not decoupled decay, so regularisation differs from what the recipe assumed.

      Spot itSearch the code for the optimiser line. If weight decay matters, it should be AdamW.
    • Changing the batch size and keeping the learning rate

      Quadrupling the batch on a bigger GPU without touching the rate makes training slower per example. Raising the rate without warmup can make it diverge.

      Spot itAny batch-size change comes with a learning-rate re-check, ideally a short sweep.
    • Schedule mismatched to the run

      A cosine schedule set for 10 epochs but stopped at 3 leaves the rate high, so the model never settles. Resuming from a checkpoint can restart warmup by accident.

      Spot itPlot the learning rate beside the loss. It should be a deliberate shape that ends low.
    • “Our new optimiser is twice as fast”

      Most such claims compare against an untuned AdamW. Careful benchmarks shrink the gain to about 10–40%, and AdamW with warmup, decay and clipping remains the right default for a bank.

      Spot itAsk whether the AdamW baseline had its own learning-rate and weight-decay sweep, at the same scale and token budget.
    • Different seed, different customers declined

      Non-convex training means two equally accurate models can disagree on individual decisions. A validator re-running your training may get a different model.

      Spot itRetrain with several seeds and count how many decisions flip. Fix seeds and record library versions.
    • AI-generated training loop with silent bugs

      Typical errors: no zero_grad(), so gradients pile up; scheduler.step() per epoch when the schedule was per batch; gradients flowing into a model meant to be frozen.

      Spot itTrain on 10 examples. A correct loop drives training loss to near zero. If it can't overfit a tiny set, the loop is broken.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A one-neuron credit model predicts p = 0.8 for a customer who repaid (y = 0). Their debt-to-income input is 0.5. What is ∂L/∂z, what is the gradient for that weight, and which way does the weight move?

    ∂L/∂z = p − y = 0.8. ∂L/∂w = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4. Descent subtracts η × 0.4, so the weight decreases: the model was wrongly alarmed, so it gives this ratio less weight towards default.

    2. A loss is ½ × 4 × w². What is the largest learning rate that converges? What happens at η = 0.4?

    The limit is 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. At η = 0.4 each step multiplies w by 1 − 0.4 × 4 = −0.6, so it flips sign every step but still converges, shrinking by 40% each time.

    3. Each of 20 sigmoid layers multiplies the backward gradient by at most 0.25. How much smaller is the gradient reaching layer 1?

    0.2520 ≈ 9 × 10−13, nearly a trillion times smaller. Hence ReLUs, residual connections and careful initialisation.

    4. With momentum β = 0.9 and a constant gradient, how much bigger is the step than plain SGD's?

    Velocity tends to g × (1 + 0.9 + 0.9² + …) = g ÷ (1 − 0.9) = 10 g. Steps are about 10 times larger along a consistent direction, while sign-alternating gradients largely cancel.

    5. Two weights have steady gradients of 0.001 and 1,000. Roughly how big are Adam's steps, and why is that useful and risky?

    Both are about η, because Adam divides each gradient by the root of its own running mean square. Useful: the flat direction finally gets a sensible step. Risky: step size no longer reflects importance, so a weight with tiny, noisy gradients is pushed as hard as one that matters. Hence warmup, decay and weight decay.

    6. True or false: in a large network, a zero-gradient point with high loss is most likely a bad local minimum.

    False, in all likelihood. With millions of directions, a flat point almost surely curves down in at least one, making it a saddle. Noise, momentum and adaptive methods help escape.

    7. You move fine-tuning from batch size 16 to 128. What should you check?

    The learning rate. The gradient is less noisy, but the same rate now takes 8 times fewer steps per epoch. Linear scaling suggests raising it up to 8 times for SGD (less for Adam), with warmup. Beyond the critical batch size the gain flattens, so run a short sweep.
