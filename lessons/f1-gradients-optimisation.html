    Gradients, Backpropagation and Optimisation

    How a model finds out which way to adjust millions of numbers at once, why training sometimes stalls or explodes, and what optimisers such as Adam actually do about it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining is walking downhill on a loss surface in fog: backpropagation is the chain rule used to feel the slope in every direction at once, and an optimiser is the rule for how big a step to take, and in which direction.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take a credit model that predicts whether a personal-loan customer will default. It has some adjustable numbers, called weights: how much to care about debt-to-income ratio, about missed payments, about salary transfers. A small logistic model has 40 of them. A fraud network on transaction sequences might have 2 million. An open-weights LLM you fine-tune on Arabic complaints has billions.

    For any setting of the weights you can measure how wrong the model is on your training data. That single number is the loss. Picture every possible setting of the weights as a position on a landscape, with the loss as the height. Training means finding a low point.

    The catch is that you're in thick fog. You can't see the landscape. You can only feel the slope under your feet. That slope is the gradient. So training is a loop: feel the slope, take a step downhill, repeat a few thousand or a few million times. How far you step each time is the learning rate, and it's the setting that most often makes or breaks a training run.

    Three things should stand out.

    • Step size alone means nothing. What matters is the learning rate times the curvature. Below 1, you slide smoothly to the bottom. Between 1 and 2 you overshoot each time and zigzag inwards. Above 2, each overshoot is bigger than the last and the loss explodes. That last case is the “loss went to NaN” message every ML engineer has seen.
    • Too small is a quiet failure. Nothing goes wrong on screen. Training just takes 20 times longer, or stops before it gets anywhere, and someone concludes the features are weak.
    • A real model has millions of directions, each with its own sharpness. One learning rate has to be safe in the sharpest direction, which makes it painfully slow in the flattest. That single tension explains almost every optimiser invented since the 1960s, and it's where this lesson ends up.
    Hold onto thisEvery training run is the same three-step loop: measure the loss, compute its gradient with backpropagation, step against the gradient. Part 2 explains where the gradient comes from and why it can vanish or explode, then how optimisers choose the step.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Derivatives: sensitivity of one number to another

    A derivative answers one question: if I nudge this input a little, how much does the output move? If a credit model's probability of default is 0.08 and its derivative with respect to debt-to-income ratio is 0.30, then raising the ratio by 0.01 raises the probability by about 0.003. That's all a derivative is, an exchange rate between two quantities, valid for small changes.

    f(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)·h

    For a small nudge h, the output moves by the derivative times the nudge. The approximation gets worse as h grows, because the curve bends. That bending is the curvature from Simulation 1, and it's why big steps go wrong.

    The gradient: every sensitivity at once

    A model has many weights, so the loss has many derivatives, one per weight. Stack them into a list and you have the gradient, written ∇L. Each entry is a partial derivative: how the loss moves if you nudge that one weight and hold every other weight still.

    ∇L=(∂L∂w1,∂L∂w2,…,∂L∂wn) w←w−η∇L

    First line: the gradient is a vector with one entry per weight. Second line: gradient descent moves every weight a small amount η (the learning rate) against its own entry.

    Two geometric facts make the gradient special, and both come from the dot product (F1.1). For a small step Δw, the loss changes by about ∇L · Δw. So:

    • The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent. Of all steps of a given length, the one that raises the loss most is the one aligned with the gradient. Its negative is steepest descent.
    • It is perpendicular to the contour lines. Moving along a contour doesn't change the loss, so that direction has zero dot product with the gradient. You'll see this in Simulation 4: plain gradient descent always crosses contours at right angles, which is exactly why it zigzags in a narrow valley.

    The size of the gradient matters too. A large gradient means a steep slope, and a gradient near zero means you're on flat ground. Flat ground could be the bottom, a plateau, or a saddle. The gradient alone can't tell you which.

    The chain rule and backpropagation

    A model is a chain of simple operations. For a one-neuron credit model: multiply each input by its weight, add them up with a bias to get z, squash z through a sigmoid to get a probability p, then score p against what really happened with log loss (F1.7). Each step is easy to differentiate on its own. The chain rule says the sensitivity of the whole chain is the product of the sensitivities of its links.

    ∂L∂w1=∂L∂p·∂p∂z·∂z∂w1=(p−y)·x1

    If the loss moves 3 units per unit of p, and p moves 0.25 per unit of z, then the loss moves 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 per unit of z. Multiply the local exchange rates along the path. For log loss and a sigmoid, the first two factors collapse to the prediction error p − y.

    Backpropagation is the chain rule organised cleverly. Run the model forwards once and keep every intermediate value. Then start at the loss and walk backwards. At each node, take the gradient arriving from above, multiply by that node's local derivative, and pass the result down to each input. Gradients that branches share, such as ∂L/∂z here, are computed once and reused by every weight below them.

    Look at what the backward pass gave you. One sweep produced the gradient for every weight, and for every input too. That's the whole trick. A naive alternative is to nudge each weight separately and re-run the model to see what changes. For 7 billion weights that's 7 billion forward passes per step. Backpropagation gets all of them for roughly the cost of two or three forward passes, whatever the number of weights. In the literature this is reverse-mode automatic differentiation. PyTorch and JAX build the graph as your code runs and do the backward sweep for you when you call loss.backward().

    The price is memory. The backward pass needs the forward values (p and x above), so every intermediate activation is stored until it's used. For large models, activation memory, not weight memory, is often what runs out first. Techniques such as gradient checkpointing trade it back for extra compute by recomputing some activations.

    Vanishing and exploding gradients

    The chain rule multiplies. A deep network is a long chain, so the gradient reaching the first layer is a product of dozens of local factors. If those factors are typically below 1, the product shrinks exponentially. If they're above 1, it grows exponentially. There's very little room in between.

    The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so a chain of 20 sigmoid layers can shrink the gradient by 0.2520, about 10−12. The early layers then get updates a trillion times smaller than the late ones, and effectively never learn. This is why networks deeper than a few layers were nearly untrainable until around 2010–2015, and why recurrent networks struggled to remember anything more than a few dozen steps back in a transaction sequence.

    The fixes became the standard toolkit of deep learning (F5.2):

    • ReLU activations, whose slope is exactly 1 wherever they're active, so they don't shrink the gradient by themselves.
    • Careful initialisation (Glorot, then He), which sets the random starting weights so that each layer neither shrinks nor grows the signal on average. The scale slider shows how narrow that window is.
    • Residual connections, which compute h + f(h) instead of f(h). The “+ h” gives the gradient a direct path back with a local derivative of 1, so it can't vanish along that route.
    • Normalisation layers, which rescale activations at every layer so that nothing drifts out of range.
    • Gradient clipping, a blunt safety net: if the gradient's overall size exceeds a limit, scale it down before stepping. Almost every LLM training and fine-tuning recipe uses it.

    Gradients as explanations

    Backpropagation gives you gradients with respect to the inputs as a free by-product, as Simulation 2 showed. ∂p/∂x answers “which inputs, nudged slightly, would move this prediction most?” Shown as a heat map over an image, a document or a customer's features, it's called a saliency map. It's the oldest explanation method for neural networks.

    It has a known weakness, and the sigmoid shows it plainly. For the one-neuron model, ∂p/∂x1 = p(1 − p) × w1. For a customer the model is 99% sure will default, p(1 − p) is about 0.01, so every input looks unimportant, precisely when the inputs are pushing hardest. The gradient measures local sensitivity, not contribution.

    Integrated gradients fixes this by adding up the gradient along a straight path from a baseline customer to this customer. Its attributions sum exactly to the difference in prediction, the same “adds up” property that makes SHAP (F8.2) attractive. Gradient-based variants of SHAP are built the same way. So if a validator asks how a neural fraud model's reason codes are produced, the answer often starts with backpropagation.

    Loss landscapes: convex, non-convex, saddles

    A loss surface is convex if it's bowl-shaped everywhere. A straight line between any two points on the surface lies above it. Convex problems have one lowest valley, so any downhill method that converges finds the best answer, and different solvers agree. Logistic regression with log loss is convex. That's a quiet reason credit scorecards are regulator-friendly: the same data gives the same coefficients, whoever fits them.

    Neural networks are non-convex. Their landscapes have many low regions, plateaus and ridges, and where you end up depends on the random starting point, the data order and the optimiser. Two networks trained on identical data with different random seeds can have the same AUC and still disagree on thousands of individual customers. That has consequences when one of those customers is declined.

    For decades the worry was getting stuck in a bad local minimum. In high dimensions it mostly isn't the problem. For a point to be a local minimum, the surface has to curve upwards in every direction. With a million directions, a flat point is far more likely to curve up in some and down in others. That's a saddle point, like a mountain pass. Saddles are what make training plateau. The gradient near them is tiny, so plain gradient descent creeps for a long time before it finds the downhill way out. Research since 2014 suggests that in large networks, most high-loss critical points are saddles, and most local minima are nearly as good as each other.

    Learning rate, noise and batch size

    Simulation 1 showed the core rule. On a bowl with curvature a, gradient descent converges only if η < 2/a. In a real network the binding constraint is the sharpest direction, measured by the largest eigenvalue of the curvature matrix (the Hessian). A striking 2021 finding, the edge of stability, is that full-batch training tends to push the sharpness up until it sits right at 2/η and then hovers there. The loss keeps falling, but not smoothly. Networks adapt their landscape to the step size you choose.

    In practice nobody computes the gradient on all the data at once. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates it from a random mini-batch of, say, 256 customers. The estimate is noisy, and the noise is mostly useful. It's cheap, it jostles the weights off saddles, and it tends to steer away from very sharp minima, which often generalise worse.

    Batch size and learning rate are linked. A bigger batch gives a less noisy gradient, so you can take bigger steps. The linear scaling rule says double the batch, double the learning rate, and it works up to a point. Beyond the critical batch size, extra examples per step stop buying faster progress and you're just spending compute. Recent work finds that the critical batch size grows with the amount of training data much more than with model size.

    Momentum, RMSProp and Adam

    Plain gradient descent has two weaknesses. It zigzags across narrow valleys, because the sharp direction limits the step size while the flat direction starves. And it crawls near saddles, where the gradient is tiny. Modern optimisers fix these with two ideas.

    Momentum keeps a running average of recent gradients and steps along that instead. Zigzag components alternate in sign, so they cancel. The consistent downhill component adds up. It works like a heavy ball rolling down the valley. With the usual setting, β = 0.9, a steady gradient gets amplified up to 10 times, because 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10.

    v←βv+gw←w−ηv

    g is the current gradient and v is the velocity, a decaying sum of past gradients.

    Per-parameter step sizes attack the curvature mismatch directly. RMSProp keeps a running average of each weight's squared gradient and divides each step by its square root. Weights with consistently large gradients get smaller steps, and weights with tiny gradients get bigger ones. Adam (2014) combines this with momentum, plus a correction for the first few steps when the averages are still near zero.

    m←β1m+(1−β1)g s←β2s+(1−β2)g2 w←w−ηm^s^+ε

    m is the momentum and s the running average of squared gradients, both kept separately for every weight. Hats mean bias-corrected. Defaults: β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, often 0.95 for LLMs. The ratio is roughly ±1 per step whatever the gradient's raw size, so η becomes a step length in weight units.

    The stretched valley is not an abstract curiosity. It's what you get when one input is an account balance in AED, ranging into the millions, and another is a ratio between 0 and 1. The loss is far more sensitive to one weight than the other, so the valley is long and thin. Standardising features before training is the cheapest optimiser upgrade there is.

    Push the shared learning rate above 0.5 on either valley and SGD diverges. That's Simulation 1's rule again: the sharp direction has curvature 4, and 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. Momentum and Adam survive well beyond it.

    The tilted valley shows Adam's limit. Its rescaling is diagonal: one number per weight, aligned with the weight axes. When the sharp and flat directions are mixtures of weights, as they mostly are in real networks, a diagonal rescaling can't fully straighten the valley. The newer optimisers in Part 4, such as Shampoo, SOAP and Muon, go after exactly this gap by working with whole weight matrices rather than individual weights.

    One more detail matters in every fine-tuning script you'll read. AdamW applies weight decay (shrinking weights towards zero) separately from the adaptive step. The older approach added an L2 penalty to the gradient, and Adam's division by √s then weakened the decay on exactly the weights that move most. AdamW decouples the two, and it's now the default almost everywhere.

    Warmup and schedules

    The best learning rate changes during training, so almost nobody uses a constant one.

    • Warmup. Start near zero and ramp up over the first few hundred or thousand steps. At the start, the weights are random, the landscape is often at its sharpest, and Adam's running averages are based on very few gradients. Full-size steps then can throw the model somewhere it never recovers from. Warmup gives the network time to move to calmer ground.
    • Decay. Towards the end, shrink the step so the weights settle into the bottom of the valley instead of bouncing around it. Common shapes: linear decay to zero, cosine decay, and step drops.
    • Warmup-stable-decay (WSD). Hold the rate constant for most of training, then decay sharply at the end. Its practical appeal for large runs is that you don't have to fix the training length in advance: you can branch off and decay from any checkpoint.

    A useful mental picture from recent work is the river valley. The high constant rate makes fast progress along the valley floor while bouncing off its walls. The final decay stops the bouncing and reveals the progress already made. That's why loss curves under WSD drop sharply once the decay begins.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Gradients are the engine underneath almost every model in the curriculum, and they turn up in places you might not expect: explanations, attacks and privacy.

    F1.7 · Cross-entropyThe loss you differentiate

    Log loss is cross-entropy. Its gradient with a sigmoid or softmax output is simply prediction minus truth, which is why it's the default classification loss.

    F4.3 · Gradient boostingGradient descent on functions

    XGBoost and LightGBM fit each new tree to the negative gradient of the loss. Their “learning rate” is the same idea: shrink each step so it doesn't overshoot.

    F4.2 · Logistic regressionConvex, so the answer is unique

    Scorecard coefficients don't depend on the solver or the starting point. Neural networks give up that guarantee, which validators notice.

    F5.2 · Initialisation, normalisation, residualsThe vanishing-gradient cure

    Everything in Simulation 3 that kept the bars level is a standard architecture choice. Transformers depend on all of it.

    F6.2 · Learning rates, schedules, batch sizeThe operational side

    How to pick a learning rate in practice, run a range test, and change batch size without breaking training.

    F6.4 · Reading loss curvesPlateaus, spikes and divergence

    A flat stretch is often a saddle, a spike is often an exploding gradient, and a rising curve is a learning rate above 2/sharpness.

    F8.2 · SHAPGradients as reason codes

    Gradient-based attributions such as integrated gradients and gradient SHAP explain neural models. Know their saturation blind spot.

    G1.4 · Adversarial examplesThe attacker's gradient

    The gradient with respect to the inputs tells an attacker the cheapest change that flips a decision. Fraudsters probing limits are doing a slow, manual version.

    A11.3 · Differential privacyPrivacy lives in the optimiser

    DP-SGD clips each customer's gradient and adds noise before the step, which bounds how much any one customer can influence the model.

    M2.4 · Fine-tuning and LoRAOptimiser memory is the bill

    Adam stores two extra numbers per weight. LoRA trains a small fraction of the weights, so the optimiser state shrinks and fine-tuning fits on bank-owned GPUs.

    R2.4 · Training efficiency: Muon, μPThe research frontier

    Matrix-aware optimisers and hyperparameter transfer across model sizes. Part 4 gives the short version.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Backpropagation is settled science, unchanged in principle since 1986. Optimisers are not settled. AdamW has been the default for about seven years and is still the safe answer. The live questions are whether matrix-aware optimisers such as Muon are worth switching to, how to schedule learning rates when you don't know how long you'll train, and how big a batch can usefully get.

    How we got here

    1. 1964
      Momentum

      Polyak's heavy-ball method: add a fraction of the previous step to the current one. Still inside every modern optimiser.

      Polyak, USSR Computational Mathematics and Mathematical Physics, 1964
    2. 1986
      Backpropagation popularised

      Reverse-mode differentiation had appeared earlier (Linnainmaa, 1970), but this paper showed it could train hidden layers that learn useful features.

      Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, Nature, 1986
    3. 1994
      Vanishing gradients explained

      Showed why gradient-trained recurrent networks fail to learn long-range dependencies: the products of local derivatives shrink or blow up exponentially.

      Bengio, Simard & Frasconi, IEEE Trans. Neural Networks, 1994 (and Hochreiter, 1991)
    4. 2010–16
      Deep networks become trainable

      Glorot initialisation (2010), ReLUs, He initialisation (2015), batch normalisation (2015) and residual connections (2016) together made 100-layer networks trainable.

      Glorot & Bengio, AISTATS 2010; He et al., ICCV 2015 and CVPR 2016
    5. 2014
      Saddles, not local minima

      Argued that in high dimensions, plateaus in training are mostly caused by saddle points.

      Dauphin et al., NeurIPS 2014
    6. 2014
      Adam

      Momentum plus per-parameter step sizes from RMSProp, with bias correction. It became the default because it works reasonably well with little tuning.

      Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015 (arXiv 2014)
    7. 2017–18
      Large batches: warmup and the critical batch size

      Trained ImageNet in an hour with a linear-scaling rule plus warmup. A year later, OpenAI showed the gradient noise scale predicts the batch size beyond which returns diminish.

      Goyal et al., 2017; McCandlish et al., 2018
    8. 2019
      AdamW

      Decoupled weight decay from Adam's adaptive step. Now the default in PyTorch fine-tuning recipes and most LLM training.

      Loshchilov & Hutter, ICLR 2019
    9. 2021
      Edge of stability

      Full-batch gradient descent drives the sharpness up to 2/η and holds it there, so the classical “step below 2/sharpness” picture is something training actively negotiates.

      Cohen et al., ICLR 2021
    10. 2024
      Schedule-free training and the AlgoPerf benchmark

      Schedule-free AdamW removes the need to know the training length in advance. It won AlgoPerf's self-tuning track, and Distributed Shampoo won the externally tuned track, beating well-tuned Adam baselines.

      Defazio et al., NeurIPS 2024; Kasimbeg et al., ICLR 2025
    11. 2024–25
      Muon

      Takes the momentum for each weight matrix and orthogonalises it with a few cheap matrix iterations, so every direction in the update gets a similar step. Introduced in late 2024 for speed-running small models. Moonshot AI then scaled it to LLMs and trained Kimi K2, a one-trillion-parameter model, on 15.5 trillion tokens with a variant called MuonClip, reporting no loss spikes.

      Jordan et al., blog, 2024; Liu et al., “Muon is Scalable for LLM Training”, 2025; Kimi K2 report, 2025
    12. 2025
      Why WSD works, and how big batches can get

      The river-valley explanation of warmup-stable-decay schedules, and evidence that the critical batch size scales mainly with data size, not model size.

      Wen et al., ICLR 2025; Zhang et al., ICLR 2025
    13. 2025–26
      The reality check on new optimisers

      A careful benchmark found that most claimed 1.4–2× speed-ups over AdamW shrink once AdamW's own hyperparameters are properly tuned. Matrix-based optimisers such as Muon and SOAP kept a real edge, about 1.3–1.4× at 0.1 billion parameters, falling to about 1.1× at 1.2 billion.

      Wen, Hall, Ma & Liang, “Fantastic Pretraining Optimizers and Where to Find Them”, ICLR 2026
    14. 2026
      Muon in production and in PyTorch

      Zhipu's GLM-5 (744 billion parameters) describes a Muon-based pretraining setup, following GLM-4.5. PyTorch now includes Muon in torch.optim.

      GLM-5 technical report, Feb 2026; PyTorch documentation
    The current consensus, in one lineAdamW with warmup, a decaying schedule and gradient clipping is still the correct default for anything a bank will train. Muon-style optimisers are a genuine but modest gain that matters at pretraining scale. Treat any claim of a 2× speed-up with suspicion until you know how well the AdamW baseline was tuned.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the training problems you'll meet in real projects and in AI-generated training code. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Loss suddenly becomes NaN or shoots upwards

      The learning rate is above the stability limit for some sharp direction, often at the very start or after a batch with extreme values, such as a single AED 40 million corporate transfer in a retail dataset.

      Spot itLog the gradient norm every step. A spike just before the loss jumps points to exploding gradients. Lower the learning rate, add warmup and clipping, and check the inputs for outliers.
    • Loss falls a little, then goes flat

      It could be a learning rate that's too small, a saddle plateau, vanishing gradients in early layers, or “dead” ReLUs that output zero for every input.

      Spot itLook at per-layer gradient norms, as in Simulation 3. If early layers are thousands of times smaller than late ones, it's an architecture or initialisation problem, not a data problem.
    • Unscaled features make a stretched valley

      Balances in AED next to ratios between 0 and 1 give the narrow valley from Simulation 4. Training is slow, unstable, or both.

      Spot itCheck that inputs are standardised or log-transformed before a neural network sees them. Tree models don't care, but neural networks and regularised regressions do.
    • Fine-tuning learning rate copied from a from-scratch recipe

      A learning rate of 10−3 on a pretrained Arabic-English model can wreck what it already knew within a few hundred steps. That's catastrophic forgetting.

      Spot itFine-tuning rates are usually 10–100 times smaller than pretraining rates. Check performance on a general held-out set, not only the new task.
    • Adam with L2 when AdamW was meant

      In PyTorch, Adam(weight_decay=…) adds an L2 penalty to the gradient. It isn't decoupled decay, so regularisation behaves differently from what the paper or recipe assumed.

      Spot itSearch the code for the optimiser line. If weight decay matters, it should be AdamW.
    • Changing the batch size and keeping the learning rate

      Moving to a bigger GPU and quadrupling the batch without touching the learning rate makes training effectively slower per example. Raising the rate without warmup can make it diverge.

      Spot itAny change of batch size should come with a learning-rate re-check, ideally a short sweep.
    • Schedule mismatched to the run

      A cosine schedule set for 10 epochs but stopped at 3 leaves the learning rate high, so the model never settles. Resuming from a checkpoint can restart warmup by accident.

      Spot itPlot the learning rate alongside the loss. It should be a deliberate shape that ends low.
    • “Our new optimiser is twice as fast”

      Most such claims compare against an AdamW baseline with untuned settings. Careful benchmarks shrink the gain to 10–40%.

      Spot itAsk whether the AdamW baseline had its own learning-rate and weight-decay sweep, at the same scale and token budget.
    • Different seed, different customers declined

      Non-convex training means two equally accurate models can disagree on individual decisions. A validator re-running your training may get a different model.

      Spot itRetrain with several seeds and measure how many decisions flip. Fix seeds and record library versions for reproducibility.
    • AI-generated training loop with silent bugs

      Typical errors: no zero_grad(), so gradients pile up across steps; scheduler.step() called per epoch when the schedule was defined per batch; gradients flowing into a model that was meant to be frozen.

      Spot itTrain on 10 examples. A correct loop should drive training loss to near zero. If it can't overfit a tiny set, something in the loop is broken.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Training a model is a controlled search. We measure how wrong it is, work out which way to adjust each of its settings to be less wrong, and repeat millions of times. Most failed projects don't fail on the idea. They fail because this search was set up badly, with steps too big, too small, or inputs on wildly different scales. The good news is these problems leave clear fingerprints in the training logs, so we can catch them early and cheaply.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Backprop is reverse-mode autodiff: one forward pass storing activations, one backward pass multiplying local Jacobians, so the full gradient costs a small multiple of a forward pass. For training I'd default to AdamW with warmup, cosine or linear decay and gradient-norm clipping, standardised inputs, and per-layer gradient-norm logging. I'd sweep the learning rate first, because it's the most sensitive setting. For reproducibility I'd fix seeds and report variation across seeds. Muon is interesting at pretraining scale, but the tuned-baseline evidence puts its gain at about 1.1–1.4×, so I wouldn't switch for a bank-sized model without a test.”
    “The training loss went to NaN overnight. What happened?”

    Most likely the update overshot in a sharp direction and the loss exploded: a learning rate too high for that point in training, a missing or too-short warmup, or an extreme input value producing a huge gradient. Mixed-precision overflow is another candidate. I'd check the gradient-norm log just before the failure, then add or tighten clipping, lower the peak learning rate, and look for outliers in that batch.

    “Why not just use the newest optimiser?”

    Because the gains are smaller than headlines suggest and the switching cost is real. Careful benchmarks find matrix optimisers such as Muon roughly 1.1–1.4× faster than a properly tuned AdamW, with the gain shrinking as models grow. For models a bank trains or fine-tunes, a well-tuned AdamW recipe is the lower-risk choice. A new optimiser would need a like-for-like test with its own tuning budget.

    “Is our scorecard's solution the only possible one? Would a neural network's be?”

    For logistic regression, yes, essentially. The loss is convex, so any solver that converges reaches the same coefficients, given the same data and regularisation. For a neural network, no. The landscape is non-convex, so different seeds and data orders give different models. They often have similar overall accuracy but disagree on individual customers, which matters for adverse-action reasons and for validation. Seed studies and ensembling are the usual responses.

    “After fine-tuning on our complaints data, the model got worse at general Arabic. Why?”

    Classic catastrophic forgetting, usually from too high a learning rate or too many steps on a narrow dataset. The large updates overwrote general-purpose weights. Remedies: a learning rate 10–100 times lower than pretraining, warmup and decay, fewer epochs, LoRA so most weights are frozen, and mixing some general data into fine-tuning. Track a general benchmark alongside the task metric.

    “Can we use gradients to explain individual decisions?”

    Yes, with care. The gradient of the prediction with respect to each input shows local sensitivity, but it fails when the model is saturated: a near-certain prediction has near-zero gradients everywhere. Integrated gradients or gradient-based SHAP fix this by accumulating gradients from a baseline, and their attributions add up to the change in prediction. Even then, they explain the model, not the world, so they need the same validation as any other reason-code method.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A one-neuron credit model predicts p = 0.8 for a customer who repaid (y = 0). Their debt-to-income input is 0.5. What is ∂L/∂z, what is the gradient for that weight, and which way will gradient descent move the weight?

    ∂L/∂z = p − y = 0.8. ∂L/∂w = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4. Gradient descent subtracts η × 0.4, so the weight decreases. The model learns to give this kind of debt-to-income ratio less weight towards default, because it was wrongly alarmed.

    2. A loss is ½ × 4 × w². What is the largest learning rate that converges? What happens at η = 0.4?

    The limit is 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. At η = 0.4 each step multiplies w by 1 − 0.4 × 4 = −0.6, so it overshoots and flips sign every step but still converges, shrinking by 40% each time.

    3. Each of 20 sigmoid layers multiplies the backward gradient by at most 0.25. How much smaller, at least, is the gradient reaching the first layer?

    0.2520 ≈ 9 × 10−13, nearly a trillion times smaller. That's why deep sigmoid networks barely trained, and why ReLUs, residual connections and careful initialisation became standard.

    4. With momentum β = 0.9 and a gradient that stays constant for many steps, how much bigger does the step become than plain SGD's?

    The velocity tends to g × (1 + 0.9 + 0.9² + …) = g ÷ (1 − 0.9) = 10 g. So the step is about 10 times larger along a consistent direction, while gradients that alternate in sign largely cancel.

    5. Two weights have steady gradients of 0.001 and 1,000. Roughly how big are Adam's steps for each, and why is that both useful and risky?

    Both are about η, because Adam divides each gradient by the root of its own running mean square, which is about its own size. That's useful because the flat direction finally gets a sensible step. It's risky because Adam no longer reflects how important each direction is. A weight with tiny, noisy gradients can be pushed as hard as one that matters, which is one reason for warmup, decay and weight decay.

    6. True or false: in a large neural network, a point where the gradient is zero and the loss is still high is most likely a bad local minimum.

    False, in all likelihood. With millions of directions, a flat point is far more likely to curve down in at least one direction, which makes it a saddle. Saddles cause plateaus that noise, momentum and adaptive methods help escape.

    7. You move fine-tuning from a batch size of 16 to 128 on a bigger GPU. What should you check?

    The learning rate. With 8 times the batch, the gradient is less noisy and the same rate takes 8 times fewer steps per epoch, so progress per example can drop. The linear scaling rule would suggest raising the rate, up to 8 times for SGD, less for Adam in practice, with warmup. Beyond the critical batch size the gain flattens. Run a short sweep rather than trusting a rule.
