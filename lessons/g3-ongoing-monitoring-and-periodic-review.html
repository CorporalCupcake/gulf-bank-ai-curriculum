    Ongoing Monitoring, Reviews and Vendor Models

    Part 1

    What these three controls are

    In one sentenceA model is approved for one use, under set conditions, until the next review: monitoring watches it every week, periodic review re-checks it every year or so, and vendor governance applies the same checks to models you did not build.

    Running example: a credit scorecard that predicts which loan customers will default. Validation showed it was sound on last year's data. It says nothing about next year's customers, a new oil price or a changed data feed. Models decay, like a car that passed its inspection in January.

    How it differs from validation

    • Validation is a one-off independent check before go-live.
    • Monitoring is the dashboard light: continuous, narrow, automatic.
    • Periodic review is the service: occasional, broad, done by people who ask whether the model still fits its purpose today.
    Part 2

    Monitoring: what to watch and when it fires

    Good monitoring is a small agreed plan, not a big dashboard. It is fixed before go-live, and the UAE Central Bank's Model Management Standards expect reporting to the model oversight committee at least quarterly.

    1. Pick metrics from four families: inputs (missing values, ranges), scores (the share of customers in each risk band), performance (does it still rank and predict well?) and use (how often people override it).
    2. Set limits as traffic lights. Green: carry on. Amber: investigate and record. Red: escalate and decide whether to restrict, recalibrate or rebuild.
    3. Name an owner who must act on each breach.
    4. Set a rhythm for reporting.

    How to read it

    • Leading signals (inputs and scores) arrive at once but only say something changed. Lagging signals (performance) say the model is wrong, but only after loan outcomes are known, perhaps a year later. Use both.
    • PSI (Population Stability Index) measures how far a distribution has moved since development. 0 means identical. Below 0.10 is conventionally stable, 0.10 to 0.25 is some shift, above 0.25 is a major shift. A move from 0.08 to 0.18 means look into it. It does not prove the model got worse, and each bank should justify its own cut-offs.
    • Gini measures how well the score ranks good customers above bad ones. Higher is better, and a steady fall below the validated level is a red flag.
    • Calibration is whether the predicted default rate matches the actual one. A model can rank perfectly yet predict 40% too few defaults, which matters for provisions. Compare predicted with actual by risk grade.

    The trade-off: a tight limit catches drift early but fires on noise, and a team paged weekly for nothing starts ignoring the page. Set amber just outside how much the metric wobbles in a stable period, and watch segments, not only the total: one product can fail behind a stable portfolio.

    Part 3

    Periodic review: how often and how deep

    Monitoring watches a fixed list. A review asks whether the list, the model and its use are still right. It covers the breach history, whether use has widened (a retail scorecard now applied to small businesses), and changes in data or regulation. It ends in one decision: continue, recalibrate, redevelop or retire.

    • Frequency follows risk. Each model gets a tier from its materiality (how much money it affects), complexity and opacity, and how automated its decisions are. Higher tiers are reviewed more often and more deeply, for example every 12, 24 or 36 months.
    • Triggers beat the calendar. A red breach, a material change, a new use or a market shock forces an out-of-cycle review.
    • Overlays need owners. Manual adjustments on top of a model must be documented and reviewed, or they become permanent undocumented models.
    Part 4

    Vendor models: governing what you cannot open

    Banks buy bureau scores, sanctions engines, fraud platforms and AI model APIs. The vendor guards its code, but buying a model does not buy out the accountability. If a vendor fraud score blocks thousands of customers, that is the bank's model risk.

    You govern it in four layers, like checking a used car you cannot open.

    LayerWhat you doIt protects against
    1. Due diligenceAsk for method, development data, validation results, limits and change historyBuying a model built for another market
    2. Test on your dataScore a sample of your customers with known outcomes and check ranking and calibration by segment"Works on average, somewhere else"
    3. Monitoring and change controlSame plan as an own model, plus a fixed reference set re-scored after every releaseSilent updates that shift scores overnight
    4. Contract and exitRights to documentation, testing and audit; change notices; incident reporting; an exit planLock-in and vendor failure becoming a bank outage

    Layer 2 matters most and is skipped most. A vendor calibrates on a pooled population, and your portfolio may sit in a corner of it, like a national average height applied to a basketball team. For example, a vendor score predicts 2.0% defaults, but your customers in a riskier segment default at 2.9%. One overall correction can fix the total while leaving each segment wrong in opposite directions, so always check by segment.

    Which control catches what

    ControlStrong atWeak at
    MonitoringEarly, cheap warningsNoise; cannot judge whether the use is still right
    Periodic reviewFit to purpose, widened useSlow; becomes last year's report with new dates
    Vendor testingNeeds no view inside the modelCannot explain why a score is wrong

    In banking, the test of any monitoring plan is simple: if this model went wrong next quarter, which number would tell us first, and who must act?

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Performance-only monitoring

      Defaults show up a year later. Pair every lagging metric with a leading one such as PSI or override rates.

    • Textbook limits

      A PSI limit copied from a book may fire constantly or never. Set limits from your own stable-period noise.

    • Review by calendar only

      Re-dating last year's report is not a review. Check the use, the data and the breaches.

    • Trusting a vendor brochure

      Average performance somewhere else proves little. Test on your own customers, by segment.

    1. Why is validation not enough on its own?

    It shows the model was sound on past data. Customers, markets and data feeds change, so models decay.

    2. PSI moves from 0.08 to 0.18. What do you do?

    Investigate and record it: that is amber. It shows the population shifted, not that the model got worse.

    3. Which is the leading signal and which the lagging one?

    Input and score metrics such as PSI are leading. Performance such as Gini and backtested default rates is lagging, because outcomes take time.

    4. What makes an out-of-cycle review happen?

    A trigger: a red breach, a material change, a new use or a market shock.

    5. A vendor score is right overall but wrong for one segment. How would you have found it?

    By outcomes testing on your own customers split by segment (layer 2).
