    Keeping Approval: Ongoing Monitoring, Periodic Review and Vendor Models

    Why validation on the day of approval proves little about month fourteen, how to design monitoring that fires early without crying wolf, how often a model should be re-examined, and what to do with a model whose insides you are not allowed to see.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceApproval is a statement about one date, so a bank keeps earning it with pre-agreed monitoring metrics and limits, reviews scaled to risk, and, for vendor models, testing on its own data plus contracts that give notice, access and an exit.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A car passes its inspection in January. The certificate says the brakes worked that day, not that they will work in November. Models are the same: validation (G3.3) shows a model was sound on the data it saw, and says nothing about next year's customers, a new oil price or a quietly changed data feed. Models decay. Three answers:

    • Ongoing monitoring is the dashboard light: a few metrics, each with a pre-set limit and a named person who must act on a breach.
    • Periodic review is the service: someone independent asks whether the model still fits the purpose it is used for today.
    • Vendor model governance is for a car with the bonnet welded shut: test it from outside on your own roads, and write into the contract what the maker must tell you.
    Hold onto thisA model is approved for a use, under conditions, until the next review. Test of any monitoring plan: if this model went wrong next quarter, which number would tell us first, and who must act?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    G3.5 Designing monitoring: what to watch, what limit, how fast

    Good monitoring is a small agreed plan, not a big dashboard. Before go-live it fixes metrics, limits, who is responsible, and the reporting route and rhythm. The UAE Central Bank's Model Management Standards require this, with reports to the model oversight committee at least quarterly.

    Metrics fall into four families. A sound plan has something from each.

    FamilyQuestion it answersTypical metricsArrives
    Data and inputsIs the model fed what it was built on?Missing-value rates, range checks, PSI per variable (F7.7)Immediately
    Score and populationAre customers and outputs shifting?Score PSI, share in each risk band, override ratesImmediately
    PerformanceDoes it still rank and calibrate?Gini or AUC, predicted against actual default rate by band (backtesting)Late: after outcomes arrive
    Use and processIs it used as designed?Override reasons, review backlog, share bypassing the modelContinuous

    Think of a smoke alarm against a fire-damage survey. The first two families are leading: they say something changed, not whether it matters. Performance is lagging: it says the model is wrong, but only once outcomes are seen. A bad batch of 12-month loans booked in March shows as defaults next year. Combine fast, noisy signals with slow, certain ones.

    Limits are traffic lights: green, carry on; amber, investigate and record; red, escalate and decide whether to restrict use, recalibrate or redevelop.

    PSI (Population Stability Index) measures how far a distribution has moved since development, like comparing this year's class marks with last year's. Zero means identical. Below 0.10 is conventionally stable, 0.10 to 0.25 some shift, above 0.25 major shift. A move from 0.08 to 0.18 means investigate. It does not say the model got worse, and the cut-offs are a rule of thumb each bank should justify.

    The trade-off is sensitivity against false alarms. A tight limit catches drift early but fires on noise, and a team paged weekly for nothing ignores the page. A loose limit is quiet and late. The simulation shows both on a model that starts drifting at week 30.

    Three design rules:

    • Pair every lagging metric with a leading one. PSI and override rates warn; backtesting confirms and sizes the damage.
    • Set limits from the noise, not a textbook. Measure how much the metric wobbles in the stable period and put amber just outside that.
    • Monitor segments, not only the total. Portfolio Gini can hold while one product or channel collapses behind a larger stable segment. Fairness metrics (G2.1) belong in the same plan.

    Calibration is the level of predicted default, not the ranking. For IFRS 9 and capital models (B4) the level drives provisions: a model can rank perfectly and still be 40% too low on the number finance books. Backtest predicted against observed default rate by grade, with intervals.

    G3.5 Periodic review: depth and frequency by risk

    Monitoring watches a fixed list; a periodic review asks whether the list, the model and its use are still right. It covers the breach history, whether use has widened (a retail scorecard now applied to small business), changes in data or regulation, and ends in a decision: continue, recalibrate, redevelop or retire.

    Frequency is risk-based. Each model gets a tier that sets how often (say every 12, 24 or 36 months) and how deeply it is reviewed, based on materiality, complexity and opacity and degree of automation (G3.1). Triggers override the calendar: a red breach, material change, new use or market shock prompts an out-of-cycle review.

    The point is the structure: a documented rule, a recorded tier, and triggers that pull a review forward. The failure to avoid is review by calendar only: last year's report with new dates. Overlays (manual adjustments on top of a model) must be documented, owned and reviewed, or they become permanent undocumented models.

    G3.6 Vendor models: governing what you cannot open

    Banks buy bureau scores, sanctions engines, fraud platforms, IFRS 9 packages and foundation-model APIs. The vendor guards its code, but buying a model does not buy out the accountability. If a vendor fraud score blocks thousands of customers, it is the bank's model risk.

    Govern the black box in four layers, like checking a used car you cannot open: paperwork, test drive, service history, contract.

    LayerWhat you doWhat it protects against
    1. Due diligenceAsk for methodology, development data, validation results, limitations and change history. Compare their intended population to yours.Buying a model built for another market or mix.
    2. Outcomes testing on your dataScore a sample of your customers with known outcomes; check ranking and calibration by segment. Needs no view inside."Works on average somewhere else", which is all a brochure proves.
    3. Monitoring and change controlSame plan as an internal model, plus a fixed reference set re-scored after every release.Silent updates that shift scores overnight.
    4. Contract and exitRights to documentation, testing and audit; change notification; incident reporting; exit plan.Lock-in, opacity, vendor failure becoming a bank outage.

    Layer 2 matters most and is most often skipped. A vendor is calibrated on a pooled population, and your portfolio may sit in a corner of it, like a national average height applied to a basketball team. In the simulation, a vendor score is right for one segment and too optimistic for another.

    G3.6 What the contract and the change process must say

    The real risk is rarely day one. It is version 4.2 in March, with "improved accuracy" and a new training window, moving your scores. Three habits:

    • Contractual change notice with lead time to test, and a right to stay on the old version for a while.
    • A canary set: a few hundred fixed cases re-scored after each release. If scores move beyond a stated tolerance, the release is a model change and goes through your change process.
    • A shadow period for major changes: run old and new in parallel and compare decisions before switching.

    Generative AI vendors are the hard case: the provider can change or deprecate a model at short notice, and training data is invisible. Same controls, pushed further: pin versions, keep a regression suite of your own prompts (P2.5), monitor outputs and have a fallback. If five departments call one provider, it is a single point of failure for the bank.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are process, not mathematics. Each has a quick check.

    • Monitoring with no limits or owner

      Charts nobody must act on; breaches are discussed and filed.

      Spot itAsk for the limits document and the last three breaches. What happened after each, and who decided?
    • Only lagging metrics, or textbook limits

      Problems surface a year late, or amber fires every month and people stop reading it.

      Spot itAsk which metric would have warned within a month. Count amber and red per metric over a year: if amber is the usual state, the limit describes noise.
    • Averages hiding segments

      Total Gini is steady while one product, channel or group has collapsed.

      Spot itAsk for monitoring by segment, including small ones that matter commercially or for fairness.
    • Rubber-stamp review and scope creep

      Last year's report with new dates, while a model validated for one product quietly serves a bigger, different population. Permanent overlays become the real model.

      Spot itCompare two consecutive reviews, and the approved intended use in the inventory with the actual consumers of the scores.
    • "The vendor validated it"

      The bank accepts the vendor's own performance claims, run on other data.

      Spot itAsk for results on the bank's portfolio, by segment, and when last refreshed.
    • Silent vendor updates

      Scores shift after an untested release; alert volumes or approval rates jump.

      Spot itLook for a step change in score distribution on a date. Does a vendor release match it? Is there a canary set?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A variable's PSI moves from 0.08 to 0.18. What does that tell you, and what does it not?

    The population has shifted enough to land in amber: investigate. It does not by itself prove the model is wrong, only that today's customers look different from the development ones.

    2. Why can Gini lag the real problem by many months?

    It needs outcomes, and outcomes take time: defaults after a 12-month window, fraud confirmations after chargeback delays. Hence the pairing with leading indicators such as PSI and override rates.

    3. Name the four things a monitoring plan fixes in advance.

    Metrics; limits; who is responsible for acting; and the reporting route and frequency.

    4. A high-exposure deep-learning model has been green for two years. Extend its review cycle to three years?

    Probably not. Tier rests on materiality, opacity and automation, not just recent performance. Opaque, high-impact models are where metrics most easily miss a failure.

    5. In Simulation 3 an overall calibration factor matches the portfolio default rate. Why isn't the vendor model now fine?

    The error is in how it treats segments. Scaling fixes the total but over-predicts A and still under-predicts B. Recalibrate by segment or change model.

    6. A vendor says its new version is "more accurate". What do you do?

    Ask for release notes and the contractual notice terms. Re-score the canary set and a recent sample on both versions, and treat it as a model change through your approval process before go-live.

    7. Does outsourcing validation to a consultancy transfer responsibility?

    No. The bank owns the validation report and must act on it. Accountability stays with the institution and its board.
