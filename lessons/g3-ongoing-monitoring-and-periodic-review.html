    Keeping Approval: Ongoing Monitoring, Periodic Review and Vendor Models

    Why validation on the day of approval proves little about month fourteen, how to design monitoring that fires early without crying wolf, how often a model should be re-examined, and what to do with a model whose insides you are not allowed to see.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceApproval is a statement about a model on one date, so a bank has to keep earning it with pre-agreed monitoring metrics and limits, reviews whose depth and frequency scale with risk, and, for vendor models, testing on the bank's own data plus contracts that give the bank notice, access and an exit.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A car passes its inspection in January. The certificate does not say the brakes will work in November. It says they worked on the day, under the tester's conditions. That is why there are service intervals, a dashboard warning light, and a rule that you re-test after a crash.

    A credit model is the same. Independent validation (G3.3) tells the bank the model was sound on the data and design it saw. It says nothing about next year's customers, next year's oil price, a new branch product, or a data feed that someone quietly changed. Models decay: the world moves, the inputs move, the people using the output change what they do. Model risk management has three answers, and this lesson covers them in order:

    • Ongoing monitoring is the dashboard light. A short list of metrics, each with a limit set in advance, reviewed on a fixed rhythm, with a named person who must act when a limit is breached.
    • Periodic review is the service. Every so often, whatever the dashboard says, someone independent asks whether the model is still fit for the purpose it is being used for today, not the purpose it was approved for.
    • Vendor model governance is what you do when the car was built by someone else and the bonnet is welded shut. You test it from the outside on your own roads, and you write down in the contract what the manufacturer must tell you.
    Hold onto thisA model is never "approved" in a permanent sense. It is approved for a use, under conditions, until the next review. Monitoring is how the bank checks the conditions still hold. The test of any monitoring plan is simple: if this model went wrong next quarter, which number would tell us first, and who would be obliged to do something about it?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    G3.5 Designing monitoring: what to watch, what limit, how fast

    Good monitoring is a small, agreed plan, not a big dashboard. For each model (or each type of model) the plan fixes four things before the model goes live: the metrics, the limits attached to them, who is responsible for running them, and the reporting route and rhythm. The UAE Central Bank's Model Management Standards require exactly this structure, with monitoring reports going to the bank's model oversight committee at least quarterly and each carrying an explicit conclusion on performance (see Part 4 for the source and caveats). In the US-style framework the same expectation sits under "ongoing monitoring" and "outcomes analysis".

    The metrics fall into four families. A sensible plan has something from each.

    FamilyQuestion it answersTypical metricsArrives
    Data and inputsIs the model being fed what it was built on?Missing-value rates, range checks, feed row counts, Population Stability Index per variable (F7.7)Immediately
    Score and populationAre the customers and the outputs shifting?Score-distribution PSI, share in each risk band, override and referral ratesImmediately
    PerformanceIs it still ranking and calibrating well against what really happened?Gini or AUC, KS, predicted against actual default or fraud rate by band (backtesting), precision at alert volumeLate: after the outcome window closes
    Use and processIs it being used as designed?Override reasons, manual-review backlog, share of decisions bypassing the model, user complaintsContinuous

    The first two families are leading indicators: they tell you something changed, but not whether it matters. The third is the lagging truth: it tells you whether the model is wrong, but only after outcomes have been observed. For a 12-month default model, a bad batch of loans booked in March does not show up as defaults until the following year. Fraud labels (label delay) take weeks; AML confirmations take longer. So the design problem is to combine fast signals that are noisy about importance with slow signals that are certain but late.

    Limits are usually set as traffic lights. Green: carry on. Amber: investigate and record the finding. Red: escalate, and decide whether to restrict use, recalibrate, or redevelop. PSI is the standard example: below 0.10 is conventionally "stable", 0.10 to 0.25 "some shift", above 0.25 "major shift". Those cut-offs are a rule of thumb from credit scoring practice, not a law of nature. Each bank should state its own and justify them.

    PSI=∑i(ai−ei)·lnaiei

    Split a variable or score into bins. ei is the share of the development population in bin i, ai the share of today's population. Each bin adds a positive amount when its share has moved in either direction. Example: two bins, 50/50 at development and 70/30 today, give (0.7−0.5)·ln(1.4) + (0.3−0.5)·ln(0.6) = 0.0673 + 0.1022 = 0.17, an amber reading.

    The central trade-off is between sensitivity and false alarms. A tight limit catches drift early but fires on ordinary noise, and a team that is paged weekly for nothing learns to ignore the page. A loose limit is quiet and late. The simulation below shows both effects on one model whose population starts to drift at week 30.

    Three design rules fall out of it.

    • Pair every lagging metric with a leading one. Watch PSI and override rates to get early warning. Use backtesting to confirm and to size the damage. Neither alone is enough.
    • Set limits from the noise, not from a textbook. Run the metric over the stable development period, see how much it wobbles, and put amber just outside that. Then agree what amber obliges someone to do.
    • Monitor segments, not only the total. A portfolio Gini can hold steady while the model collapses for one product, nationality group or channel, because a larger stable segment masks it. Fairness metrics (G2.1) belong in the same plan.

    Calibration deserves its own line. For IFRS 9 and capital models (B4) the level of predicted default, not just the ranking, drives provisions. A model can rank customers perfectly and still be 40% too low on the number the finance team books. Backtest predicted against observed default rate by rating grade, with intervals, because with a few hundred defaults per grade the observed rate is itself uncertain (F2.2).

    G3.5 Periodic review: depth and frequency by risk

    Monitoring watches a fixed list of numbers. A periodic review steps back and asks whether the list, the model and its use are still right. It is a re-validation scaled to risk. A typical review looks at: the whole monitoring history and any breaches; whether the model's business use has widened (a scorecard built for retail now applied to small business); whether the data, the market or the regulations have changed; known limitations and open validation findings; and whether the model should continue, be recalibrated, be redeveloped, or be retired.

    Frequency is risk-based. A common pattern is a tier for each model that sets both how often it is reviewed (for example every 12, 24 or 36 months) and how deep the review goes. Tier usually depends on the model's materiality (how much money or customer impact rides on it), its complexity and opacity, how automated its decisions are, and its monitoring record. The inventory from G3.1 is where the tier is stored. Triggers then override the calendar: a breach in red, a material change to the model or data, a new use, a regulatory change, an incident, or a major market shock should prompt an out-of-cycle review.

    The point is not the arithmetic but the structure: a transparent, documented rule, a recorded tier, and triggers that can pull a review forward. Reviewers also look for the opposite failure, review by calendar only, where a model passes a "review" each year that is a copy of the last one with the dates changed.

    Outputs of a review are decisions, not just comments: continue, continue with conditions (tighter limits, restricted use, a compensating overlay), recalibrate, redevelop, or retire. Open findings get owners and dates, and overdue ones feed upward to the model oversight committee. Overlays (manual adjustments applied on top of a model, such as a post-model adjustment to provisions) must themselves be documented, owned and reviewed, or they become permanent undocumented models.

    G3.6 Vendor models: governing what you cannot open

    Banks buy models constantly: bureau scores, sanctions and name-matching engines, fraud platforms, IFRS 9 packages, economic scenario generators, and increasingly foundation-model APIs. The vendor protects its code and training data as intellectual property. The regulator's position, widely shared, is that buying a model does not buy out the accountability. If a vendor's fraud score wrongly blocks thousands of customers, or its credit score is biased, it is the bank's model risk. The UAE rulebook is explicit on a related point: even if validation is given to an outside party, the bank remains the owner of the report and responsible for acting on it.

    So the working question is how to govern a black box. The answer is four layers.

    LayerWhat you doWhat it protects against
    1. Due diligence before buyingAsk for the methodology document, development data description, validation results, known limitations, change history and independent audits. Compare the vendor's intended population to yours.Buying a model built for a different market, product or customer mix.
    2. Outcomes testing on your dataScore a sample of your own customers with known outcomes and measure rank-ordering, calibration and stability by segment. This works without seeing inside."Works on average somewhere else", which is what vendor brochures prove.
    3. Ongoing monitoring and change controlThe same monitoring plan as an internal model, plus a fixed reference set of cases re-scored after every vendor release, and contractual notice of version changes.Silent updates that shift scores overnight.
    4. Contract and exitRight to documentation and testing, audit and information access, change notification, incident reporting, data-use limits, escrow or continuity arrangements, and an exit plan.Lock-in, opacity, and a vendor failure becoming a bank outage.

    Layer 2 matters most and is most often skipped. A vendor model is typically calibrated on a pooled population. Your portfolio may sit in a corner of it. The simulation shows the effect: a vendor credit score that is right for one segment and too optimistic for another, applied to a bank whose mix has more of the second segment.

    G3.6 What the contract and the change process must say

    Testing only helps if you learn about changes. The practical risk with vendor models is rarely the day-one version. It is version 4.2, released in March, with "improved accuracy" and a new training window, that moves your scores. Three habits cover most of it:

    • Contractual change notice with enough lead time to test before the release goes live, and a right to stay on the old version for a period.
    • A canary set: a few hundred fixed cases, re-scored after each release. If the average score or any band shifts beyond a stated tolerance, the release is treated as a model change and goes through the change process, not through IT's release calendar.
    • A shadow period for major changes, running old and new in parallel and comparing decisions before switching.

    Generative AI vendors raise the same problem in a harder form: the provider may deprecate or alter a model with weeks of notice, behaviour can change without a version number moving, and you cannot inspect training data at all. The controls are the same ones, pushed further: pin versions where offered, keep a regression suite of your own prompts and expected behaviours (P2.5), monitor outputs continuously, and have a fallback if the provider fails. Concentration risk matters too: if five models in five departments all call one provider, that provider is a single point of failure for the bank. Regulators increasingly treat that as a resilience question, not just a model one.

    Precision at alert volume, calibration curves and thresholds. A threshold that was optimal at approval can be wrong now.

    F3.5 · Imbalance and label delayWhy recent performance looks better or worse than it is

    Fraud and default labels mature slowly. Monitoring recall on young labels will mislead you.

    G2 · Fairness, privacy, oversightMonitor fairness over time

    Bias can appear after launch as the population shifts. Documentation and oversight duties (G2.4, G2.5) continue through the model's life.

    G1 · SecurityAttacks look like drift

    Evasion by fraudsters or poisoned retraining data first appears as a shift in inputs or scores. Monitoring is part of detection (G1.1).

    B4 · IFRS 9 and credit riskBacktesting provisions

    Provisioning models are among the most scrutinised for calibration and overlays. Expect annual backtesting and documented adjustments.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit scorecards and IFRS 9 models. Quarterly backtesting, population stability, documented overlays, and annual or biennial review. Vendor bureau scores need segment testing.
    • Fraud and AML. Alert volume, precision, and rule or model tuning. After a vendor release, alert counts can jump without any change in customer behaviour; a canary set catches this.
    • Sanctions and name screening. Almost always vendor software with a list, a matching engine and thresholds. Threshold changes are model changes (B2).
    • LLM tools. Internal copilots (B7) depend on a provider's model that can change. Regression sets and version pinning stand in for traditional backtesting.

    Where the field is now

    The three-part idea of monitoring, periodic review and vendor governance has been stable since the 2010s. What is moving in 2026 is how much prescription regulators want, how AI models fit, and how third-party concentration is treated.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are about process, not mathematics. Each comes with a quick check.

    • Monitoring with no limits or owner

      A dashboard of charts that nobody is obliged to act on. Breaches get discussed and filed.

      Spot itAsk for the limits document and the last three breaches. What happened after each, and who decided?
    • Only lagging metrics

      Performance is checked once outcomes mature, so the bank finds a problem a year after it began.

      Spot itAsk which metric would have warned within a month, and what was its value in the last bad period.
    • Alert fatigue from limits set by a textbook

      Amber fires every month, so people stop reading it.

      Spot itCount amber and red per metric over a year. If amber is the usual state, the limit describes noise.
    • Averages hiding segments

      Total Gini is steady while one product, channel or nationality group has collapsed.

      Spot itAsk for monitoring by segment, including the smallest ones that matter commercially or for fairness.
    • Rubber-stamp periodic review

      Last year's report with new dates. No change in conclusions, no open items, no use check.

      Spot itCompare two consecutive reviews. Ask what changed in use, data and environment since approval.
    • Scope creep without a new approval

      A model validated for one product is quietly applied to another, bigger, population.

      Spot itCompare the approved intended use in the inventory with the actual consumers of the scores.
    • Permanent overlays and workarounds

      Manual adjustments added after a miss and never reviewed. They become the real model.

      Spot itAsk for the list of overlays, owners, start dates and expiry or review dates.
    • "The vendor validated it"

      The bank accepts the vendor's own performance claims, run on other data.

      Spot itAsk for results on the bank's own portfolio, split by segment, and for the date they were last refreshed.
    • Silent vendor updates

      Scores shift after a release nobody tested. Alert volumes or approval rates jump.

      Spot itLook for a step change in score distribution on a given date. Ask whether a vendor release matches it, and whether there is a canary set.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A model's approval is a snapshot. To keep it, we agree before launch which few numbers we will watch, what limit triggers action, and who has to act. Fast signals, like a shift in who is applying, warn us early; slow ones, like actual defaults, confirm and size the problem. Review depth follows the risk, so our biggest, most automated models get looked at most often. For models we buy, we test them on our own customers and make sure the contract tells us before the vendor changes anything.”
    To a validator or model risk committee
    “I'd propose a monitoring plan fixed at approval: input and score stability, discrimination and calibration backtests by segment, and use metrics such as overrides. Limits come from the variation in the development period, amber and red are linked to owners and actions, and reports go to the committee at least quarterly. Review frequency follows the inventory tier with triggers for material change, red breaches and new use. For a vendor model I'd add outcomes testing on our portfolio, a canary set after each release, contractual change notice and an exit plan.”
    “The model passed validation. Why do we need all this monitoring?”

    Validation tests the model on data from the past, for the use planned. Monitoring checks whether the world and the use still match. Customers, markets, data feeds and processes all change, and nothing stops the model being used for something new. The cost of monitoring is small against a mis-priced portfolio found a year late.

    “PSI is 0.18 this quarter. Do we retrain?”

    Not automatically. PSI says the population moved, not that the model got worse. First find what drove it: one variable, a data issue, a real shift in customer mix. Then check performance on the loans that have outcomes and the calibration by band. Retrain or recalibrate if the model is actually failing, and record the decision either way.

    “How often should we review each model?”

    By risk tier. Something like annual for high-materiality, opaque or automated models, longer cycles for low-risk ones, with documented criteria. Triggers override the calendar: a red breach, material change, a new use or an incident prompts a review straight away. I'd check our regulator's minimums, as the CBUAE and SAMA texts set their own expectations.

    “The vendor won't share the model. How can we validate it?”

    We validate behaviour, not code. We review their documentation, test on our own data for rank-ordering, calibration and stability by segment, build a challenger or benchmark where practical, and test sensitivity with controlled input changes. We contract for change notice and audit rights, keep a canary set, and monitor like any internal model. If they refuse all of that, the model's risk rating goes up, or we don't buy.

    “Can we just trust the big-name LLM provider's own safety testing?”

    It informs our risk assessment but doesn't replace testing on our use case. Their tests cover general behaviour; we need our prompts, languages and edge cases, a regression suite we re-run on every model version, and monitoring of live outputs. We also need a fallback if the provider changes or withdraws the model.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A scoring variable has 50/50 bins at development and 70/30 today. What is the PSI, and what does it mean?

    (0.7−0.5)·ln(0.7/0.5) + (0.3−0.5)·ln(0.3/0.5) = 0.2·0.3365 + (−0.2)·(−0.5108) = 0.0673 + 0.1022 ≈ 0.17. Under the usual rule of thumb that is amber: the population has shifted enough to investigate, but it does not by itself prove the model is wrong.

    2. Why can a performance metric such as Gini lag the real problem by many months?

    You need outcomes, and outcomes take time: defaults after a 12-month window, fraud confirmations after chargeback delays. A bad batch booked now is invisible to performance metrics until its outcomes mature. That is why leading indicators such as PSI and override rates are paired with it.

    3. Name the four things a monitoring plan fixes in advance.

    Metrics, the limits attached to them, who is responsible for running and acting on them, and the reporting route and frequency, including the committee that receives results.

    4. A high-exposure, deep-learning model has been green for two years. Is it right to extend its review cycle to three years?

    Probably not. Tier depends on materiality, opacity and automation, not just recent performance. A clean record is evidence, but opaque, high-impact models are the ones where monitoring metrics are most likely to miss a failure. A documented argument can justify some extension, but not by default.

    5. In Simulation 3 an overall calibration factor makes the portfolio default rate match. Why isn't the vendor model now fine?

    The error is in how the model treats segments, not only its level. Scaling all predictions up fixes the total but over-predicts segment A and still under-predicts B, so pricing, limits and fairness remain off. You need segment-level recalibration or a different model, and you must document either as an adjustment.

    6. A vendor says its new version is "more accurate". What do you ask for, and what do you do?

    The release notes and what changed in data and method; contractual notice terms. Then re-score the canary set and a recent sample of your own cases on both versions, compare score distributions and decisions, and treat it as a model change through your own approval process before it goes live. Consider a shadow period.

    7. Does outsourcing validation to a consultancy transfer responsibility?

    No. According to the CBUAE rulebook summary, the bank remains the owner of the validation report and must act on it. In general, accountability stays with the institution and its board, even where work is delegated.
