    Card Fraud in Real Time: Scoring, Step-Up and Account Takeover

    How a card transaction is scored in a few dozen milliseconds, why the output is a three-way decision and not a yes or no, what the features really are, and why online fraud, with its stolen card details and hijacked accounts, needs a different defence from a stolen plastic card.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceFraud detection is a cost-weighted decision made in real time on a customer's recent behaviour, with approve, challenge and decline as the three outcomes; online fraud moved the contest from "is this card real" to "is this really the customer", and the strength of the challenge matters as much as the score.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    You tap your card at a coffee shop in Dubai Marina. In the next second or so the terminal sends a message through the card network to your bank. The bank has to answer approve or decline, and the scoring part of that answer gets only a few dozen milliseconds. There is no time to call anyone, read a statement or think. The bank's system has to look at the amount, the merchant, the place, the device, and what you normally do, and give a number.

    Now imagine the same card number typed into a website at 3 a.m. from another country. Nothing physical happens. There is no chip, no PIN, no face at the till. Either the real customer typed those digits or someone who stole them did. The only defence is to judge how well this event fits the customer's habits and, when unsure, to ask for proof such as approving the payment in the bank's app.

    That is the whole field. A model estimates how unusual and how risky the event is. A policy turns the estimate into an action. A challenge, if used, tests whether the real customer is present. Everything else is detail on those three steps, and most of the money is won or lost in the second and third.

    Three things to take from it.

    • The model is not the product, the decision is. Two teams with the same model can have very different losses because they set the cuts differently. Cuts come from the cost of fraud, the cost of friction, and what the operations team can handle.
    • The middle outcome is where modern fraud control lives. A challenge is cheap for a genuine customer (tap, approve) and nearly useless to a fraudster who does not hold the phone. It lets you act on moderate suspicion without refusing anyone. That is why the online-fraud half of this lesson is mostly about challenges.
    • Fraud is a contest, not a fixed pattern. Attackers see what gets through and adapt. A model that was excellent in March can be average in June without anything being "wrong" in the code.
    Hold onto thisScore, decide, verify. Ask of any fraud system: what is the score based on, what decides the action, and what proves the customer is there? Weakness in any one of the three can be covered only partly by the other two.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B1.1 · The real-time scoring path

    Card payments run through authorisation. The merchant's acquirer sends a message through the network (Visa, Mastercard, or a domestic scheme) to the cardholder's issuer, the bank that gave out the card. The issuer's authorisation system checks the balance, the card status and the PIN or cryptogram, and asks the fraud system for a risk score. A reply must come back within the network's timeout, often a couple of seconds end to end, so the fraud model usually gets a budget of tens of milliseconds. That limit shapes everything.

    • Everything the model uses must be ready at call time. Heavy computation (a customer's average spend over 90 days) is done ahead and stored in a fast feature store (P1.2). Only simple updates, like "transactions in the last ten minutes", are done live.
    • The model must be fast and robust. Gradient-boosted trees (F4.1) dominate because they score in under a millisecond, cope with missing values and mixed data, and need no scaling. Deep models are used, often alongside them, where the extra signal pays for the extra latency and operations.
    • It is a layered system, not one model. Typical layers: hard rules (block a card reported stolen, sanctioned countries, impossible travel), network-provided scores from the card scheme, the bank's own model, then a policy that combines them and chooses the action. Rules are not primitive; they are how analysts react to a new attack within hours, long before a model can be retrained.
    • Everything is logged. The exact features and score at decision time are stored. Without them you cannot debug a miss, audit a decision, or retrain without leakage (F3.1). P5 designs the whole system; P2 covers real-time serving.

    An issuer sees the transactions on its own cards. The acquirer sees a merchant's side and can spot a merchant that is suddenly full of tested card numbers. Networks see across banks and sell scores that reflect fraud patterns no single bank can see. A Gulf bank usually combines its own model with a network score and treats the second as a feature, not an answer.

    What the features really are

    The raw message holds the amount, merchant category code, merchant name and country, entry mode (chip, contactless, online), terminal and a few flags. Alone it is weak. The strength comes from comparing the event with the customer's own history:

    • Velocity. Count and total spend in the last 1 minute, 10 minutes, 1 hour and 24 hours, per card, per account, per device, per merchant. Card testing and a "cash-out" burst both show up here first.
    • Deviation from habit. Is this amount large for this customer (a z-score or percentile against their own history)? Is this merchant category new? Is the country new? Is the hour unusual? Bahnsen and colleagues (2016) showed that modelling the time of day as a circular quantity (a customer who shops at 11 p.m. is close to 1 a.m.) improved results over treating the hour as a plain number.
    • Geography and physics. Distance and elapsed time from the last transaction. Chip payment in Dubai at 14:00, online order shipped to another country at 14:20 on the same card, is hard to explain.
    • Merchant and terminal risk. The recent fraud rate at the merchant, computed with care (only labels old enough to be reliable; see B1.3).
    • Device and network signals for online use: device fingerprint, whether the device has been seen with this customer before, IP and its distance from home, behavioural signals such as typing and touch patterns (A9).
    • Graph links. Cards that share a device, an address or a beneficiary with known fraud. Often used as a precomputed feature rather than live.

    Here is the central pattern, a velocity feature that is correct as of the transaction time. The key is the window that closes at the current row, never after it.

    df = df.sort_values(["card_id", "ts"])
# count and total of the card's transactions in the previous 10 minutes
g = df.set_index("ts").groupby("card_id")["amount_aed"]
df["n_10m"]   = g.rolling("10min", closed="left").count().values
df["sum_10m"] = g.rolling("10min", closed="left").sum().fillna(0).values
# deviation from this customer's own habit, using only the past
df["amt_vs_usual"] = df["amount_aed"] / df["card_median_90d_asof"]

    closed="left" excludes the current transaction itself. If the current amount leaks into its own "last 10 minutes" total, the model learns an echo of the answer. Training uses historical features computed exactly like this; live scoring reads the same definitions from the feature store. Any difference between the two is called training-serving skew, a common silent cause of a good model doing badly in production.

    A single rule can be tuned to one attacker and be blind to the next. A model combines dozens of such signals at several time scales, so it can notice "a little odd on five things" when no single rule trips. It also learns that a busy shopper's burst of purchases at the same merchant category is normal, where a rigid count would flag it. This is the practical case for models over rules, and the practical case for keeping rules too.

    Labels, metrics and the economics

    Labels come from customer disputes, analyst confirmation and chargebacks, with a delay and with gaps (customers who do not notice small charges). B1.3 treats this in depth, and F3.5 covers the imbalance. With fraud at 0.05–0.2% of card transactions, accuracy is useless. Fraud teams use a fixed vocabulary:

    • Fraud basis points. Fraud losses divided by total card spend, quoted in bp. It is the number the CFO and the networks care about. Count-based and value-based views can disagree: a model can catch many small frauds and miss the few large ones.
    • Detection rate (by count and by value) at a fixed false-positive ratio or alert volume. The false-positive ratio is genuine alerts per true fraud alert, so "10 to 1" means one real case per ten disturbed customers.
    • Precision at capacity. If the team can review 400 alerts a day, how many of the top 400 are fraud? (F7.1.)
    • Customer friction. Genuine declines and challenges per 1,000 transactions, and call-centre contacts. A model that cuts fraud 5% and doubles declines may be worse for the bank.

    Choose the cuts from money. If a fraud that gets through costs A, and acting on a genuine transaction costs c, the cheapest policy acts when the probability of fraud exceeds c ÷ A (ignoring the challenge's partial effectiveness). With A = AED 1,500 and a decline costing AED 120, decline above 8%. With a challenge costing AED 4, challenge above about 0.3%. The amount matters too: a AED 40 payment and a AED 40,000 payment of the same risk deserve different actions, which is why many banks make the cut depend on the amount. This only works if the probabilities are calibrated (F7.4).

    B1.2 · Card-not-present fraud

    In a card-not-present (CNP) transaction (online, in-app, by phone) the card is not physically read. Chip and PIN, which made counterfeit and stolen-card use at the till hard, offer nothing here. Card details are stolen in bulk (merchant breaches, phishing pages, malware) and sold, then tested with tiny purchases to see which still work, then spent. Fraud migrated to this channel as the physical channel got safer, and in most markets CNP is now the largest category of card fraud. The details vary by region; ask your bank's own split rather than quote a global figure.

    The main defence is 3-D Secure 2 (EMV 3DS). The merchant passes rich data (device, history, shipping address) to the issuer, which decides whether to approve silently ("frictionless"), challenge the customer, or decline. The issuer's risk score decides. The key economic fact is the liability shift: when a payment was authenticated through 3DS, liability for fraud generally moves from the merchant to the issuer. So the bank's own score directly decides who pays.

    The strength of the challenge matters as much as the score. An SMS one-time password (OTP) sent to a phone can be intercepted by malware, redirected by a SIM swap, or simply read out by a customer tricked on the phone by a fake bank officer. An in-app approval tied to a registered device and a biometric is much harder to relay. UAE regulators moved against SMS and email OTPs in this spirit; see Part 4.

    Account takeover and the challenge

    Account takeover (ATO) is the harder sibling. The attacker does not just have a card number; they have control of the customer's account or device: a phished login, a SIM swap, a remote-access app the customer was talked into installing, or a stolen phone. Now the "customer-only" proofs may all pass. Typical sequence: credential stuffing or phishing, a login from a new device, a password or phone-number change, adding a new payee or registering a new device for the app, then a transfer or a card top-up. Often the login looks fine; the sequence afterwards is what gives it away.

    • Detect it before the payment. Score the login and the account-change events themselves: new device plus password reset plus limit increase within minutes.
    • Make the strongest actions the hardest. Adding a payee or raising a limit gets a stronger challenge than viewing a balance. A cooling-off delay for new devices is a cheap, effective control.
    • Mules and money movement. What the attacker steals must go somewhere: a mule account (B2). Seeing a flood of first-time transfers to one new payee is evidence from the receiving side.

    Because a clever attacker can pass a challenge, the design question becomes: with which challenge, at what score, do I let how much fraud through, and how many honest customers do I disturb? The third simulation puts numbers on this.

    The assumed numbers are the weak point and the whole point. Your own pass rates come from your own data: the share of confirmed fraud that went through a challenge. If you do not measure it, you will assume the challenge works and be surprised.

    A note on how much a score can do. In both models above a good score pushes fraud far to the right. Real systems face concept drift: attackers move to the region the model scores low. Hence the monitoring in F7.7 and the importance of fast-moving rules and retraining. And not every unusual transaction is fraud. For events with no labels yet, anomaly detection (F4.4) can flag oddness, but oddness is not the same as fraud, and holiday spending is odd too.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Debit and credit card authorisation (the main case), including tokenised wallet payments such as Apple Pay, where the device is a strong signal.
    • Mobile and online banking: login, device registration, payee creation, transfers, card-on-file changes. This is the ATO surface.
    • Operations: the fraud analyst queue, the card-block call to the customer, dispute and chargeback handling, and reporting to the regulator and card schemes.
    • Customer experience: a false decline on a card abroad is one of the most visible failures a bank can have. Travel notifications and in-app "was this you?" prompts are product features built on this model.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The base is stable: gradient boosting on velocity and deviation features, layered with rules and network scores. The live questions are how to learn from sequences and graphs, how to cope with delayed labels, how much authentication to push onto customers and which scams a model cannot stop at all.

    Checked on 6 November 2026. Two light searches; the regulatory item below has conflicting reports, which I note. Older items are from the literature.

    1. 2014
      Cost-based evaluation for card fraud

      Fraud models should be judged on money saved, not just classification accuracy, and thresholds chosen on cost. Bahnsen and colleagues built this into example-dependent cost-sensitive learning, and later showed circular (von Mises) time-of-day features helped.

      Correa Bahnsen et al., Expert Systems with Applications, 2016; ICDM 2014 on cost-sensitive methods
    2. 2018
      Realistic fraud learning with delayed feedback

      Analyst feedback is fast and labels from customers are slow; train and combine separate models for each. The paper also argues that the right metric is precision on the top alerts a team can actually review.

      Dal Pozzolo et al., IEEE TNNLS, 2018
    3. 2019–21
      Strong customer authentication regulation (Europe)

      Europe's PSD2 required two-factor authentication for most online payments, with exemptions for low-risk transactions based on measured fraud rates. It is the best-known example of regulation tying the strength of the challenge to the fraud rate.

      EU PSD2 and its technical standards on strong customer authentication
    4. 2025–26
      UAE moves banks off SMS and email one-time passwords

      Multiple sources describe a CBUAE requirement (cited as notice 2025/3057) that SMS and email OTPs and static passwords stop being used alone for customer authentication, with a transition deadline around 31 March 2026 and a move to in-app or biometric approval, along with a shift of liability for card fraud on OTP-authenticated payments to the bank. Sources differ on dates and wording, and at least one press report quoted the central bank as saying it had issued no specific notice cancelling SMS OTP. Treat the details as unconfirmed until you read the primary text from CBUAE or your compliance team.

      Press and vendor coverage, 2025–26 (The National, Gulf News, Khaleej Times, vendor blogs); not a primary source
    5. 2023–26
      Transformers and graph models on transaction sequences

      Research and vendor reports describe pretraining a model on customer transaction sequences and using its representations alongside trees. Vendors report gains; independent, public bank-scale comparisons are scarce. Gradient boosting is still the standard baseline to beat. See A8.

      Reported by vendors and in research papers; no single authoritative source
    6. 2024–26
      The shift to authorised fraud

      As card and login controls hardened, more losses come from customers who authorise the payment themselves after being deceived. A strong challenge cannot help here; the defence is detecting the context (new payee, pressure patterns, a call in progress) and warning the customer. Covered in A9.

      Industry trend widely reported by regulators and banks

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training-serving skew

      A feature computed one way in the warehouse for training and another in the live path. Offline AUC is excellent, live detection is poor.

      Spot itLog live features and compare distributions with the training table for the same transactions. They should match closely.
    • Leakage through the label's own trail

      Features like "card was blocked" or "analyst notes" exist because fraud was already found.

      Spot itFor each top feature, ask whether it would exist at the moment of authorisation. Suspect any AUC above 0.99.
    • Adversarial drift

      The attackers learn what passes and shift to it. Performance decays quietly because the fraud that is missed is, by definition, unlabelled for weeks.

      Spot itTrack detection by label age, input drift (PSI), and alert volume by segment. A sudden fall in alerts is as suspicious as a rise.
    • Optimising a count, ignoring value

      Catching 80% of cases but missing the few AED 50,000 ones.

      Spot itReport detection by value and count, and fraud basis points, side by side.
    • Assuming the challenge works

      Policy treats a passed SMS OTP as proof of presence, when the attacker has the phone.

      Spot itMeasure the share of confirmed fraud that passed each challenge type, by fraud type.
    • Cuts set once and forgotten

      A threshold from last year's costs and fraud rate; volumes and attack mix have changed.

      Spot itAsk when the cuts were last set and what costs they assumed. Compare the alert rate against capacity.
    • Customer-hostile false declines

      Travellers, new residents and non-Latin merchant names get declined more often because history is thin or unfamiliar.

      Spot itBreak declines and challenges down by segment and country, and by customer tenure.
    • Feedback loop blindness

      Declined transactions never get a label, so the next model never learns whether declining was right.

      Spot itAsk how the bank learns about declined events. A small random approve sample is the usual remedy (B1.3).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Every card payment is scored in a few dozen milliseconds against the customer's own habits, and the score picks one of three actions: approve, ask the customer to confirm in the app, or decline. The model matters, but the money is in where we set those lines, and they come from what a fraud costs against what it costs to bother an honest customer. The biggest gain now is making the confirmation step hard to fake, because online and account-takeover fraud is where the losses have moved.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “A gradient-boosted model on point-in-time velocity and deviation features, behind rules and a network score, served from a feature store with the live features logged. Evaluated forward in time with a label-maturity embargo, on detection by count and value at a fixed alert volume. Two cuts from the cost ratio, which need calibrated probabilities, and challenge effectiveness measured from confirmed fraud rather than assumed. Monitoring covers input drift, alert volume and detection by label age.”
    “Why not just block anything suspicious?”

    Because most suspicious transactions are genuine. At 0.1% fraud and a 1% false-positive rate, ten genuine customers are disturbed for each fraud stopped. A decline costs us a sale and trust. A challenge costs little and a real customer passes it. So we challenge on moderate risk and decline only when the probability is high enough that the expected loss exceeds the cost of refusing.

    “Why is online fraud so much harder than in-store?”

    In store, chip and PIN or a tap on a registered device proves the card is present. Online, a stolen number is enough, so the only evidence is behaviour and device. Fraudsters also test and scale it from anywhere. Our defence is risk-based 3DS with a strong in-app challenge, plus device and behavioural signals.

    “What is account takeover and how is it different?”

    The attacker controls the customer's login, phone number or device, so they can pass checks meant for the customer. We score the account events themselves (new device, password reset, new payee, limit change) as a sequence, apply stronger challenges to risky actions, and use delays for new devices. A single bad payment is the last step, not the first signal.

    “The model has 97% AUC. Why is the fraud team unhappy?”

    AUC ignores the base rate and the operating point. At 0.1% fraud even a good false-positive rate buries analysts in alerts. I would show detection by value and the false-positive ratio at the alert volume the team can handle, and check how the cuts were set and whether the probabilities are calibrated.

    “Can we use a deep model or an LLM instead?”

    Possibly for sequence features, and LLMs for analyst summaries. For the core score, trees are fast, robust and hard to beat on tabular behaviour data, so I would add a new model as a challenger in shadow mode, and only adopt it if it beats the baseline on a forward-in-time test by value and within the latency budget.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Fraud costs you AED 1,500 when it succeeds and a decline of a genuine customer costs AED 120. Calibrated, at what fraud probability should you decline?

    When p × 1,500 > (1 − p) × 120, or about p above 120 ÷ 1,620 ≈ 7.4%. The quick ratio 120 ÷ 1,500 = 8% is close. (This ignores the challenge option; with a challenge in the middle, the decline cut moves higher, as Simulation 1 shows.)

    2. A challenge costs AED 4 and stops 90% of fraud. Where does the step-up zone start?

    Challenge when the expected saving beats the cost: p × 0.9 × 1,500 > (1 − p) × 4, so p above about 4 ÷ 1,354 ≈ 0.3%. That is far below the decline cut, which is why the middle band is wide.

    3. Why does closing a rolling window on the current transaction cause leakage?

    The current amount enters its own "sum in the last 10 minutes". A large fraud amount inflates the feature that is meant to detect it, so the model gets credit for an echo of the target. Exclude the current row, as with closed="left", and compute training and live features with identical definitions.

    4. In Simulation 2, why does lengthening the window catch the slow attacker but start flagging honest customers?

    The slow attacker's 8 transactions are spread over three hours, so only a window of that order contains enough of them. A longer window also gives honest customers a larger expected count, so a busy shopper reaches the threshold more often. Single rules trade one error for another; models combine many signals to escape that trade-off.

    5. What is the liability shift in 3-D Secure and why does it make the fraud score a financial decision?

    When a payment is authenticated through 3-D Secure, liability for fraud generally moves from the merchant to the issuer. So if the bank's score lets a fraud through silently, the bank may bear the loss. Every frictionless approval is a bet that the bank pays for if wrong.

    6. Why is an SMS code a weak challenge against account takeover?

    Account takeover often includes control of the phone number (SIM swap), malware that reads messages, or a victim tricked into reading the code out. The code then goes to the attacker. An approval bound to a registered device and a biometric is harder to relay, though not immune to social engineering.

    7. A fraud model catches 85% of cases by count but fraud losses barely fell. Give two reasons.

    The missed cases may be the high-value ones, since detection by count ignores amount. Or losses may be moving to another channel or fraud type, such as authorised scams that the card model never sees. Report by value and by fraud type.
