    Card Fraud in Real Time: Scoring, Step-Up and Account Takeover

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of airport security: most passengers walk through, some get a bag check, a few are stopped. A card payment works the same way. You tap at a coffee shop in Dubai Marina, and within a second the bank must approve or decline, judging amount, merchant, place, device and what you normally do.

    Now the same card number typed into a website at 3 a.m. from another country: no chip, no PIN, no face at the till. The defence is judging how well the event fits your habits and, when unsure, asking for proof, such as approving in the bank's app.

    Three steps: a model scores how unusual the event is, a policy turns that into an action, and a challenge tests whether the real customer is present. Most of the money is won or lost in the last two.

    • The decision is the product, not the model. Two teams with one model get very different losses by setting the cuts differently.
    • The middle outcome is where modern control lives. A challenge costs a genuine customer a tap and a fraudster without the phone nearly everything.
    • Fraud is a contest. Attackers adapt, so a model excellent in March can be average in June with no code change.
    Hold onto thisScore, decide, verify. Ask of any fraud system: what is the score based on, what decides the action, and what proves the customer is there?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B1.1 · The real-time scoring path

    The merchant's acquirer sends an authorisation message via the network to the issuer, the bank that issued the card, which asks the fraud system for a risk score. The timeout is a couple of seconds, so the model gets tens of milliseconds. That limit shapes everything:

    • Inputs must be ready at call time. Heavy work (90-day average spend) is precomputed into a fast feature store (P1.2); only light updates run live.
    • The model must be fast and robust. Gradient-boosted trees (F4.1) dominate: quick, and tolerant of missing values and mixed data.
    • It is layers, not one model. Hard rules, network scores, the bank's model, then a policy. Rules let analysts react to a new attack in hours.
    • Everything is logged. Exact features and score at decision time, or you cannot debug a miss or retrain without leakage (F3.1). See P5.

    What the features really are

    The raw message (amount, merchant category, country, entry mode) is weak alone. Strength comes from comparing the event with the customer's own history, like a friend noticing you order something you never would:

    • Velocity. Count and spend in the last minute, 10 minutes, hour and day, per card, device and merchant. Card testing and cash-out bursts show here first.
    • Deviation from habit. Is the amount large for this customer? New merchant category, country or hour?
    • Geography and physics. A chip payment in Dubai at 14:00 and an online order shipped abroad at 14:20 is hard to explain.

    Other signals: merchant fraud rate, device and network (A9), and graph links to known fraud.

    A velocity feature must be correct as of the transaction time: the window closes just before the current row, or the current amount leaks into its own total. Training and live scoring must use identical definitions; any mismatch is training-serving skew, a common silent cause of a good model doing badly in production.

    A single rule fits one attacker and is blind to the next. A model combines many signals at several time scales, so it notices "a little odd on five things" when no rule trips. Keep rules too.

    Labels, metrics and the economics

    Labels come from disputes and chargebacks, with delay and gaps (B1.3). At 0.1% fraud, accuracy is useless: predicting "genuine" always scores 99.9%. Fraud teams use these instead:

    • Fraud basis points. Fraud losses as a share of card spend (1 bp = 0.01%). It is the CFO's and the networks' number.
    • Detection rate at a fixed false-positive ratio. The ratio is genuine alerts per true fraud: "10 to 1" means ten disturbed customers per real case. Report by count and by value.
    • Customer friction. Genuine declines and challenges per 1,000 transactions.

    Set the cuts from money: act when the fraud probability exceeds the cost of acting on a genuine transaction divided by the loss from a missed fraud. With AED 1,500 per fraud, a AED 120 decline means decline above about 8%; a AED 4 challenge means challenge above about 0.3%. This works only if probabilities are calibrated (F7.4).

    B1.2 · Card-not-present fraud

    A card-not-present (CNP) payment (online, in-app, phone) never reads the card, so chip and PIN offer nothing. Details are stolen in bulk, tested with tiny purchases, then spent.

    The main defence is 3-D Secure 2. The merchant sends device, history and shipping data to the issuer, which approves silently ("frictionless"), challenges, or declines. The key fact is the liability shift: when a payment is authenticated through 3DS, fraud liability generally moves from merchant to issuer. So the bank's score decides who pays.

    Challenge strength matters as much as the score. An SMS one-time password can be intercepted by malware, redirected by SIM swap, or read out by a customer fooled by a fake bank officer. An in-app approval tied to a registered device and biometric is much harder to relay.

    Account takeover and the challenge

    Account takeover (ATO) means the attacker controls the customer's account or device, like a burglar with a copied house key: the usual proofs pass. Routes include a phished login, SIM swap, a remote-access app, or a stolen phone. The login often looks fine; the sequence gives it away: new device, password change, new payee, then a transfer.

    • Detect before the payment. Score login and account-change events, not just payments.
    • Harder challenge for riskier actions. Adding a payee or raising a limit gets a stronger challenge than viewing a balance.
    • Mules. Stolen money must land somewhere (B2). A flood of first-time transfers to one new payee is a warning sign.

    A clever attacker can pass a challenge, so the design question is which challenge, at what score. Simulation 3 puts numbers on it.

    The pass rates are assumptions; yours must come from data: the share of confirmed fraud that got through each challenge. Scores also decay as attackers move to whatever the model scores low, hence drift monitoring (F7.7). Anomaly detection (F4.4) flags oddness, but odd is not fraud.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training-serving skew

      A feature is computed one way in the warehouse and another live. Offline results excellent, live poor.

      Spot itLog live features and compare their distributions with the training table for the same transactions.
    • Leakage through the label's trail

      Features like "card was blocked" exist only because fraud was already found.

      Spot itFor each top feature, ask whether it exists at authorisation time. Suspect any AUC above 0.99.
    • Adversarial drift and stale cuts

      Attackers learn what passes and shift to it, and the cuts still reflect last year's costs. Decay is quiet because missed fraud stays unlabelled for weeks.

      Spot itTrack detection by label age and alert volume by segment. A sudden fall in alerts is as suspicious as a rise. Ask when the cuts were last set.
    • Optimising count, ignoring value

      Catching 80% of cases but missing the few AED 50,000 ones.

      Spot itReport detection by value and count, plus fraud basis points, side by side.
    • Assuming the challenge works

      Policy treats a passed SMS OTP as proof of presence when the attacker has the phone.

      Spot itMeasure the share of confirmed fraud that passed each challenge type, by fraud type.
    • Unfair and unseen declines

      Travellers and new residents are declined more because their history is thin. And declined transactions never get a label, so the next model cannot learn whether declining was right.

      Spot itBreak declines by segment, country and tenure. Ask how the bank learns about declined events; a small random approve sample is the usual remedy (B1.3).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Fraud costs AED 1,500 when it succeeds; declining a genuine customer costs AED 120. Roughly where should the decline cut sit, and why?

    Around 8% fraud probability: the ratio of the two costs (120 to 1,500). Decline only when the chance of fraud is high enough that the expected loss beats the cost of upsetting an honest customer. A challenge band in the middle pushes the decline cut a little higher (Simulation 1).

    2. A challenge costs AED 4 and stops most fraud. Why is the step-up zone so much wider than the decline zone?

    Because it is so cheap. A tap costs a genuine customer almost nothing, so it pays to challenge at even a fraction of a percent of fraud risk. Declining is costly, so it is kept for the clearest cases.

    3. Why does closing a rolling window on the current transaction cause leakage?

    The current amount enters its own "sum in the last 10 minutes", so a large fraud inflates the feature meant to detect it. Exclude the current row and use identical definitions in training and live.

    4. In Simulation 2, why does a longer window catch the slow attacker but also flag honest customers?

    The slow attacker's 8 transactions span three hours, so only a long window contains enough of them. A longer window also gives honest customers more time to reach the threshold. One rule trades one error for another; models combine many signals to escape that trade.

    5. What is the liability shift in 3-D Secure, and why does it make the score a financial decision?

    When a payment is authenticated through 3-D Secure, fraud liability generally moves from the merchant to the issuer. Every frictionless approval is a bet the bank pays for if wrong.

    6. Why is an SMS code a weak challenge against account takeover?

    The attacker may control the number (SIM swap), read messages (malware) or talk the victim into reading the code out. An approval bound to a registered device and biometric is harder to relay, though not immune to social engineering.

    7. A model catches 85% of cases by count but fraud losses barely fell. Give two reasons.

    The missed cases may be the high-value ones, since count ignores amount. Or losses moved to another channel or type, such as authorised scams the card model never sees.
