    B1.1 Day one ★ Priority About 10 minutes

    Card Fraud Detection in Real Time

    Part 1

    What it is

    In one sentenceCard fraud detection is a model that gives every card payment a risk score in a fraction of a second, and a rule that turns the score into approve, ask for proof, or decline.

    Running example: you tap your card at a coffee shop in Dubai Marina. Within about a second the bank must say yes or no. Now picture the same card number typed into a website at 3 a.m. from abroad.

    How it differs from similar things

    • Fixed rules only: a rule such as "block foreign payments over AED 5,000" catches one pattern and misses the next. A model weighs many small signals at once.
    • Anomaly detection: flags what is unusual, but unusual is not the same as fraud. A holiday is unusual. A fraud model learns from past cases that were confirmed as fraud.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. The payment arrives. The shop's bank sends a request through the card network to the issuer, the bank that issued your card. The issuer asks its fraud system for a score.
    2. Features are looked up. A feature is one number the model uses. Heavy ones, such as your 90-day average spend, are worked out in advance and stored for quick lookup.
    3. The model scores it. It has tens of milliseconds. Gradient-boosted trees (many small decision trees added together) are the usual choice: fast, and fine with missing values.
    4. A policy picks the action. Two cut-offs on the score split it into approve, step up and decline.
    5. Everything is logged: the exact features, score and action.

    What the model looks at

    The raw message (amount, shop type, country) is weak on its own. The strength comes from comparing the payment with your own habits.

    • Velocity: how many payments, and how much money, in the last minute, ten minutes, hour and day. Fraudsters test stolen cards with a burst of tiny purchases.
    • Deviation from habit: is the amount large for you? Is the country, shop type or hour new?
    • Geography and physics: a chip payment in Dubai at 14:00 and an online order shipped abroad at 14:20 is hard to explain.

    A velocity feature must be worked out as of just before the payment. If the current amount is included in its own total, a large fraud inflates the very feature meant to catch it. Training and live scoring must use identical definitions too, or a good model quietly fails in production (training-serving skew).

    Part 3

    How to read it

    Setting the cut-offs

    Set them from money, not from habit. Act when the chance of fraud is higher than the cost of acting on an honest customer ÷ the loss from a missed fraud.

    • Decline: a missed fraud costs AED 1,500 and wrongly declining a customer costs AED 120. So decline above about 8% fraud probability (120 ÷ 1,500).
    • Step up: asking for proof, for example approving in the bank's app, costs an honest customer a tap, about AED 4. So challenge above about 0.3%.
    • That is why the step-up zone is wide. It is cheap, and a fraudster without your phone usually fails it.

    This only works if the scores are real probabilities ("calibrated"): a score of 8% should mean about 8 in 100 such payments are fraud.

    Which numbers to watch

    Fraud is rare, perhaps 1 payment in 1,000. A model that says "genuine" every time is 99.9% accurate and useless, so accuracy is the wrong measure.

    • Fraud basis points: losses as a share of card spend. 1 basis point is 0.01%. This is the number finance and the card networks care about.
    • Detection rate at a fixed false-alarm ratio: "10 to 1" means ten disturbed honest customers per real case caught. Report it by count and by value.
    Part 4

    Online payments and account takeover

    Card-not-present (CNP) means online, in-app or phone payments, where the card is never read. Details are stolen in bulk, tested with tiny purchases, then spent. The main defence is 3-D Secure 2: the shop sends device, history and delivery data to the issuer, which approves silently, challenges, or declines. The key fact is the liability shift. When a payment is authenticated this way, fraud liability generally moves from the shop to the issuer. So the bank's score decides who pays.

    Account takeover (ATO) means the attacker controls the customer's account or phone, like a burglar with a copied key, so the usual proofs pass. Routes include a phished login or a SIM swap (taking over the phone number).

    • Score the login and account changes, not just payments. The sequence gives it away: new device, password change, new payee, then a transfer.
    • Match challenge strength to risk. An SMS code can be redirected by a SIM swap, stolen by malware or read out by a victim fooled by a fake bank officer. An in-app approval tied to a registered device and biometric is much harder to relay, though not immune to persuasion.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    ApproachSpeedStrong atWeak at
    Scoring modelMillisecondsMany weak signals combinedBrand-new attacks, until retrained
    Hard rulesMillisecondsReacting to a new attack in hoursBlind to the next pattern
    Anomaly detectionMillisecondsSpotting the never-seenOdd is not fraud
    Chargeback analysisWeeksFormal, confirmed labelsFar too late to stop the payment

    In practice a bank layers them: hard rules, the card network's score, the bank's own model, then the policy.

    Weak spot: attackers adapt. A model that is excellent in March can be average by June with no code change, because fraudsters move to whatever scores low.

    In banking, this is the front line for card losses, and every cut-off is a trade between fraud losses and annoyed customers.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging by accuracy

      With 0.1% fraud, "approve everything" is 99.9% accurate. Use fraud basis points and detection at a fixed false-alarm ratio.

    • Leaking the answer into features

      "Card was blocked" exists only because fraud was already found. Ask of every top feature whether it exists at the moment of payment. An AUC above 0.99 is suspicious.

    • Trusting the challenge blindly

      Treating a passed SMS code as proof the customer is present, when the attacker holds the phone. Measure how much confirmed fraud passed each challenge type.

    1. Missed fraud costs AED 1,500 and a wrongful decline costs AED 120. Roughly where does the decline cut-off sit?

    Around 8% fraud probability, the ratio 120 ÷ 1,500. Below that, the expected fraud loss is smaller than the cost of upsetting an honest customer.

    2. Why is the step-up zone so much wider than the decline zone?

    A challenge costs an honest customer almost nothing, so it pays at even a fraction of a percent of risk. Declining is costly, so it is kept for the clearest cases.

    3. Why must a velocity feature exclude the current payment?

    Otherwise a large fraud adds itself to the total meant to detect it, which leaks the answer.

    4. What is the liability shift in 3-D Secure 2?

    When a payment is authenticated through it, fraud liability generally moves from the shop to the issuer. Each silent approval is a bet the bank pays for if wrong.

    5. Why is an SMS code a weak challenge against account takeover?

    The attacker may control the number through a SIM swap, read messages with malware, or talk the victim into reading the code out.
