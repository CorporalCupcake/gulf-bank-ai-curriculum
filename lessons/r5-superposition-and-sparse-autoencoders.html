    Superposition, Sparse Autoencoders and Attribution Graphs: Reading a Model's Internals

    Why a model's neurons are not its concepts, how sparse autoencoders pull the concepts back out, and how transcoders and attribution graphs trace a chain of internal steps from prompt to answer, with an honest account of what still goes wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA network packs far more concepts than it has neurons by storing them as overlapping directions, which works only because few are active at once; sparse autoencoders unpack those directions into readable features, and transcoders with attribution graphs use them to trace the path from prompt to answer, so far for a minority of prompts and with a large unexplained remainder.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a hotel with 1,000 rooms and 10,000 guests, where only a few dozen are ever in at once. Guests share rooms by taking turns. A model does the same with concepts: it has far more concepts than neurons, so it shares.

    The result is a polysemantic neuron: one neuron that fires for unrelated things, such as Arabic honorifics, DNA strings and a punctuation mark in code. The neuron is not the unit of meaning. Instead, the model gives each concept its own direction in the layer's activation space and lets directions overlap slightly. This is superposition.

    It works only because of sparsity: few concepts are active at once. Sparsity also lets us undo the packing. If the true concepts are sparse, we can search for a dictionary of directions where each activation needs only a handful of entries. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) does that search.

    An SAE shows which ingredients are present at one point. Transcoders and attribution graphs show how the model got from prompt to answer: they swap the dense layers for sparse, readable stand-ins and trace which features feed which. Asked for "the capital of the state containing Dallas", the model internally activates something like Texas before saying Austin. You can see and test that middle step.

    Three ideas to carry forward.

    • The unit of meaning is a direction, not a neuron. Read single neurons and you see a smear: the concepts are there, but rotated and overlapped.
    • Sparsity buys capacity. d dimensions hold far more than d nearly-separate directions if few are used at a time. Part 2 gives numbers.
    • A feature dictionary is a model of the model. It has its own reconstruction error and failure modes. Treat its outputs as evidence to test, not a readout of what the model "thinks".
    Hold onto thisNeurons are the wrong basis; features (directions found by a sparse dictionary) are better. Attribution graphs chain features into a pathway. Each step is a hypothesis you check by intervening, not by looking.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R5.1a · Superposition: why it is possible

    In d dimensions you get exactly d perfectly perpendicular directions. Allow small overlaps and you fit many more, because random directions in high dimensions are almost perpendicular. The typical cosine between two random unit vectors is

    cosθ~1d

    At d = 4 that is 0.5, a big overlap. At d = 1,000 it is 0.03. So a 1,000-dimension layer can hold many thousands of directions with pairwise overlaps around 3% (a relative of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, F1.1). Geometry makes the trick available.

    The cost is interference. Read concept i by projecting onto its direction and every other active concept leaks in a little, like several conversations bleeding into one in a café. With k concepts active, the leakage adds like a random walk, with typical size √((k − 1)/d) against a signal of 1. For d = 256 and k = 17 that is 0.25, a quarter of the signal. Readout stays clean while k stays well below d: sparse use, dense storage.

    • Importance and sparsity set what gets packed. In the toy models of Elhage et al. (2022), a small network trained on sparse inputs gives dedicated dimensions to the most important features, packs rarer ones into shared geometry (pairs, triangles, pentagons, as in Simulation 1) and drops the least valuable. The sparser the inputs, the more goes into superposition. Evidence for real models is indirect: we infer it from the success of the methods below.
    • Neurons are still partly meaningful. ReLU and GELU act neuron by neuron, which biases models to align some features with neurons. So you sometimes find a clean neuron, and polysemantic ones look like a few clean features plus many odd ones.

    Superposition is why "one neuron, one idea" analyses, such as single-unit inspection or the attention-head studies of F5.5, are incomplete. It is also why a linear probe (R5.3, the next lesson) can find a concept no single neuron shows: a probe fits a direction.

    R5.1b · Sparse autoencoders: learning the dictionary

    If activations are sums of a few sparse feature directions, we can learn those directions from data. Collect hundreds of millions of tokens' activations from one place (say the residual stream after layer 12) and train a network to compress each into a wide, sparse code, then rebuild it. A standard SAE has one hidden layer wider than its input, by a factor of 8 up to hundreds (thousands to millions of latents):

    z=ReLU(Wencx+b),x^=Wdecz

    x is the activation, z the code (one entry per feature), and each column of Wdec a feature's direction. The loss is reconstruction error plus λ times the L1 norm of z, which pushes most entries to exactly zero. An activation becomes "a bit of feature 4,201, a bit of feature 77,018…": a short list of directions with strengths. TopK SAEs keep only the k largest entries and JumpReLU SAEs use a learned threshold per feature; both avoid L1 shrinkage (below).

    The trade-off is unavoidable. More sparsity gives cleaner features but worse reconstruction; less sparsity reconstructs well but lets features blur again. The simulation trains a real, tiny SAE in your browser on the Simulation 1 toy, so you can slide along it.

    The toy's effects appear at full scale as named problems.

    • Shrinkage. L1 drags strengths down, so the SAE under-reports how strongly a feature fires. TopK and JumpReLU reduce this.
    • Feature splitting. A bigger dictionary splits a broad feature ("a city") into narrower ones ("Gulf cities", "Texan cities"). No size is "right"; it is a choice of resolution.
    • Feature absorption. A general feature stops firing exactly where a more specific one does, so "starts with S" has holes. Sparsity pressure causes it, and it makes features unreliable detectors.
    • Dead and dense latents. Dead ones never fire and waste capacity. Dense ones fire on a large share of tokens with no clear meaning and are partly artefacts.
    • The unexplained remainder. The share of signal left unexplained is your first quality number: lower is better, and 0 is perfect. It is not harmless noise. Swapping the real activation for the SAE's reconstruction typically raises language-model loss noticeably, so some of what the model uses lives in the remainder. L0, the latents firing per input, shows how sparse the code is: smaller means a shorter, more readable explanation.

    How do you know a feature means something? Three kinds of evidence, in rising strength. Look: read the text where it fires most and see whether a human or model can name it. Predict: does the label forecast where it fires on new text? Intervene: push the feature up or down and check that behaviour changes as expected. The famous case is a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature in Claude 3 Sonnet: amplify it and the model brings the bridge into everything (Templeton et al., 2024). Intervention is the strongest test because it shows the model uses the feature, not that it merely correlates with something.

    R5.2a · Transcoders: sparse stand-ins for the model's own layers

    An SAE reads one snapshot. To explain computation, how one layer's features produce the next layer's, you must represent the layers. A transcoder looks like an SAE but does not rebuild its own input: it takes an MLP block's input and predicts that block's output through a wide sparse layer.

    MLP(x)≈WdecReLU(Wencx+b)

    Same shape as an SAE, different target. The original MLP is dense and tangled; the replacement is a short list of active features, each with an input pattern (when it fires) and an output direction (what it writes). That split makes tracing possible (Dunefsky et al., 2024): feature A's effect on feature B is a clean product of A's output direction and B's input pattern.

    Anthropic's circuit-tracing work (Ameisen et al., 2025) uses cross-layer transcoders: a feature reads the residual stream at one layer and writes to all later MLP outputs. Otherwise a concept computed once and used many times would be copied as one feature per layer, cluttering graphs. One set of these replaces all the MLPs, giving a replacement model: a stand-in that tries to match the original's outputs using sparse, nameable pieces.

    It is imperfect. On any prompt its activations differ from the real model's by a leftover, kept as explicit error nodes. The share of the answer flowing through error nodes rather than features directly measures how much the explanation fails to explain: near 0% is a full account, most of it means the graph explains little.

    R5.2b · Attribution graphs: tracing one prompt

    An attribution graph is a wiring diagram for one prompt and one output token. Nodes are the active features, plus prompt tokens, the output and error nodes. An edge A to B says how much A directly pushed up B: A's activation times the linear effect of A's output on B's input. To make that linear, the method builds a local replacement model that freezes the prompt's attention patterns and normalisation scales as constants. Edge weights are then exact for that stand-in.

    Raw graphs have thousands of nodes. Researchers prune to those that matter, hand-group similar features into labelled supernodes, and read the story off. Frozen attention is a real limit: the graph shows that a feature was moved between positions, not why attention chose to.

    The headline example is two-step reasoning. For "Fact: the capital of the state containing Dallas is", Dallas features feed Texas features, which with capital features feed an output promoting Austin. A weaker direct Dallas-to-Austin path skips Texas. A tidy graph is only a hypothesis; the test is to intervene. Suppress the Texas features and the answer weakens. Swap in California features and the model should say Sacramento. Anthropic reported that it does (Lindsey et al., 2025). The simulation lets you do this on a schematic.

    Three habits for reading real graphs.

    • Check the error-node share (the replacement score). If most influence passes through error nodes, the picture does not explain the model.
    • Validate by intervening on the real model, not the replacement. If perturbing a feature should change the output, do it.
    • Remember a graph is one prompt. A pattern on a handful of hand-picked prompts can fail on the next thousand.

    Tooling is open. Anthropic published its circuit-tracing library in May 2025, with a viewer on Neuronpedia, supporting open-weight models such as Gemma-2-2B and Llama-3.2-1B. A bank team can run these methods on a small model it controls, which is the realistic way to learn them.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure appears in papers, vendor demos and internal write-ups, with its quickest check.

    • Taking a feature's name as its meaning

      The label "deception" was written from the top ten examples. Beyond those, the feature may fire on something else entirely.

      Spot itAsk how the label was validated on random activations, not just top ones, and whether anyone intervened on the feature.
    • Ignoring the reconstruction error

      An SAE that rebuilds the activation poorly makes any feature story partial. Splicing it into the model can raise loss a lot.

      Spot itAsk for the loss recovered when the SAE is spliced in, and for the share of variance left unexplained.
    • Cherry-picked examples

      A beautiful graph for one prompt, with no sense of how many failed.

      Spot itAsk how many prompts were tried and how many gave a usable account. Demos that never mention a failure rate are marketing.
    • Mistaking correlation for use

      A feature fires when the model handles fraud-related text, but the model's behaviour does not depend on it.

      Spot itAsk for the intervention: suppress or amplify the feature and see if behaviour changes. No ablation, no claim of causal role.
    • Explanations of the replacement, not the model

      The graph is of a stand-in with frozen attention. Its edges can differ from what the real model does.

      Spot itCheck the share of the answer that flows through error nodes, and whether interventions were tested on the original model.
    • Treating dictionary size as a fact

      "The model has 34 million features." It has as many as the researchers chose to train, with splitting and absorption varying by size.

      Spot itAsk whether results hold at two dictionary sizes, and with a different seed.
    • Beating a baseline you never ran

      An SAE-based detector for harmful requests is reported without comparing a linear probe or a fine-tuned classifier.

      Spot itAsk for the best simple baseline and the numbers side by side.
    • Claiming "we can see what the model is thinking"

      Overclaim in a vendor deck or a risk paper. Interpretability gives partial, hypothesis-grade evidence about internal computation.

      Spot itLook for the words "complete", "guarantee", "audit-ready". They are red flags in this field at the end of 2026.
    • Confusing this with model explanation for customers

      Proposing feature dictionaries as the answer to a regulator's request for credit-decision reasons.

      Spot itAsk whether the model in question is a transformer language model or a scorecard or gradient-boosted model. For the latter, use F8 methods.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A layer has 256 dimensions. About how large is the typical overlap (cosine) between two random directions, and what is the typical interference when 17 concepts are active at once?

    Cosine about 1/√256 = 1/16 ≈ 0.06. Interference ≈ √((17 − 1)/256) = √0.0625 = 0.25, a quarter of the signal. This is why packing works only when few concepts are active.

    2. Why does sparsity make superposition possible, and what happens if concepts are dense?

    When few concepts are active at once, the interference between overlapping directions is small, so each can still be read back. If many are active together the leakage adds up and readouts become unreliable. The model would need dedicated dimensions for its most important concepts.

    3. In an SAE, what does raising the L1 penalty do, and what do you lose?

    It forces more latents to zero, so each input is explained by fewer, usually cleaner features. You lose reconstruction accuracy, and the L1 term also shrinks strengths. TopK and JumpReLU designs address the shrinkage.

    4. Explain feature splitting and feature absorption in one sentence each.

    Splitting: with a bigger dictionary a broad feature divides into narrower ones. Absorption: a general feature stops firing where a more specific feature already covers the case, leaving gaps in the general one.

    5. How is a transcoder different from an SAE, and why does that help trace computation?

    An SAE rebuilds its own input at one point; a transcoder predicts an MLP block's output from its input through a sparse layer. That gives each feature an input pattern and an output direction, so the effect of one feature on a later one is a clean product, which is what attribution edges need.

    6. An attribution graph shows Dallas leading to Texas leading to Austin. What would count as evidence that the Texas step is really used?

    An intervention on the real model: suppress the Texas features and the Austin probability should fall; substitute California features and the model should move to Sacramento. A tidy graph without intervention is only a story.

    7. What do error nodes measure, and why are they the first thing you check?

    The part of the model's computation that the replacement model fails to capture on that prompt. If much of the influence on the answer flows through error nodes, the graph is explaining little.

    8. A vendor says its SAE-based detector finds harmful prompts better than anything else. What do you ask for?

    A comparison against a linear probe and a fine-tuned classifier on the same held-out data, with intervals, performance in Arabic as well as English, and the false alarm rate at the operating point. Published evidence so far shows simple probes are strong baselines.
