    R5.1 · R5.2 Edge ★ About 40 minutes

    Superposition, Sparse Autoencoders and Attribution Graphs: Reading a Model's Internals

    Why a model's neurons are not its concepts, how sparse autoencoders pull the concepts back out, and how transcoders and attribution graphs trace a chain of internal steps from prompt to answer, with an honest account of what still goes wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA network stores far more concepts than it has neurons by packing them in as overlapping directions, which works only because few concepts are active at once; sparse autoencoders learn a dictionary that unpacks those directions into readable features, and transcoders with attribution graphs use that dictionary to draw the pathway from prompt to answer, though so far for a minority of prompts and with a large unexplained remainder.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Open a language model and look at one neuron in the middle of the network. You might hope it means something: "Arabic names", "refund requests", "legal text". Mostly it doesn't. The same neuron fires for a clump of unrelated things: some Arabic honorifics, some DNA sequences, a kind of punctuation in code. Researchers call this a polysemantic neuron. The neuron is not the unit of meaning.

    Why would a network do that? Because it has more things to represent than it has neurons. A model has to track thousands upon thousands of concepts (every language, entity, style, fact pattern and code idiom), and a layer might have a few thousand neurons. The model's answer is to superpose concepts: give each concept its own direction in the layer's activation space, not its own neuron, and let the directions overlap slightly. The picture is a long list of people sharing a small office by taking turns at the desks. It works if few people are in at once.

    That condition, that few concepts are active at once, is called sparsity, and it is the key to everything in this lesson. Sparsity is why packing works. It is also why we can undo the packing: if the true concepts are sparse, we can search for a dictionary of directions in which every activation is explained by only a handful of entries. That search is what a sparse autoencoder (SAE) does.

    An SAE tells you what ingredients are present at one place in the network. The next question is how the model got from the prompt to its answer. Transcoders and attribution graphs answer that: they replace the model's dense layers with sparse, readable stand-ins, then trace which features feed which, from the prompt, through intermediate concepts, to the output. In one published example, asked for "the capital of the state containing Dallas", the model internally activates something like Texas before it says Austin. It does not jump straight from "Dallas" to "Austin". You can see, and test, the middle step.

    Three ideas to carry through the rest of the lesson.

    • The unit of meaning is a direction, not a neuron. If you read individual neurons, you see a smear. The concepts are there, but rotated and overlapped.
    • Sparsity buys capacity. A space with d dimensions can hold far more than d nearly-separate directions, as long as only a few are used at a time. Part 2 puts numbers on it.
    • Interpretability is now a measurement science with open questions. A feature dictionary is a model of the model, with its own reconstruction error and failure modes. Treat its outputs as evidence to test, not as a readout of what the model "thinks".
    Hold onto thisNeurons are the wrong basis. Features, meaning directions found by a sparse dictionary, are a better one. Attribution graphs chain features into a pathway. Each step is a hypothesis about the real computation that you check by intervening, not by looking.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R5.1a · Superposition: why it is possible

    Start with the geometry from F1.1. In a space of d dimensions you can have exactly d perfectly perpendicular directions. But if you allow small overlaps, you can fit many more. Random directions in a high-dimensional space are almost perpendicular: the cosine between two random unit vectors has a typical size of

    cosθ~1d

    At d = 4 that is 0.5, a big overlap. At d = 1,000 it is 0.03. So in a 1,000-dimensional layer you can place a huge number of directions (many thousands, far beyond 1,000) whose pairwise overlaps stay around 3%. This is a property of geometry, a relative of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, and it is why the trick is available to a network.

    The cost is interference. If you read off concept i by projecting the activation onto its direction, every other active concept leaks in a little. With k concepts active at once, each with random overlap of size about 1/√d, the leakage adds up like a random walk. Its typical size is √((k − 1)/d), against a true signal of 1. So the readout stays clean while the number of simultaneously active concepts stays well below d. That is the whole bargain: sparse use, dense storage.

    Two refinements matter in practice.

    • Importance and sparsity set what gets packed. In the toy models of Elhage et al. (2022), a small network trained to reproduce sparse inputs stores the most important features in dedicated dimensions, packs rarer and less important ones into shared geometry (pairs, triangles, pentagons, the shapes in Simulation 1), and drops the least valuable. As sparsity rises, more features go into superposition. Real models seem to do something like this, but the evidence is indirect: we infer it from the success of the methods below.
    • Neurons are still partly meaningful. Activation functions such as ReLU or GELU act neuron by neuron, so models are biased to align some features with the neuron basis. This is why you do sometimes find a clean neuron, and why polysemantic neurons look like a mix of a few clean features and many odd ones.

    Superposition is why analyses that assume "one neuron, one idea", for example inspecting single units or the attention-head studies of F5.5, are incomplete. It is also why a simple linear probe (R5.3, the next lesson) can find a concept that no single neuron shows: the concept is a direction, and a probe fits a direction.

    R5.1b · Sparse autoencoders: learning the dictionary

    If activations are sums of a few sparse feature directions, we can learn the feature directions from data. Collect a very large pile of activation vectors from one place in the model (say the residual stream after layer 12), for example hundreds of millions of tokens' worth. Train a small network to compress each activation into a wide, sparse code and then rebuild it. A standard SAE has one hidden layer that is wider than the input, often by a factor of 8 up to hundreds, in the thousands to millions of latents:

    z=ReLU(Wencx+b),x^=Wdecz

    x is the model's activation, z the code (one entry per learned feature), and each column of Wdec is a feature's direction. The loss is reconstruction error plus a penalty λ times the sum of the code's entries (the L1 norm), which pushes most entries of z to exactly zero. The model's activation is then written as "a bit of feature 4,201, a bit of feature 77,018, and so on", a short list of directions with strengths. Other designs get sparsity directly: TopK SAEs keep only the k largest entries, and JumpReLU SAEs use a learned threshold per feature. Both avoid the L1 shrinkage problem below.

    There is an unavoidable tension. More sparsity gives cleaner, more interpretable features but a worse reconstruction. Less sparsity reconstructs well but lets features blur together again. The simulation below trains a real, tiny SAE in your browser on the five-concept toy of Simulation 1, so you can slide along that trade-off and see exactly what is gained and lost.

    What you saw in the toy shows up at full scale as named problems.

    • Shrinkage. The L1 penalty drags feature strengths down, so the SAE under-reports how strongly a feature fires. TopK and JumpReLU designs reduce this.
    • Feature splitting. Enlarge the dictionary and a broad feature ("a city") splits into narrower ones ("Gulf cities", "Texan cities"). Neither dictionary size is "the right one"; it is a choice of resolution.
    • Feature absorption. A general feature stops firing exactly where a more specific one does, so a feature that looks like "starts with S" has holes. Sparsity pressure causes this, and it makes features less reliable as detectors.
    • Dead and dense latents. Some latents never fire, and a few fire on a large share of tokens without any clear meaning. Dead latents waste capacity; dense ones are partly artefacts.
    • The unexplained remainder. Even large SAEs leave a reconstruction error, and that error is not harmless noise: swapping the model's real activation for the SAE's reconstruction typically raises the language-model loss noticeably. Some of what the model uses lives in the remainder.

    How do you know a feature means something? Three kinds of evidence, in rising strength. Look at the text where it fires most and see if a human or another model can name it (automated interpretation). Predict: does the label forecast where it fires on new text? Intervene: push the feature up or down and check that behaviour changes in the expected way. The most famous demonstration is a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature in Claude 3 Sonnet: amplify it and the model starts to bring the bridge into everything (Templeton et al., 2024). Intervention is the strongest test because it shows the feature is used by the model, not merely correlated with something.

    R5.2a · Transcoders: sparse stand-ins for the model's own layers

    An SAE reads the activation at one point. To explain computation, how one layer's features produce the next layer's, you need to represent the layers, not just the snapshots. A transcoder is built like an SAE, but it does not rebuild its own input. It takes the input to an MLP block and predicts that block's output, through a wide sparse layer of features:

    MLP(x)≈WdecReLU(Wencx+b)

    Same shape as an SAE, different target. The original MLP is dense and tangled; its sparse replacement is a short list of active features, each with an input pattern that says when it fires and an output direction that says what it writes. That separation is what makes tracing possible (Dunefsky et al., 2024): the effect of feature A on feature B is a clean product of A's output direction and B's input pattern.

    Anthropic's circuit-tracing work (Ameisen et al., 2025) extends this to cross-layer transcoders: a feature reads from the residual stream at one layer and can write to all the later layers' MLP outputs. This matters because a concept that the model computes once and uses many times would otherwise be copied as many separate features, one per layer, making graphs cluttered. A single set of cross-layer transcoders replaces all the MLPs together, giving what they call a replacement model: a stand-in that tries to match the original's outputs while being made of sparse, nameable pieces.

    The replacement is not perfect. On any particular prompt, the stand-in's activations differ from the real model's by some leftover, and the method keeps that leftover as explicit error nodes. The share of the final answer that flows through error nodes rather than features is a direct measure of how much the explanation fails to explain.

    R5.2b · Attribution graphs: tracing one prompt

    An attribution graph is a diagram for a single prompt and a single output token. Nodes are the active features (plus the prompt tokens, the output, and error nodes). An edge from A to B says how much A's activity directly pushed up B's, calculated as the product of A's activation and the linear effect of A's output on B's input. To make this effect well defined and linear, the method builds a local replacement model for that prompt: it takes the real model's attention patterns and normalisation scale factors for that prompt and freezes them as constants. With those frozen, the path from features to features is linear, and edge weights are exact for that stand-in.

    Raw graphs have thousands of nodes. The researchers prune them to those that matter for the output, group similar features into labelled supernodes by hand, and read the story off the result. The fixed attention patterns are an important limit. The graph can tell you that a feature was moved from one position to another by attention, but not why attention chose to. That is a separate analysis.

    The headline example is two-step reasoning. For "Fact: the capital of the state containing Dallas is", the graph shows features for Dallas feeding features for Texas, which together with capital features feed an output promoting Austin. There is also a weaker direct path from Dallas to Austin that skips Texas. A tidy graph is only a hypothesis. The test is to intervene: suppress the Texas features and the answer should weaken; replace them with California features and, if the story is right, the model should say Sacramento. Anthropic reported that this works (Lindsey et al., 2025). The simulation below lets you do the same on a schematic of that graph.

    A few practical points on reading real graphs. Always check the replacement score or the share of influence on the answer that passes through error nodes: if most of it does, the picture is not explaining the model. Always validate with interventions on the real model, not just the replacement: the graph says that perturbing a feature should change the output, so do it and see. And remember that a graph is one prompt. A pattern that holds on a handful of hand-chosen prompts can fail on the next thousand.

    Tooling is open. Anthropic published its circuit-tracing library in May 2025, with an interactive viewer hosted on Neuronpedia, and the library supports open-weight models such as Gemma-2-2B and Llama-3.2-1B. That means a bank team can run these methods on a small model it controls, which is the realistic way to learn them.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson sits on top of the representation ideas from the foundations track and feeds into safety and model-risk questions.

    F1.1 – F1.2 · Vectors and geometryNear-orthogonality in high dimensions

    Superposition is the geometry of dot products and angles. The 1/√d overlap of random directions is the quantitative core.

    F5.1 · F5.2 · Neurons and residual connectionsThe residual stream is the shared workspace

    SAEs are usually trained on the residual stream, the running sum that every layer reads from and writes to.

    F5.5 · AttentionWhat attention moves, and what attribution graphs freeze

    Attribution graphs hold attention patterns fixed. They show what attention carried, not why it chose.

    M1.2 · The transformer, end to endWhere MLP blocks sit

    Transcoders replace the MLP blocks of the architecture you learned there.

    F5.7 · Self-supervised learningAutoencoders and reconstruction

    An SAE is an autoencoder with a wide, sparse middle: the same reconstruction idea with a sparsity constraint.

    R5.3 · Linear representations and probingA concept as a direction

    Probes and steering vectors find or use one direction at a time. SAEs find a whole dictionary of directions without being told what to look for.

    R4.3 · Chain-of-thought faithfulnessDoes the written reasoning match the circuit?

    Attribution graphs give an independent view of what the model computed, which can be compared with the reasoning it writes out.

    R4.2 · Sleeper agents and misalignmentInspecting for hidden behaviours

    A central hope for interpretability is checking what a model is doing internally, not only what it says. Today that is a research aim, not a dependable audit.

    F8 · Global and local explanationsNot the same as explaining a credit decision

    SHAP and partial dependence explain tabular models to customers and regulators. Mechanistic interpretability explains a network's internal computation to researchers. Do not mix up the two.

    G3 · Independent validationWhat a validator can reasonably ask

    A feature dictionary or circuit story is a model of a model. A validator would ask the usual questions: how was it tested, on what data, and how large is the error.

    Where it touches the bank:

    • Choosing and assuring open-weight models on-premises. If you host Gemma, Llama or Qwen yourself, public SAEs (such as Gemma Scope) let you probe for features such as sycophancy, deception, or sensitive topics. That is useful evidence in a model-risk review, but not an approval on its own.
    • Investigating odd behaviour. When an internal copilot gives a strange answer for a certain kind of Arabic or English prompt, features and graphs on a small open model may point to which concept is interfering. It is a diagnostic aid.
    • Steering and monitoring. Research uses features as monitors, for example flagging prompts where "deception" or "jailbreak" features fire, and as steering handles. Treat this as pilot-grade, with evaluation against ordinary classifiers.
    • Where it does not apply. Customer-facing explanations for credit or fraud decisions on classical models. Those use the methods of F8 and the model-risk documentation of G3. Nothing in this lesson substitutes for them, and I am not aware of any regulator in the Gulf that accepts feature dictionaries as an explanation standard; check CBUAE and SAMA guidance yourself.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Mechanistic interpretability went from toy models to production-scale models in about three years. The live arguments are whether SAE features are the right unit, whether this will scale to the point of useful safety guarantees, and whether simpler methods do the job just as well.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with a small number of searches. Items from the 2022–2025 papers are well known; items marked "from memory" were not re-verified.

    1. 2022
      Toy models of superposition

      Elhage et al. showed in small networks that features are stored in superposition when they are sparse, in geometric patterns, and tied it to polysemantic neurons.

      Elhage et al., Transformer Circuits Thread, 2022
    2. 2023
      Sparse autoencoders find interpretable features

      Two independent groups applied SAEs to language-model activations and found far more interpretable directions than neurons. Anthropic's version, on a one-layer transformer, found features for things such as Arabic script, DNA and base64 text, and showed that feature splitting happens as the dictionary grows.

      Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023
    3. 2024
      Scaling to frontier models

      Anthropic trained SAEs with up to about 34 million features on Claude 3 Sonnet and demonstrated feature steering (the Golden Gate Bridge). OpenAI trained TopK SAEs with up to 16 million latents on GPT-4 and studied scaling laws. Google DeepMind released Gemma Scope, hundreds of open SAEs for Gemma 2, so outsiders could work on them.

      Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024; Lieberum et al., 2024
    4. 2024
      Transcoders for circuits

      Dunefsky, Chlenski and Nanda showed that transcoders make circuit analysis cleaner than SAEs on MLP inputs and outputs, because the input-to-output relationship is factored.

      Dunefsky et al., NeurIPS 2024
    5. 2025
      Attribution graphs on a production model, then open tools

      Anthropic published circuit tracing with cross-layer transcoders and the "biology" case studies on Claude 3.5 Haiku: multi-step reasoning, planning rhymes in poetry before writing a line, shared multilingual concepts, refusal and hallucination circuits. Its own account is frank that the method gives a satisfying picture for only a minority of prompts (from memory, roughly a quarter) and leaves much unexplained. In May the library was open-sourced, with Neuronpedia hosting an interactive graph viewer.

      Ameisen et al., Lindsey et al., Transformer Circuits, March 2025; Anthropic, May 2025
    6. 2025
      Doubts about SAEs as a safety tool

      Benchmarks such as SAEBench found no single SAE design best on every measure, and that gains on reconstruction did not reliably mean gains on usefulness. Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported negative results for SAEs on downstream tasks such as detecting harmful intent, where simple linear probes did as well or better, and said it was shifting emphasis (from memory; check their post for the exact claims).

      Karvonen et al., SAEBench, 2025; Google DeepMind alignment-research posts, 2025
    7. Late 2025 – 2026
      Larger open SAE suites and continued debate

      DeepMind released Gemma Scope 2. Its technical paper says there is still no consensus on a reliable quality metric for SAEs or their latents, that costs grow quickly as more layers are covered, and that its list of open problems reflects the team's changed views over the previous year. Recent work also questions how reliable SAE benchmarks themselves are.

      Gemma Scope 2 technical paper, Google DeepMind; arXiv 2605.18229 (checked by search only; not read in full)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in papers, vendor demos and internal write-ups. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Taking a feature's name as its meaning

      The label "deception" was written from the top ten examples. Beyond those, the feature may fire on something else entirely.

      Spot itAsk how the label was validated on random activations, not just top ones, and whether anyone intervened on the feature.
    • Ignoring the reconstruction error

      An SAE that rebuilds the activation poorly makes any feature story partial. Splicing it into the model can raise loss a lot.

      Spot itAsk for the loss recovered when the SAE is spliced in, and for the share of variance left unexplained.
    • Cherry-picked examples

      A beautiful graph for one prompt, with no sense of how many failed.

      Spot itAsk how many prompts were tried and how many gave a usable account. Demos that never mention a failure rate are marketing.
    • Mistaking correlation for use

      A feature fires when the model handles fraud-related text, but the model's behaviour does not depend on it.

      Spot itAsk for the intervention: suppress or amplify the feature and see if behaviour changes. No ablation, no claim of causal role.
    • Explanations of the replacement, not the model

      The graph is of a stand-in with frozen attention. Its edges can differ from what the real model does.

      Spot itCheck the share of the answer that flows through error nodes, and whether interventions were tested on the original model.
    • Treating dictionary size as a fact

      "The model has 34 million features." It has as many as the researchers chose to train, with splitting and absorption varying by size.

      Spot itAsk whether results hold at two dictionary sizes, and with a different seed.
    • Beating a baseline you never ran

      An SAE-based detector for harmful requests is reported without comparing a linear probe or a fine-tuned classifier.

      Spot itAsk for the best simple baseline and the numbers side by side.
    • Claiming "we can see what the model is thinking"

      Overclaim in a vendor deck or a risk paper. Interpretability gives partial, hypothesis-grade evidence about internal computation.

      Spot itLook for the words "complete", "guarantee", "audit-ready". They are red flags in this field at the end of 2026.
    • Confusing this with model explanation for customers

      Proposing feature dictionaries as the answer to a regulator's request for credit-decision reasons.

      Spot itAsk whether the model in question is a transformer language model or a scorecard or gradient-boosted model. For the latter, use F8 methods.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There is real progress in looking inside language models. Researchers can now pull out thousands of internal concepts and, for a single question, trace how the model arrived at its answer, for example going from a city to its state to the capital. It is useful for understanding and testing models we host ourselves. But it is a research tool: it explains a minority of cases and leaves a lot unexplained. I would not put it in front of a regulator as proof of how a model decides. For that we rely on testing behaviour, which we can evidence today.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Neurons are polysemantic because the model stores more sparse features than it has dimensions, in superposition. A sparse autoencoder learns an overcomplete dictionary with a sparsity constraint, and its quality is a trade-off between sparsity and reconstruction, with known issues of shrinkage, splitting and absorption. Transcoders replace MLP blocks with sparse stand-ins so that edges between features are well defined, and attribution graphs freeze attention to make the path linear for one prompt. I'd always check the error-node share and validate with interventions on the real model, and compare against a linear-probe baseline before claiming SAEs add anything.”
    “What is a feature, exactly?”

    A direction in a layer's activation space that the model appears to use to represent some concept, found by a method such as a sparse autoencoder. It is a working definition. Different dictionaries carve the space differently, and a feature's meaning is a hypothesis you test by looking, predicting and intervening.

    “Why not just look at neurons?”

    Because a single neuron mixes several unrelated concepts. The model has more concepts than neurons, so it overlaps them. Concepts are directions across many neurons. A neuron is a bad unit of analysis, though occasionally a neuron is clean.

    “Can this tell us why the model gave a wrong answer to a customer?”

    Sometimes, on a model we can inspect, for a specific prompt, with effort. It might show that the model retrieved the wrong entity or ignored an instruction. It is a research-grade diagnostic for open-weight models. For hosted proprietary models we cannot do it ourselves, and for routine incident work we start with logs, retrieval traces and evaluation sets.

    “Does this solve the black-box problem for regulators?”

    Not yet. The methods explain a fraction of cases, leave a measurable unexplained remainder, and have not been standardised or accepted as evidence in any supervisory framework I know of. We should keep to behavioural testing, documentation and monitoring, and watch the research.

    “I read that people are moving away from SAEs. Is that true?”

    Partly. Some groups report that SAEs fell short of simple probes on downstream safety tasks and have shifted effort, while others continue to scale SAEs and transcoders for discovery, and open suites keep appearing. The sensible reading: SAEs are good for exploring and hypothesis-generation, and not yet shown superior for practical detection. I'd check the specific claim against the source.

    “Should our team build this?”

    Learn it, don't build it from scratch. Use public SAEs and the open circuit-tracing library on a small open model to build skill and to run occasional investigations. A production monitor or a training run of our own SAEs is expensive and has a weak track record against linear probes, so it needs a clear business case first.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A layer has 256 dimensions. About how large is the typical overlap (cosine) between two random directions, and what is the typical interference when 17 concepts are active at once?

    Cosine about 1/√256 = 1/16 ≈ 0.06. Interference ≈ √((17 − 1)/256) = √0.0625 = 0.25, a quarter of the signal. This is why packing works only when few concepts are active.

    2. Why does sparsity make superposition possible, and what happens if concepts are dense?

    When few concepts are active at once, the interference between overlapping directions is small, so each can still be read back. If many are active together the leakage adds up and readouts become unreliable. The model would need dedicated dimensions for its most important concepts.

    3. In an SAE, what does raising the L1 penalty do, and what do you lose?

    It forces more latents to zero, so each input is explained by fewer, usually cleaner features. You lose reconstruction accuracy, and the L1 term also shrinks strengths. TopK and JumpReLU designs address the shrinkage.

    4. Explain feature splitting and feature absorption in one sentence each.

    Splitting: with a bigger dictionary a broad feature divides into narrower ones. Absorption: a general feature stops firing where a more specific feature already covers the case, leaving gaps in the general one.

    5. How is a transcoder different from an SAE, and why does that help trace computation?

    An SAE rebuilds its own input at one point; a transcoder predicts an MLP block's output from its input through a sparse layer. That gives each feature an input pattern and an output direction, so the effect of one feature on a later one is a clean product, which is what attribution edges need.

    6. An attribution graph shows Dallas leading to Texas leading to Austin. What would count as evidence that the Texas step is really used?

    An intervention on the real model: suppress the Texas features and the Austin probability should fall; substitute California features and the model should move to Sacramento. A tidy graph without intervention is only a story.

    7. What do error nodes measure, and why are they the first thing you check?

    The part of the model's computation that the replacement model fails to capture on that prompt. If much of the influence on the answer flows through error nodes, the graph is explaining little.

    8. A vendor says its SAE-based detector finds harmful prompts better than anything else. What do you ask for?

    A comparison against a linear probe and a fine-tuned classifier on the same held-out data, with intervals, performance in Arabic as well as English, and the false alarm rate at the operating point. Published evidence so far shows simple probes are strong baselines.
