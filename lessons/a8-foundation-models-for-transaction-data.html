    Foundation Models for Transaction Data, and the Synthetic Data Privacy Trap

    How a bank's transaction history becomes a "language" a transformer can pretrain on, why that helps most when labels are scarce, what Nubank, Stripe and Featurespace have actually claimed, and why synthetic tabular data that looks anonymous can still leak who was in the training set.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA transaction foundation model learns a general-purpose summary of customer behaviour from unlabelled history, which you then reuse for fraud, credit and marketing, while synthetic data is a useful way to share the shape of your data that is not automatically private and must be tested like an attack surface.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A language model reads trillions of unlabelled words and learns grammar, facts and style. Afterwards a few examples are enough to teach it a small task, like a student who has read widely and then crams for one exam.

    A bank has the same setup. Every card swipe, transfer and salary credit is a "word"; a customer's year is a "sentence". Almost none of it is labelled: fraud is confirmed on a tiny share, defaults on a few percent. A transaction foundation model pretrains on the unlabelled sentences by hiding the next transaction and guessing it. To guess well it must learn that this customer buys groceries on Fridays, that salary lands on the 25th, that a 3 a.m. taxi in Riyadh is odd for someone who never travels. That knowledge is stored as a vector per customer or payment, an embedding, which fraud, credit and marketing models reuse.

    The second topic is the reverse worry. To share data without exposing customers, a team trains a generator on the real table and releases synthetic rows. A generator is a model of the data, and models can memorise, like a student who recites last year's paper word for word. Then the "fake" table carries real customers.

    Hold onto thisBoth topics are about a model that has absorbed customer data. In the first you want patterns and a reusable summary. In the second you must prove it kept only patterns, no individuals.

    Three questions to ask throughout:

    • What are the tokens? Turning amount, merchant, time and country into something a transformer can read is the hardest design choice (Simulation 1).
    • Where does the gain come from? Scarce labels and long histories. With plentiful labels and good features, a tuned gradient-boosted model is hard to beat (Simulation 2).
    • Who could tell this row was in the training set? That decides whether synthetic data is safe (Simulation 3).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A8.1 · Foundation models for transaction data

    Step 1: turn transactions into tokens. A transaction mixes fields, so there are two ways to tokenise it.

    • Serialise as text. "AED 212.40, Carrefour Marina, Dubai, 14:05". Reuses a language model and its tokeniser (see tokenisation), but costs about 15 tokens per transaction, splits numbers into fragments, and fills the context window after a few hundred transactions.
    • Use a structured vocabulary. One token each for merchant category, an amount bucket, a bucket of the time since the previous transaction, and country. Four tokens, so the model sees a long history. The price is rounding, like quoting prices to the nearest AED 10: a bucket stands for a range.

    Nubank sits between the two: text tokens for descriptions, special tokens for amount and date, and time as a gap since the previous event rather than a calendar stamp (Nubank engineering blog; Braithwaite et al., 2025).

    Step 2: pretrain with a self-supervised objective. Predict the next token from the ones before, as GPT-style models do (F5.7, self-supervised learning). Attention over the whole history links today's purchase to a pattern from three months ago. The data supplies its own answers, so no fraud or default label is needed and every transaction the bank has seen is training signal. Variants mask random tokens (BERT-style, as in IBM's TabFormer on synthetic card data, Padhi et al., 2021) or train contrastively so two windows from one customer land close together.

    Step 3: reuse it. Two patterns:

    • Frozen embedding as features. Take the final vector from a customer's recent history and add its 64 or 256 numbers as columns beside your existing features in a gradient-boosted model. Lowest risk, fits the current stack. Featurespace sells this as an API.
    • Fine-tune end to end. Attach a small head for one task and train everything, often jointly with hand-built tabular features. Nubank's reported nuFormer does this.
    L=−∑t=1Tlogpθ(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)

    The pretraining loss. xt is the t-th token and pθ the probability the model gave it, having seen only what came before. It measures surprise: lower means the model predicts what a customer does next better, so it has learned rhythms and habits. Do not read it as accuracy on fraud; it says nothing about that until you test the embedding. Same cross-entropy as language modelling (F1.5, likelihood and entropy).

    Why this helps when labels are scarce

    Learning to spot fraud from 200 confirmed cases is like learning a language from 200 sentences: too little to find structure in a huge input (the effective sample size is the number of positives, per imbalanced data). A pretrained embedding has already learned which directions of behaviour matter, so the classifier only draws a boundary in a small, tidy space. A stylised rule: fitting a linear classifier in d dimensions from n positives costs accuracy roughly in proportion to d ÷ n. Shrink d and the penalty falls. AUC reads as the chance a random fraud scores above a random non-fraud: 0.5 is a coin flip, 0.9 is strong. Going from 0.80 to 0.85 means five more fraud/non-fraud pairs in a hundred are ranked correctly.

    • The gain is often modest on a mature, label-rich task. Nubank's headline was a 1.25% relative AUC gain over a strong LightGBM baseline, about three times the lift of a typical model launch (Braithwaite et al., 2025). Small as a number, large as a win across 100 million customers.
    • The embedding is shared infrastructure. One model feeds fraud, credit limit, churn and offers. Cost is shared, and so is risk: a flaw or bias reaches every product. Validate it on its own (independent validation).
    • Time is a first-class input. Ignore the gap between events and you lose the rhythm that makes behaviour predictable, so the time-gap bucket matters as much as the amount.

    A8.2 · Synthetic tabular data and its privacy risks

    Banks want synthetic customers to give a vendor or hackathon realistic data without real records, to test pipelines in lower environments, to share across entities, and to add rare cases. The method: fit a generative model to the real table, then sample new rows. Common generators are CTGAN and TVAE (Xu et al., 2019), diffusion models such as TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., 2023), and fine-tuned language models that write rows as text. Simpler options (resampling with noise, copulas, Bayesian networks) also work.

    "The rows are fake, so there is no privacy issue" fails structurally. A generator that fits the data well is a compressed copy of it, like a forger who studied one painting so closely that the copy is the original. How much is pattern and how much is individuals depends on model capacity, training length and how unusual each record is. Three attacks matter:

    • Membership inference. Was this real record in the training set? Knowing that reveals the person was a customer, or in a segment such as a defaulters table.
    • Attribute inference. The attacker knows some fields about a person and uses the synthetic data to guess a sensitive one, such as balance band or arrears flag.
    • Reconstruction of outliers. A client with a unique profile, such as one huge transfer pattern, is often reproduced almost verbatim. Rows far from everyone else are most exposed, and often the most sensitive people.

    A popular check is distance to closest record (DCR): how far is each synthetic row from its nearest real training row? Near zero means a copy. It catches exact copies only, as Simulation 3 shows.

    Three lessons from the simulation:

    • Utility and privacy trade off; no free setting exists. Settings that preserve rare, valuable patterns (large exposures, unusual fraud) also preserve the individuals behind them.
    • Simple filters leak by their own pattern. Dropping rows near a real record leaves a hole around each training customer, like a gap in a crowd where someone stood. The hole is the signal. Recent work says distance-based metrics give false comfort.
    • The only formal guarantee is differential privacy (DP). Train the generator with DP (DP-SGD, or a DP marginal-based method) and the guarantee bounds what any attacker can learn about one record, whatever side information they hold (Dwork and Roth, 2014). The cost is lower fidelity, especially on small groups and rare events, where banks care most. Pick an epsilon (smaller means stronger privacy), measure the fidelity loss, and run attacks anyway.

    Gulf regulation: the UAE and Saudi Arabia both have personal data protection laws, and central banks add rules on data handling and outsourcing. Whether synthetic data counts as personal data depends on whether people can be re-identified from it. I have not seen a regulator-published threshold; ask your data protection officer and legal team.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Comparing to a weak baseline

      "Beats logistic regression by 8 points." A tuned boosted model with good rolling features may close most of that gap.

      Spot itAsk what the baseline was and whether it got equal effort and data. Compare against the best production model.
    • Pretraining on the future

      The embedding was computed on history that includes the label period, so it "knows" the fraud it predicts.

      Spot itAsk for the cut-off date of every sequence per training row. Embeddings must be point-in-time (F3.2).
    • Same customer in pretraining and test

      The model memorised customers in pretraining; the test set holds the same customers' later months.

      Spot itCheck that evaluation customers and window are separated in time, or that you knowingly evaluate on the future of known customers and deploy the same way.
    • Quantisation hides the signal

      Coarse buckets merge payments structured just under a reporting threshold, the pattern AML cares about.

      Spot itCheck bucket edges near regulatory or internal thresholds; test whether fine amounts there matter.
    • Opaque, shared and stale

      One embedding feeds many models, drifts after a launch or holiday season, and nobody owns it.

      Spot itAsk who owns it, how often it is retrained, and whether downstream models are re-validated on change.
    • Proxies in the embedding

      Merchants and timing (prayer-time patterns, remittance corridors) can encode nationality, religion or gender. A credit model can discriminate with no protected column.

      Spot itCan a simple model predict a protected attribute from the embedding? If yes, test downstream outputs for disparity (G2).
    • "Synthetic, so it's anonymous"

      Released externally with no attack testing, on the theory that no row is a real person.

      Spot itAsk for a membership-inference test against the release, reported for outliers as well as on average.
    • Trusting DCR alone

      "No synthetic row is near a real one, so nothing is copied." The gap itself is a signal (Simulation 3).

      Spot itAsk whether distances were also compared with held-out real data, and whether stronger attacks were run.
    • Averages hide the exposed few

      Overall attack AUC looks near 50% but the ten wealthiest clients are identified.

      Spot itAsk for results on the rarest 1% of records, not only the whole table.
    • Small groups wrongly modelled

      A high-privacy setting erases rare segments such as a niche business line, so models trained on it fail where it matters.

      Spot itCompare synthetic-trained against real-trained models by segment, on real test data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a transaction foundation model be trained with no fraud labels at all?

    The target is the next token in the customer's own history, so the data supplies the answer. Labels are needed only later, for the small classifier step.

    2. Amounts are bucketed on a log scale from AED 1 to 100,000 into 64 buckets. What is the worst-case relative error?

    Each bucket spans a factor eln(100000)/64 = e0.18 ≈ 1.197. The geometric centre is off by half that in log terms: e0.09 − 1 ≈ 9.4% too high, or about 8.6% too low. Roughly ±9%.

    3. Why does the pretrained embedding help most with a few dozen positives and little with 100,000?

    With tens of positives, a boundary in 200 raw dimensions is noisy; in 10 tidy dimensions it is not. With 100,000 positives the raw model learns the boundary precisely and the embedding's lost signal becomes the limit. In Simulation 2 the crossover is near 80 cases at default settings; it moves with the signal kept, and the curve is a rule of thumb.

    4. Nubank reports a 1.25% relative AUC lift. Is that small?

    1.25% relative on an AUC like 0.80 is about one AUC point: small in absolute terms. Nubank called it roughly three times a typical launch's lift, and across millions of decisions it matters financially. Quote both framings and say it is the company's figure.

    5. A frozen embedding was computed from the customer's last 12 months, up to the date the fraud case was closed. What is wrong?

    Leakage. The window runs past the transaction being scored and may include the fraud or its aftermath. Use history up to decision time only (point-in-time).

    6. Why are outlier customers the first to be exposed by a memorising generator?

    For a typical customer, many others sit nearby, so a close synthetic row says little about any one of them. For an outlier, a close synthetic row is almost certainly a copy of that person.

    7. Removing synthetic rows that are too close to real ones sounds safe. Why can it leak?

    It leaves empty space around each training record. A candidate with fewer synthetic neighbours than a random person would have is probably a member. Absence is as informative as presence.

    8. What does differential privacy give you that attack testing does not, and what does it cost?

    A mathematical bound on what any attacker can learn about one record; attack testing shows resistance only to the attacks you tried. The cost is lower fidelity on rare groups and tails, which banks often need.
