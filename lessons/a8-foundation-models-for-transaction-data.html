    Foundation Models for Transaction Data, and the Synthetic Data Privacy Trap

    How a bank's transaction history becomes a "language" a transformer can pretrain on, why that helps most when labels are scarce, what Nubank, Stripe and Featurespace have actually claimed, and why synthetic tabular data that looks anonymous can still leak who was in the training set.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA transaction foundation model learns a general-purpose summary of customer behaviour from unlabelled history, which you then reuse for fraud, credit and marketing, while synthetic data is a useful way to share the shape of your data that is not automatically private and must be tested like an attack surface.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A large language model reads trillions of words with no labels and ends up knowing grammar, facts and style. Later you can point it at a small task, such as classifying a complaint, and it needs only a few examples because it already understands the language.

    A bank has the same situation with money. Every card swipe, transfer and salary credit is a "word". A customer's year is a "sentence". The bank holds billions of these and almost none carry a label: fraud is confirmed on a tiny share, defaults on a few percent. The idea behind a transaction foundation model is to pretrain on the unlabelled sentences, by hiding the next transaction and asking the model to guess it. To do that well, the model must learn that this customer buys groceries on Fridays, that salary arrives on the 25th, that a taxi in Riyadh at 3 a.m. is odd for someone who never travels. That knowledge is stored as a vector per customer or per payment, an embedding, which fraud, credit and marketing models can then use.

    The second half of this lesson is the reverse worry. To share or test on data without exposing customers, a team trains a generator on the real table and releases the synthetic rows. The generator is a model of the data too, and models can memorise. If it does, the "fake" table carries real customers inside it.

    Hold onto thisBoth topics are about a model that has absorbed a lot of customer data. In the first you want it to absorb the patterns and hand you a reusable summary. In the second you need to prove it absorbed only the patterns and no individuals.

    Three questions will keep you honest throughout.

    • What are the tokens? A transaction has an amount, a merchant, a time, a country. Turning that into something a transformer can read is the hardest design choice, and Simulation 1 shows why.
    • Where does the gain come from? Mostly from labels being scarce, and from long histories that hand-built features squeeze too hard. Where labels are plentiful and features are good, a tuned gradient-boosted model is hard to beat. Simulation 2 gives the shape of this trade.
    • Who could tell this row was in the training set? That is the question that decides whether synthetic data is safe. Simulation 3 runs the attack.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A8.1 · Foundation models for transaction data

    Step 1: turn transactions into tokens. A text model has a vocabulary of sub-words. A transaction is a bundle of mixed fields, and there are two broad ways to tokenise it.

    • Serialise it as text. "AED 212.40, Carrefour Marina, Dubai, 14:05". This reuses a language model and its tokeniser (see tokenisation), but a single transaction costs perhaps 15 tokens, numbers are split into fragments, and a context window fills after a few hundred transactions.
    • Use a structured vocabulary. One token each for merchant category, a bucket of the amount, a bucket of the time since the previous transaction, and the country. Four tokens per transaction, and the model can see a long history. The price is rounding: a bucket stands for a range.

    Nubank's published design sits between the two: it keeps text tokens for descriptive fields but gives amount and date their own special tokens, and encodes time as a gap since the previous event rather than a calendar stamp (Nubank engineering blog; Braithwaite et al., 2025). Simulation 1 makes the bucket trade-off concrete.

    Step 2: pretrain with a self-supervised objective. The standard choice is the same as for GPT-style models: predict the next token given the ones before (F5.7, self-supervised learning). Because the model uses attention over the whole history, it can link a purchase today to a pattern from three months ago. No fraud label, no default label, no human annotation is needed, so every transaction the bank has ever seen is training signal. Variants mask random tokens instead (BERT-style), as IBM's TabFormer did on synthetic card data (Padhi et al., 2021), or train contrastively so that two windows from one customer land close together.

    Step 3: reuse it. Two patterns are common.

    • Frozen embedding as features. Run the customer's recent history through the model, take the final vector, and add its 64 or 256 numbers as columns next to your existing features in a gradient-boosted model. This is the lowest-risk path and fits the existing stack. Featurespace sells this pattern as an API (see Part 4).
    • Fine-tune end to end. Attach a small head for one task and train the whole thing, often jointly with the hand-built tabular features. Nubank's reported nuFormer design does this and combines the learned user embedding with existing tabular inputs.
    L=−∑t=1Tlogpθ(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)

    The pretraining loss. xt is the t-th token in a customer's history and pθ is the probability the model gave it, seen only what came before. Minimising this means the model gets better at predicting what a customer does next, which forces it to learn rhythms, habits and relationships between merchants. It is the same cross-entropy as language modelling (F1.5, likelihood and entropy).

    Why this helps when labels are scarce

    A fraud classifier learned directly on 200 confirmed cases has to find structure in a high-dimensional input using very few positives (the effective sample size is the number of positives, as imbalanced data showed). A pretrained embedding has already learned which directions of variation in behaviour matter, from billions of unlabelled events. The classifier then only needs to learn a boundary in a small, well-organised space. A stylised rule from classical statistics captures the effect: fitting a linear classifier in d dimensions from n positives costs accuracy roughly in proportion to d ÷ n. Cut the dimension that has to be learned and the penalty falls.

    Three practical points follow from the picture.

    • The gain is often modest on a mature, label-rich task. Nubank's headline figure was a 1.25% relative AUC gain over a strong LightGBM baseline, which they described as about three times the lift of a typical model launch (Braithwaite et al., 2025). That is small as a number and large as an engineering win at a bank with 100 million customers.
    • The embedding is shared infrastructure. One model can feed fraud, credit limit, churn and offers. Its cost is spread across all of them, but so is its risk: a flaw or bias in the embedding reaches every product. Treat it as a model that needs validation of its own (independent validation).
    • Time is a first-class input. Models that ignore the gap between events lose the rhythm that makes behaviour predictable. That is why the time-gap bucket in Simulation 1 matters as much as the amount.

    A8.2 · Synthetic tabular data and its privacy risks

    Why would a bank want synthetic customers? To give a vendor or a hackathon realistic data without sending real records, to test pipelines in a lower environment, to share data across entities, and to supplement rare cases. The approach: fit a generative model to the real table, then sample new rows. Common generators for tables are CTGAN and TVAE (Xu et al., 2019), diffusion models such as TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., 2023), and fine-tuned language models that write rows as text. Simpler approaches (resampling with noise, copulas, Bayesian networks) work too.

    The argument "the rows are fake so there is no privacy issue" fails for a structural reason. A generator that fits the data well is a compressed copy of the data. How much of the copy is the general pattern and how much is individuals depends on model capacity, training length and how unusual each record is. Three attacks matter:

    • Membership inference. Given a real record, was it in the training set? If the attacker can tell, then they learn something about the person: that they were a customer of this bank, or in a particular segment (for example, a defaulters table).
    • Attribute inference. The attacker knows some fields about a person and uses the synthetic data to guess a sensitive one, such as balance band or arrears flag.
    • Reconstruction of outliers. A client with a unique profile, such as one extremely large transfer pattern, is often reproduced almost verbatim. Rows far from everyone else are the most exposed, and they are often the most sensitive people.

    A popular check is distance to closest record (DCR): for each synthetic row, how far is the nearest real training row? Exact copies are easy to spot. Simulation 3 shows what the check does and does not catch.

    The simulation shows why privacy evaluation of synthetic data is hard. Three lessons:

    • Utility and privacy trade off, and no free setting exists. The settings that preserve rare, valuable patterns (large exposures, unusual fraud) are the settings that preserve the individuals who produced them.
    • Simple filters can leak by their own pattern. Dropping rows too close to a real record leaves a hole around each training customer. The hole is a signal. Recent work argues that distance-based privacy metrics give false comfort (see Part 4).
    • The only formal guarantee is differential privacy. Train the generator with DP (for example with DP-SGD, or a DP marginal-based method) and the guarantee bounds what any attacker can learn about one record, whatever side information they hold (Dwork and Roth, 2014). The cost is lower fidelity, especially on small groups and rare events, exactly where banks care most. In practice you pick an epsilon, measure the fidelity loss, and run attacks anyway.

    On the Gulf regulatory side: the UAE and Saudi Arabia both have personal data protection laws, and banks are also subject to their central bank's rules on data handling and outsourcing. Whether a given synthetic dataset counts as personal data depends on whether people can be re-identified from it, and I have not seen a regulator-published threshold for synthetic data. Treat that as a question for your data protection officer and legal team, not something to assume.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Both topics sit at the crossing of sequence modelling, representation learning and privacy.

    F5.7 · Self-supervised learningThe pretraining recipe

    Next-token prediction and contrastive learning on unlabelled history are the engine of every model in A8.1.

    F5.3 · AttentionWhy long histories work

    Attention lets today's transaction look at one from months ago directly, which is what recurrent models struggled with.

    R9 · Tabular and relational modelsBeyond a single table

    Transactions live in a graph of customers, merchants and accounts. Relational deep learning and time-series foundation models attack the same problem from other angles.

    B1 · Transaction fraudThe main customer for these embeddings

    Fraud has scarce labels and fast-changing patterns. See also delayed labels: pretraining sidesteps the label delay, fine-tuning does not.

    B2 · AML monitoringBehavioural baselines

    A good customer-behaviour embedding supports "unusual for this customer" signals, valuable where confirmed cases are very rare.

    F3.3 · Feature engineeringWhat the embedding replaces

    Rolling sums, counts and ratios are hand-made summaries of a history. An embedding is a learned one. Point-in-time rules (leakage) apply fully.

    P1 · Feature storesServing an embedding

    Embeddings must be computed consistently offline and online, versioned, and refreshed. A stale vector is a drift source.

    R11 · Memorisation and privacyThe same attack, on any model

    Simulation 3 is membership inference. The foundation model itself can also memorise customers; the lesson covers defences such as DP training.

    G2.2 · Privacy basicsAnonymisation has limits

    Synthetic data does not remove the need for minimisation and purpose limits; it is a technique inside that framework. See also synthetic data and model collapse for a different risk.

    F7.7 · DriftPretrained does not mean permanent

    Spending patterns change with prices, seasons (Ramadan, Eid, summer travel) and new products. Plan refreshes and monitor embedding drift.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card fraud and scams. Behavioural embeddings flag a payment that does not fit this customer, even if the amount and merchant look normal.
    • Credit and limit decisions. Cash-flow behaviour from current accounts can complement bureau data, especially for thin-file customers. Explainability and fairness demands rise here: an opaque embedding needs a validation and a reason-code strategy.
    • Marketing and personalisation. Next-best-offer and lifetime-value models share one customer representation.
    • Testing and vendor work. Synthetic data for sandboxes, for sharing with a fintech partner, or for data science competitions. The privacy test must precede the release.
    • Model risk. A shared embedding becomes a single point of failure; model risk teams will ask what it was trained on and how it is monitored.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Transaction foundation models moved from papers to vendor products and bank deployments between 2023 and 2026. The evidence is mostly company reports, which is worth knowing when you quote it. On synthetic data, the research story has been a steady erosion of trust in simple privacy checks.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Comparing to a weak baseline

      "The foundation model beats logistic regression by 8 points." A well-tuned boosted model with good rolling features may close most of that gap.

      Spot itAsk what the baseline was, and whether it got the same effort and the same data. The honest comparison is against the best existing production model.
    • Pretraining on the future

      The embedding for a customer was computed on history that includes the label period, so it "knows" about the fraud it is predicting.

      Spot itAsk for the cut-off date of every sequence fed to the model for each training row. Embeddings must be point-in-time (F3.2).
    • Same customer in pretraining and test

      The model memorised customer behaviour in pretraining; the test set contains the same customers' later months.

      Spot itCheck that the evaluation window and customers are separated in time, or that you evaluate on the future of known customers knowingly and deploy accordingly.
    • Quantisation hides the signal

      Coarse buckets merge small transfers and structured payments just under a reporting threshold, the pattern AML cares about.

      Spot itLook at the bucket edges near regulatory or internal thresholds. Test whether fine amounts near them matter.
    • Opaque, shared and stale

      One embedding feeds many models. It drifts after a product launch or a holiday season, and nobody owns it.

      Spot itAsk who owns the embedding, how often it is retrained, and whether downstream models are re-validated when it changes.
    • Proxies in the embedding

      Behavioural vectors can encode nationality, religion or gender through merchants and timing (prayer-time patterns, remittance corridors). A credit model using it can discriminate without any protected column.

      Spot itProbe the embedding: can a simple model predict a protected attribute from it? If yes, test the downstream outputs for disparity (G2).
    • "Synthetic, so it's anonymous"

      Data released externally with no attack testing, on the theory that no row is a real person.

      Spot itAsk for a membership-inference test on the real training set against the synthetic release, reported for outliers as well as on average.
    • Trusting DCR alone

      "No synthetic row is within a small distance of a real one, so nothing is copied." The gap itself is a signal, as Simulation 3 showed.

      Spot itAsk whether distance was compared against held-out real data too, and whether stronger attacks were run.
    • Averages hide the exposed few

      Overall attack AUC looks near 50% but the ten wealthiest clients are identified.

      Spot itAsk for results on the rarest 1% of records, not only the whole table.
    • Small groups wrongly modelled

      Synthetic data from a high-privacy setting erases rare segments such as a niche business line, so models trained on it fail where it matters.

      Spot itCompare model performance trained on synthetic against trained on real, by segment, on real test data.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Instead of building each fraud or credit model from scratch, we can train one model on our whole transaction history, without needing any labels, so it learns what normal behaviour looks like for each customer. Other teams then reuse that. Published results from Nubank show a modest but real accuracy gain, about what three normal model upgrades would give. The upside is largest where confirmed cases are few. For synthetic data, I'd say it is useful for testing but not private by default; we should test every release the way an attacker would.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd begin with the strongest boosted baseline and test whether a pretrained sequence embedding adds lift on a time-ordered split, with embeddings computed point-in-time and customers separated across pretraining and evaluation. I'd choose a structured tokenisation with time-gap tokens and check bucket resolution near thresholds that matter. For synthetic data I'd measure fidelity by segment, and privacy with membership-inference attacks including on outliers, not only distance to closest record, and I'd use DP training if the release is external.”
    “Is this just another LLM hype project?”

    The mechanism is the same as language models, self-supervised pretraining then reuse, but applied to structured events, and it is justified by one specific condition: many unlabelled events, few labels. The published gains are modest and vendor numbers are vendor-reported. So I'd run it as a bounded experiment against our best current model and decide on measured lift, cost, and the added validation burden.

    “Why not just feed transactions to an LLM as text?”

    You can, but numbers tokenise poorly, each transaction costs around 15 tokens, so context fills quickly, and a general language model has no training on payment dynamics. A purpose-built vocabulary with amount and time-gap tokens fits far more history per window and is cheaper to run. Hybrids that keep text for descriptions are reasonable.

    “Do we build one or buy one?”

    It depends on scale and data position. A vendor embedding trained on a network sees more fraud across institutions, and is quick to integrate, but we depend on a black box and send data out. In-house needs enough history, GPUs and a team to validate and refresh it. A reasonable path: pilot a vendor embedding as extra features, measure lift, and decide whether our own data justifies building.

    “We generated synthetic data. Can we give it to the vendor?”

    Not on that basis alone. I'd run membership-inference attacks using the real training set and a held-out set, report results including for outliers, compare fidelity with and without a differential privacy guarantee, and get sign-off from data protection and legal on whether the result counts as personal data. If the vendor only needs to test an integration, rule-based fake data may be safer and simpler.

    “Our DCR check shows no copies, so we're fine?”

    It rules out exact copying, which is the weakest threat. Filtering by distance can itself leave a detectable gap, and stronger attacks use more than distance. I'd keep the DCR check as a minimum and add attack-based tests.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can a transaction foundation model be trained with no fraud labels at all?

    Its pretraining target is the next token in the customer's own history. The data supplies the answer, so labels are not needed. The fraud label is only needed later, for the small fine-tuning or classifier step.

    2. Amounts are bucketed on a log scale from AED 1 to 100,000 into 64 buckets. What is the worst-case relative error?

    Each bucket spans a factor eln(100000)/64 = e0.18 ≈ 1.197. Reading back the geometric centre is off by at most half that in log terms: e0.09 − 1 ≈ 9.4% too high, or about 8.6% too low from the other end. Roughly ±9%.

    3. Why does the pretrained embedding help most with a few dozen positives and little with 100,000?

    With few positives (tens), learning a boundary in 200 raw dimensions is noisy; in 10 well-organised dimensions it is not. With 100,000 positives the raw model can learn the boundary precisely, and the embedding's lost signal becomes the limit. In Simulation 2 the crossover is at roughly 80 cases with the default settings; the exact point depends on how much signal the embedding keeps, and the whole curve is a stylised rule of thumb.

    4. Nubank reports a 1.25% relative AUC lift. Is that small?

    A relative gain of 1.25% on an AUC like 0.80 is about one AUC point. That is small in absolute terms. Nubank described it as roughly three times a typical model launch's lift, and on millions of decisions it is financially significant. Quote both framings and say it is the company's figure.

    5. A frozen embedding was computed from the customer's last 12 months, up to the date the fraud case was closed. What is wrong?

    Leakage. The window includes the period after the transaction being scored, and may include the fraud itself or its aftermath. The embedding must be computed using history up to the decision time only (point-in-time).

    6. Why are outlier customers the first to be exposed by a memorising generator?

    The generator reproduces the training data it can fit. For typical customers, many other customers sit nearby, so a close synthetic row says little about any one of them. For an outlier, a close synthetic row is almost certainly a copy of that person.

    7. Removing synthetic rows that are too close to real ones sounds safe. Why can it leak?

    It leaves empty space around each real training record. An attacker who finds that a candidate has no synthetic neighbours nearby, more than expected for a random person, infers membership. The pattern of absence is as informative as the pattern of presence.

    8. What does differential privacy give you that attack testing does not, and what does it cost?

    A mathematical bound on what can be learned about any single record, against any attacker. Attack testing only shows resistance to the attacks you tried. The cost is lower fidelity, particularly on rare groups and tails, which are often what a bank needs.
