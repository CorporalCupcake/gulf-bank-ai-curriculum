    Foundation Models for Transaction Data, and the Synthetic Data Privacy Trap

    How a bank's transaction history becomes a "language" a transformer can pretrain on, why that helps most when labels are scarce, what Nubank, Stripe and Featurespace have actually claimed, and why synthetic tabular data that looks anonymous can still leak who was in the training set.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA transaction foundation model learns a general-purpose summary of customer behaviour from unlabelled history, which you then reuse for fraud, credit and marketing, while synthetic data is a useful way to share the shape of your data that is not automatically private and must be tested like an attack surface.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A language model reads trillions of unlabelled words, then needs only a few examples to learn a small task: a student who has read widely and then crams for one exam.

    A bank has the same setup. Every card swipe, transfer and salary credit is a "word"; a customer's year is a "sentence". Almost none of it is labelled. A transaction foundation model pretrains on the unlabelled sentences by guessing the next transaction. To guess well it must learn that this customer buys groceries on Fridays, that salary lands on the 25th, that a 3 a.m. taxi in Riyadh is odd for someone who never travels. That knowledge is stored as a vector per customer, an embedding, which fraud, credit and marketing models reuse.

    The second topic is the reverse worry. A team trains a generator on the real table and releases synthetic rows. But a generator can memorise, like a student reciting last year's paper word for word, and then the "fake" table carries real customers.

    Hold onto thisIn both topics a model has absorbed customer data. In the first you want reusable patterns. In the second you must prove it kept only patterns, no individuals.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A8.1 · Foundation models for transaction data

    Step 1: turn transactions into tokens. There are two ways.

    • Serialise as text. "AED 212.40, Carrefour Marina, Dubai, 14:05". Reuses a language model (see tokenisation), but costs about 15 tokens per transaction and fills the context window after a few hundred.
    • Use a structured vocabulary. One token each for merchant category, an amount bucket, a time-gap bucket and country. Four tokens, so the model sees a long history. The price is rounding, like quoting prices to the nearest AED 10.

    Nubank sits between the two (Braithwaite et al., 2025). Time is stored as a gap since the previous event, which carries the customer's rhythm.

    Step 2: pretrain by predicting the next token (self-supervised learning). Attention lets today's purchase link to a pattern from three months ago. The data supplies its own answers, so no fraud label is needed. A lower training loss means the model predicts habits better; it says nothing about fraud accuracy until you test the embedding.

    Step 3: reuse it.

    • Frozen embedding as features. Add the customer's vector as extra columns beside your existing features in a gradient-boosted model. Lowest risk. Featurespace sells this as an API.
    • Fine-tune end to end. Attach a small head for one task and train everything. Nubank's reported nuFormer does this.

    Why this helps when labels are scarce

    Learning fraud from 200 confirmed cases is like learning a language from 200 sentences (see imbalanced data). A pretrained embedding has already learned which directions of behaviour matter, so the classifier draws its boundary in a small, tidy space. AUC reads as the chance a random fraud scores above a random non-fraud: 0.5 is a coin flip, 0.9 is strong.

    • The gain is often modest when labels are plentiful. Nubank's headline was a 1.25% relative AUC gain over a strong LightGBM baseline (Braithwaite et al., 2025). Small as a number, large across 100 million customers.
    • The embedding is shared infrastructure. One model feeds fraud, credit limit and offers, so a flaw or bias reaches every product. Validate it on its own (independent validation).

    A8.2 · Synthetic tabular data and its privacy risks

    Banks want synthetic customers to hand a vendor realistic data, to test pipelines, or to add rare cases. The method: fit a generative model to the real table, then sample new rows.

    "The rows are fake, so there is no privacy issue" fails. A generator that fits the data well is a compressed copy of it, like a forger who studied one painting so closely that the copy is the original. Three attacks matter:

    • Membership inference. Was this person in the training set? That alone can reveal they were a customer, or in a defaulters table.
    • Attribute inference. The attacker knows some fields and guesses a sensitive one, such as an arrears flag.
    • Outlier reconstruction. A client with a unique profile is often reproduced almost verbatim.

    A popular check is distance to closest record (DCR): how far is each synthetic row from its nearest real row? It catches exact copies only, as Simulation 3 shows.

    Three lessons from the simulation:

    • Utility and privacy trade off. Settings that preserve rare, valuable patterns also preserve the individuals behind them.
    • Simple filters leak by their own pattern. Dropping rows near a real record leaves a hole where each training customer stood, like a gap in a crowd. The hole is the signal.
    • The only formal guarantee is differential privacy. It bounds what any attacker can learn about one record, at the cost of fidelity on small groups and rare events. Measure the loss, and run attacks anyway.

    Gulf regulation: whether synthetic data counts as personal data depends on whether people can be re-identified from it. Ask your data protection officer and legal team.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Weak baseline

      "Beats logistic regression by 8 points." A tuned boosted model may close most of that gap.

      Spot itCompare against the best production model.
    • Pretraining on the future

      The embedding was computed on history that includes the label period.

      Spot itAsk for the cut-off date per training row (F3.2).
    • Same customer in pretraining and test

      The model memorised customers; the test set holds their later months.

      Spot itCheck evaluation is separated in time, the way you will deploy.
    • Quantisation hides the signal

      Coarse buckets merge payments structured just under a reporting threshold.

      Spot itCheck bucket edges near regulatory thresholds.
    • Opaque, shared and stale

      One embedding feeds many models, drifts, and nobody owns it.

      Spot itAsk who owns it and whether downstream models are re-validated on change.
    • Proxies in the embedding

      Merchants and timing (prayer-time patterns, remittance corridors) can encode nationality or religion, so a credit model can discriminate with no protected column.

      Spot itCan a simple model predict a protected attribute from the embedding? Then test outputs for disparity (G2).
    • "Synthetic, so it's anonymous"

      Released with no attack testing.

      Spot itAsk for a membership-inference test, reported for outliers too.
    • Trusting DCR alone

      "No synthetic row is near a real one, so nothing is copied." The gap itself is a signal.

      Spot itAsk whether distances were also compared with held-out real data.
    • Averages hide the exposed few

      Overall attack AUC looks near 50% but the ten wealthiest clients are identified.

      Spot itAsk for results on the rarest 1% of records.
    • Small groups wrongly modelled

      A high-privacy setting erases rare segments, so models trained on it fail there.

      Spot itCompare synthetic-trained and real-trained models by segment.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a transaction foundation model be trained with no fraud labels at all?

    The target is the next token in the customer's own history, so the data supplies the answer. Labels are needed only later, for the small classifier step.

    2. Why bucket amounts on a log scale, and what goes wrong with too few buckets?

    Spending is proportional: AED 18 versus AED 46 matters, AED 24,500 versus AED 24,530 does not. With too few buckets, a coffee and a taxi ride share a token and the model cannot tell them apart.

    3. Why does the embedding help most with a few dozen positives and little with 100,000?

    With tens of positives, a boundary across 200 raw columns is mostly noise. With 100,000 the raw model learns it precisely, and the signal the embedding discarded becomes the limit.

    4. Nubank reports a 1.25% relative AUC lift. Is that small?

    About one AUC point: small in absolute terms, but financially meaningful across millions of decisions. Quote both framings and say it is the company's figure.

    5. A frozen embedding was computed from the last 12 months, up to the date the fraud case was closed. What is wrong?

    Leakage. The window runs past the decision and may include the fraud or its aftermath. Use history up to decision time only.

    6. Why are outlier customers the first to be exposed by a memorising generator?

    For a typical customer, many others sit nearby, so a close synthetic row says little. For an outlier, a close synthetic row is almost certainly a copy of that person.

    7. Removing synthetic rows that are too close to real ones sounds safe. Why can it leak?

    It leaves empty space around each training record. A person with fewer synthetic neighbours than expected is probably a member. Absence is as informative as presence.

    8. What does differential privacy give you that attack testing does not, and what does it cost?

    A bound on what any attacker can learn about one record; attack testing covers only the attacks you tried. The cost is lower fidelity on rare groups, which banks often need.
