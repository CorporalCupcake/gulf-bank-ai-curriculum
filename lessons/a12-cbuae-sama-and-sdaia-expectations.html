    Who Expects What: CBUAE, SAMA, SDAIA and the Data Protection Laws

    A working map of the rules and expectations that touch an AI system at a UAE or Saudi bank: what is hard law, what is supervisory expectation, what is principle, and which questions the compliance team will ask you before launch.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceThere is no single "AI law" for a Gulf bank; there are three layers (the central bank's supervisory expectations, national data protection law, and national AI principles), and the data scientist's job is to know which layer a given system triggers and to leave evidence for each.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine you ship a model that declines some personal-finance applications, and a customer complains. Three different people may now be interested. The central bank supervisor asks: who at the bank was accountable for this model, was it validated, and was the customer treated fairly? The data protection authority asks: what personal data went in, on what legal basis, and did the customer get an explanation or a way to object? A national AI body, if it has a view, asks whether the system respected published principles on fairness and transparency.

    Those are different questions with different owners, different strength, and different paperwork. Mixing them up is the commonest mistake: calling a voluntary principle "the law", or assuming a data protection duty is "just a compliance thing" that engineers can ignore.

    A useful way to hold the whole subject is three layers, from strongest to softest.

    • Hard law. Statutes and regulations with penalties: the UAE and Saudi personal data protection laws, and in the DIFC, its Data Protection Law and Regulation 10. Breaking these can mean fines and orders.
    • Supervisory expectation. What the central bank (CBUAE in the UAE, SAMA in Saudi Arabia) expects of the institutions it licenses: governance, model management, consumer treatment, outsourcing, cyber. Not always written as a statute, but a bank that ignores it will hear about it in an inspection. Treat it as binding in practice.
    • Principles and strategy. National AI strategies and ethics principles, such as those from Saudi Arabia's SDAIA. They shape what regulators and boards consider reasonable, and they often become the vocabulary of later rules.
    Hold onto thisAsk two questions of every system: where does it sit (which supervisor and which data law) and what does it touch (personal data, a customer decision, a vendor, data leaving the country). The answers, not the technology label "AI", decide which rules apply.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Central bank and national AI expectations (A12.1)

    UAE. The CBUAE supervises banks, insurers, finance companies and other licensed financial institutions onshore. In February 2026 it issued a guidance note on consumer protection and the responsible adoption and use of AI and machine learning. Press coverage of the note says it puts accountability on the board and senior management for AI systems across selection, deployment and monitoring, expects a documented AI governance framework scaled to the institution, expects human oversight, and says systems that produce discriminatory or manipulative outcomes should not be deployed or should be stopped. It builds on earlier CBUAE material: the 2021 guidelines on adopting enabling technologies, and the Model Management Standards, which are the ground rules for how any model, AI or not, is inventoried, validated and monitored (see G3: what counts as a model).

    Two cautions. First, I have read secondary summaries rather than the note itself, and sources differ on its exact publication date (reported as 11 and 23 February 2026). Read the original on the CBUAE rulebook before quoting it. Second, one law-firm summary reads the note as covering onshore institutions supervised by the CBUAE, not firms in the DIFC or ADGM free zones, which have their own regulators (DFSA and FSRA). That reading is plausible but I could not confirm it from the text.

    Saudi Arabia. SAMA supervises banks, insurers and finance companies. It runs frameworks that matter to any AI build regardless of label, most visibly its cyber security framework and its rules on outsourcing and third parties. I could not confirm a single SAMA document dedicated to AI or machine learning in banks; claims online that one exists were from low-quality sources. Treat that as an open question to put to your compliance team rather than something to assert. The national AI body is SDAIA (the Saudi Data and AI Authority), which also administers the data protection law. Its published AI Ethics Principles (adopted in 2023, with sources disagreeing on the date and the exact count of principles) cover themes like fairness, privacy and security, accountability, transparency and human benefit. Law-firm commentary describes them as not legally binding on their own.

    What the two sets of expectations share is more useful than their differences. In plain terms the supervisor wants:

    • Named accountability. A senior owner, not "the model". A model inventory with owner, purpose, data and risk tier.
    • Independent challenge. Validation by people who did not build it, and periodic review (G3: independent validation, G3: monitoring and review).
    • Fair, explainable treatment of customers. Evidence you tested for discriminatory outcomes (G2: fairness) and can explain a decision in plain words.
    • Human oversight where stakes are high. Someone can review, override and stop the system.
    • Control of third parties. If a vendor model or cloud sits in the chain, the bank still owns the outcome.

    Data protection laws, and DIFC Regulation 10 (A12.2)

    UAE onshore. The federal Personal Data Protection Law (Federal Decree-Law 45 of 2021) sets rules on consent and other lawful bases, notices, data subject rights, security, breach notification and cross-border transfers. As I understand it, it contains a right to object to decisions based solely on automated processing, and it carves out some categories, including data governed by other sector legislation and data inside free zones that have their own data law. Whether banking and credit data fall under the carve-out, and how far the implementing regulations have progressed, are exactly the questions to put to the bank's Data Protection Officer rather than answer from a lesson. Say "I understand" and ask.

    DIFC. The Dubai International Financial Centre has its own Data Protection Law (Law No. 5 of 2020) and, from 1 September 2023, Regulation 10 on personal data processed through autonomous and semi-autonomous systems. This is the one rule in the region written specifically about AI-type processing of personal data. Commentary describes its main features: the deployer (like a controller) carries the main duty and the operator (the technical provider) works for it; users must be told when and how such a system is used; and for commercial, high-risk processing there are requirements around certification and appointing an Autonomous Systems Officer. One law-firm article reports full enforcement from 1 January 2026, and a consultation has proposed changes, including a new Regulation 11 on accreditation and certification. I could not confirm whether those changes have been enacted. Sources also disagree on penalty detail. Check the DIFC Commissioner of Data Protection's own pages.

    ADGM. The Abu Dhabi Global Market has its own data protection regulations. If your bank sits there, the DIFC rules do not apply but the same questions do.

    Saudi Arabia. The Personal Data Protection Law (Royal Decree M/19 of 2021, amended in 2023) became enforceable in September 2024 after a transition period, with SDAIA as the competent authority. It covers lawful basis, notice, data subject rights, limits on transfers outside the Kingdom, and penalties that can include fines and, for some offences, imprisonment. Its implementing regulations include rules on transfers and on sensitive data. Again, the safe habit is to ask Legal which version and which regulation applies.

    The engineer's translation of all of this is a short list of design facts you must be able to state for any system:

    • Which personal data fields go in, and why each is needed (minimisation, see G2: privacy basics).
    • The legal basis, which is a Legal decision but depends on what you actually do with the data.
    • Where the data is processed and stored, including inside a vendor's LLM or a cloud region. See A11: sovereign AI and A11: privacy-enhancing technologies.
    • Whether a decision about a person is made or heavily driven by the system, and what the customer can ask for: an explanation, a human review, an objection.
    • How long data and logs are kept and how a deletion request flows through training sets, feature stores and prompt logs.

    Turning it into build practice

    The skill is converting a legal sentence into an artefact a reviewer can inspect. A pattern that works in most banks is a short AI use-case record, filled in before build and updated at launch, that answers the questions above in one page: purpose, owner, data fields, legal basis agreed with Legal, vendors and regions, who sees outputs, how a human can intervene, how fairness and accuracy were tested, and the monitoring plan. It does two jobs. It makes you think before building, and it gives the supervisor or the privacy officer something concrete when they ask.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Regulation is where almost every other topic in the curriculum turns into paperwork.

    G3 · Model riskInventory first

    The CBUAE's model management expectations apply to models generally. The inventory and tiering in G3 are the base on which an AI-specific note sits.

    G3 · Validation and monitoringIndependent challenge

    Accountability language in supervisory notes cashes out as validation reports and periodic reviews. Monitoring is the evidence that approval was kept.

    G2 · Fairness and privacyFair outcomes and minimisation

    "No discriminatory outcomes" needs a metric you can defend; "data protection" needs minimisation and a retention rule. Those lessons supply both.

    A12 · Other regimesEU AI Act and ISO/IEC 42001

    Gulf banks with European operations or customers meet the EU Act, and many adopt ISO 42001 as a management-system framework that maps to local expectations.

    A11 · Sovereignty and privacy techWhere data sits

    Cross-border transfer rules are why in-country hosting and privacy-enhancing techniques matter commercially.

    G1 · SecurityCyber is a regulatory topic too

    SAMA's and the CBUAE's cyber and third-party expectations apply to AI systems and their vendors as much as to any other software.

    A15 · Controls on toolsGateways and guardrails

    A central gateway that logs prompts, masks personal data and routes by region is the practical control behind many of the rules above.

    A7 · ValidatorsWhat validators can accept today

    How model risk teams are adapting to LLM systems, which is where supervisory expectations meet practice.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit decisioning. The clearest high-stakes case: fairness, explanation, human review, and a model risk file (B4).
    • KYC and onboarding. Heavy personal and sometimes biometric data, plus AML duties that pull the other way from minimisation (B3).
    • Customer-facing generative AI. Chatbots and voice agents raise disclosure, accuracy and complaint-handling questions on top of data protection (A4).
    • Staff copilots. Data protection and confidentiality questions about what staff paste into a tool (B7).
    • Vendor and cloud models. Third-party risk, data location and the bank's duty to retain control.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The direction in the Gulf is clear: lots of ambition for AI, a principles-and-supervision approach rather than one comprehensive AI act, and a steady tightening of detail for regulated sectors. Rules are moving, so what follows is dated.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with a few web searches. Most sources were law-firm summaries and press releases, not the primary texts. Items that I could not confirm are marked.

    1. 2021
      First laws and guidelines

      The UAE federal data protection law (Decree-Law 45) and Saudi Arabia's PDPL were issued; the CBUAE issued guidelines on adopting enabling technologies.

    2. 2023
      DIFC Regulation 10 and SDAIA principles

      The DIFC added Regulation 10 on autonomous and semi-autonomous systems from 1 September 2023 (Clyde & Co). SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles were adopted around the same time, reported as September or October 2023.

    3. 2024
      Saudi PDPL enforcement begins

      Law-firm sources report that enforcement started in September 2024, after a one-year transition, with SDAIA as the competent authority.

    4. 2026
      CBUAE guidance note on responsible AI

      The CBUAE issued a guidance note on consumer protection and the responsible adoption of AI and ML by licensed institutions, reported in February 2026 (WAM; Pinsent Masons).

    5. 2026
      DIFC Regulation 10 reported at full enforcement; changes proposed

      One law-firm article reports full enforcement from 1 January 2026, with a consultation proposing amendments and a new Regulation 11 on accreditation and certification (Mayer Brown). Whether the amendments are in force: not confirmed.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the habits that create regulatory trouble. Each has a quick check.

    • Quoting a principle as law, or the reverse

      "SDAIA requires X" when a document is a set of principles; or dismissing a supervisory note because it says "guidance".

      Spot itAsk which layer the statement comes from, and read the original wording. Say "expectation" or "principle" unless there is a penalty attached.
    • Citing rules from memory or from a blog

      Rules and dates here are moving; summaries conflict on dates, scope and penalties.

      Spot itIs there a link to the primary text, and a date you checked it? If not, treat the claim as unverified.
    • Treating a vendor as a shield

      "The cloud provider is compliant, so we are." The bank remains accountable for outcomes and for data location.

      Spot itCan you name where prompts and customer data are processed and stored, and under which contract terms?
    • Personal data in prompts and logs

      Staff paste customer details into a tool; prompt logs keep them indefinitely.

      Spot itSample the prompt log for names, Emirates ID or national ID numbers and IBANs. Look for retention settings.
    • "Anonymised" data that is not

      Removing names but keeping date of birth, postcode and transaction patterns that identify people.

      Spot itTry to re-identify a few records using fields an outsider could know. If it is easy, it is personal data.
    • Assuming one jurisdiction's rules cover all

      A DIFC rule applied to an onshore bank, or Saudi transfer limits forgotten in a regional model.

      Spot itList each legal entity and each country whose customers' data the system touches. Check each separately.
    • Human oversight in name only

      A reviewer clicks approve on hundreds of AI decisions an hour.

      Spot itLook at override rates and time per review. An override rate near zero is a warning, not a success.
    • Building first, asking Legal last

      The proof of concept becomes a pilot with real data before anyone has agreed a legal basis.

      Spot itIs there a dated use-case record with Compliance and Legal sign-off before real customer data was used?
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We are not waiting for one AI law. Three things already apply: the central bank expects named accountability, independent validation and fair treatment of customers for any model; the data protection laws govern what customer data we can use and where it goes; and national AI principles set the tone. For this project I'll bring a one-page record of data, vendors, human oversight and testing, agreed with Legal and Compliance before we use real customer data.”
    To Compliance or a validator
    “I'd like to confirm which regime applies by legal entity and data flow. For this system the inputs are these fields, the decision is advisory with human review, the vendor processes data in this region, and here is how a customer objection or deletion request would flow. Could you confirm the legal basis and whether any transfer rule applies? I'll draft the use-case record and put fairness and accuracy test results in the validation pack.”
    “Does the CBUAE ban or license AI models?”

    As far as I know it does not license individual models. Its expectations are about governance, model management and consumer outcomes, and it expects institutions to be able to show those. The February 2026 guidance note is where the AI-specific expectations are set out; I'd read the primary text before being more precise.

    “Is the SDAIA ethics framework binding on us?”

    Commentary describes the principles as not legally binding on their own. But SDAIA also administers the data protection law, which is binding, and the principles influence what a regulator considers reasonable. I'd meet both and ask Compliance how the bank treats the principles internally.

    “Can we send customer data to a hosted LLM?”

    Only after three answers: what data, where it is processed and stored, and what the contract says about retention and training. Then Legal confirms the basis and any transfer rule. Often the right design is to mask or avoid personal data, use an in-region or private deployment, and log through a gateway. That is an engineering answer with a legal sign-off, not a refusal.

    “What is DIFC Regulation 10 and does it apply to us?”

    It is the DIFC rule on personal data processed through autonomous and semi-autonomous systems, with duties on deployers and operators and extra steps for high-risk commercial use. It applies to entities in the DIFC. If we are onshore it does not apply directly, but it shows where regulation may head. I'd confirm current status with Legal, as amendments were being proposed.

    “Is a human in the loop enough?”

    Only if the human can really review, has the information to disagree, and does sometimes override. I'd track override rates and review time and write down when the human must decide rather than approve.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the three layers and give one example of each for a Gulf bank.

    Hard law, such as the UAE or Saudi data protection law or DIFC Regulation 10. Supervisory expectation, such as CBUAE model management standards and AI guidance note, or SAMA's frameworks. Principles, such as SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles.

    2. A bank in the DIFC is building a loan-pricing model. Which of the CBUAE note and DIFC Regulation 10 is more likely to bind it directly, and why the hedge?

    Regulation 10 is a DIFC regulation, so it is the more likely direct fit if personal data are processed by an autonomous system. The CBUAE note is reported as aimed at onshore CBUAE-licensed institutions, but I could not confirm the scope in the text, and the firm is supervised by the DFSA. So the answer is "likely", with Legal to confirm.

    3. What five design facts should you be able to state about any system touching personal data?

    Which fields and why; the legal basis; where data are processed and stored; whether and how a decision about a person is driven by the system and what the customer can ask for; and retention, including how deletion reaches training sets and logs.

    4. Your vendor says its LLM service is "fully compliant". What do you ask?

    Compliant with what, in which region, under which contract? Where do prompts go, how long are they kept, are they used for training, and who are the sub-processors? The bank stays accountable, so you need the facts yourself.

    5. Why is "human in the loop" sometimes a weak control?

    When reviewers lack time, information or authority, they approve almost everything, so the loop only adds delay. Measure override rate and time per case and design the review so that disagreeing is possible.

    6. A colleague says "SAMA requires banks to do X with AI". What do you do before repeating it?

    Ask for the document and the clause. I could not confirm a dedicated SAMA AI instrument, so the claim may come from the general frameworks, from SDAIA, or from a blog. Verify it with Compliance.
