    Who Expects What: CBUAE, SAMA, SDAIA and the Data Protection Laws

    A working map of the rules and expectations that touch an AI system at a UAE or Saudi bank: what is hard law, what is supervisory expectation, what is principle, and which questions the compliance team will ask you before launch.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceThere is no single "AI law" for a Gulf bank; there are three layers (the central bank's supervisory expectations, national data protection law, and national AI principles), and the data scientist's job is to know which layer a given system triggers and to leave evidence for each.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Say your model declines some personal-finance applications and a customer complains. Three different people now ask three different questions:

    • Central bank supervisor: who was accountable for this model, was it validated, was the customer treated fairly?
    • Data protection authority: what personal data went in, on what legal basis, could the customer get an explanation or object?
    • National AI body: did the system respect published principles on fairness and transparency?

    Think of a restaurant: the health inspector can close it, the tax office can fine it, and a food guide only gives opinions. Different owners, different strength, different paperwork. The commonest mistake is mixing them up: calling a voluntary principle "the law", or treating data protection as "just a compliance thing".

    So hold the subject as three layers, strongest to softest:

    • Hard law. Statutes with penalties: the UAE and Saudi personal data protection laws, and in the DIFC its Data Protection Law and Regulation 10. Breaking them means fines and orders.
    • Supervisory expectation. What the central bank (CBUAE in the UAE, SAMA in Saudi Arabia) expects of banks it licenses: governance, model management, consumer treatment, outsourcing, cyber. Often not a statute, but ignore it and you will hear about it in an inspection. Treat it as binding in practice.
    • Principles and strategy. National AI strategies and ethics principles, such as SDAIA's in Saudi Arabia. They shape what regulators call reasonable and often become the vocabulary of later rules.
    Hold onto thisAsk two questions of every system: where does it sit (which supervisor, which data law) and what does it touch (personal data, a customer decision, a vendor, data leaving the country). The answers, not the label "AI", decide which rules apply.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Central bank and national AI expectations (A12.1)

    UAE. The CBUAE supervises onshore banks, insurers and finance companies. In February 2026 it issued a guidance note on consumer protection and responsible AI/ML. Press coverage says it puts accountability for AI (selection, deployment, monitoring) on the board and senior management, expects a documented governance framework scaled to the institution and human oversight, and says systems with discriminatory or manipulative outcomes should not be deployed or should be stopped. It builds on the 2021 guidelines on enabling technologies and the Model Management Standards, the ground rules for inventorying, validating and monitoring any model, AI or not (G3: what counts as a model).

    Two cautions. I read secondary summaries, not the note, and sources differ on its date (11 or 23 February 2026), so read the original on the CBUAE rulebook before quoting it. One law-firm summary reads it as covering CBUAE-supervised onshore firms, not DIFC or ADGM firms (regulated by the DFSA and FSRA). Plausible, but unconfirmed.

    Saudi Arabia. SAMA supervises banks, insurers and finance companies. Its cyber security framework and its outsourcing and third-party rules matter to any AI build. I could not confirm a SAMA document dedicated to AI in banks; online claims came from low-quality sources. Put it to Compliance as an open question. SDAIA (Saudi Data and AI Authority) is the national AI body and also administers the data protection law. Its AI Ethics Principles (2023; sources disagree on date and count) cover fairness, privacy and security, accountability, transparency and human benefit. Law-firm commentary says they are not binding alone.

    What the expectations share is more useful than how they differ. The supervisor wants:

    • Named accountability. A senior owner, not "the model"; an inventory with owner, purpose, data and risk tier.
    • Independent challenge. Validation by people who did not build it, plus periodic review (G3: validation, G3: monitoring).
    • Fair, explainable treatment. Evidence you tested for discriminatory outcomes (G2: fairness) and can explain a decision in plain words.
    • Human oversight where stakes are high. Someone can review, override and stop the system.
    • Control of third parties. With a vendor model or cloud in the chain, the bank still owns the outcome. Like hiring a courier: the parcel is still yours if it is lost.

    Data protection laws, and DIFC Regulation 10 (A12.2)

    • UAE onshore. The federal PDPL (Decree-Law 45 of 2021) covers lawful basis, notices, data subject rights, security, breach notification and cross-border transfers. As I understand it, it includes a right to object to solely automated decisions and carves out some data (other sector laws, free zones with their own law). Whether banking and credit data fall in the carve-out is a question for the bank's Data Protection Officer. Say "I understand" and ask.
    • DIFC. Data Protection Law (Law No. 5 of 2020) plus, from 1 September 2023, Regulation 10 on personal data processed by autonomous and semi-autonomous systems, the one regional rule written for AI-type processing. The deployer (like a controller) carries the main duty; the operator (technical provider) works for it; users must be told when and how the system is used; high-risk commercial processing needs certification and an Autonomous Systems Officer. One law-firm article reports full enforcement from 1 January 2026, and a consultation proposes changes including a new Regulation 11 on accreditation. I could not confirm those are enacted, and sources disagree on penalties. Check the DIFC Commissioner's pages.
    • ADGM. Its own data protection regulations. DIFC rules do not apply there, but the same questions do.
    • Saudi Arabia. The PDPL (Royal Decree M/19 of 2021, amended 2023) became enforceable in September 2024, with SDAIA as competent authority. It covers lawful basis, notice, rights, limits on transfers outside the Kingdom, and penalties including fines and, for some offences, imprisonment. Ask Legal which version and regulation applies.

    The engineer's translation is five design facts you must be able to state for any system:

    • Which personal data fields go in, and why each (minimisation: G2: privacy basics).
    • The legal basis. Legal decides, but it depends on what you actually do with the data.
    • Where data is processed and stored, including in a vendor LLM or cloud region (A11: sovereign AI, A11: privacy tech).
    • Whether the system makes or heavily drives a decision about a person, and what the customer can ask for: explanation, human review, objection.
    • How long data and logs are kept, and how a deletion request reaches training sets, feature stores and prompt logs.

    Turning it into build practice

    Convert each legal sentence into something a reviewer can inspect. The usual tool is a one-page AI use-case record, filled in before build and updated at launch: purpose, owner, data fields, legal basis agreed with Legal, vendors and regions, who sees outputs, how a human intervenes, fairness and accuracy tests, monitoring plan. It makes you think before building and gives the supervisor or privacy officer something concrete. Like a pilot's pre-flight checklist: boring, and it catches things.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting a principle as law, or the reverse

      "SDAIA requires X" about principles; or dismissing a supervisory note because it says "guidance".

      Spot itAsk which layer it comes from and read the original wording. Say "expectation" or "principle" unless a penalty is attached.
    • Citing rules from memory or a blog

      Rules are moving; summaries conflict on dates, scope and penalties.

      Spot itIs there a link to the primary text and a date you checked it? If not, it is unverified.
    • Treating a vendor as a shield

      "The cloud provider is compliant, so we are." The bank stays accountable for outcomes and data location.

      Spot itCan you name where prompts and customer data are processed and stored, and under which contract terms?
    • Personal data in prompts and logs

      Staff paste customer details into a tool; prompt logs keep them indefinitely.

      Spot itSample the prompt log for names, Emirates ID or national ID numbers and IBANs. Check retention settings.
    • "Anonymised" data that is not

      Names removed, but date of birth, postcode and transaction patterns still identify people.

      Spot itTry to re-identify a few records with fields an outsider could know. If easy, it is personal data.
    • Assuming one jurisdiction covers all

      A DIFC rule applied to an onshore bank, or Saudi transfer limits forgotten in a regional model.

      Spot itList each legal entity and each country whose customers' data the system touches. Check each separately.
    • Human oversight in name only

      A reviewer clicks approve on hundreds of AI decisions an hour.

      Spot itLook at override rates and time per review. Near-zero overrides is a warning, not a success.
    • Building first, asking Legal last

      The proof of concept becomes a pilot with real data before anyone agrees a legal basis.

      Spot itIs there a dated use-case record with Compliance and Legal sign-off from before real customer data was used?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name the three layers and give one example of each for a Gulf bank.

    Hard law: UAE or Saudi data protection law, DIFC Regulation 10. Supervisory expectation: CBUAE model management standards and AI guidance note, or SAMA's frameworks. Principles: SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles.

    2. A bank in the DIFC is building a loan-pricing model. Which of the CBUAE note and DIFC Regulation 10 more likely binds it directly, and why the hedge?

    Regulation 10, if personal data is processed by an autonomous system. The CBUAE note is reported as aimed at onshore CBUAE-licensed firms, and a DIFC firm is supervised by the DFSA. But I could not confirm the note's scope from the text, so the answer is "likely", with Legal to confirm.

    3. What five design facts should you be able to state about any system touching personal data?

    Which fields and why; the legal basis; where data is processed and stored; whether the system drives a decision about a person and what the customer can ask for; retention, including how deletion reaches training sets and logs.

    4. Your vendor says its LLM service is "fully compliant". What do you ask?

    Compliant with what, in which region, under which contract? Where do prompts go, how long are they kept, are they used for training, who are the sub-processors? The bank stays accountable, so you need the facts yourself.

    5. Why is "human in the loop" sometimes a weak control?

    Reviewers short of time, information or authority approve almost everything, so the loop only adds delay. Measure override rate and time per case, and design review so disagreeing is possible.

    6. A colleague says "SAMA requires banks to do X with AI". What do you do before repeating it?

    Ask for the document and clause. I could not confirm a dedicated SAMA AI instrument, so the claim may come from the general frameworks, SDAIA, or a blog. Verify with Compliance.
