    Treasury Risk: Liquidity, Asset-Liability Management, VaR and Hedging

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank borrows short and lends long, like a hotel that rents rooms by the night but sells long leases on them. If enough depositors ask for their money at once, the loans cannot be sold in time. Healthy banks have failed this way.

    Treasury manages that gap, with three jobs.

    • Liquidity (B5.1). Can we pay everything due in the next month, even in a bad scenario? Answer: forecasts of customer behaviour plus a buffer of assets that turn into cash fast.
    • Market risk (B5.2). How much could the trading book lose on a bad day? Answer: Value at Risk and expected shortfall.
    • Pricing and hedging (B5.3). A fixed-rate loan book loses value when rates rise. Swaps offset this.

    All three are forecasting under uncertainty, with heavy tails and short histories. A model fitted to five calm years will say deposits never fall more than 4% in a month; the one time they did, you were not in the data.

    Hold onto thisTreasury risk is about how fast and how many at once, not solvency on paper. A bank whose assets exceed its liabilities can still fail if it cannot turn them into cash in time. Run-off rates, VaR and duration each put a number on a different part of that question.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B5.1 · Liquidity forecasting and asset-liability management

    The maturity ladder

    Asset-liability management (ALM) puts every asset and liability into time buckets by when its cash arrives. Inflows minus outflows in a bucket is the gap. A bank funding 25-year mortgages with 1-month deposits has a large negative short-term gap and must keep rolling funding. Two risks sit side by side:

    • Liquidity risk: no cash when a payment is due. Measured by cash-flow gaps and buffers.
    • Interest-rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB): loan and deposit values and income move with rates.

    The regulatory yardsticks

    Basel III sets two liquidity ratios, which the CBUAE and SAMA apply in their own versions.

    MeasureHorizonIdeaIn short
    LCR liquidity coverage ratio30 days, acute stressSurvive a month of run-off without selling anything illiquid.Liquid assets ÷ net 30-day outflows, at least 100%.
    NSFR net stable funding ratioOne year, structuralFund long assets with long, stable money.Stable funding available ÷ required, at least 100%.

    Reading the LCR. It asks: could the bank survive 30 days of a run using only its liquid buffer? Each outflow is a balance times a prescribed run-off rate (5% of stable retail, 100% of financial-institution deposits), and inflows count only up to 75% of outflows. In Simulation 1, a 30bn buffer against 25.25bn net outflow gives 119%. The ratio does not say the run-off rates are right: they are the regulator's, not the bank's.

    The bank's own stress tests and early warning need a forecast of real customer behaviour. That is where the data science sits.

    What is actually forecast

    • Non-maturity deposits (NMDs). A current account is due on demand, yet much of it stays for years, like a tap that is always on though it could be shut. Treasury splits it into a sticky core and a volatile part by customer type, and estimates the core's behavioural life. That sets how long the bank can fund loans with these deposits.
    • Prepayment, early withdrawal and drawdowns. Mortgages repay early when rates fall; term deposits are broken when rates rise; corporates draw credit lines in a crisis, when the bank can least afford it. Survival analysis (F2.8) with rate incentives fits the first two.
    • Daily cash and payment flows. Branch and ATM cash, salary days, Ramadan and Eid peaks. Classic time series (F9.1) with calendars, including the Islamic one, which drifts 10 to 11 days earlier each year.

    Simulation 2 is a statistics lesson (F2.1 and F2.2). Judging a "once in a hundred" event from three years is like judging a coin from two flips, and overlapping windows fake a large sample. So:

    • Segment customers. A government deposit does not behave like a salary account.
    • Use scenarios and expert judgement for the tail; use data for the body: seasonality, trend, the typical week.
    • Quote intervals, not single numbers, and stress the worst period you have upward.

    Where ML genuinely helps

    Short-horizon, high-volume forecasting: tomorrow's cash by branch and ATM, payment peaks, early signs a large depositor will move. Score with F7.5 metrics, preferably quantile loss, since a treasurer cares about the upper quantile of cash need, not the mean. For the tail, ML adds little: the training data contain no tail.

    Always check what the forecast feeds. A regulatory ratio uses the regulator's rates, so your model is an early-warning supplement.

    B5.2 · Market risk: VaR and expected shortfall

    The trading book (bonds, FX, equities, derivatives) is repriced daily. Market risk asks: how much could it lose in a bad but not impossible scenario?

    Value at Risk (VaR) at 99% is a flood marker on a wall: "one-day VaR is AED 5m" means we expect to lose less than 5m on 99 days in 100. On the 100th we lose at least 5m, and VaR says nothing about how much more. Raising it from 5m to 6m means the 1-in-100 day has moved out, not that the worst case is 6m.

    Expected shortfall (ES) answers the missing question: the average loss on the days VaR is breached. VaR is where the tail starts, ES is how deep it goes. For well-behaved (normal) losses, 99% VaR and 97.5% ES come out about equal, which is why regulators could switch between them without a capital shock.

    Three ways to compute it

    MethodHowStrengthWeakness
    ParametricAssume normal returns; VaR is a multiple of the standard deviation.Fast, explainable.Tails far too thin; poor for options.
    Historical simulationReprice today's portfolio under each of the last 250 to 500 daily moves; take the quantile.No distribution assumed; real fat tails.Future looks like the window. Calm window, low VaR.
    Monte CarloSimulate many scenarios from a fitted model and reprice in full.Handles complex derivatives.Only as good as the model; slow.

    Why regulators moved to expected shortfall

    • VaR ignores loss size beyond the threshold: two books with the same 99% VaR can hide very different disasters.
    • VaR can penalise diversification: two portfolios combined can show a higher VaR than the sum of their parts. ES always rewards it.

    The Basel trading-book rules (FRTB) therefore use 97.5% ES.

    Backtesting. Count the days the loss beat VaR (exceptions). At 99% over 250 days expect about 2.5. The Basel traffic light: up to 4 green, 5 to 9 yellow (higher capital multiplier), 10 or more red. Green means breaches were as frequent as expected, not that they were small.

    AI/ML helps with volatility forecasts and fast pricers, but a model trained on calm data gives a small VaR. That is procyclical: VaR falls in calm times, allowing more risk, then jumps in the crash. Regulators require stressed calibration.

    B5.3 · Pricing and hedging intuition

    Four ideas, not Black-Scholes.

    1. A price is a discounted cash flow. A bond's price is its future payments, each shrunk by how far away it is. Yields up, price down. That is why rising rates hurt fixed-rate loans.

    2. Duration is the first-order sensitivity. It roughly gives the percentage price change for a one-point yield move: a 5-year 4.5% bond has a duration of about 4.4, so a one-point rise costs about 4.4% of value. Convexity is the small correction. Banks track DV01, the AED change per one basis point. Duration is not the maturity.

    3. No arbitrage fixes forwards. A forward is not a forecast; it is what you could lock in today by borrowing and lending. With the dirham pegged at 3.6725, forward points are almost purely the interest differential: with invented 3-month rates of AED 4.0% and USD 4.3%, the forward sits about 27 pips below spot. Do not read it as an expected devaluation.

    4. Hedging is replication. A bank that pays fixed and receives floating on an interest-rate swap gains when rates rise, offsetting losses on fixed-rate assets. Size the swap so its DV01 cancels the book's. It is never perfect: curve shifts are not parallel and deposit behaviour changes with rates. The residual is basis risk.

    Islamic banks use Shariah-compliant structures instead of conventional swaps. The sensitivities are the same; the legal form differs.

    The banking-book measure: economic value of equity

    EVE is the change in the present value of assets minus liabilities when rates move. Basel prescribes six shocks (parallel, steepener, flattener, short-rate); a bank is an "outlier" if EVE falls by more than 15% of Tier 1 capital in any one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A deposit model trained on calm years

      Core deposits look 90% stable because nothing happened in the sample, and overlapping windows make about 25 independent observations look like 750.

      Spot itAsk which stress episodes are in the window and how the estimate changes without them.
    • Averaging heterogeneous depositors

      One bank-wide run-off rate hides that 15% of funding is a few large, fast-moving depositors.

      Spot itAsk for the concentration table: top-20 depositors' share and run-off history.
    • Quoting VaR as the worst case

      "We will not lose more than AED 5m" really means exceeded one day in a hundred, by an unknown amount.

      Spot itAsk for expected shortfall and the largest backtest exceptions beside VaR.
    • A calm window or a normal assumption in VaR

      Two quiet years, or thin normal tails, give a low VaR just before the regime changes.

      Spot itCheck window dates against known stress periods; ask for stressed VaR or ES.
    • Hedging only the first-order risk

      A DV01-neutral hedge fails for large moves (convexity) and non-parallel shifts (curve risk).

      Spot itAsk for all prescribed shocks, including steepening and flattening.
    • Static behavioural assumptions

      Deposits are assumed to behave the same after rates rise 3 points, but customers move to higher-yield products and the core shrinks.

      Spot itAsk whether run-off depends on the rate level.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A bank's liquid assets exactly equal its net 30-day outflows. What is its LCR, and what does a fall from 119% to 105% tell you?

    The LCR is 100%, the minimum: the buffer just covers a month of stressed net outflows. Falling from 119% to 105% means most of the spare cushion has gone, even though the bank still passes.

    2. Why does a 99th-percentile run-off estimated from three years of daily data deserve little trust?

    The tail rests on very few independent events: about 25 independent 30-day windows in three years, so the 99th percentile is the worst one or two. If no stress episode is in the sample, the estimate says nothing about stress.

    3. How do 99% VaR and 97.5% expected shortfall compare when losses are normal, and what changes when tails are fat?

    They come out nearly equal, by design. With fat tails, ES pulls ahead because it counts how deep the tail goes while VaR only marks where it starts.

    4. Two portfolios each have 99% VaR of AED 10m. Which is riskier?

    VaR cannot say. The one with the larger expected shortfall has worse losses beyond the threshold. Ask for ES, the largest historical losses and the distribution shape.

    5. You have 250 days of backtest and a 99% VaR. You see 7 exceptions. What is the zone, and what are the possible causes?

    Yellow (5 to 9); expected is 2.5. Causes: volatility rose and the model reacted slowly, tails fatter than assumed, missing risk factors, or bad luck. Investigate before calling the model broken.

    6. A bond has a duration of 4.4 and yields rise by half a percentage point. Roughly how does its value change, and is the true loss larger or smaller than that?

    Duration is roughly the percentage price change for a one-point move, so about 2.2% down. Convexity makes the true loss slightly smaller.

    7. AED 3-month rate 4.0%, USD 4.3%, spot 3.6725. Is the 3-month forward above or below spot, and why?

    Below. AED is the lower-interest currency, so the forward sits under spot. It implies no view on the peg, only arbitrage.

    8. Why is a DV01-neutral hedge not a perfect hedge?

    DV01 matches only the first-order effect of a small parallel move. Large moves expose convexity, non-parallel moves expose curve risk, and deposit behaviour changes with rates. The residual is basis risk.
