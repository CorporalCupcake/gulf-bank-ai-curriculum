    Treasury Risk: Liquidity, Asset-Liability Management, VaR and Hedging

    How a bank makes sure it can always pay what it owes, how it forecasts deposits that have no maturity, how trading risk becomes a single number (and why that number lies in the tail), and enough pricing and hedging to hold your own with treasury.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTreasury is the part of the bank that manages the gap between what it owes (mostly deposits that can leave at any time) and what it holds (loans that cannot), and every number it uses (a run-off rate, a VaR, a rate sensitivity) is an estimate from limited history whose tail you must question.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank borrows short and lends long. Think of a hotel that rents rooms by the night but sells long leases on them. Customers can withdraw deposits today; the bank has lent most of that money out for five to twenty-five years. If enough depositors ask at once, the loans cannot be sold in time. Healthy banks have failed this way.

    Treasury manages that gap. It has three jobs, one per topic below.

    • Liquidity (B5.1). Can we pay everything due in the next day, week or month, even in a bad scenario? Answer: forecasts of customer behaviour plus a buffer of assets that turn into cash fast.
    • Market risk (B5.2). Trading positions gain and lose as prices move. How much could we lose on a bad day? Answer: Value at Risk and expected shortfall.
    • Pricing and hedging (B5.3). A fixed-rate loan book loses value when rates rise. Treasury offsets this with swaps. You need to know how a price is built from rates and why a hedge works.

    All three are forecasting under uncertainty, with heavy tails and short histories. A tree model fitted to five calm years will confidently say deposits never fall more than 4% in a month. The one time they did, you were not in the data.

    Hold onto thisTreasury risk is about how fast and how many at once, not solvency on paper. A bank whose assets exceed its liabilities can still fail if it cannot turn them into cash in time. Run-off rates, VaR and duration each put a number on a different part of that question.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B5.1 · Liquidity forecasting and asset-liability management

    The maturity ladder

    Asset-liability management (ALM) places every asset and liability into time buckets by when its cash arrives: overnight, a week, a month, three months, a year, five years and beyond. Inflows minus outflows in a bucket is the gap. A bank funding 25-year mortgages with 1-month deposits has a large negative short-term gap and must keep rolling funding. That is normal; ALM decides how big the gaps may be. Two risks pull in different directions:

    • Liquidity risk: no cash when a payment is due. Measured by cash-flow gaps and buffers.
    • Interest-rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB): loan and deposit values and income move with rates. Measured by earnings and economic-value sensitivity (below).

    The regulatory yardsticks

    Basel III sets two liquidity ratios; the CBUAE and SAMA apply versions of them. Check the local rulebook for exact calibration.

    Each outflow is a balance times a prescribed run-off rate: 5% of stable retail, 40% of non-operational corporate, 100% of financial-institution deposits. Inflows count only up to 75% of outflows. In Simulation 1: 37.25bn outflows, inflows capped at 27.9bn, net 25.25bn; 30bn of buffer gives 119%.

    Reading the LCR. It answers: could the bank survive 30 days of a run using only its liquid buffer? 100% means the buffer exactly covers net outflows; 119% means 19% spare. Moving from 119% to 105% means a fifth of the cushion is gone. It does not say the run-off rates are right: they are the regulator's, not the bank's.

    Beyond the regulatory ratio, the bank's own stress tests, funds-transfer pricing and early warning need a forecast of real customer behaviour. That is where the data science sits.

    What is actually forecast

    • Non-maturity deposits (NMDs). A current account is due on demand, yet much of it stays for years, like a tap that is always on though it could be shut. Treasury splits it into a core (sticky) and a volatile part, by customer type (salary, small business, government-related, corporate operating balances), and estimates the core's behavioural life. That number sets how long the bank can fund loans with these deposits.
    • Prepayment and early withdrawal. Mortgages repay early when rates fall; term deposits are broken when rates rise. Use survival analysis (F2.8) with rate incentives as covariates.
    • Drawdowns of committed lines. Corporates draw undrawn facilities in a crisis, when the bank can least afford it. Forecast per segment.
    • Daily cash and payment flows. Branch and ATM cash, salary days, month-end, Ramadan and Eid peaks, intraday payment timing. Classic time series (F9.1) with calendars, including the Islamic one, which drifts 10 to 11 days earlier each year.

    Simulation 2 is a statistics lesson (F2.1 and F2.2). Judging a "once in a hundred" event from three years is like judging a coin from two flips. Overlapping windows (a 30-day decline starting Monday and Tuesday share 29 days) fake a large sample; the tail has only a handful of independent events. So:

    • Segment customers. A large government deposit does not behave like a salary account.
    • Use scenarios and expert judgement for the tail; use data for the body: seasonality, trend, the typical week.
    • Quote intervals (block bootstrap, not single days) and compare with the regulatory rate.
    • Accept that data cannot show a loss of confidence. Borrow history from other banks or countries, or the bank's worst period, and stress it upward.

    Where ML genuinely helps

    Short-horizon, high-volume forecasting: tomorrow's cash by branch and ATM, next week's payment peaks, corporate receivable timing, early signs a large depositor will move. Gradient boosting and calendar-aware time-series models beat naive averages. Score them with F7.5 metrics, preferably quantile loss, since a treasurer cares about the upper quantile of cash need, not the mean. For the tail, ML adds little and can mislead: the training data contain no tail.

    Always check what the forecast feeds. A regulatory ratio uses the regulator's rates, so your model is an early-warning supplement. Funds-transfer pricing (the internal price of money charged to each business) moves profit between departments, so expect arguments.

    B5.2 · Market risk: VaR and expected shortfall

    The trading book (bonds, FX, equities, derivatives) is repriced daily. Market risk asks: how much could it lose over the next 10 days in a bad but not impossible scenario?

    Value at Risk (VaR) at confidence c is the loss not exceeded with probability c. Like a flood marker on a wall: "one-day 99% VaR is AED 5m" means we expect to lose less than 5m on 99 days in 100. On the 100th we lose at least 5m, and VaR says nothing about how much more. Raising it from 5m to 6m means the 1-in-100 day has moved out, not that the worst case is 6m.

    Expected shortfall (ES), or conditional VaR, answers the missing question: the average loss on the days VaR is breached. Mental model: VaR is where the tail starts, ES is how deep it goes.

    VaRc=−q1−c(P&L)ESc=−E[P&L|P&L≤q1−c]

    q1−c is the (1 − c) quantile of profit and loss, e.g. the 1st percentile for c = 99%. For a normal distribution with standard deviation σ, VaR at 99% is 2.33σ and ES at 97.5% is 2.34σ. They match by design, so regulators could move from 99% VaR to 97.5% ES with no capital change for well-behaved risks.

    Three ways to compute it

    MethodHowStrengthWeakness
    Parametric (variance-covariance)Assume normal returns; combine sensitivities with a covariance matrix; VaR is a multiple of the standard deviation.Fast, simple, explainable.Normal tails far too thin; poor for options.
    Historical simulationReprice today's portfolio under each of the last 250 to 500 daily moves; take the quantile.No distribution assumed; real correlations and fat tails that occurred.Future looks like the window. Calm window, low VaR. At 99% with 250 days it rests on 2 or 3 days.
    Monte CarloSimulate many scenarios from a fitted model and reprice in full.Handles complex derivatives and non-normal models.Only as good as the model; slow; hard to explain to a validator.

    Why regulators moved to expected shortfall

    • VaR ignores loss size beyond the threshold: two books with the same 99% VaR can hide very different disasters.
    • VaR is not sub-additive: two portfolios combined can have a VaR above the sum of their separate VaRs, penalising diversification. ES is coherent and always rewards it.

    The Basel "Fundamental Review of the Trading Book" (FRTB) therefore uses 97.5% ES, with liquidity horizons of 10 to 120 days by how fast a risk factor can be exited, and a stricter test for "non-modellable" factors lacking real price data.

    Backtesting. Each day, compare actual P&L with the VaR forecast; a loss beyond VaR is an exception. At 99% over 250 days expect 2.5. The Basel traffic light: up to 4 green, 5 to 9 yellow (higher capital multiplier), 10 or more red. ES cannot be backtested as simply, so supervisors keep the 99% VaR backtest and add checks that the P&L used is attributable to the modelled risk factors. A green light means the threshold was breached about as often as expected, not that the breaches were small.

    AI/ML in market risk: better volatility and correlation forecasts, generative models of joint market moves, fast neural-network pricers for full revaluation, anomaly detection on P&L explain. The caution is the same: a model trained on calm data gives a small VaR. This is procyclical: VaR falls in calm times, allowing more risk, then jumps in the crash. Regulators require stressed calibration, with part of the data from a stress period.

    B5.3 · Pricing and hedging intuition

    You need four ideas, not Black-Scholes.

    1. A price is a discounted cash flow. A bond's price is its coupons and principal, each divided by (1 + yield) raised to the years until it arrives. Like a see-saw: yields up, price down. This is why rising rates hurt fixed-rate loans: the bank locked in a below-market rate.

    2. Duration is the first-order sensitivity. For a small yield shift Δy, price change ≈ −D × Δy × price, where D is modified duration (roughly the weighted average time to cash flow). A 5-year 4.5% bond has D about 4.4, so a one-point rise costs about 4.4% of value. Convexity is the correction: price falls less than the line says when yields rise, gains more when they fall. Banks track DV01, the AED change per one basis point (0.01%). Misreading to avoid: duration is not the maturity.

    3. No arbitrage fixes forwards. A forward is not a forecast; it is what you could lock in today by borrowing and lending. Covered interest parity: F = spot × (1 + domestic rate × T) ÷ (1 + foreign rate × T). With the dirham pegged at 3.6725, forward points are almost purely the interest differential. Invented rates, three-month AED 4.0%, USD 4.3%: F = 3.6725 × 1.0100 ÷ 1.01075 ≈ 3.6698, about 27 pips below spot. The same logic prices the riyal against its 3.75 peg. A large deviation from parity signals stress in the basis, not an expected devaluation.

    4. Hedging is replication. An interest-rate swap exchanges fixed for floating. A bank that pays fixed and receives floating gains when rates rise, offsetting losses on fixed-rate assets. Choose the notional so the swap's DV01 cancels the book's net DV01. It is never perfect: convexity differs, curve shifts are not parallel, and deposit behaviour changes with rates. The residual is basis risk.

    Options add one idea: price depends on volatility, and can be hedged by holding delta units of the underlying (the option's sensitivity to the underlying price), rebalanced as delta changes; that cost is gamma. Put-call parity (call minus put equals discounted forward minus strike) sanity-checks any quote.

    Islamic banks cannot use conventional swaps. They use Shariah-compliant structures such as profit-rate swaps on murabaha or wakala contracts, or unilateral promises (wa'ad), standardised in ISDA/IIFM master agreements. The sensitivities are the same; the legal form differs and Shariah board approval is required.

    The banking-book measure: economic value of equity

    EVE is the change in present value of assets minus liabilities when rates move. Basel (BCBS 368, 2016) prescribes six shocks: parallel up, parallel down, steepener, flattener, short-rate up, short-rate down. A bank is an "outlier" if EVE falls by more than 15% of Tier 1 capital in any one. The standard also asks for 12-month net interest income (NII) sensitivity.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A deposit model trained on calm years

      Core deposits look 90% stable because nothing happened in the sample.

      Spot itAsk which stress episodes are in the training window and how the estimate changes without them. Compare with the regulatory run-off.
    • Overlapping windows treated as independent

      A 99th-percentile 30-day outflow from 750 overlapping windows looks like 750 observations. It is about 25.

      Spot itAsk for the number of independent windows and block-bootstrap intervals.
    • Averaging heterogeneous depositors

      One bank-wide run-off rate hides that 15% of funding is a few large, fast-moving depositors.

      Spot itAsk for the concentration table: top-20 depositors' share and run-off history.
    • Quoting VaR as the worst case

      "We will not lose more than AED 5m" really means exceeded one day in a hundred, by an unknown amount.

      Spot itAsk for expected shortfall and the largest backtest exceptions beside VaR.
    • A short, calm window in historical simulation

      Two quiet years give a low VaR just before the regime changes.

      Spot itCheck window dates against known stress periods; ask for stressed VaR or ES.
    • Normal assumption on fat-tailed data

      Parametric VaR understates tail losses: few exceptions when calm, many in crisis.

      Spot itCompare return kurtosis with 3 and count exceptions in the worst year.
    • Hedging only the first-order risk

      A DV01-neutral hedge fails for large moves (convexity) and non-parallel shifts (curve risk).

      Spot itAsk for all prescribed shocks, including steepening and flattening.
    • Static behavioural assumptions

      Deposits are assumed to behave the same after rates rise 3 points. Customers move to term and higher-yield products, and the core shrinks.

      Spot itAsk whether deposit betas (the share of a market-rate change passed to customers) and run-off depend on the rate level.
    • Forecasting the mean, buffering for the mean

      The cash forecast is right on average; the ATM network runs dry on salary day.

      Spot itCheck the forecast is scored on the upper quantile treasury uses.
    • Confusing the forward with a prediction

      "The forward says the dirham will weaken." It reflects the interest differential.

      Spot itReproduce the forward from the two rates. If it matches, it holds no information about expectations.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A bank has AED 24bn of liquid assets, 40bn of stressed outflows and 16bn of contractual inflows. What is its LCR?

    The inflow cap is 75% × 40 = 30bn; 16bn is below it, so all counts. Net outflow = 40 − 16 = 24bn. LCR = 24 ÷ 24 = 100%, exactly the minimum.

    2. Why does a 99th-percentile run-off estimated from three years of daily data deserve little trust?

    The tail rests on very few independent events: about 25 independent 30-day windows in three years, so the 99th percentile is the worst one or two. Overlapping windows share days and add no information. If no stress episode is in the sample, the estimate says nothing about stress.

    3. Daily P&L is normal with a standard deviation of AED 3m. What is the 99% one-day VaR? And the 97.5% ES?

    VaR = 2.326 × 3 ≈ AED 7.0m. ES = 3 × φ(1.96) ÷ 0.025 = 3 × 0.0584 ÷ 0.025 ≈ AED 7.0m (2.338σ). Under normality they are nearly equal, by design. They diverge under fat tails.

    4. Two portfolios each have 99% VaR of AED 10m. Which is riskier?

    VaR cannot say. The one with the larger expected shortfall has worse losses beyond the threshold. Ask for ES, the largest historical losses and the distribution shape.

    5. You have 250 days of backtest and a 99% VaR. You see 7 exceptions. What is the zone, and what are the possible causes?

    Yellow (5 to 9); expected is 2.5. Causes: volatility rose and the model reacted slowly, tails fatter than assumed, missing positions or risk factors, or bad luck. Investigate before calling the model broken; the supervisor may raise the capital multiplier.

    6. A bond has modified duration 4.4 and price AED 100m. Yields rise 50bp. Approximate price change? Is the true loss larger or smaller?

    −4.4 × 0.005 × 100m ≈ −AED 2.2m. Convexity makes the true loss slightly smaller than the linear estimate, by a small amount for 50bp.

    7. AED 3-month rate 4.0%, USD 4.3%, spot 3.6725. Is the 3-month forward above or below spot, and why?

    Below, about 3.6698. AED is the lower-interest currency, so a forward buyer of dollars pays less than spot to offset the interest dollars earn. It implies no view on the peg, only arbitrage.

    8. Why is a DV01-neutral hedge not a perfect hedge?

    DV01 matches only the first-order effect of a small parallel move. Large moves expose convexity, non-parallel moves expose curve risk, and deposits' behavioural life changes with rates. The residual is basis risk and model risk.
