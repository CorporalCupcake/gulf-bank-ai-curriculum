    Treasury Risk: Liquidity, ALM and VaR

    Part 1

    What treasury risk is

    In one sentenceTreasury risk asks whether the bank can pay what it owes, how much it could lose on a bad day, and how much a rate rise would hurt.

    A bank borrows short and lends long, like a hotel that rents rooms by the night but sells long leases. If enough depositors ask for their money at once, the loans cannot be sold in time. Healthy banks have failed this way, so being solvent on paper is not enough: the question is how fast and how many at once.

    Treasury has three jobs:

    • Liquidity. Can we pay everything due in the next month, even in a bad scenario?
    • Market risk. How much could the trading book lose on a bad day?
    • Pricing and hedging. A fixed-rate loan book loses value when rates rise. How do we offset that?

    How it differs from credit risk

    Credit risk is about borrowers not paying back. Treasury risk is about timing and prices.

    Part 2

    How each piece works

    Liquidity and the maturity ladder

    Asset-liability management (ALM) sorts every asset and liability into time buckets by when its cash arrives. Inflows minus outflows in a bucket is the gap. A bank funding 25-year mortgages with 1-month deposits has a big negative short-term gap and must keep rolling its funding.

    Basel III sets two liquidity ratios, which the CBUAE and SAMA apply in their own versions:

    • LCR (liquidity coverage ratio). Liquid assets ÷ net 30-day outflows in a stress. It must be at least 100%. It asks: could we survive a month of run-off using only the buffer?
    • NSFR (net stable funding ratio). Stable funding available ÷ stable funding required, over one year. At least 100%. It asks: are long assets funded by long, stable money?

    Each outflow is a balance times a prescribed run-off rate, for example 5% of stable retail deposits but 100% of deposits from other financial institutions.

    What data science forecasts

    • Non-maturity deposits. A current account is due on demand, yet much of it stays for years. Split it into a sticky core and a volatile part.
    • Prepayments and drawdowns. Mortgages repay early when rates fall. Companies draw credit lines in a crisis.

    Machine learning helps most for short-horizon forecasts such as tomorrow's cash by ATM. For the tail it adds little: three calm years hold only about 25 independent 30-day windows.

    Market risk: VaR and expected shortfall

    Value at Risk (VaR) at 99% is like a flood marker on a wall. "One-day VaR is AED 5m" means we expect to lose less than 5m on 99 days in 100. On the 100th we lose at least 5m, and VaR says nothing about how much more.

    Expected shortfall (ES) answers the missing question: the average loss on the days VaR is breached.

    There are three ways to compute VaR:

    • Parametric. Assume normal returns. Fast and explainable, but the tails are far too thin.
    • Historical simulation. Reprice today's portfolio under the last 250 to 500 daily moves. Real tails, but a calm window gives a low VaR.
    • Monte Carlo. Simulate many scenarios from a fitted model. Handles derivatives, but is only as good as the model.

    Regulators moved to ES (97.5% in the Basel trading-book rules) because VaR ignores how bad losses are beyond the line, and can penalise diversification.

    Pricing and hedging

    1. A price is discounted cash flow. Yields up, price down. That is why rising rates hurt fixed-rate loans.
    2. Duration is the first-order sensitivity: roughly the percentage price change for a one-point yield move. A 5-year 4.5% bond has a duration near 4.4, so a one-point rise costs about 4.4%. It is not the same as maturity.
    3. A forward is not a forecast. It is what you could lock in today. With the dirham pegged, forward points mostly reflect the interest gap, not an expected devaluation.
    4. Hedging is replication. Paying fixed and receiving floating on a swap gains when rates rise, offsetting loan losses. Size it so the two sensitivities cancel. What is left over is basis risk.
    Part 3

    How to read the numbers

    LCR of 119%
    A 30bn buffer against 25.25bn of net stressed outflows. It passes, with a cushion. A fall to 105% means most of the cushion has gone, even though it still passes.
    One-day 99% VaR of AED 5m
    Expect to beat it about 2.5 days in 250. Moving it to 6m means the 1-in-100 day has moved out, not that the worst case is 6m.
    Backtest with 250 days
    Up to 4 exceptions is green, 5 to 9 yellow (more capital), 10 or more red. Green means breaches were as frequent as expected, not that they were small.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    MeasureAnswersBlind spot
    LCR / NSFRCan we survive 30 days, and is funding stable for a year?Run-off rates are the regulator's, not yours
    VaRWhere does the bad-day tail start?Says nothing about how deep it goes
    Expected shortfallHow bad is the average tail day?Harder to backtest
    Duration / DV01How much does value move per rate point?Misses big moves and curve twists
    • Strong at: giving each risk a number a committee can track.
    • Weak at: tails. All of them lean on history, and history is short and calm.

    Treasury also checks EVE (economic value of equity): the change in the present value of assets minus liabilities when rates move. Basel prescribes six shocks, and a bank is an outlier if EVE falls by more than 15% of Tier 1 capital in any one.

    Show the formulas

    LCR = liquid assets ÷ (stressed outflows − capped inflows). Inflows count only up to 75% of outflows.

    Price change ≈ −duration × change in yield. DV01 is the AED change for one basis point (0.01%).

    In banking, a model fitted to calm years will say deposits never fall more than a few per cent in a month. Ask which stress episodes are in the data, and ask for stressed VaR or ES beside every VaR.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Training a deposit model on calm years

      Core deposits look 90% stable because nothing happened. Overlapping windows make 25 independent observations look like 750.

    • Quoting VaR as the worst case

      "We will not lose more than AED 5m" really means "exceeded about one day in a hundred, by an unknown amount".

    • One bank-wide run-off rate

      It hides that a few large, fast-moving depositors may make up 15% of funding. Ask for the concentration table.

    • Hedging only the first-order risk

      A duration-neutral hedge fails for large moves and non-parallel curve shifts. Check all prescribed shocks.

    1. Liquid assets exactly equal net 30-day outflows. What is the LCR?

    100%, the minimum. The buffer just covers a month of stress.

    2. Why distrust a 99th-percentile run-off from three years of data?

    It rests on about 25 independent 30-day windows, so it is the worst one or two. With no stress episode in the sample it says nothing about stress.

    3. Two books both have 99% VaR of AED 10m. Which is riskier?

    VaR cannot say. The one with the larger expected shortfall has the deeper tail.

    4. Why do rising rates hurt a fixed-rate loan book?

    Its price is discounted future payments. Higher yields shrink their present value.

    5. What does a pay-fixed swap do for the bank?

    It gains when rates rise, offsetting the loss on fixed-rate assets. Size it so the sensitivities cancel. Basis risk remains.
