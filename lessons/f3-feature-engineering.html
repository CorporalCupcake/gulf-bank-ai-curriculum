    Feature Engineering: Behaviour, Encoding, Missingness and Scale

    How to turn a customer's raw history into numbers a model can use: what they did lately, how that compares with their own normal, how to encode a merchant list without cheating, what a blank field is telling you, and which models care about units.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceShow the model behaviour relative to each customer's own baseline, encode categories without leaking the label, treat "missing" as possible signal, and match scaling to the model.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    At 2:10 a.m. a debit card buys AED 380 of electronics in another emirate. Fraud? One row can't say: the amount is ordinary, the merchant real, the card valid.

    Add context. In the last 40 minutes the same card made five other purchases at five merchants, three of them new to this customer. Over the previous 90 days it averaged 1.4 purchases a day. Now it looks suspicious, and none of that was in the single row.

    That is feature engineering. The raw table has one row per event. The model needs one row per decision, with columns summarising the history around that decision. Most of the accuracy in a bank's fraud, credit and churn models comes from this step, not from the algorithm. Four ideas run through the lesson:

    • Behaviour beats attributes. Age and segment change slowly. Recent activity, relative to the customer's own normal, carries the signal. AED 9,000 of spend is alarming for one customer and a quiet Tuesday for another.
    • Encoding can cheat without you noticing. A merchant seen once, in a fraud case, looks "100% fraud". Encode that naively and the model memorises training data, then fails on new data.
    • A blank is data. Leaving income empty often relates to income, so to risk. Filling it with the average throws that away.
    • Units matter to some models. Put balance in dirhams beside "transactions per month" and a distance-based model hears almost only the balance.
    Hold onto thisEvery feature answers: "what would we have known about this customer, at this moment, that helps predict what happens next?" "At this moment" keeps the model honest (F3.1). "About this customer" is why ratios to their own baseline work.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Recency, frequency, monetary value and time since last event

    Think of a gym member. Visited yesterday, three times a week, on the premium plan: safe. Last visit five weeks ago: drifting out. RFM captures this per customer, as of a chosen date:

    • Recency: time since the last event (transaction, login, salary credit).
    • Frequency: number of events in a window (30, 90, 365 days).
    • Monetary value: total and average amount in the same windows.

    It works because behaviour persists and decays. Recency shows where the customer is on the path to going quiet, frequency how strong the habit was, monetary value what is at stake. Recent, frequent and high-value is healthy. Long gap, falling frequency and shrinking value is an attrition risk. The misreading to avoid: a long gap is only worrying relative to the customer's own rhythm.

    Four rules for time since last event:

    • Fix the reference time. Compute it as of the decision (application date, transaction time, month-end), never as of "today" when you build the table. This is the point-in-time rule from F3.1.
    • Compare with the customer's rhythm. Thirty days of silence is normal for a monthly user and alarming for a daily one. Use recency divided by the customer's typical gap.
    • It is censored. An event not yet seen means the true gap is "at least this long". Survival analysis handles this properly (F2.8). In a feature table, use log1p(days) or a cap.
    • Never-seen needs a missing value. No events means no "last event". Use a blank plus an indicator, not zero days, which would mean "just transacted".

    The BG/NBD models (Fader, Hardie and Lee, 2005) show how far recency and frequency alone go: they estimate each customer's probability of still being "alive" and their expected future transactions.

    Rolling windows, velocity and ratios to the customer's own baseline

    A rolling window feature counts or sums events in the last w minutes, hours or days before the decision. Velocity is the same thing named for speed: transactions per hour, AED out in 15 minutes, failed PINs in 10 minutes. Three choices decide whether it works:

    • Which window. Too short and a shopping trip looks like an attack. Too long and a burst drowns in normal days. Match the attack: card testing takes minutes, account takeover hours, mule activity days. Build several (5 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days) and let the model pick.
    • What to count. Distinct counts (merchants, countries, devices) are often sharper than raw counts. Add "first time" flags: new merchant, new country, new device.
    • Relative to what. The choice that matters most: compare the window with the customer's own longer history.

    Think of a smoke alarm tuned to each room. A kitchen needs a higher threshold than a bedroom. The ratio to own baseline does this per customer:

    ratio=events in the last w(customer's usual rate)×w+c

    Top: what happened in the window. Bottom: what you would expect from this customer over that time, from their past 60 to 90 days, plus a small constant c so a near-dormant customer doesn't divide by almost zero. 1 is business as usual, 4 is four times normal; going from 2 to 4 means the burst doubled relative to habit. The baseline must come from before the window, or the burst contaminates its own yardstick.

    A raw count of "six transactions in three hours" flags the heavy card user every Friday and misses the quiet pensioner whose card suddenly buzzes. Dividing by their own rate turns a population-wide threshold into a personal one. Simulation 1 shows it. Other forms of the same idea:

    • Z-scores against own history: (this week's spend − own mean) ÷ own standard deviation. Sharper for steady customers, fragile with short history. A robust version uses the median and median absolute deviation.
    • Exponentially weighted counts: weight each event by 0.5^(age / half-life). No cliff when an event leaves the window.
    • Trends: 30-day spend over the previous 30 days, or the slope of monthly balance. Falling salary credits and balances are early signs of stress and attrition.
    • Cyclic time: 23:00 is next to 00:00, so encode hour and weekday as sine and cosine. For fraud, model each customer's usual hours (Bahnsen et al., 2016, use a von Mises distribution) and ask how unusual this hour is.
    • Ratios between features: spend over income, balance over limit (utilisation), debt service over salary. Models struggle to learn division, so supplying the ratio is often the biggest single gain.

    In SQL this is a window function over the customer's own rows:

    SELECT txn_id, card_id, txn_ts, amount_aed,
  COUNT(*) OVER w1h                       AS txn_count_1h,
  SUM(amount_aed) OVER w1h                AS spend_1h,
  AVG(amount_aed) OVER w90d_before        AS avg_amount_90d
FROM transactions
WINDOW
  w1h AS (PARTITION BY card_id ORDER BY txn_ts
          RANGE BETWEEN INTERVAL '1 hour' PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW),
  w90d_before AS (PARTITION BY card_id ORDER BY txn_ts
          RANGE BETWEEN INTERVAL '90 days' PRECEDING AND INTERVAL '1 day' PRECEDING);

    Two details carry the risk. The 90-day baseline stops a day before the transaction, so a burst can't inflate its own baseline. The one-hour window ends at CURRENT ROW: fine when scoring a transaction as it arrives, wrong for a feature predicting something after it. See F3.3 and F3.1.

    Three takeaways. The best window matches the attack's time scale, not the shortest or longest. The ratio wins where raw counts struggle, because a count can't tell a busy customer from a compromised one. And no window catches the first fraud in a burst, since no burst exists yet: velocity catches the repeat. Pair it with single-transaction features such as new merchant, new country or unusual amount for this customer.

    Encoding categories: one-hot, target encoding and high cardinality

    Models need numbers, but banking data is full of labels: merchant, MCC (merchant category code), branch, employer, device, country. What to do depends on cardinality, the number of distinct values.

    • Low (up to about 20), e.g. product or channel: one-hot encoding, one 0/1 column per value. For linear models drop one level (or regularise) so columns don't sum to a constant.
    • Ordered, e.g. risk grade A to E: integers 1 to 5 if steps are roughly even, otherwise one-hot. Never give unordered labels integer codes in a linear model: it would treat "Dubai = 3" as bigger than "Sharjah = 2".
    • High, e.g. merchants, employers, postcodes, device IDs: one-hot gives tens of thousands of mostly empty columns. Use one of these:
    MethodHow it worksStrengthRisk
    Group the rare onesKeep the top 50 merchants; map the rest to "other", or group by MCC or region.Simple, safe.Drops the long tail, where fraud often lives.
    Frequency encodingReplace each value with how often it appears.Never touches the label. Rarity is itself a signal.Different merchants with equal counts look the same.
    Target encodingReplace each value with its average outcome in training data (e.g. the merchant's fraud rate).One predictive column, any model.Leaks the label unless done carefully.
    HashingHash the label into one of, say, 1,024 buckets.Fixed size, handles new values.Unrelated values collide; opaque.
    Learned embeddingsA neural net learns a short vector per value (Guo and Berkhahn, 2016), as for words.Captures similarity; scales to millions.Needs data per value and a serving pipeline.
    Native handlingCatBoost's ordered target statistics; LightGBM's categorical splits.Best default for boosted trees; leakage handled inside.Tied to the library; rare values still need care.

    Target encoding is the most powerful and the most dangerous. If a merchant had a 3% fraud rate in training, every transaction there gets the value 0.03: a ready-made risk score. The trap is small samples. Think of a restaurant rated 5 stars by its only reviewer: the rating is mostly noise. A merchant with 2 transactions, one fraud, shows 50%. Worse, if you compute the encoding from the rows you train on, each row's own label is part of its own feature. A once-seen fraud merchant gets 1.0 exactly where the answer is 1. The model learns "high means fraud", looks brilliant in training, and fails on new data.

    Two fixes, used together. First, shrink small samples towards the overall rate:

    encc=nc·y¯c+m·y¯nc+m

    Smoothing (shrinkage). nc is the category's row count, ȳc its observed rate, ȳ the overall rate, m a strength you choose. It is a weighted average whose weight on the category grows with its sample size. A merchant with 2 transactions, 1 fraud, overall rate 3% and m = 20 gets (1 + 20 × 0.03) ÷ 22 = 7.3%, not 50%. One with 2,000 transactions barely moves. Larger m trusts the category less. It is the same idea as a Bayesian prior (F1.4) and ridge regression.

    Second, out-of-fold (cross-fitted) encoding: split training data into, say, five folds and encode each row from the other four, so its own label never reaches its own feature. For test or live data use the whole training set. scikit-learn's TargetEncoder does this when fitting (since 1.3).

    • Smoothing alone isn't enough. It reduces the leak; a singleton merchant still carries a share of its own label. Out-of-fold removes it. Simulation 2 shows the gap.
    • Time matters. Random folds aren't enough if merchants change or labels arrive late (fraud labels can take 30 to 90 days). Encode only from rows whose labels were known at the time.

    The gap appears because on training rows a singleton fraud merchant scores 1.0 and a singleton clean one 0.0, lining up with the labels by construction. Out-of-fold breaks that alignment. If cross-validation scores far exceed held-out scores and you used target encoding, suspect this first.

    A merchant first seen after training gets the global rate, the right fallback. But "never seen" is informative in fraud, so also give the model a count or frequency feature to tell "average merchant" from "unknown merchant".

    Missing values: why a blank is information

    Why a value is missing decides what you can safely do. Picture a survey with unanswered questions. The standard classification (Rubin, 1976) has three cases:

    • Missing completely at random (MCAR). Missingness is unrelated to anything. An outage wipes the field for whoever applied that afternoon. Rare. Dropping or averaging only loses information.
    • Missing at random (MAR). Missingness depends on columns you have. Under-25s skip "years at employer" more often, and you know their age. You can model the field from other columns.
    • Missing not at random (MNAR). Missingness depends on the missing value itself. Self-employed applicants leave income blank because it is low or irregular. Nothing observed fully corrects this, and the blank is itself a signal.

    Bank data is full of MNAR: a blank income, a bureau "no file", an address with no landlord. Consequences:

    • Filled-in values are a biased sample. If low earners skip income, the average of the rest overstates it (the selection problem from F2.6).
    • Mean imputation hides the signal. Probable low earners are scored as average.
    • Dropping incomplete rows is the same bias, worse. You train only on people who fill in forms, then score everyone.

    Toolkit, simple to sophisticated:

    • Add a missingness indicator. A 0/1 "income was blank" column beside the imputed value. Nearly free, lets the model use the blank, and the single most useful move for any column with more than a percent or two blank. It helps most when missingness is informative (Van Ness et al., 2023).
    • Impute sensibly. Median, not mean, for skewed amounts. Group medians (income by job category) beat a global one. Model-based imputation uses other columns. Multiple imputation gives honest uncertainty but is rare in production scoring.
    • Let the model handle it. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost learn which side of each split blanks go. A strong default, but check that the blank pattern matches between training and live.
    • Sentinel values such as −1 work only for trees and hide the missingness from readers.
    • Ask why. Blank because some products don't collect the field? Then it is a product flag. Blank because of a migration bug? Fix the data. Ask the data owner first.

    The indicator is safe only while the reason for the blank is stable. If the form makes income mandatory, the indicator goes quiet and model behaviour shifts. Track each feature's missing rate in production as a drift metric.

    The oracle line is the best any approach could do if the blank hid nothing. The gap to the others is the information lost. The indicator recovers only what the fact of being blank carries, not the income itself. That is the limit of MNAR: you can use the blank, but you can't un-hide what it hides.

    Scaling, transforms, and which models care

    Scale causes many silent failures. Comparing a person's height in metres with their salary in dirhams: any "distance" between two people is just the salary gap. The rule: a model that uses distances, dot products, gradients or penalties is affected by units. A model that only asks "is this value above a threshold?" is not.

    Model familyDoes scale matter?Why
    k-NN, k-means, clustering, RBF-kernel SVMStronglyBuilt on distances. A feature in tens of thousands (AED) drowns one in single digits (F1.1; Simulation 4).
    Neural networksStronglyInputs of very different sizes make the loss surface a long narrow valley (F1.3), break initialisation, and saturate activations.
    Penalised linear/logistic (ridge, lasso, elastic net)For the penaltyPredictions are unchanged by rescaling, but a feature in small units needs a large coefficient and is shrunk more. Standardise first or the penalty picks winners by units.
    Plain linear/logistic, no penaltyConvenience onlyFit is equivalent. Standardising makes coefficients comparable and the optimiser stable.
    PCA and variance-based methodsStronglyThe first component follows the feature with the biggest units.
    Trees, random forests, boosted treesNo"balance > 12,400" splits the same rows as "balance in thousands > 12.4". Any strictly increasing transform (log, root, rank) leaves splits unchanged.

    Common transformations:

    • Standardisation (z-score): subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. Default for neural nets and penalised models. Outliers hurt: one AED 50 million transfer stretches the scale for everyone.
    • Min–max to 0–1: simple, but one extreme value squashes the rest into a sliver.
    • Log (log1p): for amounts, balances and counts. Money is multiplicative: AED 100 vs 200 matters about as much as 10,000 vs 20,000. A right-skewed variable becomes roughly bell-shaped. Reach for this first on money fields.
    • Robust scaling: subtract the median, divide by the interquartile range. Outliers don't throw it.
    • Rank or quantile: replace values with percentiles. Tames outliers, discards exact magnitudes.
    • Winsorising: clip at, say, the 1st and 99th percentiles. For fraud the tail can be the signal, so check first.

    Two disciplines. Fit the scaler on training data only, then apply the same mean, standard deviation or quantiles to validation, test and live data; computing them on everything leaks test information (F3.1). And version the scaler with the model: deployed without its exact parameters, the model silently produces nonsense.

    Asymmetry to remember: scaled or logged inputs do no harm to a tree, while raw inputs can ruin a distance or gradient model. When in doubt, scale.

    With plain dirhams, "similar customer" means "similar balance", and the informative feature is nearly invisible. Nothing in the data changed, only the unit. Any model that adds up weighted inputs without regard to size has the same flaw.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Target encoding computed on the training rows

      Training AUC superb, held-out mediocre. Rare categories carry their own labels.

      Spot itCompare cross-validated and held-out scores. Check the encoding is fitted inside each fold, and compare the feature's label correlation on training versus new data.
    • Windows that include the future

      "Transactions in the last 7 days" computed with a window centred on the row, or with today's date as reference.

      Spot itRecompute a few cases by hand as of decision time. Offline and live values must match; a feature far stronger offline is the classic sign.
    • Baseline contaminated by the event

      A 90-day average that includes the burst, shrinking the ratio.

      Spot itCheck the baseline window ends before the feature window starts.
    • Wrong window for the problem

      A 30-day window for 10-minute card testing, or a 1-hour window for mule activity over weeks.

      Spot itPlot known cases across several windows, as in Simulation 1. If they only separate at a window you didn't build, add it.
    • Zero used for "never happened"

      Days since last transaction = 0 for customers with none, reading as "just transacted".

      Spot itLook for a spike at zero. Ask what the code does with no prior event.
    • Mean imputation hiding informative blanks

      Income, employer or bureau score blank for specific groups, replaced by the average, with no indicator.

      Spot itCompare outcome rates for blank versus filled. If they differ, signal was discarded.
    • Missing pattern changes between training and live

      A form change makes a field mandatory, or a feed starts sending defaults instead of nulls.

      Spot itMonitor each feature's missing rate and distribution in production against training. Alert on shifts.
    • Silent sentinel values

      −1, 9999 or "01/01/1900" treated as real numbers by a scaler or linear model.

      Spot itCheck histograms for impossible values and spikes at round extremes (F3.2).
    • Scaler fitted on all the data

      Mean and standard deviation (or quantiles) computed before the train/test split.

      Spot itCheck scaling lives inside the training pipeline and is refit per fold.
    • Unscaled inputs to a distance or gradient model

      k-NN, clustering, PCA or a neural net fed dirhams beside counts. The largest numbers win.

      Spot itCompare feature standard deviations. If they differ by orders of magnitude and the model isn't a tree, scale.
    • Outliers distorting standardisation

      One huge value squashes everyone else to near zero.

      Spot itCheck min, max and 99th percentile. Use log or robust scaling for money.
    • Proxy features for protected attributes

      Postcode, employer, device or merchant encodings can carry nationality, religion or gender. Target encoding makes the proxy as strong as the data allows.

      Spot itTry predicting the protected attribute from the engineered features; examine outcomes by group (F2.6).
    • Hundreds of auto-generated features, no stories

      A feature set nobody can explain to a validator or regulator. Some are leaks.

      Spot itFor each top feature, ask for its definition, its as-of time, and why it should predict the outcome.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A customer's average weekly card spend over the previous 90 days is AED 540. This week they spent AED 2,700. A second customer averages AED 6,000 a week and spent AED 7,500. Compare raw spends and ratios to baseline. Who looks more unusual?

    Ratios: 2,700 ÷ 540 = 5.0 and 7,500 ÷ 6,000 = 1.25. The second spent more in raw terms, but the first is five times their own norm, so the first is the unusual one. (In practice add a small constant to the denominator and check transaction and merchant counts.)

    2. A merchant has 5 training transactions, 2 of them fraud. The overall fraud rate is 2% and m = 25. What is the smoothed encoding, and what would naive encoding give?

    Naive: 2 ÷ 5 = 40%. Smoothed: (2 + 25 × 0.02) ÷ (5 + 25) = 2.5 ÷ 30 = 8.3%. Five transactions is little evidence, so it stays near the overall rate and moves further as volume grows.

    3. Your target-encoded model scores AUC 0.91 in cross-validation but 0.74 on next month's data. Name two causes to check, in order.

    First, in-sample or leaky encoding: was it fitted on the rows it was evaluated on, rather than inside each fold? Second, time: random folds mix future and past, so merchant behaviour and label availability differ from the real forward-looking case. Also check other leaks (windows, fields filled after the event) and genuine drift.

    4. On a personal-finance application, 25% leave "monthly income" blank, and their default rate is 14% against 7% for those who fill it in. What does this tell you, and what do you do?

    The blank is informative: missingness relates to risk, probably via income or applicant type (self-employed, irregular income). Add a missing indicator and impute with a group median, or let a boosted tree route the blanks. Don't drop the rows or impute the global mean alone. Ask the business why it's blank, and monitor the blank rate in production.

    5. Why does ridge regression need standardised inputs when ordinary least squares does not?

    In ordinary least squares, rescaling a feature just rescales its coefficient, so predictions are unchanged. Ridge penalises coefficient size. A feature in large units (AED) needs a tiny coefficient and is barely penalised, while one in small units (a 0 to 1 ratio) needs a big one and is penalised heavily. The penalty then favours features by their units, not usefulness. Standardising puts them on equal footing.

    6. Does a log transform change the predictions of a random forest? Of a k-NN model?

    Random forest: no (bar tie-breaking), since a log is strictly increasing and leaves the order of values, and so every split, unchanged. k-NN: yes, because distances change and so do the neighbours. Large values are compressed, so one huge transaction no longer dominates.

    7. Your best velocity window still misses 17% of fraud transactions in the test set, however you tune it. Is this a bug?

    Probably not. The first transaction of each burst has no earlier burst activity to count. If bursts average about six transactions, about one in six is the first, which is the roughly 83% ceiling in Simulation 1. To catch the first, add features that judge a transaction alone: new merchant, unusual amount for this customer, new device or location.

    8. You use "days since last salary credit" in a model predicting loan default over 12 months, but compute it as of the table build date, not each loan's start date. What goes wrong?

    It uses information from after the loan began. A borrower who later defaulted may have lost their job and stopped salary credits, so the gap is large because of the default. The feature looks very predictive offline and is unavailable, or means something else, at decision time. Recompute every behavioural feature as of each loan's application date.
