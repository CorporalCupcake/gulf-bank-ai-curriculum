    Feature Engineering

    Part 1

    What feature engineering is

    In one sentenceFeature engineering is turning raw records into the columns a model learns from, so that each column answers one useful question about the case.

    Running example: a café has a loyalty card and wants to predict which customers will stop coming. The raw data is a list of visits: date, drink, amount. A model cannot do much with a list of visits. It needs one row per customer with columns like "days since last visit".

    Those columns are the features. Choosing and building them usually matters more than choosing the model.

    Every feature should answer: what would we have known about this customer, at the moment of the decision, that helps predict what happens next? The words "at the moment" matter. Features must not use anything from the future (see the next lesson on leakage).

    Part 2

    The main kinds of feature

    How it differs from other steps

    • Cleaning fixes errors in the data (typos, duplicates). Feature engineering adds meaning on top of data that is already correct.
    • Model choice picks the learning algorithm. Features decide what the algorithm gets to see. A simple model with good features often beats a fancy one with raw columns.
    • Feature selection drops features. Engineering creates them.

    How to do it, step by step

    1. Pick the decision moment (for example, "the first of each month").
    2. Ask what behaviour would signal the outcome (visits slowing down).
    3. Summarise the history before that moment into numbers.
    4. Turn labels into numbers and handle blanks.
    5. Rescale if the model needs it.

    Recency, frequency and amount

    • Recency: time since the last visit.
    • Frequency: number of visits in a window, such as the last 30 days.
    • Amount: total or average spend.

    A long gap only matters relative to the person. Thirty days of silence is normal for someone who visits monthly and alarming for someone who visits daily.

    Ratio to the customer's own normal

    Divide recent activity by what is typical for that customer over, say, the previous 90 days. A ratio of 1 means business as usual. 0.3 means a third of normal. This makes one threshold work for busy and quiet customers alike.

    Ratios between columns help too (spend over income, balance over limit), because many models find division hard to learn by themselves.

    Windows: a rolling window counts events just before the decision. Match its length to what you want to catch. Too short and you miss the pattern, too long and it drowns in old behaviour.

    Turning categories into numbers

    Models need numbers, so labels such as drink or branch must be encoded.

    • One-hot: one 0/1 column per value ("drink_latte" is 1 or 0). Good when there are few values.
    • Grouping or frequency: with thousands of values (merchants, devices), lump rare ones into "other", or use how often each appears.
    • Target encoding: replace each value with its average outcome, such as the share of that branch's customers who left. Powerful but risky. A branch with two customers gives a number that is mostly noise, and if each row's own outcome feeds its own feature, the model looks brilliant in training and fails on new data. Fix it by smoothing (pulling small groups towards the overall average) and by computing it only from other rows.

    Blanks are information

    Why a value is missing matters. Sometimes it is random (a broken form). Often it depends on the value: people leave "age" blank because they would rather not say. Then the blank itself predicts something.

    • Add an indicator: a 0/1 column "was this blank?" beside the filled-in value.
    • Fill with the median (or a group median), not zero. Zero reads as a real answer.
    • Don't drop the rows unless blanks are rare and random.

    Scale: which models care

    Imagine height in metres next to weight in grams. In any "distance" between two people, the weight gap swamps the height gap, only because of the units.

    • Care about units: nearest-neighbour methods, clustering, PCA, neural nets, and penalised regression (ridge, lasso). Standardise them: subtract the average, divide by the spread.
    • Don't care: trees and boosted trees, which only ask "above this threshold?".
    • Money amounts: take a log first, since £100 against £200 matters about as much as £10,000 against £20,000.

    Always work out the average and spread on training rows only, then apply them to everything else.

    Part 3

    Which tool for which problem

    SituationUseWatch out for
    Behaviour over timeRecency, frequency, windowsWindows that run past the decision date
    Busy and quiet people mixedRatio to own normalA baseline that includes the event itself
    Few category valuesOne-hotToo many columns if values multiply
    Thousands of category valuesGrouping, frequency, smoothed target encodingLeaky target encoding
    Blank fieldsIndicator plus median fillSilently filling with the average

    Strong at: adding context a single row lacks, and often giving bigger gains than a new algorithm.

    Weak at: it takes domain thinking, it can leak the future if careless, and every feature must be rebuilt the same way in production.

    Show the smoothing formula

    Smoothed rate = (outcomes in the group + m × overall rate) ÷ (rows in the group + m). Here m is a small number such as 10. A group of 5 customers leans heavily on the overall rate. A group of 5,000 barely moves.

    In banking, this is how fraud and churn models get their signal: five card purchases in 40 minutes from a card that normally makes 1.4 a day is far more telling than any single purchase.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Target encoding on the training rows

      Training score looks superb, new-data score is mediocre. Compute the encoding inside each cross-validation fold.

    • Zero for "never happened"

      "Days since last visit = 0" reads as "just visited". Use a blank plus an indicator, or a large value.

    • Windows or baselines that include the future

      A window counted to today rather than to the decision date. Recompute a few cases by hand as of the decision.

    • Scaling before the split

      Computing the average and spread on all data lets test information leak into training. Fit the scaler on training rows only.

    1. A customer usually spends £20 a week and spent £100. Another usually spends £300 and spent £375. Who looks more unusual?

    The first: five times their norm, against 25% above. The ratio makes the comparison personal.

    2. A branch has 5 customers, 2 of whom left. Why is 40% a poor encoding?

    Five customers is little evidence, so 40% is mostly noise. Smoothing pulls it towards the overall rate.

    3. 25% of people leave age blank and they churn more. What do you do?

    The blank is informative. Add a missing indicator and fill with a group median. Don't drop the rows.

    4. Does a log transform change a random forest? A nearest-neighbour model?

    Not the forest, because the order of values, and so every split, is unchanged. It does change nearest-neighbour, because distances change.

    5. Why use a ratio to the customer's own normal instead of a raw count?

    A raw count cannot tell a busy customer from an unusual one. The ratio shows change against their own habit.
