    Feature Engineering: Behaviour, Encoding, Missingness and Scale

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A fitness app shows you walked 12,000 steps today. Is that a lot? For someone who usually walks 2,000, it is a big change. For a marathon runner, it is a lazy day. One number alone says little. The same number beside the person's own history says a lot.

    Models are the same. A single row (one card purchase, one customer, one day) rarely tells the story. Add context, such as five purchases in 40 minutes from a card that normally makes 1.4 a day, and a pattern appears. In banking, this is how fraud and churn models get their signal.

    Feature engineering means summarising the history around a decision into columns. Every feature answers one question: what would we have known about this person or account, at this moment, that helps predict what happens next? "At this moment" keeps the model honest (F3.1).

    Part 2

    How it really works

    Recency, frequency and monetary value

    Think of a gym member: visited yesterday, three times a week is safe; last visit five weeks ago is drifting out. RFM captures this: time since the last event, number of events in a window, and total or average amount. A long gap only matters relative to the customer's own rhythm. Thirty days of silence is normal for a monthly user and alarming for a daily one. Compute it as of the decision date, and use a blank, not zero, for "never seen".

    Rolling windows, velocity and ratios to the customer's own baseline

    A rolling window counts events just before the decision. Velocity is the same idea named for speed, such as transactions per hour. Match the window to the attack: minutes for card testing, hours for account takeover, days for mule activity.

    The ratio to own baseline is the smoke alarm tuned to each room. Divide recent activity by what is normal for that customer over their previous 60 to 90 days. A ratio of 1 is business as usual, and 4 is four times normal. Ratios between features (spend over income, balance over limit) help too, because models struggle to learn division.

    The idea in one line: look back as far as the thing you want to catch lasts, and compare against the person's own normal. The best window matches the attack's time scale. The ratio beats the raw count, which can't tell a busy customer from a compromised one. No window catches the first fraud in a burst, so pair velocity with single-transaction features such as new merchant.

    Encoding categories

    Models need numbers, so labels like shop, city or branch must be encoded. With few values, use one-hot columns. With thousands (merchants, devices), group the rare ones, use frequency, or let CatBoost or LightGBM handle them natively.

    Target encoding replaces each value with its average outcome, such as the merchant's fraud rate. It is powerful and dangerous. Like a restaurant rated five stars by its only reviewer, a merchant with two transactions is mostly noise. Worse, if each row's own label feeds its own feature, the model looks brilliant in training and fails on new data. Fix it with smoothing (pull small samples towards the overall rate) and out-of-fold encoding (encode each row from the other folds).

    If cross-validation far exceeds held-out scores and you used target encoding, suspect this leak first.

    Missing values: why a blank is information

    Why a value is missing decides what you can do. Sometimes it is random, such as an outage. Often it depends on the value itself: self-employed applicants leave income blank because it is low or irregular. Then the blank is a signal, and filling it with the average scores probable low earners as typical.

    The best move is a missingness indicator, a 0/1 column beside the imputed value. Impute with a median or group median, and let boosted trees route blanks themselves. Monitor the blank rate in production, because the indicator breaks if the form changes.

    You can use the blank, but you can't un-hide what it hides.

    Scaling, transforms, and which models care

    Height in metres beside weight in grams means any "distance" between two people is just the weight gap. A 1 cm height difference is lost next to a 500 g one. Models built on distances, dot products, gradients or penalties (k-NN, clustering, PCA, neural nets, ridge and lasso) care about units. Trees and boosted trees only ask "above a threshold?", so they don't.

    Standardise for neural nets and penalised models. Use log1p on money fields, since 100 against 200 matters about as much as 10,000 against 20,000 (in banking, money amounts). Fit the scaler on training data only, and version it with the model.

    Nothing in the data changed, only the unit.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Target encoding computed on the training rows

      Training AUC superb, held-out mediocre.

      Spot itCompare cross-validated and held-out scores; check the encoding is fitted inside each fold.
    • Windows and baselines that include the future or the event

      A reference date of today, or a baseline that includes the burst and shrinks the ratio.

      Spot itRecompute a few cases by hand as of decision time.
    • Zero used for "never happened"

      Days since last transaction = 0 reads as "just transacted".

      Spot itLook for a spike at zero.
    • Mean imputation hiding informative blanks

      Blanks replaced by the average with no indicator, or a form change makes the field mandatory.

      Spot itCompare outcome rates for blank versus filled, and watch the missing rate in production.
    • Unscaled inputs, or a scaler fitted on all the data

      The largest numbers win in distance-based models; scaling before the split leaks test information.

      Spot itCompare feature standard deviations; keep scaling inside the training pipeline.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A customer who usually spends AED 540 a week spent 2,700. Another who usually spends 6,000 spent 7,500. Who looks more unusual?

    The first, at five times their norm against 25% above. The ratio makes the threshold personal.

    2. A merchant has 5 transactions, 2 of them fraud. Why is the raw 40% a poor encoding?

    Five transactions is little evidence, so 40% is mostly noise. Smoothing pulls it towards the overall rate, less so as volume grows.

    3. A target-encoded model scores AUC 0.91 in cross-validation but 0.74 next month. Name two causes.

    Leaky encoding (not fitted inside each fold), and random folds mixing future and past.

    4. 25% leave income blank, and their default rate is 14% against 7%. What do you do?

    The blank is informative. Add a missing indicator and impute with a group median. Don't drop the rows.

    5. Does a log transform change a random forest? A k-NN model?

    Not the forest, since order and so every split is unchanged. It does change k-NN, because distances change.
