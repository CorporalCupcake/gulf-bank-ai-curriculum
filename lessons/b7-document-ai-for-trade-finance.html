    Reading Paper and Listening to Calls: Document AI and Contact-Centre AI

    How a bank turns letters of credit, bills of lading, KYC packs and customer calls into data it can act on; why confidence and checking matter more than raw accuracy; and why a Gulf Arabic call is a harder speech problem than most vendor demos admit.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth document AI and contact-centre AI convert messy human artefacts (scans, calls) into structured fields; the value comes from knowing which fields to trust, sending the rest to a person, and refusing to let a fluent output hide a wrong amount, name or date.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An importer in Jebel Ali wants to buy steel from a supplier in Turkey. Neither trusts the other, so a bank issues a letter of credit (LC): a promise to pay the supplier if, and only if, the supplier presents the right documents. A commercial invoice. A bill of lading showing the goods were shipped. A packing list. An insurance certificate. Each must match the LC's terms: the amount, the goods description, the ports, the dates, the names.

    The bank pays against documents, not goods. Under the ICC rules (UCP 600) a checker reads each page and compares it with the credit and with the other pages. One typo in a consignee name, a shipment date a day late, or a description that says "steel bars" where the LC says "reinforcing steel bars" is a discrepancy. Someone reads dozens of pages, many scanned, some in two languages, and must catch all of it.

    Now the call centre. A customer rings at 9 p.m. in Gulf Arabic, switching mid-sentence to English for "transfer" and "limit", from a car with the window open. The agent must find the account, understand the problem, fix it and write notes afterwards. Typing those notes takes minutes of each call.

    These look like different problems. They are the same one: a human artefact (page, voice) must become fields in a system, and every conversion step can be wrong in a way that looks perfectly normal on screen.

    Hold onto thisThe model's job is not to be right. It is to be right and know when it is not. A system that reads 97% of fields correctly but cannot say which 3% are wrong is far less useful than one that reads 90% correctly and flags the rest.

    Three ideas carry the whole lesson.

    • Extraction is cheap; verification is the product. Pulling an amount off a page is now routine. The hard, valuable part is deciding which amounts can go straight through and which need a person, and proving that to an auditor.
    • The error that matters is the confident one. A blurry stamp read as "blurry" is fine. A blurry stamp read confidently as the wrong date is a paid claim on non-compliant documents.
    • Averages hide entities. A speech model with a 22% word error rate gets most words wrong in a long account number, and almost never misses that the call is about a card. Entities (digits, names, amounts) are what banking cares about, and they are what averages hide.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    From a scanned page to a checked field

    Trade and onboarding documents arrive as PDFs, phone photos, faxed scans and, increasingly, structured files. A modern document pipeline has a few stages. Each can be done by a classic component or by a large vision-language model, and real systems mix them.

    1. CaptureClean the image: deskew, denoise, split a 40-page bundle into documents.
    2. ClassifyIs this an invoice, a bill of lading, a Trade Licence, a passport?
    3. ReadOCR and layout, or a vision-language model reading the page directly.
    4. ExtractTurn text into named fields with a confidence for each.
    5. CheckCompare fields to the LC, to each other and to rules.
    6. RouteAuto-accept, or send to a person with the problem highlighted.

    Reading. Classic OCR finds characters and gives text with positions. That is not enough on its own: on an invoice, "5,000" could be the quantity, the unit price or a postcode. Layout-aware models such as LayoutLM (Xu et al., 2020) take the text and where it sits on the page, so they can learn that a number beside "Total" is an amount. OCR-free models such as Donut (Kim et al., 2022) read the image directly and emit structured output. Today's general vision-language models (the Claude, Gemini and GPT families, plus open models) can do the same from a prompt and a schema, with no per-document-type training. They are flexible and slower, and they need guardrails (below). See parsing messy PDFs and scans and search over document images for the retrieval-side view of the same problem.

    Extraction with a schema. You define what you want: {"lc_amount": number, "currency": string, "latest_shipment_date": date, "goods": string, "beneficiary": string}. The model fills it and, crucially, you ask for evidence: the page and the box the value came from, so a person can click through and see it.

    {
  "latest_shipment_date": {
    "value": "2026-11-15",
    "source": {"page": 3, "box": [412, 188, 590, 210]},
    "confidence": 0.94
  }
}

    Checking. Many LC checks are not AI problems at all. Is the invoice amount within the LC amount and tolerance? Was shipment before the latest date? Does the port in the bill of lading match the credit? These are deterministic rules on extracted fields, and rules should do them. AI is needed for the soft comparisons: does "reinforcing steel bars, grade 500B" in the credit match "rebar B500B" on the invoice? Is this a clean on-board bill of lading or does a stamp say otherwise? The sound design is rules for the exact, a language model for the fuzzy, and a human for the disputed.

    Confidence, calibration and straight-through processing

    The share of documents that flow through with no human touch is called straight-through processing (STP). Raising it saves money. Raising it carelessly lets errors through. The control is a confidence threshold per field, and it is only as good as the confidence is honest.

    Two ways to get a confidence: from the OCR or extraction model's own scores, or by checking agreement (extract twice by different methods and compare, or check a value against a second source such as the LC data). A confidence is calibrated if, among fields scored "1% chance of error", about 1% really are wrong. That is exactly the calibration idea of F7, now applied to fields. Language models are often overconfident on text they find plausible, so measure it on real documents and never trust the raw number.

    Notice what the person does in the "sent to a person" bucket. They do not retype. They see the field, the highlighted source box and the suggested value, and confirm or fix it in seconds. Good document AI is mostly good review design. Corrections they make are also your best training data, the feedback loop covered in delayed labels and feedback loops.

    Discrepancy detection: precision, recall and who bears the miss

    Under UCP 600 the issuing bank has a limited time (five banking days after presentation) to decide whether the presentation complies; it can refuse on discrepancies. Industry sources routinely say a large share of first presentations contain at least one discrepancy; I could not confirm a clean primary figure, so treat the often-quoted "60–70%" as folklore and ask the trade operations team for your own bank's rate. What matters for the model is the cost shape:

    • A false alarm costs a checker a few minutes to look and dismiss.
    • A missed discrepancy means the bank may pay against non-compliant documents. The loss falls on the bank if the applicant refuses to accept the documents. That can be tens or hundreds of thousands of dirhams, plus a reputation problem.

    That asymmetry is the same cost logic as fraud thresholds in F7: set the flag threshold from costs, not from a default. Because misses are so expensive, document AI for LCs is normally built as a second pair of eyes: the AI flags suspected discrepancies, the checker still decides. The saving comes from faster reading and consistent coverage, not from removing the human. The part that really can be automated early is the exact, rule-based comparison.

    Onboarding and KYC packs

    A corporate onboarding pack is the same problem in another costume: trade licence, memorandum of association, board resolution, passports of directors and ultimate beneficial owners, proof of address, sometimes in Arabic and English side by side. The fields feed customer due diligence (B3): names go to sanctions screening (B2), owner percentages build the ownership graph, dates drive licence expiry alerts. Three features differ from trade finance.

    • Authenticity, not just content. A perfectly extracted forged licence is still forged. Document AI is paired with tamper checks (font, metadata, template matching) and, for identity documents, with eKYC liveness. Generated fakes are now a live threat; see deepfakes against eKYC.
    • Names are the hard field. One person is "Mohammed", "Muhammad" and "محمد" across documents. Extraction must keep the original script and the transliteration, because screening and entity resolution need both.
    • Regulated data. Passport images and Emirates ID numbers are personal data. Where the model runs, what is logged and who can see the page are governance questions, not details. See privacy basics.

    Arabic adds script-level difficulty: right-to-left text mixed with left-to-right numbers, joined letterforms, diacritics, stamps over text, and fonts that general OCR has seen less of. Arabic OCR and document AI covers it. The practical rule is the same as everywhere: measure field accuracy on your documents in your languages, never on a vendor's demo set.

    Contact-centre AI, part 1: turning speech into text

    The contact-centre stack has a speech layer and a language layer. Speech first.

    Automatic speech recognition (ASR) turns audio into text. Modern systems (OpenAI's Whisper from 2022 is the best-known open example) are large transformers trained on huge amounts of paired audio and text. They are scored by word error rate:

    WER=S+D+IN

    S substituted words, D deleted words, I inserted words, N words in the correct transcript. A WER of 20% means roughly one word in five is wrong. It can exceed 100% if the system inserts many extra words.

    Two reasons Gulf Arabic is harder than the leaderboard suggests.

    • Dialect. Arabic speech comes as formal Modern Standard Arabic (news, speeches) and many spoken dialects that differ in vocabulary, pronunciation and grammar. Most training audio is MSA or Egyptian, because that is what is published. Gulf, and within it Emirati, Najdi, Hijazi and Khaleeji varieties, are less represented. Models that score well on MSA benchmarks can do much worse on a Gulf phone line. The Arabic language models lesson covers the text side of dialect and Arabizi.
    • Code-switching and the phone line. Customers mix Arabic with English ("I want to increase my limit, يا أخي"), speak on 8 kHz narrowband audio with compression, and talk over agents. Each factor adds error. Published benchmarks on this are still sparse; the field is moving (Part 4).

    Two audio-handling choices matter. Diarisation separates "who spoke when" (customer versus agent), because a summary must not attribute the agent's offer to the customer. Often the two sides are recorded on separate channels, which makes this easy. Streaming versus batch: live agent assist needs a transcript within a second or so and accepts higher error; post-call summaries can use a slower, more accurate pass.

    What word error rate hides

    WER is an average over all words. Banking decisions depend on specific words. Three tasks are affected very differently:

    • Working out what the call is about needs only a few cue words ("card", "stolen", "transfer", "loan"). Even a noisy transcript usually contains one.
    • Capturing a name needs every word of the name right, and names are the words speech models know least.
    • Capturing a 12-digit number, such as an account or reference number, needs every digit right. One wrong digit is a wrong account.

    If each word were wrong independently with probability w (a simplification), the chance of getting all k words right is (1 − w)k. That compounding is why a model that sounds fine produces unusable account numbers.

    Contact-centre AI, part 2: summaries, routing and assist

    Once there is a transcript, language models do the work that used to need an agent's attention.

    • Call summaries and wrap-up notes. The model writes a short structured note: reason for call, actions taken, promises made, follow-up due. Done well, this removes minutes of after-call typing and makes notes consistent. The danger is a fluent summary containing something nobody said, or an omitted promise. Mitigations: structured templates, quotes from the transcript as evidence, a "never invent amounts or dates" rule checked by code, and agent review before saving. See hallucination detection and groundedness.
    • Routing and triage. A classifier (or the first turns of a voice bot) decides which queue: cards, payments, complaints, collections, fraud. Misrouting wastes the customer's time. Mistakes with vulnerable or urgent cases (fraud in progress, bereavement, complaint with a regulatory clock) cost much more than the average.
    • Agent assist. Live suggestions: the next best answer from the knowledge base (retrieval, see hybrid search), a compliance reminder ("have you read the disclosure?"), a prompt about a product. The agent stays in charge.
    • Quality and compliance monitoring. Instead of a supervisor listening to 2% of calls, the model scores 100%: did the agent verify identity, mention fees, avoid prohibited promises? Flagged calls go to a human. It also finds themes, such as a rising complaint about a new app release.
    • Voice bots. Automated handling of simple requests (balance, card block) by speech. This is the riskiest of the five because no human is in the loop, and it should start with narrow, reversible actions behind authentication.

    Security applies on the voice channel too. Voice biometrics have been used for authentication; synthetic voices make that weaker (deepfakes against eKYC and call centres). Recordings are personal and often sensitive data, with retention and consent rules to be taken from your own legal and compliance team. I won't guess at specific UAE or Saudi requirements here.

    Deciding what to automate

    The right level of automation depends on the cost of an error, whether it can be reversed, and whether a human sees the output before it matters. A useful ladder: assist (the human decides, AI suggests), automate with sampling (the AI acts, humans audit a sample), automate fully (only for low-stakes, reversible work with monitoring).     Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Trade operations. LC issuance and examination, import and export collections, guarantees. High document volume, strict clocks, high stakes per item. The first win is usually faster extraction and rule checks; discrepancy suggestions come next.
    • Onboarding and periodic KYC refresh. Corporate packs and expiring documents. The win is turnaround time for the customer and consistency for compliance.
    • Lending operations. Salary certificates, bank statements and financials for credit memos (LLMs for credit memos).
    • Contact centre. After-call notes, routing, QA scoring and complaint themes, in Arabic and English, often under tight regulator scrutiny of complaints handling.
    • Fraud and scams. Calls are where social-engineering scams show up. Transcript signals ("someone told me to move my money") can feed scam detection (A9).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Document extraction is now close to a commodity capability; the competition has moved to reliability, evidence and workflow. Speech recognition for Gulf Arabic is improving fast, with new regional models and datasets, but independent evidence is thin. The honest summary is that the technology is ahead of the published proof.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • One headline accuracy number

      "The system is 97% accurate" averages easy fields (document type) with expensive ones (amount, date, beneficiary).

      Spot itAsk for accuracy per field, with a count of documents, and the error cost of each field.
    • Confident wrong reads

      A smudged digit read as another digit, with a high score. The reviewer is never shown it.

      Spot itSample auto-accepted fields at random each week and compare with a human. If the error rate is above the reported one, the confidence is miscalibrated.
    • The model fills in what it expects

      A language model "reads" a standard clause that is actually absent or altered. Generative readers tend to repair the page.

      Spot itRequire each value to point to a source box. If the box does not contain the value, reject it. Test with documents where a field is deliberately missing.
    • Arabic and English versions disagree

      The Arabic and English columns of a bilingual document differ in a value and the system reads only one.

      Spot itExtract from both and compare. A mismatch is a flag, not a pick.
    • Template drift

      A big supplier changes its invoice layout and field accuracy halves quietly.

      Spot itTrack accuracy and the auto-accept rate by document source weekly. A sudden drop for one source is a new template.
    • Forgery passes as clean

      Perfect extraction of a fabricated or altered document.

      Spot itSeparate checks for authenticity (metadata, font and template consistency, cross-checks with the registry or the issuing party) and a red-team test with altered samples.
    • Demo audio versus real calls

      A speech vendor's 8% word error rate was measured on clean studio MSA. On your calls it is 30%.

      Spot itTranscribe 200 of your own calls by hand, split by dialect, channel and noise, and compute WER and entity accuracy yourself.
    • Entities lost inside a fluent summary

      The summary reads well and states the wrong amount, or leaves out that the customer was promised a call back.

      Spot itCheck amounts, dates and commitments in the summary against the transcript automatically, and score summaries on those, not on fluency.
    • Speaker mix-ups

      The agent's offer is attributed to the customer, or the reverse, in a mono recording.

      Spot itUse separate channels where possible; test diarisation on your recordings.
    • Automating a queue the model barely understands

      A voice bot misroutes fraud-in-progress calls to general queries.

      Spot itMeasure recall on urgent categories separately, and add an escape route to a human at any sign of distress or repeated failure.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The goal in trade operations is not to remove the checker; it is to give them a head start. The system reads the documents, fills the fields, runs the exact checks and flags what looks wrong, and the checker confirms. We would measure it by checker time per presentation and by discrepancies missed on a sampled audit. In the contact centre, the first win is after-call notes and routing, with the agent approving the note. The thing to be careful about is account numbers and names on Gulf Arabic calls, where speech accuracy is lowest.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd decompose the pipeline: classification, reading, schema extraction with source boxes, deterministic validation, then model-based fuzzy checks, with per-field calibrated confidence driving routing. I'd evaluate field-level and entity-level accuracy on our own documents, split by source and language, and set thresholds from error costs. For speech I'd report WER by dialect and channel and, separately, entity error rate on digits and names, since averages hide those. Summaries get checked against the transcript for amounts, dates and commitments. Authenticity checks sit alongside extraction, not inside it.”
    “Can we just give the whole LC bundle to a large model and ask if it complies?”

    You can get a plausible answer, which is the danger. For a payment decision you need a reproducible check, a source for every claim and a measured miss rate. I'd use the model to extract and to suggest, run exact comparisons in code, and keep the checker accountable. Then I'd test on a set of past presentations with known discrepancies, including ones deliberately hidden, and report how many it caught.

    “What accuracy do we need before we remove human review?”

    It depends on the field and the cost of an error, not on one number. Low-risk fields with well-calibrated confidence can go straight through above a threshold; amounts and beneficiaries stay under review longer. I'd start with assist, move to sampling when audits show an error rate below an agreed limit, and keep monitoring by document source. The decision belongs with risk and operations, with evidence from us.

    “The vendor says their Arabic speech model has 8% error. Why are you worried?”

    Which audio, which dialect, which channel? An 8% rate on clean formal Arabic says little about Gulf dialect on a compressed phone line with English mixed in. And word error rate averages; a 12-digit account number is right only if every digit is. I'd ask for a test on a few hundred of our own calls, split by dialect and noise, with entity accuracy reported separately.

    “Won't an LLM summary save agents several minutes per call?”

    Probably, and it is a good first use case because the agent reviews the note before saving. I'd measure time saved against the summary's error rate on amounts, dates and promises, since one fabricated commitment can become a complaint. I'd check those details against the transcript in code and sample reviewed notes to see how often agents correct them.

    “What is your biggest worry about onboarding automation?”

    That extraction accuracy gets mistaken for document authenticity. Reading a forged licence perfectly doesn't make it genuine. I'd pair extraction with tamper and cross-source checks, make sure names are kept in original script and transliteration for screening, and measure on adversarial samples, not only on clean ones.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why is "97% accuracy" a weak claim for an LC extraction system?

    It averages fields with very different consequences. A wrong document type is easily caught; a wrong amount, date or beneficiary can lead to paying on non-compliant documents. You need per-field accuracy, the cost of each error type, and calibrated confidence so the risky fields go to a person.

    2. A model says it is 99% sure of a field's value. Among 1,000 such fields, 40 are wrong. What does that tell you?

    It is overconfident: it implied about 10 errors and had 40, four times too many. Raw model confidences cannot be used to set auto-accept thresholds until they have been checked against human labels and, if needed, recalibrated, which is the idea from F7 applied to fields.

    3. Why should the exact LC checks (amount within tolerance, shipment before the latest date) not be left to a language model?

    They are deterministic: code does them perfectly, reproducibly and cheaply, and can show the rule that fired. Use the language model for the fuzzy comparisons, such as whether two goods descriptions mean the same thing, and for reading.

    4. A word error rate of 20% is acceptable for intent routing but not for account numbers. Show why with a number.

    Under the simple independent model, a 12-digit number is captured right with probability 0.812 ≈ 6.9%. A call has at least one of three cue words recognised with probability 1 − 0.2³ = 99.2%. The model is stylised, but the contrast is the point: entities need every symbol right.

    5. In Simulation 2, why does the best cut flag more aggressively than a "balanced" detector would?

    Because a miss costs AED 15,000 against AED 60 for a false flag, a ratio of 250 to 1. The cost-minimising rule flags whenever the chance that a presentation has a real discrepancy exceeds 60 ÷ 15,060, about 0.4%, so it accepts many false flags to avoid misses. In practice this is acceptable because the checker reads flagged items anyway.

    6. Why is a clean extraction of a trade licence not evidence the licence is genuine?

    Extraction only reads what the page says. A forged or altered document can be read perfectly. Authenticity needs separate checks: file and font metadata, template consistency, a lookup against the issuing registry where one exists, and for identity documents liveness and eKYC.

    7. Name two reasons Gulf call-centre audio can give far higher error than a published Arabic benchmark.

    Dialect coverage (training and test sets lean towards MSA and a few better-resourced dialects), and the channel: narrowband compressed phone audio, background noise, and mid-sentence switching to English. Add vocabulary: product names and local place names the model has not seen.

    8. You must choose between full automation and assist for a card-block request on a voice bot. What do you ask?

    Blocking a card is reversible and protective, so lower risk than moving money; the key question is authentication, since a false block is an annoyance but a block requested by a fraudster is a denial of service. I'd allow it behind strong authentication, with an easy route to a human and monitoring. A transfer or limit increase would stay in assist.
