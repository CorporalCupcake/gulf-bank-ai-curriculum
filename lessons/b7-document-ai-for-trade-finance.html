    Reading Paper and Listening to Calls: Document AI and Contact-Centre AI

    How a bank turns letters of credit, bills of lading, KYC packs and customer calls into data it can act on; why confidence and checking matter more than raw accuracy; and why a Gulf Arabic call is a harder speech problem than most vendor demos admit.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDocument AI and contact-centre AI both turn messy human artefacts (scans, calls) into structured fields; the value lies in knowing which fields to trust, sending the rest to a person, and never letting a fluent output hide a wrong amount, name or date.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Jebel Ali importer buys steel from a Turkish supplier. Neither trusts the other, so a bank issues a letter of credit (LC): a promise to pay the supplier if, and only if, the right documents are presented (invoice, bill of lading, packing list, insurance certificate). Each must match the LC's amount, goods, ports, dates and names.

    The bank pays against documents, not goods. Under the ICC rules (UCP 600) a checker compares every page with the credit and with the other pages. A typo in a consignee name, a shipment one day late, or "steel bars" where the LC says "reinforcing steel bars" is a discrepancy. Dozens of pages, many scanned, some bilingual, and the checker must catch all of it.

    Now the call centre. A customer rings at 9 p.m. in Gulf Arabic, slips into English for "transfer" and "limit", from a car with the window open. The agent must find the account, fix the problem and type up notes afterwards.

    Same problem twice: a human artefact (page, voice) must become fields in a system, and every conversion step can be wrong in a way that looks perfectly normal on screen.

    Hold onto thisThe model's job is not just to be right. It is to be right and know when it is not. A system that is 90% right and flags the doubtful rest beats one that is 97% right and cannot say which 3% are wrong.
    • Extraction is cheap; verification is the product. Reading an amount is routine. Deciding which amounts go straight through, and proving it to an auditor, is the hard part.
    • The confident error is the one that matters. A blurry stamp read as "unclear" is fine. Read confidently as the wrong date, it becomes a paid claim on non-compliant documents.
    • Averages hide entities. A 22% word error rate still spots that a call is about a card, yet garbles most 12-digit account numbers. Digits, names and amounts are what banking cares about.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    From a scanned page to a checked field

    Documents arrive as PDFs, phone photos and faxed scans. A pipeline has six stages, each done by a classic component or a vision-language model (VLM), often mixed.

    1. CaptureClean the image: deskew, denoise, split a 40-page bundle.
    2. ClassifyInvoice, bill of lading, Trade Licence, passport?
    3. ReadOCR and layout, or a VLM reading the page directly.
    4. ExtractTurn text into named fields, each with a confidence.
    5. CheckCompare fields to the LC, each other and the rules.
    6. RouteAuto-accept, or send to a person with the problem highlighted.

    Reading. OCR gives characters with positions, but "5,000" could be a quantity, a price or a postcode. Layout-aware models such as LayoutLM (Xu et al., 2020) use text and position, so a number beside "Total" is learned to be an amount. OCR-free models such as Donut (Kim et al., 2022) read the image straight into structured output. General VLMs (Claude, Gemini, GPT, open models) do the same from a prompt and a schema with no per-template training: flexible and slower, and they need guardrails. See parsing messy PDFs and scans and search over document images.

    Extraction with a schema. Define the fields (lc_amount, currency, latest_shipment_date, goods, beneficiary) and ask for evidence, the page and box each value came from, so a person can click through.

    {
  "latest_shipment_date": {
    "value": "2026-11-15",
    "source": {"page": 3, "box": [412, 188, 590, 210]},
    "confidence": 0.94
  }
}

    Checking. Think of a spell-checker versus an editor. Exact checks are the spell-checker: invoice amount within LC amount and tolerance, shipment before the latest date, port matches. These are deterministic rules, so code should do them. Fuzzy checks are the editor: does "reinforcing steel bars, grade 500B" match "rebar B500B"? Does a stamp make a bill of lading not "clean on board"? Use a language model there. Design: rules for the exact, a model for the fuzzy, a human for the disputed.

    Confidence, calibration and straight-through processing

    Straight-through processing (STP) is the share of documents that flow through with no human touch. Raising it saves money; raising it carelessly lets errors through. The control is a per-field confidence threshold, only as good as the confidence is honest.

    A confidence comes from the model's own scores, or from agreement (extract twice by different methods, or check against a second source such as the LC). It is calibrated if, among fields scored "1% chance of error", about 1% really are wrong: a weather forecaster whose "30% rain" days really are wet 30% of the time. This is the F7 idea applied to fields. Language models are often overconfident on plausible text, so measure on real documents and never trust the raw number.

    In the "sent to a person" bucket the reviewer does not retype. They see the field, the highlighted source box and a suggested value, and confirm or fix it in seconds. Good document AI is mostly good review design, and the corrections are your best training data (delayed labels and feedback loops).

    Discrepancy detection: precision, recall and who bears the miss

    Under UCP 600 the issuing bank has five banking days after presentation to decide whether documents comply. Sources often quote "60–70%" of first presentations having a discrepancy; I could not confirm a primary figure, so ask trade operations for your bank's own rate. The cost shape matters more:

    • False alarm: a checker spends a few minutes and dismisses it.
    • Missed discrepancy: the bank may pay on non-compliant documents and bear the loss if the applicant refuses them. Tens or hundreds of thousands of dirhams, plus reputation.

    Like a smoke alarm, tolerate some false alarms to avoid one missed fire. Set the threshold from costs, as with fraud in F7. Because misses are costly, build LC AI as a second pair of eyes: AI flags, the checker decides. The saving is faster reading and consistent coverage, not removing the human. The exact rule-based comparison is what can be automated early.

    Reading the metrics: recall (real discrepancies caught) answers "how many fires did the alarm catch?"; 90% means 1 in 10 slips through. Precision (flags that are real) answers "when it rings, is there a fire?"; 20% means checkers dismiss four of five flags. Loosening the threshold raises recall and lowers precision. Misreading to avoid: picking the "balanced" point instead of the cost-based one.

    Onboarding and KYC packs

    Same problem, new costume: trade licence, memorandum of association, board resolution, director and beneficial-owner passports, proof of address, often Arabic and English side by side. Fields feed due diligence (B3): names to sanctions screening (B2), ownership percentages to the ownership graph, dates to expiry alerts. Three differences from trade finance:

    • Authenticity, not just content. A perfectly read forged licence is still forged. Pair extraction with tamper checks (font, metadata, template matching) and, for IDs, eKYC liveness. See deepfakes against eKYC.
    • Names are the hard field. "Mohammed", "Muhammad" and "محمد" are one person. Keep the original script and the transliteration; screening and entity resolution need both.
    • Regulated data. Passport images and Emirates ID numbers are personal data. Where the model runs, what is logged and who sees the page are governance questions (privacy basics).

    Arabic adds right-to-left text mixed with left-to-right numbers, joined letterforms, diacritics and stamps over text (Arabic OCR and document AI). Measure field accuracy on your documents in your languages, never on a vendor demo set.

    Contact-centre AI, part 1: turning speech into text

    Automatic speech recognition (ASR) turns audio into text. Modern systems (Whisper, 2022, is the best-known open one) are large transformers trained on paired audio and text. They are scored by word error rate:

    WER=S+D+IN

    S substituted, D deleted, I inserted words; N words in the correct transcript. 20% means about one word in five is wrong; it can exceed 100% if many words are inserted.

    Reading a value: going from 10% to 20% WER means twice as many wrong words, which hurts long entities far more than short ones (next section). Typical figures depend entirely on the audio, so a WER means nothing without knowing dialect, channel and noise. Two reasons Gulf Arabic is harder than leaderboards suggest:

    • Dialect. Formal Modern Standard Arabic (MSA, news and speeches) differs from spoken dialects in vocabulary, pronunciation and grammar. Training audio is mostly MSA or Egyptian because that is what is published; Gulf varieties (Emirati, Najdi, Hijazi, Khaleeji) are thinly covered. A model strong on MSA can be far worse on a Gulf phone line. See Arabic language models.
    • Code-switching and the line. Customers mix Arabic and English ("I want to increase my limit, يا أخي"), on compressed 8 kHz audio, talking over agents. Each adds error. Published benchmarks on Gulf calls are sparse.

    Two handling choices. Diarisation labels who spoke when, so a summary does not attribute the agent's offer to the customer; separate recording channels make it easy. Streaming versus batch: live agent assist needs text within a second and accepts more error; post-call summaries can use a slower, more accurate pass.

    What word error rate hides

    WER averages all words, but banking decisions hang on specific ones. Compare a shop assistant who gets the gist of your order with one who must copy a 12-digit code perfectly:

    • Call topic needs one cue word ("card", "stolen", "loan") out of a few. Usually present.
    • A name needs every word right, and names are what models know least.
    • A 12-digit number needs every digit right. One wrong digit is a wrong account.

    If each word fails independently with probability w (a simplification), all k words are right with probability (1 − w)k. That compounding is why a transcript that sounds fine yields unusable account numbers.

    Contact-centre AI, part 2: summaries, routing and assist

    Given a transcript, language models take over work that used to need agent attention.

    • Summaries and wrap-up notes. A structured note: reason, actions, promises, follow-up. It saves minutes of typing and makes notes consistent. Danger: a fluent summary with something nobody said, or a missing promise. Mitigate with templates, transcript quotes as evidence, code that blocks invented amounts or dates, and agent review (groundedness).
    • Routing and triage. A classifier picks the queue: cards, payments, complaints, collections, fraud. Errors on urgent or vulnerable cases (fraud in progress, bereavement, complaint with a regulatory clock) cost far more than average.
    • Agent assist. Live suggestions from the knowledge base (hybrid search) and compliance reminders. The agent stays in charge.
    • Quality and compliance monitoring. Score 100% of calls instead of a supervisor sampling 2%: identity verified, fees mentioned, no prohibited promises. Flagged calls go to a human. It also surfaces themes, such as complaints after an app release.
    • Voice bots. Speech handling of simple requests (balance, card block). Riskiest, since no human is in the loop: start with narrow, reversible actions behind authentication.

    Voice biometrics are weakened by synthetic voices (deepfakes against eKYC and call centres). Recordings are sensitive personal data; take retention and consent rules from your legal and compliance team, as I won't guess at UAE or Saudi requirements.

    Deciding what to automate

    Choose by the cost of an error, whether it is reversible, and whether a human sees the output first. Ladder: assist (human decides, AI suggests), automate with sampling (AI acts, humans audit a sample), automate fully (low-stakes, reversible, monitored). Classify the cases below; more than one answer can be defensible.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • One headline accuracy number

      "97% accurate" averages easy fields (document type) with costly ones (amount, date, beneficiary).

      Spot itAsk for per-field accuracy, document counts and the error cost of each field.
    • Confident wrong reads

      A smudged digit read as another, with a high score. The reviewer never sees it.

      Spot itSample auto-accepted fields weekly against a human. If errors exceed the reported rate, confidence is miscalibrated.
    • The model fills in what it expects

      A language model "reads" a standard clause that is absent or altered. Generative readers repair the page.

      Spot itRequire a source box for each value and reject it if the box lacks the value. Test with deliberately missing fields.
    • Arabic and English versions disagree

      The two columns of a bilingual document differ in a value and the system reads only one.

      Spot itExtract both and compare. A mismatch is a flag, not a pick.
    • Template drift

      A big supplier changes its invoice layout and accuracy halves quietly.

      Spot itTrack accuracy and auto-accept rate by document source weekly. A sudden drop for one source is a new template.
    • Forgery passes as clean

      Perfect extraction of a fabricated or altered document.

      Spot itSeparate authenticity checks (metadata, font and template consistency, registry cross-checks) and red-team with altered samples.
    • Demo audio versus real calls

      A vendor's 8% WER was measured on clean studio MSA. On your calls it is 30%.

      Spot itHand-transcribe 200 of your own calls, split by dialect, channel and noise, and compute WER and entity accuracy yourself.
    • Entities lost inside a fluent summary

      The summary reads well but states the wrong amount or omits a promised call-back.

      Spot itCheck amounts, dates and commitments against the transcript in code, and score summaries on those, not fluency.
    • Speaker mix-ups

      The agent's offer is attributed to the customer, or the reverse, in a mono recording.

      Spot itUse separate channels where possible; test diarisation on your recordings.
    • Automating a queue the model barely understands

      A voice bot sends fraud-in-progress calls to general queries.

      Spot itMeasure recall on urgent categories separately, and give a human escape route at any sign of distress or repeated failure.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is "97% accuracy" a weak claim for an LC extraction system?

    It averages fields with very different consequences. A wrong document type is easily caught; a wrong amount, date or beneficiary can mean paying on non-compliant documents. You need per-field accuracy, error costs and calibrated confidence so risky fields go to a person.

    2. A model says it is 99% sure of a field's value. Among 1,000 such fields, 40 are wrong. What does that tell you?

    It is overconfident: it implied about 10 errors and had 40, four times too many. Check raw confidences against human labels, and recalibrate if needed, before setting auto-accept thresholds.

    3. Why should exact LC checks (amount within tolerance, shipment before the latest date) not be left to a language model?

    They are deterministic: code does them perfectly, reproducibly and cheaply, and can show the rule that fired. Keep the model for reading and fuzzy comparisons, such as whether two goods descriptions mean the same.

    4. A word error rate of 20% is acceptable for intent routing but not for account numbers. Show why with a number.

    A 12-digit number is captured right with probability 0.812 ≈ 6.9%. At least one of three cue words is recognised with probability 1 − 0.2³ = 99.2%. The model is stylised, but entities need every symbol right.

    5. In Simulation 2, why does the best cut flag more aggressively than a "balanced" detector would?

    A miss costs AED 15,000 against AED 60 for a false flag, 250 to 1. The cost-minimising rule flags whenever the chance of a real discrepancy exceeds 60 ÷ 15,060, about 0.4%, accepting many false flags. That is tolerable because the checker reads flagged items anyway.

    6. Why is a clean extraction of a trade licence not evidence the licence is genuine?

    Extraction only reads what the page says; a forgery can be read perfectly. Authenticity needs separate checks: file and font metadata, template consistency, registry look-up where one exists, and liveness and eKYC for identity documents.

    7. Name two reasons Gulf call-centre audio can give far higher error than a published Arabic benchmark.

    Dialect coverage (training and test sets lean towards MSA and better-resourced dialects), and the channel: narrowband compressed audio, noise and mid-sentence English. Also vocabulary: product and place names the model has not seen.

    8. You must choose between full automation and assist for a card-block request on a voice bot. What do you ask?

    Blocking is reversible and protective, so lower risk than moving money. The key question is authentication: a false block is an annoyance, but a fraudster-requested block is a denial of service. Allow it behind strong authentication, with an easy route to a human and monitoring. Transfers and limit increases stay in assist.
