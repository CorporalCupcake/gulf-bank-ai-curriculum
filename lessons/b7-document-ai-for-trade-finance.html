    Document AI and Contact-Centre AI

    Part 1

    What it is

    In one sentenceDocument AI and contact-centre AI turn pages and phone calls into fields in a bank system, and flag the fields they are unsure about for a person to check.

    Running example: a letter of credit (LC). An importer in Jebel Ali buys steel from a Turkish supplier. A bank promises to pay the supplier only if the right documents arrive and match the LC on amount, goods, ports, dates and names. A typo in a name, or a shipment one day late, is a discrepancy. The bank pays against documents, not goods.

    The call centre is the same problem. A customer rings in Gulf Arabic, slips into English for "transfer" and "limit", and the words must become notes and fields.

    How it differs from other AI work

    • Not a chatbot. The output is structured fields that feed a system, not a conversation.
    • Not plain OCR. Optical character recognition (OCR) only reads characters. Document AI also decides that "5,000" is an amount, not a postcode, then checks it.
    • Every step can be wrong in a way that looks normal on screen. So the model must be right and know when it is not.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Capture and classify. Clean the image, split a 40-page bundle, and label each page: invoice, bill of lading, trade licence.
    2. Read. OCR with layout, or a vision-language model (VLM) that reads the page directly from a prompt. VLMs are flexible but slower and need guardrails.
    3. Extract. Pull named fields, each with a confidence and the page box it came from, so a person can click through to the evidence.
    4. Check. Code does exact checks (amount within tolerance, shipment before the latest date). A language model handles fuzzy ones, such as whether "reinforcing steel bars, grade 500B" matches "rebar B500B".
    5. Route. Auto-accept confident fields. Send the rest to a person, with the problem highlighted.

    Rule of thumb: code for the exact, a model for the fuzzy, a person for the disputed. Build it as a second pair of eyes: the AI flags, the checker decides.

    The call-centre side

    Automatic speech recognition (ASR) turns audio into text. Its score is the word error rate (WER): the share of words substituted, dropped or invented. A WER of 20% means about one word in five is wrong. Then a language model writes the summary, picks the queue and scores quality.

    Part 3

    How to read the numbers

    • Confidence is only useful if honest. A model is calibrated if, of fields it scores "1% chance of error", about 1% really are wrong. Among 1,000 fields scored 99% sure, 10 errors is fine. 40 means it is overconfident.
    • Straight-through processing (STP) is the share of documents with no human touch. Raising it saves money but lets errors through. Set the confidence cut-off per field, using real documents.
    • Misses cost more than false alarms. A false flag costs a checker a few minutes. A missed discrepancy may mean paying on bad documents. Like a smoke alarm, accept some false alarms. High recall (real problems caught) matters more than high precision (flags that are real).
    • WER hides what matters. An average over all words says nothing about names and numbers.

    Reading it: a topic needs one cue word out of a few ("card", "stolen"), so it usually survives. A name needs every word right. A 12-digit number needs every digit right, and one wrong digit is a wrong account. So a transcript that sounds fine gives unusable account numbers. Use keypad entry, a customer look-up or read-back for digits.

    Also, a vendor's 8% WER on clean studio Modern Standard Arabic can be 30% on your Gulf calls: dialect, mixed English and phone compression all add error. Measure on your own audio.

    Show the arithmetic

    If each word fails independently at rate w, a run of n words is all right with probability (1 − w)n. At w = 0.2: three words give 0.8³ ≈ 51%, twelve digits give 0.8¹² ≈ 7%. Real errors cluster, so treat this as a guide, not a measurement.

    Part 4

    How much to automate

    Choose by the cost of an error, whether it can be undone, and whether a person sees the output first.

    LevelWho decidesExample
    AssistPerson decides, AI suggestsLC discrepancies, transfers, limit increases
    Automate with samplingAI acts, people audit a sampleCall summaries, routing, field extraction on low-value items
    Automate fullyAI alone, monitoredLow-stakes, reversible steps; a card block behind strong authentication, with an easy route to a human

    Strong at: clearing routine pages fast, giving reviewers the source box, scoring every call instead of a 2% sample.

    Weak at: confident wrong reads, forged documents (a perfectly read fake is still fake), names in Arabic and English ("Mohammed", "Muhammad" and "محمد" are one person), and fluent summaries that drop a promised call-back or invent an amount.

    Onboarding packs (trade licence, passports, proof of address) are the same pipeline with three additions: authenticity and liveness checks, name matching across scripts, and care over where passport images are processed.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • One headline accuracy

      "97% accurate" averages easy fields (document type) with costly ones (amount, date). Ask for accuracy per field and the cost of each error.

    • Trusting a demo

      Vendor numbers come from clean documents and audio. Hand-label about 200 of your own calls and documents, and measure yourself.

    • Silent drift

      A supplier changes its layout and accuracy halves unnoticed. Track accuracy by document source every week and sample auto-accepted fields against a person.

    • Believing the summary

      Check amounts, dates and commitments against the transcript in code, not by eye.

    1. Why is "97% accuracy" a weak claim for LC extraction?

    It averages cheap errors with costly ones. A wrong amount or date can mean paying on non-compliant documents.

    2. A model is "99% sure" on 1,000 fields and 40 are wrong. What does that tell you?

    It is overconfident: 99% sure implies about 10 errors. Check confidence against human labels before setting thresholds.

    3. Why not leave exact LC checks to a language model?

    Code does them perfectly, cheaply and can show the rule that fired. Keep the model for reading and fuzzy matching.

    4. Why is 20% WER fine for routing but not for account numbers?

    Routing needs one cue word. An account number needs every digit right, and errors compound: about 7% of 12-digit numbers come out right.

    5. A trade licence is extracted cleanly. Is it genuine?

    Unknown. Extraction only reads the page, and a forgery reads perfectly. Authenticity needs separate checks.

    In banking, this is trade finance checking, onboarding packs and call-centre notes: the work that turns human paper and speech into system records.
