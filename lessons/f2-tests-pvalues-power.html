    Hypothesis Tests, P-values and Power

    Part 1

    What a p-value is

    In one sentenceA p-value is how often pure luck would produce a result at least this striking. Small means "this would be rare by chance".

    A friend claims to be psychic and calls 9 of 10 coin tosses. A plain guesser manages 9 or more only about 1 time in 100. That 1 in 100 (p = 0.01) is the p-value: it measures your surprise, and nothing else.

    How it differs from what people assume

    • It is not the chance the idea is true. It assumes "nothing is going on" and asks how odd your data look under that assumption.
    • It is not the size of the effect. A tiny effect can have a tiny p-value if you have enough data.
    • A hypothesis test is just the routine: assume nothing is happening (the null), compute the p-value, and call the result "significant" if p is below a cut-off, usually 0.05.

    How a test works

    Running example: a website tests a new button. Old button clicks 4% of the time, new one 5%.

    1. Assume the new button does nothing.
    2. Measure the gap in click rates between the two groups of visitors.
    3. Ask how often a gap this big appears by luck if the button does nothing.
    4. That frequency is the p-value. Below 0.05, call it significant; above, say you cannot tell.
    Part 2

    How to read it

    • p = 0.03: if the button did nothing, a gap this big shows up 3 times in 100. Surprising, but not proof.
    • p = 0.30: luck produces this often. You have learned very little, which is not the same as "no effect".
    • "97% likely to work" is wrong. If only 1 idea in 10 really works, a large share of significant results (a third or more) are still false alarms.
    • Significant is not big. With two million visitors a 0.01 point lift is "highly significant" and worthless. Report the effect with its confidence interval: "up 0.6 points, between 0.4 and 0.8" says far more than "p = 0.01".

    Many looks, one winner

    Every test has a 1 in 20 chance of a false alarm. Test 40 customer slices when nothing works and you expect about 2 false winners. Try it.

    • The fix: raise the bar when you look at many things (the Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg methods do this), or simply rerun the winning slice as a fresh test.
    • Peeking is the same trap. Check daily and stop the first time p dips under 0.05, and about 27% of tests with no effect declare a winner. Fix the sample size in advance, or use a method built for repeated looks.
    • Choosing after the fact (which days, which users to exclude) does it invisibly. Name the metric, sample size and slices before launch.

    Not significant is not "no effect"

    Power is the chance a test spots an effect that is really there, conventionally aimed at 80%. Like a metal detector, a weak test only finds big coins.

    • Halve the effect you want to detect and you need about four times the visitors. Rare events such as fraud need huge samples.
    • Weak tests exaggerate. Only estimates that noise pushed up clear the bar, so a significant small pilot overstates the effect. A 40% pilot uplift may be 12% at rollout.
    • Check the interval. If it includes effects that matter, the honest answer is "we don't know yet".

    In banking, a champion-challenger test of a new collections message or credit rule needs its sample size and success measure set before launch.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading p as the chance it works

      p = 0.03 is not 97%. It assumes nothing is happening and measures how odd the data look.

    • Celebrating one slice

      One of 40 slices at p = 0.02 is what luck produces. Treat it as a hypothesis and retest.

    • Stopping when p dips below 0.05

      Daily peeking inflates false alarms. Decide the sample size first.

    • Calling a small test "no effect"

      A low-power test misses real effects. Look at the interval.

    1. What does p = 0.04 mean in plain words?

    If nothing were going on, luck alone would give a result this striking about 4 times in 100.

    2. The overall test is flat, but 1 of 40 slices shows p = 0.02. What now?

    Forty looks give about 2 false winners by luck. Rerun that slice as a fresh test before acting.

    3. With millions of visitors the p-value is tiny. Is the change worth shipping?

    Not necessarily. Check the effect size and interval: it may be real but far too small to matter.

    4. A pilot with 20% power shows a significant 30% uplift. What should the business case assume?

    Much less, since weak tests exaggerate. Run a properly powered confirmation test.
