    Hypothesis Tests, P-values, Many Comparisons and Power

    What a p-value really tells you, why slicing a campaign forty ways always finds a winner, why checking a test every morning breaks it, and how many customers a test needs before it can show anything at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA p-value only says how surprising the data would be if nothing were going on. Whether a “significant” result is real depends on how many things were tried, how many times someone looked, how plausible it was to begin with, and whether the test had enough customers to see a real effect at all.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    It's Monday, and the cards team is presenting a campaign. Last month they sent 10,000 credit card customers an SMS offering 0% instalments on any purchase over AED 2,000. Another 10,000 customers, chosen at random, got nothing. Overall, uptake was almost identical in the two groups.

    Then comes the slide everyone remembers: “Among expatriates aged 25–34 who bank through the app, uptake rose 41%, p = 0.02. We recommend a targeted rollout.” The head of retail turns to you. Is it real?

    You can't answer from the p-value alone, and the reasons are what this lesson is about. A p-value answers one narrow question: if the offer did nothing at all, how often would chance alone produce a result at least this striking? Here that's about 2 times in 100. Rare, for one test. But the team looked at 40 segments. If you roll forty dice, some will come up six.

    The simulation below makes the narrow question concrete. Each run is a whole campaign: 5,000 customers per group, 4% normal uptake, and a two-group test at the end. Start with a world where no idea ever works, and look at the p-values that come out.

    Three things should stand out.

    • When nothing is going on, every p-value is equally likely. A p of 0.03 is no rarer than a p of 0.73. Below 0.05 you get exactly 5% of the campaigns, because that's what 0.05 means. Those are the false alarms you signed up for.
    • Real effects pile up near zero, if the test is big enough to see them. They don't all make it. At this size, about a third of the offers that genuinely work still come out “not significant”.
    • The chance a significant result is real is a base-rate question. If only 1 idea in 10 works, the 5% of false alarms from the nine duds is comparable to the hits from the one good idea. This is exactly the fraud-alert arithmetic from F1.4, with “idea” in place of “transaction”.
    Hold onto thisA p-value measures surprise under “nothing is happening”. It doesn't measure the chance the effect is real, or how big it is. To judge a result, you also need to know how many tests and looks produced it, how plausible it was before anyone looked, and whether the test was big enough to see a real effect.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The null hypothesis is a straw man, on purpose

    A hypothesis test is proof by contradiction, with noise. You set up the claim you'd like to knock down, the null hypothesis: “the offer changes nothing”. You assume it's true, work out what kind of data you'd usually see, and ask whether your actual data look out of place.

    The null is a straw man by design. It's chosen because it's precise enough to calculate with, not because anyone believes it. That has two consequences people forget:

    • Rejecting the null doesn't prove your explanation. It says “exactly nothing” fits poorly. A broken randomisation, a tracking bug, a holiday that hit one group harder or, in non-randomised data, a confounder (F2.6) would all knock the straw man down too.
    • Failing to reject doesn't prove the null. “Not significant” often just means the test was too small to tell. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, unless the test had the power to find something (see power).

    And a quirk: in a big enough sample, the null is almost always false. No offer changes behaviour by exactly zero. With two million card customers, a 0.02-point change in activation will be “highly significant” and commercially worthless. That's why significance and size have to be kept apart.

    What a p-value is, and what it isn't

    p=P(a result at least this extreme∣H0is true)

    The probability, computed assuming the null hypothesis H0 and the model's other assumptions, of seeing data at least as far from “nothing” as yours. It is a statement about the data given the null, never about the null given the data.

    Because it's a probability computed under the null, its distribution under the null is flat, as Simulation 1 showed. That flatness is the whole guarantee: “reject when p < 0.05” means you'll wrongly reject 5% of the time when nothing is going on. Nothing more.

    In 2016 the American Statistical Association took the unusual step of issuing a formal statement on p-values, because misuse had become so widespread. Its six principles, paraphrased:

    1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
    2. They do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by chance alone.
    3. Business and policy decisions should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a threshold.
    4. Proper inference needs full reporting and transparency: every test run, not just the ones shown.
    5. A p-value does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
    6. By itself, a p-value is not a good measure of evidence for a model or hypothesis.

    So when someone says “p = 0.03, so there's a 97% chance the offer works”, that's principle 2 broken. The 97% figure needs a prior, as the next sections show. And “p = 0.03 means a 3% chance the result is a fluke” is the same mistake in a different coat.

    One more trap is worth naming because segment analyses are full of it: the difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not itself significant. “The offer worked for men (p = 0.03) but not for women (p = 0.20)” is not evidence that it worked differently. The two estimates may be nearly identical, with one interval just touching zero. To claim a difference between groups, test the difference.

    Significance is not size: report the effect and its interval

    The p-value mixes two things: how big the effect looks, and how much data you have. A tiny effect with huge data and a big effect with little data can give the same p. Decisions need them separated, and a confidence interval does that.

    estimate±1.96×SE

    A 95% interval: the estimated effect plus or minus about two standard errors (F2.1). It contains exactly the values that a 5% test would not reject, so it carries the test inside it, plus the size and the uncertainty.

    Compare two ways of reporting the same campaign:

    • “Uptake increased significantly (p = 0.01).”
    • “Uptake rose by 0.6 percentage points, 95% interval 0.1 to 1.1. At our margins that's worth between AED 0.4m and AED 4.5m a year; break-even is AED 1m.”

    The second tells the head of retail what she needs: the plausible range, and where it sits against the number that matters. The interval straddles break-even, so the honest conclusion is “probably worth it, not certain”, which a p-value can't express. Wherever you can, translate the interval into AED, customers or hours.

    The tests you'll meet, at intuition level

    Almost every classical test is the same shape: signal divided by noise. Take the difference you observed, subtract what the null says it should be (usually zero), and divide by its standard error. If the result is far from zero in standard-error units, the null looks implausible.

    z=p^B−p^Ap¯(1−p¯)(1nA+1nB)

    The two-proportion z-test used in Simulation 1. The top is the difference in uptake rates. The bottom is its standard error if the null were true, using the pooled rate p̄ across both groups. |z| above 1.96 gives p below 0.05.

    You won't need to derive these, and Claude Code will write them. You do need to know which question each one answers, and which assumption breaks first.

    QuestionUsual testWhat breaks it in a bank
    Did uptake, approval or default rates differ?Two-proportion z-test; chi-square for several groups; Fisher's exact test for small countsTreating transactions as independent when you randomised customers. Analyse at the unit you randomised.
    Did average spend or balance differ?Welch's t-test (doesn't assume equal variances); a z-test at large n by the central limit theoremHeavy-tailed AED amounts. A few huge spenders make the mean jumpy and the normal approximation slow to kick in (F1.5). Consider trimming, logs or a bootstrap (F2.2).
    Did the distribution shift?Mann–Whitney (rank) test; Kolmogorov–SmirnovReading Mann–Whitney as a test of means. It isn't, and the two can disagree.
    Same customers or questions, two conditions?Paired t-test; McNemar's test for paired yes-or-noAnalysing paired data as two independent groups, which throws away most of the precision. Common in LLM evals.
    Is complaint category linked to channel?Chi-square test of independenceLarge tables of sparse cells. And “linked” is not “caused”.

    A good rule for reading any test: find the standard error, and ask whether it was computed at the right level. If you randomised branches, the effective sample size is the number of branches, not the number of customers in them. Getting that wrong shrinks the standard error and manufactures significance. It is one of the most common errors in real bank analyses. Behind most of these tests sits a likelihood ratio (F1.6), which matters again when we get to sequential testing.

    When is a significant result real?

    Think of your whole experimentation programme as a classifier. Its inputs are ideas; it flags some as “significant”. The p-value threshold sets its false positive rate on duds, α. Power sets its detection rate on ideas that work, 1 − β. And how often ideas really work is the base rate, π. Then the chance a flagged idea is real is precision, worked out with Bayes' rule exactly as in F7:

    P(real∣significant)=π(1−β)π(1−β)+(1−π)α

    π is the share of ideas that truly work, 1 − β is power, α is the significance level. This is the “theory” figure in Simulation 1's last box.

    The base rate of good ideas is lower than most teams believe. Kohavi, Deng and Vermeer collected success rates from large experimentation programmes: about a third of experiments succeeded at Microsoft, about 15% at Bing, and around 10% at Booking.com, Google Ads and Netflix. Airbnb's search team saw 8%.

    Plug in the median, 10%, with 80% power. Shipping decisions only count wins in the good direction, so the relevant false positive rate is half of 0.05, which is 0.025. Then 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08 of ideas are true wins and 0.9 × 0.025 = 0.0225 are false ones. So about 22% of “significant wins” are false, not 5%. At Airbnb's 8%, it's about 26%. In a bank, where many campaigns are small tweaks to mature products, there's little reason to think the rate is higher.

    Two practical consequences follow. A surprising result about an implausible idea needs a lower p, or a replication, before anyone acts on it. And low power doesn't just mean fewer wins; it means a larger share of your wins are false. Power is in the formula's numerator.

    Watch out“Significant at 95%” does not mean “95% likely to be real”. That phrase appears on vendor dashboards and in AI-written summaries all the time. The real figure depends on the base rate and the power, and can easily be 60–80%.

    Forty slices: family-wise error and the false discovery rate

    Back to Monday's slide. Five age bands, two nationality groups and four channels make 40 segments. If the offer did nothing anywhere, each segment still has a 5% chance of a p below 0.05. The chance that at least one does is:

    P(at least one false winner)=1−0.9540≈87%

    This is the family-wise error rate (FWER). In general it is 1 − (1 − α)m for m independent tests: each segment escapes a false alarm with probability 0.95, and all 40 must escape. On average you'd expect 40 × 0.05 = 2 false winners per campaign. The slide is almost guaranteed to have something on it.

    There are two families of fix, and they control different things.

    • Control the family-wise error rate: keep the chance of even one false winner at 5%. Bonferroni tests each segment at α ÷ m, here 0.05 ÷ 40 = 0.00125. Holm gives the same guarantee and is never worse: sort the p-values, test the smallest at α ÷ m, the next at α ÷ (m − 1), and so on, stopping at the first one that fails.
    • Control the false discovery rate (FDR): accept some false winners, but keep their share of the winners at about q, say 5%. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) sorts the p-values and finds the largest rank k whose p-value is at most k × q ÷ m, then declares the top k all winners.
    BH: reject theksmallest, wherek=max{i:p(i)≤imq}

    p(i) is the i-th smallest p-value. The threshold grows with rank, so once a few strong results are in, weaker ones get an easier bar. That's why BH finds more real effects than Bonferroni when several exist.

    Notice that with no real effects at all, all three corrections give about the same 5% chance of any false winner. They differ only when something is really there. That's why the choice between them is about the cost of each kind of mistake:

    • Use FWER control (Holm, rather than Bonferroni) when any single false claim is expensive: a result going to a regulator or the board, a fair-lending finding about a customer group, or the one headline number in a press release.
    • Use FDR control (BH) when you're screening many candidates for follow-up: which of 200 AML scenarios to retune, which branches to investigate, which segments deserve a proper retest. A few false leads are acceptable if most are real.

    Every correction needs an honest m: all the comparisons made, including the ones that never reached the slides. And none of them is as good as the cheapest fix of all: run the winning segment again, in a fresh test, as the only hypothesis. A real effect survives replication. A lucky one shrinks back to nothing.

    A more modern alternative to corrections is to estimate all 40 segments together in a hierarchical model, which pulls each noisy segment estimate towards the overall average in proportion to how little data it has. Gelman, Hill and Yajima argue this usually makes multiple-comparison corrections unnecessary, because the shrinkage does the work: a 41% uplift from 300 customers gets pulled most of the way back to the overall result.

    Forking paths and p-hacking

    Forty segments is the visible version of the problem. The invisible version is worse, because no one is consciously running multiple tests. Gelman and Loken call it the garden of forking paths. At each step of an analysis there's a reasonable choice that depends on what the data look like:

    • Measure uptake at 30 days, or 60? Count instalment plans, or spend converted to instalments?
    • Exclude dormant accounts? Staff accounts? Customers who called the contact centre during the campaign?
    • Trim the top 1% of spenders, or not? Adjust for tenure, or not?
    • Analyse all customers, or only those who opened the SMS?

    Each choice is defensible. But if the choices are made after seeing the data, even one at a time and in good faith, the analysis has effectively tried many versions and reported one. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed that just four common flexibilities (choice among two outcomes, adding more data, controlling for a variable, dropping a condition) could push the false positive rate from 5% to about 61%.

    P-hacking is the deliberate version: rerunning with different choices until p dips below 0.05. Its fingerprints are recognisable. P-values cluster just under 0.05. The metric in the results isn't the one in the plan. Odd exclusions appear with no rationale. The words “trending towards significance” appear. Results were run “a few different ways” and one is shown.

    How much do analyst choices matter even without bad intent? In a famous study, 29 teams were given the same football dataset and the same question: are darker-skinned players given more red cards? Twenty teams found a significant effect and nine didn't, with estimates ranging from slightly below no effect to nearly triple the odds. Same data, same question, different reasonable paths.

    Peeking, and tests built to be watched

    Now add time. A digital onboarding team runs a 28-day test of a new account-opening flow. The dashboard updates every morning, and on day 9 the p-value dips to 0.04. They stop the test and ship. What's wrong?

    The 5% guarantee assumes you look once, at a sample size fixed in advance. A running test's z-statistic wanders as data arrive, like a random walk. Check it every day and stop the first time it crosses 1.96, and you've given chance 28 opportunities instead of one. Armitage and colleagues worked this out in 1969: with enough looks, a test that stops at the first p < 0.05 will eventually “succeed” with probability approaching 1, even when nothing is going on.

    The sequential designs in the simulation are the two main families used in industry today.

    • Group sequential tests come from clinical trials, where stopping early for a drug that clearly works, or clearly harms, matters ethically. You fix the maximum duration and the looks in advance, and spend the 5% error budget across them. The O'Brien–Fleming shape spends almost nothing early, so the bar at the final look is barely above 1.96 and power is close to that of a single fixed look.
    • Always-valid inference lets you look whenever you like, stop whenever you like, and even extend the test, with the guarantee intact. The mixture sequential probability ratio test (mSPRT), adapted for A/B testing by Johari and colleagues with Optimizely, is the best-known version. The price is a wider boundary, so less power at any fixed sample size.

    Spotify's experimentation team compared the two and concluded that group sequential tests are as good or better whenever you can estimate the maximum sample size in advance. Always-valid methods earn their keep when you can't, when tests may run longer than planned, or when you need to catch a harmful change in streaming data quickly, such as a bad app release.

    The theory underneath always-valid inference is now usually told through e-values, and the intuition is a bet. You start with AED 1 and bet against the null, using bets that would be fair if the null were true. If the null is true you can't expect to make money, however cleverly you bet, or when you stop. If your wealth reaches AED 20, you reject.

    PH0(wealth ever reaches1/α)≤α

    Ville's inequality. A fair game can't often multiply your money by 20, so under the null, the chance of ever hitting AED 20 is at most 1 in 20, however long you watch. The running wealth is an e-process; the mSPRT's likelihood ratio is one.

    Power, the minimum detectable effect, and the 1/effect² rule

    Everything so far has been about false positives. Power is about the other error: the chance your test detects an effect that really exists. Four quantities are locked together, and fixing any three determines the fourth:

    • α, the false positive rate you accept, usually 5%.
    • Power (1 − β), the chance of detecting a real effect of a given size, conventionally 80%.
    • The effect size you care about. The smallest effect worth detecting, often the one that would pay for the change, is the minimum detectable effect (MDE).
    • The sample size, n per group.
    n≈(z1−α/2+z1−β)2[p0(1−p0)+p1(1−p1)]δ2

    Customers per group to compare rates p0 and p1, which differ by δ. With α = 5% and 80% power, the bracketed constant (1.96 + 0.84)² is about 7.85. The key fact is the δ² underneath: halve the effect you want to detect, and you need four times the customers.

    For a relative change r on a low base rate p, the formula simplifies to about 15.7 ÷ (p × r²) per group. The base rate is in the denominator. Rare outcomes need enormous tests, which is why fraud and default experiments are so hard to run:

    Run the power calculation before the test, and treat its answer as a design constraint. If the cards team only has 3,000 eligible customers per arm, the test can only reliably detect an uplift of about a third (5% → 6.7%). Better to know that before spending the campaign budget than to discover it in a “not significant” result that no one can interpret.

    Underpowered tests lie twice: the winner's curse

    An underpowered test doesn't just miss real effects. When it does produce a significant result, that result is systematically too big. The reason is visible in Simulation 4. With low power, the bar sits far out in the tail of the green curve. The only estimates that clear it are the ones where noise happened to push the estimate well above the truth. Filtering on significance selects the lucky ones.

    Gelman and Carlin named the two resulting errors. A type M (magnitude) error is a significant estimate that exaggerates the true effect; the exaggeration ratio is how much, on average. A type S (sign) error is a significant estimate pointing the wrong way. At 80% power the exaggeration is small, about 1.1×. At 20% power it's about 2.3×. At 8% power it's nearly 5×, and almost 1 significant result in 10 has the wrong sign.

    This is the pattern behind the pilot that “delivered a 40% uplift” and then delivered 12% at rollout. Nobody did anything wrong at rollout. The pilot's number was inflated by the act of only reporting it because it was significant. It's also why the 41% uplift in Monday's segment should be read as “if anything is there, it's probably much smaller”: that segment had a few hundred customers, so only an inflated estimate could have reached significance.

    One related trap: post-hoc power, computed by plugging the observed effect back into the power formula, adds nothing. It's just a re-expression of the p-value. Power is a property of the design, computed in advance for an effect size you care about.

    Variance reduction: CUPED and its successors

    If you can't get more customers, reduce the noise. Much of the variation in what customers do during a test is variation in who they are: heavy spenders keep spending heavily, whichever group they're in. CUPED (controlled experiments using pre-experiment data), introduced by Microsoft's experimentation team in 2013, removes the part of each customer's outcome that was predictable from before the test started.

    Yadj=Y−θ(X−X¯),Var(Yadj)=(1−ρ2)Var(Y)

    Y is the outcome during the test, such as card spend. X is the same customer's value before the test, such as last quarter's spend. θ is the regression slope of Y on X, and ρ is their correlation. Since variance drives the sample size, the test needs a factor of 1 − ρ² fewer customers.

    The adjustment doesn't bias the comparison, because X was fixed before randomisation, so it's balanced across groups on average. All it does is remove noise. And the gains are large where bank metrics are persistent. Spend, balances and transaction counts are often correlated 0.6–0.8 with their own past. At ρ = 0.7, the variance roughly halves, so the test needs about half the customers or half the time. For a yes-or-no outcome like first-time uptake of a new product, the correlation with past behaviour is usually much weaker, and so is the gain.

    The successors widen the covariate set. CUPAC, from DoorDash, uses a machine-learning prediction of the outcome, built only from pre-experiment features, as the covariate. Regression adjustment with several covariates is the general form, and it's what Statsig's 2025 CURE uses, with lasso to pick the covariates. Work from Etsy also folds in carefully chosen in-experiment covariates. The rule that keeps all of them honest is the same: covariates must be unaffected by the treatment. Adjust for something the offer itself changed, and you bias the result. Stratified randomisation, for example balancing groups by segment and prior spend before the test starts, achieves similar gains at the design stage.

    Pre-registration in a bank analytics team

    Almost every problem in this lesson has the same cure: make the decisions before you see the data. In science that's called pre-registration. In a bank analytics team it can be a one-page analysis plan, agreed with the business owner and saved in the ticket or the repository before launch:

    • Hypothesis and decision. “The instalment SMS raises 30-day uptake. If the interval's lower end clears break-even, we roll out.”
    • Population and randomisation unit. Who is eligible, who is excluded and why, and whether customers, accounts or branches are randomised.
    • One primary metric, its exact definition and window. Others are secondary or guardrails: complaints, opt-outs, contact-centre calls, credit losses.
    • MDE, sample size and duration from the power calculation, and the stopping rule: fixed horizon, or a named sequential design.
    • Pre-declared segments, at most two or three, with a reason. Everything else is labelled exploratory and needs a retest.
    • The analysis: test, CUPED covariate, handling of outliers, and the code if possible.

    It takes an hour. It protects the analyst as much as the bank: when the result disappoints, nobody can ask you to “try it a few other ways”, and when it succeeds, model risk, internal audit and the business can trust it. Results that weren't in the plan can still be shown. They just carry the label “exploratory: needs confirmation”.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Tests, corrections and power sit underneath every claim that something “worked”. These are the links you'll use most.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rule and base ratesA significant result is a positive test

    The chance a win is real is P(real | significant), Bayes' rule with the share of good ideas as the prior. Same maths as the fraud alert that's usually a false alarm.

    F1.5 · Distributions and the CLTWhy z-tests work, and when they don't

    The central limit theorem makes most test statistics roughly normal. Heavy-tailed AED amounts slow that down, so tests on mean spend need more care.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodTests are likelihood ratios

    The likelihood ratio test underlies most classical tests, and the sequential probability ratio test, mSPRT and e-values all run on likelihood ratios too.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision and recallFDR is one minus precision

    Your testing programme is a classifier of ideas. α is its false positive rate, power its recall, and the false discovery rate is one minus its precision.

    F2.1 · Standard errors and intervalsThe interval is the test, turned inside out

    A 95% interval holds every value a 5% test wouldn't reject. Report the interval; the p-value is a lossy summary of it.

    F2.2 · The bootstrapTests for awkward metrics

    Median spend, a ratio of sums, a 90th percentile: when there's no neat formula for the standard error, resample.

    F2.6 · Confounding and selection biasA p-value says nothing about cause

    Without randomisation, a tiny p-value can come entirely from who chose to take the offer. Significance is not identification.

    F3.6 · A/B testing and experimental designThe design around the inference

    Randomisation units, sample ratio mismatch checks, guardrails, novelty effects and holdouts. This lesson is the inference; F3.6 is the experiment.

    F3.7 · Writing an analysis that survives reviewPopulation, baseline, effect, uncertainty

    Every result reported with its interval, the number of comparisons made, and whether it was pre-planned.

    B6.2 · Propensity and upliftSegment effects, done properly

    Uplift models estimate who responds to an offer. They're the principled answer to “which segment did it work in”, and they need holdouts to validate.

    A4.1 · Statistics for evalsLLM evals are experiments too

    Two models on 200 questions is a paired test with a standard error. Trying 15 prompts and keeping the best is a multiple comparison.

    G3.3 · Independent validationWhat a validator will ask

    Was the analysis pre-specified? How many specifications were tried? Was the test powered for the effect claimed? Expect these questions.

    R8.3 · Modern A/B testingThe research frontier

    E-values, CUPED variants, switchback designs and interference between customers, in depth.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core theory is a century old. What's changed is the scale: companies run thousands of experiments a year, analysts can try hundreds of specifications in an afternoon, and AI models are compared on leaderboards with no error bars. The live work is in tests you can watch safely, squeezing more power out of the same customers, and bringing basic statistical hygiene to AI evaluation.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in campaign reviews, vendor pitches, model validation packs and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the fastest check.

    • “p = 0.03, so we're 97% sure it works”

      The p-value is P(data | nothing going on), not P(nothing going on | data). The real chance depends on the base rate of good ideas and the power.

      Spot itAsk how often ideas like this have worked before, and what the test's power was. Then do the Bayes sum.
    • “Not significant, so the offer doesn't work”

      A small test can't see a moderate effect. “No significant difference” from 2,000 customers is mostly a statement about the test.

      Spot itAsk for the confidence interval. If it includes effects big enough to matter, the answer is “we don't know”, not “no”.
    • The segment winner

      The overall result is flat, but one of many segments is significant, and that's the headline.

      Spot itAsk how many segments were examined in total, whether this one was pre-declared, and whether the effect replicated in a fresh test.
    • Stopped on a good day

      The test was planned for four weeks and stopped on day 9 when p dipped below 0.05.

      Spot itAsk what the planned duration was and what the stopping rule was. If there wasn't a sequential design, the p-value is invalid.
    • The metric changed after the fact

      The plan said 30-day uptake; the result reports 60-day spend converted, which happened to be significant.

      Spot itCompare the reported primary metric with the pre-launch plan or ticket. If there was no plan, assume the forking paths were walked.
    • Significant but trivial

      “A highly significant improvement (p < 0.001)” of 0.02 percentage points, measured on two million customers.

      Spot itAsk for the effect in AED or customers, with an interval, against the cost of the change.
    • The pilot that shrank at rollout

      A small pilot showed a 40% uplift; rollout delivered 12%. The pilot was underpowered, so only an exaggerated estimate could have reached significance.

      Spot itAsk what the pilot's power was for a realistic effect. Below about 50%, expect the significant estimate to be inflated, and plan the business case on a smaller number.
    • The wrong unit of analysis

      Customers were randomised, but the analysis treats each of their transactions as independent, or branches were randomised and customers counted. The standard error is far too small.

      Spot itCheck that the n in the test matches the number of things randomised. If it's ten times larger, the p-value is wrong.
    • Comparing significance across groups

      “It worked for nationals (p = 0.02) but not expatriates (p = 0.30), so we'll target nationals.”

      Spot itAsk for a test of the difference between the two effects. Usually their intervals overlap heavily.
    • LLM evals with no error bars

      “The new prompt scores 84% versus 82% on our 200-question test set.” The standard error of each score is about 2.7 points, and the best of 15 prompt variants was picked on the same set.

      Spot itAsk for the standard error, a paired comparison on the same questions, how many variants were tried, and a score on held-out questions.
    • AI-generated analysis that tests everything

      Typical signs: a table of 30 p-values with no correction, “p = 0.000”, “trending towards significance”, post-hoc power from the observed effect, or a t-test on heavily skewed spend with no comment.

      Spot itCount the tests in the output, and ask the tool which were planned. Then ask for intervals in business units. If it can't separate confirmatory from exploratory, you'll have to.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The campaign didn't move uptake overall. The team found one segment where it looked strong, but they checked 40, and by chance alone you'd expect a couple to look like that. That segment was also small, so if there's an effect it's probably much smaller than 41%. I wouldn't roll out on this. I'd run a short, properly sized test on that segment alone. It needs about four weeks and costs little, and if it holds up we'll know it's real.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I treat the p-value as surprise under the null, and report effects with intervals in business units. Before launch I fix the primary metric, MDE and sample size from a power calculation, with CUPED on pre-period data to cut variance, and pre-declare a few segments. Anything else gets Holm or BH depending on whether a single false claim or the false share matters, and goes to replication. If the business will watch the dashboard, I use a group sequential design when the horizon is known, or an always-valid one when it isn't. And I discount significant results from low-power tests for the winner's curse.”
    “The p-value is 0.04. So it's real, isn't it?”

    It means the result would be fairly surprising if the change did nothing. Whether it's real depends on things the p-value doesn't include: how many tests and segments we looked at, whether we stopped the test early, and how plausible the change was to start with. For a typical idea, a single p of 0.04 is maybe a 60–80% chance of something real. The interval tells us how big it might be, and that's what the decision should rest on.

    “Why can't we just stop the test as soon as it's significant? We'd save weeks.”

    Because the 5% error rate assumes one look. Checking every day and stopping at the first p below 0.05 gives a false positive rate of around 25–30% over four weeks. If early stopping matters, we can use a sequential design built for it. It sets a stricter bar early on and still lets us stop as soon as the evidence is strong, with the 5% guarantee intact.

    “The test wasn't significant. Can we conclude the new process makes no difference?”

    Only if the test could have seen a difference that matters. I'd look at the confidence interval. If it runs from −0.2 to +0.3 points and anything under 0.5 is commercially irrelevant, then yes, we can say any effect is too small to matter. If it runs from −1 to +2, the test was too small and we've learnt very little.

    “Why do we need 750,000 transactions to test a fraud rule? The marketing tests use 30,000.”

    Because the sample size scales with one over the base rate and one over the effect squared. Fraud is about 0.2% of transactions, against roughly 5% uptake for a card offer, so each transaction carries far less information. Detecting a 10% relative change in a rate that low needs hundreds of thousands per group. Alternatives are to test on a higher-rate population, use a more frequent proxy outcome, or use variance reduction. Or accept that we can only detect larger effects.

    “Should we use Bonferroni on everything to be safe?”

    Not everything. Bonferroni protects against even one false claim, which is right for a regulatory finding or a board headline. Holm gives the same protection with more power, so I'd use Holm there. For screening many candidates for follow-up, like which AML scenarios to retune, controlling the share of false leads with Benjamini–Hochberg finds far more real ones. The bigger win is pre-declaring a small number of primary comparisons so there's less to correct.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A test gives p = 0.03. Which are true? (a) There's a 3% chance the null is true. (b) If the null were true, results this extreme would occur about 3% of the time. (c) The effect is large. (d) There's a 97% chance the effect is real.

    Only (b). (a) and (d) treat the p-value as the probability of the hypothesis, which needs a prior. (c) confuses significance with size. A tiny effect in a huge sample can give p = 0.03.

    2. You check 20 segments, and the offer truly did nothing anywhere. What's the chance at least one segment shows p < 0.05? How many false winners do you expect?

    1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64% chance of at least one. Expected number: 20 × 0.05 = 1.

    3. Ten sorted p-values: 0.001, 0.008, 0.039, 0.041, 0.042, 0.06, 0.074, 0.205, 0.212, 0.216. How many are declared at 5% by Bonferroni, by Holm, and by Benjamini–Hochberg?

    Bonferroni: threshold 0.05 ÷ 10 = 0.005, so only 0.001: 1.

    Holm: 0.001 ≤ 0.005, yes. Next, 0.008 against 0.05 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.0056: fails, stop. 1.

    BH: thresholds are k × 0.005: 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, … The largest k with p(k) under its threshold is k = 2 (0.008 ≤ 0.010). Ranks 3 to 10 all fail. 2.

    4. Uptake is 5%. You want 80% power to detect a rise to 5.5%. Roughly how many customers per group? And if you only care about a rise to 6%?

    n ≈ 7.85 × (0.05 × 0.95 + 0.055 × 0.945) ÷ 0.005² ≈ 7.85 × 0.0995 ÷ 0.000025 ≈ 31,000 per group. Doubling the effect to 1 point divides n by about 4: 7.85 × (0.0475 + 0.0564) ÷ 0.0001 ≈ 8,200.

    5. Only 10% of your team's ideas truly work. Tests have 80% power and α = 0.05. What share of significant results are real?

    True hits: 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08. False alarms: 0.9 × 0.05 = 0.045. Share real: 0.08 ÷ 0.125 = 64%. If you only count wins in the good direction (α effectively 0.025), it's 0.08 ÷ 0.1025 ≈ 78%, so about 22% are false: Kohavi's figure.

    6. A pilot had about 20% power for a realistic effect, and came back significant with a 30% uplift. What should the business case assume?

    At 20% power, significant estimates exaggerate the truth by about 2.3× on average, so something nearer 13% is a more realistic planning figure, and the true value could be lower still. Better: run a properly powered confirmation test before committing.

    7. The metric is monthly card spend, and last quarter's spend has a correlation of 0.6 with it. How much does CUPED reduce the sample you need?

    Variance is multiplied by 1 − 0.6² = 0.64, and the sample size scales with variance, so you need about 36% fewer customers, or the same customers for 64% of the time.

    8. A product owner checks a 4-week test daily and plans to stop at the first p < 0.05. What's roughly the false positive rate, and what would you propose instead?

    With about 28 looks, around 27% rather than 5%. Propose a sequential design. A group sequential test (for example an O'Brien–Fleming boundary at weekly looks) keeps most of the power when the horizon is known. An always-valid method such as mSPRT or a confidence sequence suits daily watching with no fixed end. Either way, agree the rule before launch.
