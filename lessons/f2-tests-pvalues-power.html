    Hypothesis Tests, P-values, Many Comparisons and Power

    What a p-value really tells you, why slicing a campaign forty ways always finds a winner, why checking a test every morning breaks it, and how many customers a test needs before it can show anything at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA p-value only says how surprising the data would be if nothing were going on. Whether a “significant” result is real depends on how many things were tried, how many times someone looked, how plausible it was to begin with, and whether the test had enough customers to see a real effect at all.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The cards team presents a campaign. 10,000 credit card customers got an SMS offering 0% instalments on purchases over AED 2,000; 10,000 random others got nothing. Overall uptake was almost identical.

    Then the slide: “Among expatriates aged 25–34 who bank through the app, uptake rose 41%, p = 0.02. We recommend a targeted rollout.” Is it real?

    The p-value alone can't tell you. It answers one narrow question: if the offer did nothing, how often would chance alone produce a result at least this striking? Here, about 2 times in 100. That is rare for one test, but the team looked at 40 segments. Roll forty dice and some will come up six.

    The simulation runs whole campaigns (5,000 customers per group, 4% normal uptake). Start with a world where no idea ever works and watch the p-values.

    • With nothing going on, every p-value is equally likely. 0.03 is no rarer than 0.73. Exactly 5% land below 0.05; that is what 0.05 means. Those are the false alarms you signed up for.
    • Real effects pile up near zero, if the test is big enough. Not all make it: here about a third of genuine offers still come out “not significant”.
    • Whether a significant result is real is a base-rate question. If only 1 idea in 10 works, the false alarms from the nine duds rival the hits from the one good idea. It is the fraud-alert arithmetic from F1.4, with “idea” in place of “transaction”.
    Hold onto thisA p-value measures surprise under “nothing is happening”. It is not the chance the effect is real, nor its size. To judge a result, also ask how many tests and looks produced it, how plausible it was beforehand, and whether the test was big enough to see a real effect.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The null hypothesis is a straw man, on purpose

    A hypothesis test is proof by contradiction, with noise. Like a court, it starts from “innocent until proven guilty”: assume the null hypothesis (“the offer changes nothing”), work out what data you'd usually see, and ask whether yours looks out of place.

    The null is a straw man by design: chosen because it is precise enough to calculate with, not because anyone believes it. Two consequences:

    • Rejecting the null doesn't prove your explanation. It says “exactly nothing” fits badly. A broken randomisation, a tracking bug, a holiday that hit one group, or (without randomisation) a confounder (F2.6) would knock it down too.
    • Failing to reject doesn't prove the null. “Not significant” usually means the test was too small. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, unless the test had the power to find something.

    Also, with a big enough sample the null is almost always false, because no offer changes behaviour by exactly zero. With two million customers, a 0.02-point change is “highly significant” and worthless. Keep significance and size apart.

    What a p-value is, and what it isn't

    p=P(a result at least this extreme∣H0is true)

    The probability, assuming the null H0 and the model's other assumptions, of data at least as far from “nothing” as yours. It is about the data given the null, never the null given the data.

    How to read it. Think of a smoke alarm: p is how rarely the alarm would sound if there were no fire. A small p (0.001) means “very hard to explain by chance”; p = 0.04 means only “somewhat hard”. Under the null it is flat (Simulation 1), so “reject when p < 0.05” means wrongly rejecting 5% of the time when nothing is going on. Nothing more.

    The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement, in three points:

    • A p-value shows how incompatible the data are with a specified model. It is not the probability the hypothesis is true, or that chance alone produced the data.
    • It does not measure effect size or importance, and alone is weak evidence. Decisions shouldn't hinge on a threshold.
    • Honest inference needs full reporting: every test run, not just those shown.

    So “p = 0.03, so there's a 97% chance the offer works” is wrong; the 97% needs a prior (next sections). So is “a 3% chance it's a fluke”.

    One more trap in segment work: the difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not itself significant. “It worked for men (p = 0.03) but not women (p = 0.20)” doesn't show different effects; the estimates may be nearly identical, one interval just touching zero. To claim a difference, test the difference.

    Significance is not size: report the effect and its interval

    The p-value blends effect size with amount of data. A tiny effect with huge data and a big effect with little data can give the same p. A confidence interval separates them.

    estimate±1.96×SE

    A 95% interval: the estimate plus or minus about two standard errors (F2.1). It holds exactly the values a 5% test would not reject, so it carries the test plus the size and uncertainty.

    Compare two reports of the same campaign:

    • “Uptake increased significantly (p = 0.01).”
    • “Uptake rose by 0.6 percentage points, 95% interval 0.1 to 1.1. At our margins that's worth between AED 0.4m and AED 4.5m a year; break-even is AED 1m.”

    The second gives the plausible range against the number that matters. The interval straddles break-even, so the honest conclusion is “probably worth it, not certain”, which a p-value can't say. Translate intervals into AED, customers or hours.

    The tests you'll meet, at intuition level

    Almost every classical test is signal divided by noise: the observed difference, minus what the null says (usually zero), divided by its standard error. Far from zero in standard-error units means the null looks implausible.

    z=p^B−p^Ap¯(1−p¯)(1nA+1nB)

    The two-proportion z-test from Simulation 1. Top: the difference in uptake. Bottom: its standard error under the null, using the pooled rate p̄. |z| above 1.96 gives p below 0.05.

    Claude Code will write these. You need to know which question each answers and which assumption breaks first.

    QuestionUsual testWhat breaks it in a bank
    Did uptake, approval or default rates differ?Two-proportion z-test; chi-square for several groups; Fisher's exact test for small countsTreating transactions as independent when you randomised customers. Analyse at the unit you randomised.
    Did average spend or balance differ?Welch's t-test (doesn't assume equal variances); a z-test at large n by the central limit theoremHeavy-tailed AED amounts. A few huge spenders make the mean jumpy and the normal approximation slow to kick in (F1.5). Consider trimming, logs or a bootstrap (F2.2).
    Did the distribution shift?Mann–Whitney (rank) test; Kolmogorov–SmirnovReading Mann–Whitney as a test of means. It isn't, and the two can disagree.
    Same customers or questions, two conditions?Paired t-test; McNemar's test for paired yes-or-noAnalysing paired data as two independent groups, which throws away most of the precision. Common in LLM evals.
    Is complaint category linked to channel?Chi-square test of independenceLarge tables of sparse cells. And “linked” is not “caused”.

    Find the standard error and ask whether it was computed at the right level. If you randomised branches, the effective n is the number of branches, not customers. Getting this wrong shrinks the standard error and manufactures significance; it is one of the most common errors in bank analyses. Behind most tests sits a likelihood ratio (F1.6), which returns in sequential testing.

    When is a significant result real?

    Treat your experimentation programme as a classifier of ideas. The threshold sets its false positive rate on duds, α. Power sets its detection rate on good ideas, 1 − β. How often ideas truly work is the base rate π. The chance a flagged idea is real is then precision, by Bayes' rule as in F7:

    P(real∣significant)=π(1−β)π(1−β)+(1−π)α

    π is the share of ideas that truly work, 1 − β is power, α the significance level. This is the “theory” figure in Simulation 1's last box.

    Good ideas are rarer than teams believe. Kohavi, Deng and Vermeer report success rates of about a third at Microsoft, 15% at Bing, around 10% at Booking.com, Google Ads and Netflix, and 8% for Airbnb's search team.

    Take 10% with 80% power. Shipping only counts wins in the good direction, so the relevant false positive rate is 0.025. True wins: 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08. False: 0.9 × 0.025 = 0.0225. So about 22% of “significant wins” are false, not 5%; at Airbnb's 8%, about 26%. Bank campaigns are often small tweaks to mature products, so expect no better.

    Two consequences. A surprising result about an implausible idea needs a lower p or a replication before anyone acts. And low power means a larger share of wins are false, because power sits in the numerator.

    Watch out“Significant at 95%” does not mean “95% likely to be real”. It appears on vendor dashboards and AI summaries constantly. The real figure depends on base rate and power, and can easily be 60–80%.

    Forty slices: family-wise error and the false discovery rate

    Back to Monday's slide: five age bands, two nationality groups and four channels make 40 segments. Even if the offer did nothing anywhere, each has a 5% chance of p < 0.05. The chance at least one does:

    P(at least one false winner)=1−0.9540≈87%

    The family-wise error rate (FWER). In general 1 − (1 − α)m for m independent tests: each escapes with probability 0.95 and all 40 must. Expect 40 × 0.05 = 2 false winners per campaign, so the slide is almost guaranteed to show something.

    Two families of fix control different things.

    • Control the family-wise error rate: keep the chance of even one false winner at 5%. Bonferroni tests each segment at α ÷ m (0.05 ÷ 40 = 0.00125). Holm has the same guarantee and is never worse: sort the p-values, test the smallest at α ÷ m, the next at α ÷ (m − 1), and so on, stopping at the first failure.
    • Control the false discovery rate (FDR): accept some false winners but keep their share of all winners near q, say 5%. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) sorts the p-values, finds the largest rank k whose p-value is at most k × q ÷ m, and declares the top k winners.
    BH: reject theksmallest, wherek=max{i:p(i)≤imq}

    p(i) is the i-th smallest p-value. The bar rises with rank, so once a few strong results are in, weaker ones get an easier bar. That's why BH finds more real effects than Bonferroni when several exist.

    With no real effects, all three corrections give about the same 5% chance of any false winner. They differ when something is real, so choose by the cost of each mistake:

    • FWER (Holm) when one false claim is expensive: a regulator, the board, a fair-lending finding, a press-release number.
    • FDR (BH) when screening for follow-up: which of 200 AML scenarios to retune, which branches to investigate. A few false leads are fine if most are real.

    Every correction needs an honest m: all comparisons made, including those that never reached the slides. The cheapest fix beats them all: rerun the winning segment in a fresh test as the only hypothesis. A real effect survives; a lucky one shrinks to nothing.

    The modern alternative is a hierarchical model fitting all 40 segments together, pulling each noisy estimate towards the overall average in proportion to how little data it has. Gelman, Hill and Yajima argue this usually removes the need for corrections: a 41% uplift from 300 customers gets pulled most of the way back.

    Forking paths and p-hacking

    The invisible version of multiple testing is worse, because nobody consciously runs many tests. Gelman and Loken call it the garden of forking paths: at each step a reasonable choice depends on how the data look.

    • Uptake at 30 days or 60? Instalment plans, or spend converted?
    • Exclude dormant accounts, staff accounts, customers who called the contact centre?
    • Trim the top 1% of spenders? Adjust for tenure?
    • All customers, or only those who opened the SMS?

    Each is defensible, but choices made after seeing the data mean the analysis tried many versions and reported one. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed that four common flexibilities (two outcomes, adding data, a control variable, dropping a condition) push the false positive rate from 5% to about 61%.

    P-hacking is the deliberate version. Fingerprints: p-values clustered just under 0.05, a reported metric that isn't the planned one, unexplained exclusions, “trending towards significance”, results run “a few different ways” with one shown.

    Even without bad intent, analyst choices matter. In one study 29 teams got the same football data and question (are darker-skinned players given more red cards?). Twenty found a significant effect, nine didn't, with estimates from slightly below no effect to nearly triple the odds.

    Peeking, and tests built to be watched

    Now add time. An onboarding team runs a 28-day test. The dashboard updates each morning, and on day 9 p dips to 0.04. They stop and ship. What's wrong?

    The 5% guarantee assumes you look once, at a sample size fixed in advance. A running z-statistic wanders like a random walk. It is like asking a coin-flipper “are you ahead?” after every flip and stopping the first time they are. Stop the first time it crosses 1.96 and chance gets 28 tries instead of one. Armitage and colleagues showed in 1969 that with enough looks this eventually “succeeds” with probability approaching 1, even when nothing is going on.

    The two sequential families in the simulation:

    • Group sequential (from clinical trials, where stopping early for a clearly working or harmful drug matters): fix the maximum duration and the looks in advance, and spend the 5% error budget across them. The O'Brien–Fleming shape spends almost nothing early, so the final bar is barely above 1.96 and power is close to a single fixed look.
    • Always-valid inference: look whenever you like, stop whenever, even extend, with the guarantee intact. The mixture sequential probability ratio test (mSPRT), adapted for A/B testing by Johari and colleagues with Optimizely, is the best known. The price is a wider boundary, so less power at a fixed sample size.

    Spotify found group sequential as good or better when you can estimate the maximum sample size in advance. Always-valid earns its keep when you can't, when tests may overrun, or when you must catch a harmful change in streaming data fast, such as a bad app release.

    Always-valid theory is now often told through e-values, as a bet. Start with AED 1 and bet against the null, with bets that would be fair if the null were true. Under the null you can't expect to profit, whenever you stop. If your wealth reaches AED 20, reject.

    PH0(wealth ever reaches1/α)≤α

    Ville's inequality. A fair game rarely multiplies money by 20, so under the null the chance of ever hitting AED 20 is at most 1 in 20, however long you watch. The running wealth is an e-process; the mSPRT's likelihood ratio is one.

    Power, the minimum detectable effect, and the 1/effect² rule

    Power is the chance your test detects an effect that really exists. Think of a metal detector: a weak one finds only large or shallow coins. Four quantities are locked together; fix any three and the fourth follows:

    • α, the accepted false positive rate, usually 5%.
    • Power (1 − β), conventionally 80%: you miss a real effect 1 time in 5. At 90% it is 1 in 10.
    • Effect size: the smallest worth detecting, often the one that pays for the change, is the minimum detectable effect (MDE).
    • Sample size, n per group.
    n≈(z1−α/2+z1−β)2[p0(1−p0)+p1(1−p1)]δ2

    Customers per group to compare rates p0 and p1 that differ by δ. At α = 5% and 80% power, (1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.85. The key is δ²: halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the customers.

    For a relative change r on a low base rate p, this is about 15.7 ÷ (p × r²) per group. Rare outcomes need huge tests, which is why fraud and default experiments are hard:

    Run the power calculation before the test and treat it as a design constraint. With only 3,000 eligible customers per arm, the cards team can reliably detect only about a one-third uplift (5% → 6.7%). Better to know that before spending the budget than to get a “not significant” nobody can interpret.

    Underpowered tests lie twice: the winner's curse

    An underpowered test that does reach significance gives a result that is systematically too big. With low power the bar sits far out in the tail of the green curve (Simulation 4), so only estimates that noise pushed well above the truth clear it. Filtering on significance selects the lucky ones, like judging a batter only by his best games.

    Gelman and Carlin named two errors. A type M (magnitude) error is a significant estimate that exaggerates the truth; the exaggeration ratio is by how much, on average. A type S (sign) error points the wrong way. At 80% power the exaggeration is about 1.1×. At 20% it is about 2.3×. At 8% it is nearly 5×, and almost 1 significant result in 10 has the wrong sign.

    This is the pilot that “delivered a 40% uplift” and then 12% at rollout. Nobody erred at rollout; reporting only the significant result inflated the pilot. Likewise Monday's 41% should be read as “if anything is there, probably much smaller”: with a few hundred customers only an inflated estimate could reach significance.

    Post-hoc power, plugging the observed effect into the power formula, adds nothing; it restates the p-value. Power belongs to the design, computed in advance for an effect you care about.

    Variance reduction: CUPED and its successors

    If you can't get more customers, reduce noise. Much of the variation in outcomes is who customers are: heavy spenders stay heavy in either group. CUPED (controlled experiments using pre-experiment data; Microsoft, 2013) removes the part of each customer's outcome that was predictable from before the test.

    Yadj=Y−θ(X−X¯),Var(Yadj)=(1−ρ2)Var(Y)

    Y is the outcome during the test (card spend). X is the same customer's pre-test value (last quarter's spend). θ is the regression slope of Y on X; ρ is their correlation. Variance drives sample size, so you need a factor 1 − ρ² as many customers.

    It doesn't bias the comparison, because X was fixed before randomisation and is balanced on average; it only removes noise. Gains are large where bank metrics persist: spend, balances and transaction counts often correlate 0.6–0.8 with their own past. At ρ = 0.7 variance roughly halves, so you need about half the customers or time. For yes/no outcomes like first-time uptake, the correlation with past behaviour is weaker, and so is the gain.

    Successors widen the covariate set. CUPAC (DoorDash) uses an ML prediction of the outcome, built from pre-experiment features. Multi-covariate regression adjustment is the general form, used in Statsig's 2025 CURE (with lasso). Etsy work adds carefully chosen in-experiment covariates. One rule keeps all of them honest: covariates must be unaffected by the treatment. Adjust for something the offer changed and you bias the result. Stratified randomisation (balancing groups by segment and prior spend up front) gets similar gains at the design stage.

    Pre-registration in a bank analytics team

    Nearly every problem here has one cure: call your shot before you see the data. In a bank team that is a one-page analysis plan, agreed with the business owner and saved in the ticket or repository before launch:

    • Hypothesis and decision. “The instalment SMS raises 30-day uptake. If the interval's lower end clears break-even, we roll out.”
    • Population and randomisation unit. Who is eligible or excluded and why; whether customers, accounts or branches are randomised.
    • One primary metric, with exact definition and window. Others are secondary or guardrails: complaints, opt-outs, contact-centre calls, credit losses.
    • MDE, sample size, duration from the power calculation, and the stopping rule: fixed horizon or a named sequential design.
    • Pre-declared segments, two or three at most, with reasons. Everything else is exploratory and needs a retest.
    • The analysis: test, CUPED covariate, outlier handling, code if possible.

    It takes an hour and protects the analyst as much as the bank: nobody can ask you to “try it a few other ways”, and model risk and audit can trust a success. Unplanned results can still be shown, labelled “exploratory: needs confirmation”.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in campaign reviews, vendor pitches, validation packs and AI-generated analyses. Each has a fast check.

    • “p = 0.03, so we're 97% sure it works”

      The p-value is P(data | nothing going on), not P(nothing going on | data). The real chance depends on the base rate and the power.

      Spot itAsk how often ideas like this have worked before, and the test's power. Then do the Bayes sum.
    • “Not significant, so the offer doesn't work”

      A small test can't see a moderate effect. “No significant difference” from 2,000 customers is mostly a statement about the test.

      Spot itAsk for the interval. If it includes effects big enough to matter, the answer is “we don't know”, not “no”.
    • The segment winner

      The overall result is flat, but one of many segments is significant, and that's the headline.

      Spot itAsk how many segments were examined, whether this one was pre-declared, and whether it replicated in a fresh test.
    • Stopped on a good day

      Planned for four weeks, stopped on day 9 when p dipped below 0.05.

      Spot itAsk for the planned duration and stopping rule. With no sequential design, the p-value is invalid.
    • The metric changed after the fact

      The plan said 30-day uptake; the result reports 60-day converted spend, which happened to be significant.

      Spot itCompare the reported primary metric with the pre-launch plan. No plan means assume the forking paths were walked.
    • Significant but trivial

      “A highly significant improvement (p < 0.001)” of 0.02 percentage points on two million customers.

      Spot itAsk for the effect in AED or customers, with an interval, against the cost of the change.
    • The pilot that shrank at rollout

      A small pilot showed a 40% uplift; rollout delivered 12%. The pilot was underpowered, so only an exaggerated estimate could reach significance.

      Spot itAsk the pilot's power for a realistic effect. Below about 50%, expect inflation and plan the business case on a smaller number.
    • The wrong unit of analysis

      Customers were randomised but the analysis treats each transaction as independent, or branches were randomised and customers counted. The standard error is far too small.

      Spot itCheck the n in the test matches the number of things randomised. If it is ten times larger, the p-value is wrong.
    • Comparing significance across groups

      “It worked for nationals (p = 0.02) but not expatriates (p = 0.30), so we'll target nationals.”

      Spot itAsk for a test of the difference between the two effects. Usually the intervals overlap heavily.
    • LLM evals with no error bars

      “The new prompt scores 84% versus 82% on our 200-question set.” Each score has a standard error of about 2.7 points, and the best of 15 prompt variants was picked on the same set.

      Spot itAsk for the standard error, a paired comparison on the same questions, how many variants were tried, and a score on held-out questions.
    • AI-generated analysis that tests everything

      Signs: a table of 30 p-values with no correction, “p = 0.000”, “trending towards significance”, post-hoc power, or a t-test on heavily skewed spend with no comment.

      Spot itCount the tests and ask which were planned. Ask for intervals in business units. If it can't separate confirmatory from exploratory, you will have to.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A test gives p = 0.03. Which are true? (a) There's a 3% chance the null is true. (b) If the null were true, results this extreme would occur about 3% of the time. (c) The effect is large. (d) There's a 97% chance the effect is real.

    Only (b). (a) and (d) treat p as the probability of the hypothesis, which needs a prior. (c) confuses significance with size: a tiny effect in a huge sample can give p = 0.03.

    2. You check 20 segments, and the offer truly did nothing anywhere. What's the chance at least one shows p < 0.05? How many false winners do you expect?

    1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64%. Expected: 20 × 0.05 = 1.

    3. Ten sorted p-values: 0.001, 0.008, 0.039, 0.041, 0.042, 0.06, 0.074, 0.205, 0.212, 0.216. How many are declared at 5% by Bonferroni, by Holm, and by Benjamini–Hochberg?

    Bonferroni: threshold 0.05 ÷ 10 = 0.005, so only 0.001: 1.

    Holm: 0.001 ≤ 0.005 passes. Next, 0.008 against 0.05 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.0056 fails, so stop: 1.

    BH: thresholds are k × 0.005: 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, … The largest k with p(k) under its threshold is 2 (0.008 ≤ 0.010); ranks 3 to 10 fail: 2.

    4. Uptake is 5%. You want 80% power to detect a rise to 5.5%. Roughly how many customers per group? And for a rise to 6%?

    n ≈ 7.85 × (0.05 × 0.95 + 0.055 × 0.945) ÷ 0.005² ≈ 7.85 × 0.0995 ÷ 0.000025 ≈ 31,000. Doubling the effect to 1 point divides n by about 4: 7.85 × (0.0475 + 0.0564) ÷ 0.0001 ≈ 8,200.

    5. Only 10% of your team's ideas truly work. Tests have 80% power and α = 0.05. What share of significant results are real?

    Hits: 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08. False alarms: 0.9 × 0.05 = 0.045. Real: 0.08 ÷ 0.125 = 64%. Counting only wins in the good direction (α effectively 0.025): 0.08 ÷ 0.1025 ≈ 78%, so about 22% are false, Kohavi's figure.

    6. A pilot had about 20% power for a realistic effect, and came back significant with a 30% uplift. What should the business case assume?

    At 20% power, significant estimates exaggerate by about 2.3×, so plan on something nearer 13%, possibly lower. Better: run a properly powered confirmation test first.

    7. The metric is monthly card spend, and last quarter's spend has a correlation of 0.6 with it. How much does CUPED reduce the sample you need?

    Variance is multiplied by 1 − 0.6² = 0.64, and sample size scales with variance, so about 36% fewer customers, or the same customers for 64% of the time.

    8. A product owner checks a 4-week test daily and plans to stop at the first p < 0.05. What's roughly the false positive rate, and what would you propose instead?

    With about 28 looks, around 27% rather than 5%. Propose a sequential design: group sequential (for example O'Brien–Fleming at weekly looks) when the horizon is known, or an always-valid method such as mSPRT or a confidence sequence for daily watching with no fixed end. Agree the rule before launch.
