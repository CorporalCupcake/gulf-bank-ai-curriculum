    Hypothesis Tests, P-values, Many Comparisons and Power

    Contents
    Part 1

    The idea

    A p-value answers one question: if nothing were going on, how often would chance alone produce data at least this striking? A small p-value means “this would be rare by luck”. It does not say how big the effect is, or how likely it is to be real.

    Think of a friend who claims to be psychic and calls 9 of 10 coin tosses. A guesser manages that about 1 time in 100, so you are surprised. The p-value measures that surprise, and nothing more. If she were one of 40 friends you had tested, you would be less impressed.

    Part 2

    An example

    A website team tests a new green sign-up button. Overall, the click rate is flat. But for one slice of visitors (mobile users aged 25–34 arriving from email) clicks rose 41%, p = 0.02. Is the new button working for that group?

    They compared visitors who saw the new button with those who saw the old one, and asked how often a gap this large appears if the button does nothing. About 2 times in 100. That is unusual, but it is not proof.

    The idea: a p-value tells you how surprising the data are if nothing is going on, so some surprises will happen by luck. The simulation below shows why. Run 1,000 experiments where nothing works and the p-values spread evenly between 0 and 1, so about 1 in 20 lands below 0.05 by pure luck. Then raise the share of ideas that truly work. If only 1 in 10 does, a “significant” result is false roughly 4 times in 10.

    Part 3

    How to think about it

    What a small p-value does and doesn't say

    “p = 0.03 means a 97% chance it works” is wrong. The p-value assumes nothing is happening and measures how odd your data look. Whether the idea is real also depends on how often such ideas work. Good ideas are rarer than teams believe, so a surprising result needs a replication. Likewise, rejecting “nothing is happening” does not prove your explanation: a bug or a confounder would break it too.

    Significant is not big

    With two million visitors, a tiny change is “highly significant” and worthless. Report the effect and its interval: “clicks up 0.6 points, somewhere between 0.4 and 0.8” says far more than “p = 0.01”. In banking this is the difference between a result and a result worth acting on. Also analyse at the level you randomised, such as visitors rather than page views, or you manufacture significance.

    Many looks, one winner

    Forty slices with no real effect give about 2 false winners. Holm keeps the chance of even one false winner at 5%, so use it when a false claim is expensive. Benjamini–Hochberg keeps the share of false winners near 5% and finds more real effects, so use it for screening. The cheapest fix is to rerun the winning slice as a fresh test. The same happens invisibly when you choose thirty days or sixty, or exclude inactive users, after seeing the data. Name the metric, sample size and slices before launch.

    Peeking

    The 5% guarantee assumes one look at a sample size fixed in advance. Check daily and stop the first time p dips below 0.05, and about 27% of tests with no effect will declare a winner. If you must look early, use a sequential design.

    Not significant, and the lucky pilot

    “Not significant” does not mean “no effect”. Power is the chance a test detects an effect that is really there, conventionally 80%. Like a metal detector, a weak test finds only big coins. Halve the effect you want to detect and you need about four times the visitors, so rare outcomes (fraud is one) need huge tests. Ask for the interval: if it includes effects that matter, the answer is “we don't know”.

    Weak tests also exaggerate. Only estimates that noise pushed up clear the bar, so a significant pilot overstates the effect, which is why a 40% pilot gives 12% at rollout. Run the power calculation before launch.

    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Only 1 idea in 10 works. Is a result at p = 0.03 about 97% likely to be real?

    No. The many duds produce nearly as many false alarms as the good ideas produce hits, so a large share of significant results, a third or more, are false.

    2. The overall result is flat, but one of 40 slices shows p = 0.02. What do you tell the team?

    Forty looks will throw up about 2 false winners by luck. Treat it as a hypothesis and rerun it as a fresh test before acting.

    3. A 20%-power pilot shows a significant 30% uplift. What should the business case assume?

    Something much smaller, because weak tests exaggerate. Run a properly powered confirmation test.
