    Hypothesis Tests, P-values, Many Comparisons and Power

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A cards team reports that among expatriates aged 25–34 who use the app, uptake rose 41%, p = 0.02. Overall uptake was flat. Is it real?

    A p-value asks: if the offer did nothing, how often would chance look this striking? About 2 times in 100. But the team looked at 40 segments, and roll forty dice and some come up six.

    • With nothing going on, p-values spread evenly. Exactly 5% land below 0.05.
    • Whether a result is real is a base-rate question. If only 1 idea in 10 works, false alarms rival the hits.
    Hold onto thisA p-value measures surprise under “nothing is happening”. It is not the chance the effect is real, nor its size.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The null hypothesis

    A test assumes the null, “the offer changes nothing”, and asks whether your data look out of place, like “innocent until proven guilty”. Rejecting it doesn't prove your explanation; a bug or confounder would break it too. Failing to reject doesn't prove it either; the test may be too small.

    What a p-value is

    The p-value is the chance of data at least this extreme, assuming the null is true. Think of a smoke alarm: p is how rarely it would sound with no fire. So “p = 0.03 means a 97% chance it works” is wrong, and it says nothing about size.

    Significance is not size

    With two million customers, a tiny change is “highly significant” and worthless. Report the effect and its interval in AED: “up 0.6 points, worth AED 0.4m to 4.5m against a AED 1m break-even” says far more than “p = 0.01”.

    The tests you'll meet

    Almost every classical test is signal divided by noise. Claude Code will write them. The usual culprit is the wrong unit: analyse at the level you randomised, not per transaction, or you manufacture significance.

    Is a significant result real?

    Good ideas are rarer than teams believe; success rates near 10% are reported at large tech firms. At that rate about 22% of “significant wins” are false, not 5%. A surprising result needs a replication.

    Watch out“Significant at 95%” does not mean “95% likely to be real”.

    Forty slices

    Forty segments with no real effect give about 2 false winners. Family-wise rules (Bonferroni, Holm) keep the chance of even one false winner at 5%. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) keeps the share of false winners near 5% and finds more real effects.

    Use Holm when one false claim is expensive, BH when screening. The cheapest fix is to rerun the winning segment as a fresh test.

    Forking paths and p-hacking

    Multiple testing also happens invisibly: thirty days or sixty? Exclude dormant accounts? Choices made after seeing the data mean many versions were tried and one reported, which can lift false positives from 5% to about 61%. P-hacking is the deliberate version.

    Peeking

    The 5% guarantee assumes one look at a sample size fixed in advance. Stopping the first time p dips below 0.05 gives chance 28 tries. Fixes are sequential designs: group sequential (looks fixed in advance) or always-valid methods such as mSPRT (look any time, less power).

    Power

    Power is the chance a test detects an effect that really exists, conventionally 80%. Like a metal detector, a weak test finds only big coins. Halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the customers, so rare outcomes like fraud need huge tests. Run the calculation before launch.

    The winner's curse

    A significant result from an underpowered test is systematically too big, because only estimates noise pushed up clear the bar. At 20% power the exaggeration is about 2.3×.

    Variance reduction: CUPED

    If you can't get more customers, reduce noise. CUPED removes the part of each outcome predictable from pre-test behaviour; for persistent metrics like spend it can roughly halve the sample needed. Adjust only for things the treatment cannot have changed.

    Pre-registration

    Call your shot before you see the data. A one-page plan, saved before launch, names the decision, one primary metric, the sample size, the stopping rule and two or three segments. Everything else is exploratory and needs a retest.

    Part 3

    How it fails

    • “p = 0.03, so we're 97% sure it works”

      The real chance depends on the base rate and power.

      Spot itAsk how often such ideas have worked.
    • “Not significant, so it doesn't work”

      A small test can't see a moderate effect.

      Spot itAsk for the interval. If it includes effects that matter, the answer is “we don't know”.
    • The segment winner, or the metric that changed

      The overall result is flat, but one segment or metric becomes the headline.

      Spot itAsk how many were examined and whether it replicated.
    • Stopped on a good day

      Planned for four weeks, stopped on day 9.

      Spot itAsk for the stopping rule.
    • The pilot that shrank at rollout

      A pilot showed 40%; rollout gave 12%.

      Spot itAsk the pilot's power.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What does p = 0.03 mean?

    If the null were true, results this extreme would occur about 3% of the time. It is not a 97% chance of being real.

    2. When would you choose Holm over Benjamini–Hochberg, and when the reverse?

    Holm when one false claim is costly. BH when screening for follow-up; it finds more real effects.

    3. Only 10% of ideas work. Is “significant” 95% likely to be real?

    No. Roughly a fifth or more are false, because the many duds produce nearly as many false alarms as good ideas produce hits.

    4. A 20%-power pilot shows a significant 30% uplift. What should the business case assume?

    Something much smaller, because weak tests exaggerate. Run a properly powered confirmation test.

    5. A 4-week test is checked daily and stopped at the first p < 0.05. What is wrong?

    The false positive rate is nearer 27% than 5%. Use a sequential design.
