    Hybrid Search and Reranking

    Part 1

    What hybrid search and reranking are

    In one sentenceHybrid search runs two different searches and merges their lists. Reranking then has a slower, more careful model re-sort the short merged list so the best passages sit at the top.

    Our running example: a staff member asks, "What is the daily ATM cash limit on a Platinum card?" The answer is in three places:

    • A page titled "Platinum: daily cash limit".
    • A paragraph saying "the most you can take from a cash machine per day is AED 20,000". It never uses the words "ATM" or "limit".
    • A tariff table called "FS-2207", mostly numbers.

    Keyword search finds the page and the table but misses the paragraph. Meaning (embedding) search finds the page and the paragraph but can miss the table. Neither list is complete. Together they are.

    Think of hiring: a quick CV screen over everyone, then proper interviews for a shortlist. In RAG (retrieval-augmented generation, where a language model answers from passages we fetch), this decides which five passages the model gets to read.

    Part 2

    How it differs from a single search

    Retrieval has two jobs. Recall means the right passage is somewhere in the candidates. Precision at the top means it is in the first few. Hybrid search buys recall. Reranking buys precision.

    The two searches fail on different questions, which is the case for using both.

    Kind of questionKeyword (BM25)Meaning (embeddings)
    Exact code, such as "FS-2207"StrongWeak
    Different wording: "cash machine" for "ATM"Weak, no shared wordStrong
    Arabic question, English policyVery weakStrong, with a multilingual model
    Negation: "who is not eligible"Ignores "not"Weak, "eligible" and "not eligible" sit close together

    BM25 is the standard keyword scoring method: it rewards passages that contain the question's words, especially rare ones. The last row is the gap the reranker closes.

    Part 3

    How it works

    1. Filter first. Apply language, date and access rights before searching, so restricted documents never enter either list.
    2. Search twice. Run keyword search and meaning search, each returning its top 50 or so.
    3. Merge by rank. Combine the two lists with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), explained below.
    4. Rerank. A cross-encoder reads the question and each of the top 20 to 100 merged candidates and scores each one.
    5. Keep the top 5 by reranker score and give them to the language model.

    Merging: why ranks, not scores

    BM25 might score a passage 14.2 and cosine similarity 0.72. Those numbers are on different scales, so adding them lets one side swamp the other. RRF ignores scores and uses only position, like combining two judges' placings rather than their marks.

    Each list gives a passage a vote of 1 ÷ (60 + rank). Votes add up, and a list that lacks the passage gives nothing. Appearing in both lists roughly doubles the score, so agreement is rewarded.

    Reranking: why a cross-encoder is better but slower

    • Embedding search (bi-encoder) turns every passage into a vector in advance. Millions can be searched in milliseconds, but one vector has to serve every future question. It is like judging a book by its blurb.
    • Cross-encoder reads the question and one passage together as a single input. It sees that "not" belongs to "eligible", or that "60 days" contradicts "120 days". It is like reading the book with your question in hand.
    • The price: nothing can be precomputed, so one question costs one model pass per candidate. That is why it only runs on the short merged list.
    Part 4

    How to read it

    PassageKeyword rankMeaning rankRRF score
    A: first in keyword only1not found1/61 ≈ 0.016
    B: second in keyword, seventh in meaning271/62 + 1/67 ≈ 0.031

    Passage B wins by almost double, because both searches found it. With the usual constant of 60, being found twice beats topping one list.

    • Small constant (say 1): a top place counts for far more. One confident search can win alone.
    • Large constant: votes flatten out, so agreement matters most.
    • Candidate recall: out of 100 test questions, how many have the right passage somewhere in the merged 50? Say 62. Then the reranker can never do better than 62%, however good it is. It cannot promote a passage it never sees.
    • MRR (mean reciprocal rank) is the average of 1 ÷ the rank of the first correct passage. An MRR of 0.5 means the right passage is typically second.

    Measure each stage separately: recall after the merge, then rank quality after reranking. The most common mistake is tuning the wrong stage.

    Part 5

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    SetupSpeedFinds exact codesFinds paraphrasesTop-of-list accuracy
    Keyword onlyVery fastYesNoLow
    Meaning onlyFastSometimesYesMedium
    Hybrid (RRF)FastYesYesMedium
    Hybrid + rerankerSlowerYesYesHigh

    Strong at:

    • Finding answers that either search alone would miss.
    • Needing no training data: RRF works out of the box, and Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search and Qdrant ship it built in.

    Weak at:

    • Truncation. Rerankers read only the first part of a passage (often 512 tokens). A relevant sentence beyond the cut scores low.
    • Latency. Reranking 1,000 candidates is slow for a small gain. Pick the smallest number where quality stops improving.
    • Scores differ across questions. Use reranker scores to sort, not as a fixed "good enough" cut-off, unless you have tested a threshold on labelled questions.
    Show the formula

    RRF score of a passage = the sum, over each list it appears in, of 1 ÷ (k + its rank in that list), with k usually 60 (Cormack et al., 2009). In words: a high place in a list is worth more, and every list that finds it adds a vote.

    In banking, a policy assistant must find both the exact tariff code and the plain-language rule, and must never rank a document the asker is not allowed to see.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Tuning the language model when retrieval is the problem

      Check 50 failed answers by hand. If the right passage was never retrieved in most of them, fix retrieval first.

    • Reranking a list that misses the answer

      An expensive reranker gives little if the first stage returned the wrong 20. Measure recall before the reranker.

    • Adding raw scores from different searches

      One signal silently dominates. Merge by rank, or normalise carefully and test.

    • Judging by a few impressive demos

      Five hand-picked questions prove little. Use a few hundred labelled questions, split by type.

    1. Why does RRF rank a passage found by both searches above one that is first in a single list?

    Two votes add up (about 0.031) to nearly double a lone first place (about 0.016). Agreement is rewarded.

    2. Why can RRF merge BM25 and cosine scores without any adjustment?

    It uses positions, not values. The best passage is rank 1 whether its score is 14.2 or 0.74.

    3. Why can a cross-encoder rerank 100 passages but not a million?

    It needs a full model pass per passage for every question, because nothing can be precomputed.

    4. A reranker upgrade changed almost nothing, and recall at 50 candidates is 62%. What is wrong?

    The answer is missing from the candidates 38% of the time, and the reranker can't fix that. Improve the first stage (add the missing search, fix chunking, widen the lists), then retest.

    5. Why might both searches rank "eligible customers" above "who is not eligible"?

    Keyword search ignores "not", and meaning search places "eligible" and "not eligible" close together. A cross-encoder reads them together and sees the difference.
