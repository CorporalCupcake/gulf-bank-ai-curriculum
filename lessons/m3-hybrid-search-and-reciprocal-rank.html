    Hybrid Search, Rank Fusion and Reranking: Two Searches, Then a Careful Reader

    Why keyword search and meaning search fail on different questions, how reciprocal rank fusion merges their lists without comparing incomparable scores, and why a slower cross-encoder pass over the top candidates is usually the biggest single upgrade to a RAG system.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRun a keyword search and an embedding search because each finds what the other misses, merge their rankings by position rather than by score, then let a slower model that reads the question and each candidate together reorder a short list, and measure every stage on your own labelled questions.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An analyst at a Gulf bank types into the policy assistant: "What is the daily ATM cash limit on a Platinum card?" The answer sits in three places. One is a clear page titled "Platinum: daily cash limit". One is a paragraph that says "the maximum you can take out of a cash machine per day is AED 20,000" and never uses the words ATM or limit. The third is a tariff table, "FS-2207", where the only match is the exact product name and a number.

    A keyword search (BM25, M3.1) finds the first and the third, because the words are there. It misses the paragraph with different words. An embedding search (M1 and F1.1) finds the first and the paraphrase, because it matches meaning. It can miss the table, which is a grid of numbers with little meaning to embed, and it can also drift to generic "card limits" pages. Neither list is complete. Together they are.

    That gives two problems, and this lesson is the answer to both.

    • How do you merge two lists? BM25 gives scores like 14.2. An embedding search gives cosine similarities like 0.72. The numbers are on different scales and mean different things. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) sidesteps the problem by ignoring scores and using only each document's position in each list.
    • How do you get the best five to the top? Both first-stage searches are fast because they never read the question and the document together. They compare a query to precomputed summaries of documents. A cross-encoder reranker reads the query and one candidate together, which is far more accurate and far too slow to run over everything. So you use it only on the 20–100 candidates that fusion produced.

    The shape is a funnel: cheap and broad first, expensive and sharp last. Search engineers have used it for decades. Recommender systems use it too (F9.5). In a RAG system it decides which five passages the language model gets to read, which is the largest influence on whether the answer is right.

    Hold onto thisRetrieval has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. Recall: make sure the right passage is somewhere in the candidate list. Precision at the top: make sure it is in the first few. Hybrid search and fusion buy recall. Reranking buys precision. Confusing the two is the most common reason teams tune the wrong stage.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why one retriever is not enough

    The two first-stage retrievers fail on different kinds of question. That difference is the entire case for combining them.

    Kind of queryKeyword (BM25)Embeddings (dense)
    Exact identifiers: "FS-2207", an IBAN prefix, a circular number, a person's nameStrong. The token is rare, so it carries a lot of weight.Weak. The embedding model may have never seen the string and maps it to something vaguely similar.
    Paraphrase: "cash machine" for "ATM", "take out" for "withdraw"Weak. No shared word, no score.Strong. Similar meaning, nearby vectors.
    Arabic question over English policy, or the reverseVery weak. No shared tokens at all.Strong with a multilingual model. Weaker on dialect and on transliterated names.
    Rare domain terms and acronyms (a regulator's term, an internal system name)Strong if spelled the same way.Varies. Depends on whether the term appeared in the model's training data.
    Long, vague, conceptual questionsMatches common words and returns noise.Strong at topic, weaker at precise conditions.
    Negation and conditions: "who is not eligible", "after 60 days"Ignores the negation. "Eligible" matches.Also weak. "Eligible" and "not eligible" have close embeddings.

    The last row matters for what follows: neither first-stage retriever handles negation and conditions well, because both compare a query with a document that was summarised without knowing the query. That is the gap the reranker closes. BEIR, a benchmark of 18 datasets across many domains, found that plain BM25 is a hard baseline to beat zero-shot and that dense models can fall below it on out-of-domain text (Thakur et al., 2021). That is why serious systems keep both.

    Reciprocal rank fusion

    You now have two ranked lists. The obvious move is to add the scores. The trouble: a BM25 score is unbounded and depends on the query length and the corpus; a cosine similarity sits in a narrow band; neither is comparable between queries. You would need to normalise, and the normalisation is fragile (Simulation 2). RRF avoids it by keeping only the rank:

    score(d)=∑r∈listswrk+rankr(d)

    Each list gives a document a vote worth w ÷ (k + rank). A document absent from a list gets no vote from it. The usual w is 1 and the usual k is 60, from Cormack et al. (2009). Example: rank 1 in one list and rank 5 in the other: 1/61 + 1/65 = 0.0164 + 0.0154 = 0.0318.

    What the constant k does is the part most people miss. With k = 0, rank 1 is worth 1, rank 2 is worth 0.5, rank 3 is worth 0.33: the top spot dominates. With k = 60, rank 1 is worth 0.0164 and rank 10 is worth 0.0143: nearly flat. A large k turns RRF into a consensus vote: a document in both lists at any decent rank beats a document that is first in only one. A small k lets one retriever's confident top pick through. Neither is "right". The point of the simulation is to feel the trade.

    def rrf(rankings, k=60, weights=None):
    """rankings: list of ordered lists of doc ids, best first."""
    scores = {}
    for w, ranking in zip(weights or [1] * len(rankings), rankings):
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            scores[doc] = scores.get(doc, 0) + w / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

    Three things to take from it.

    • Fusion is mostly a recall tool. Its best property is that the union of two lists contains answers neither found alone. Do not expect the fused top three to be perfect. Expect the fused top 30 to be good, and plan a reranker to sort it out.
    • RRF is a sensible default, not an optimum. It needs no training data and no tuning, which is why search engines ship it. If you have a few hundred labelled queries, a tuned weighted combination of normalised scores can beat it (Bruch et al., 2023). Using the ranks throws away how confident each retriever was. A rank-1 with a huge margin and a rank-1 by a hair get the same vote.
    • You can add more than two lists. A title search, a dense search on a summary and a dense search on the body are three votes. Multi-query RAG (M3.7) fuses the lists from several rewrites of the same question the same way.

    In practice you rarely write this yourself. Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search, Qdrant and several other engines run both searches and fuse with RRF as a built-in option. What you control is k, the weights, how many results each side contributes (50–100 is typical), and any filters (language, entity, date, access rights) applied before the search so that restricted documents never enter either list.

    Bi-encoders, cross-encoders and why the second is better but slower

    Both kinds of model read text and output a relevance judgement. They differ in when the query and the document meet.

    A bi-encoder is the embedding model of M3's dense search. It encodes the query into a vector and encodes each document into a vector separately, ahead of time. Relevance is a dot product of two vectors. Because documents never see the query, their vectors can be computed once, stored and searched in milliseconds across millions of passages. The price: a single vector must summarise everything in a passage that any future question might ask about, and it cannot know which part matters for this question.

    A cross-encoder takes the query and one passage concatenated into a single input, [CLS] query [SEP] passage [SEP], and runs the whole transformer over the pair. Attention (F5.5) lets every query word look at every passage word. It can see that "not" attaches to "eligible", that "60 days" in the question matches "within 60 days" but contradicts "after 120 days" in the passage. It outputs one relevance number. Nogueira and Cho (2019) showed that this simple setup fine-tuned on a passage-ranking dataset sharply improved ranking quality over the strongest first stage of the time.

    The cost is the whole point. Nothing can be precomputed, so one query means one forward pass per candidate. Scoring 50 candidates of 300 tokens each pushes 15,000 tokens through the model for a single question, against a few dozen for the bi-encoder query. Scoring a corpus of a million passages this way per query is out of the question. Hence the funnel.

    from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

reranker = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3")   # multilingual, includes Arabic

    A cross-encoder is trained on triples of (query, relevant passage, non-relevant passage), usually with a ranking loss so that relevant scores above non-relevant. The hard part is the non-relevant examples: ones that are retrieved by a first stage and look plausible. Those "hard negatives" are exactly what the reranker will face in production, which is also why fine-tuning on your own bank's documents and queries can help far more than swapping to a bigger generic model.

    The funnel: how deep to rerank, and what it costs

    Every RAG team faces one design choice: how many candidates N to send to the reranker. The trade-off is simple to state and easy to get wrong.

    • Too small an N: the right passage was not in the list. The reranker cannot promote what it never sees. The first stage's recall at N is a hard ceiling.
    • Too large an N: latency grows linearly, cost grows, and you give the reranker more plausible-looking wrong passages to be fooled by. Past a point the final quality is flat or slightly worse.

    So you find the knee. Plot recall of the candidate list against N, plot final top-5 quality against N, and pick the smallest N where both have flattened. The simulation builds that picture.

    Details that decide whether the funnel works in production.

    • Truncation. Rerankers have a maximum input length (often 512 tokens, some models far more). A long chunk is cut, and if the cut falls before the relevant sentence, the score is wrong. Chunk size (M3.5) and reranker window must be designed together.
    • Scores are not comparable across queries. A cross-encoder's raw output is a ranking signal. A score of 3.1 on one question and 3.1 on another does not mean the same confidence. Use it to sort, and be careful if you try to use it as a cut-off for "no good answer found". Calibrate it on labelled queries first (F7.4).
    • What the language model sees. After reranking you keep the top 3–8, not all 50. More passages cost tokens and dilute the prompt; models also use information in the middle of a long context less reliably than at the ends (Liu et al., 2023; M1.5). A good reranker lets you send fewer, better passages.
    • Where to fuse the reranker with other signals. RRF discards score magnitudes, so fusing a reranker's score into it throws away the thing the reranker is good at. The common pattern is: fuse the first-stage lists with RRF to build candidates, then sort only by the reranker score, optionally blended with recency or authority features.
    • Measure the stages separately. For each labelled question record the rank of the correct passage after each stage. Candidate recall at N tells you whether the first stage is the bottleneck. MRR and nDCG at 5 (F9.5) tell you about the reranker. Without labels, you are tuning by anecdote.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson is the middle of the RAG pipeline. What comes before decides what the two searches can find; what comes after decides what the model does with the survivors.

    M3.1 · M3.2 · Keyword search and BM25The first half of "hybrid"

    BM25's strengths and blind spots are what make fusion worthwhile. Knowing how IDF rewards rare terms explains the outlier in Simulation 2.

    F1.1 · F5.5 · F5.7 · Vectors, attention, contrastive learningWhere bi-encoders and cross-encoders come from

    A bi-encoder is a vector space trained contrastively. A cross-encoder is the attention mechanism applied across a query and a passage.

    M3.5 · M3.6 · ChunkingWhat the lists contain

    Both searches rank chunks, not documents. Chunk size changes BM25 scores, embedding quality and whether the answer fits the reranker's window.

    M3.7 · M3.8 · Query rewritingMore lists to fuse

    Multi-query retrieval produces several ranked lists for one question. RRF is how they are merged, which is why it appears in almost every such system.

    M3.9 · M3.10 · Parsing PDFsGarbage in, garbage ranked

    A table flattened into jumbled text defeats both searches and the reranker. Retrieval quality is capped by parsing quality.

    F9.5 · Recommenders and rankingThe same two-stage design

    Candidate generation then a heavier ranking model is the standard pattern for feeds and recommendations too. The ranking metrics (nDCG, MRR) are shared.

    M1.5 · M2.5 · Context and costFewer, better passages

    Reranking lets you cut the prompt from twenty passages to five. That saves tokens and latency (M2.5), and often improves the answer.

    A6.1 · A6.3 · Visual and agentic retrievalWhere it goes next

    ColPali brings late interaction to page images; agentic RAG lets a model decide to search again when the first results are poor.

    A4.3 · GroundednessWhy retrieval errors become hallucinations

    If the right passage is not retrieved, the model answers from memory or from a near-miss. Many "hallucinations" are retrieval failures in disguise.

    B2.3 · B2.4 · Sanctions and name matchingExact plus fuzzy plus verify

    Name screening has the same shape: a cheap, wide candidate generation (phonetic, n-gram, embedding), then a more careful scoring pass on the survivors.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Internal policy and procedures assistant (B7.3). Staff ask in English or Arabic; documents are in both. Exact circular numbers, product codes and clause references favour keyword search. Paraphrase and cross-language favour embeddings. This is the textbook case for hybrid.
    • Credit memos and trade documents. Clause lookups ("cross-default", a letter of credit number) need exact matches; conceptual questions ("what covenants apply to this borrower") need meaning.
    • Regulatory and compliance search. Finding which CBUAE or SAMA requirement applies. Precision at the top matters because a wrong clause is worse than none; reranking is where precision comes from. Treat any regulatory text you index as versioned and dated, and show the source and date to the user.
    • Contact-centre agent assist. A customer's description is conversational, in mixed dialect Arabic and English. Retrieval must be both robust and fast, so the reranker depth is a latency decision measured in milliseconds.
    • Complaint and case similarity. "Find past cases like this" is dense retrieval; "find cases mentioning this merchant or account number" is keyword. Analysts want both.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The architecture is settled: hybrid first stage, rank fusion, a reranker on a short list. Live questions are how much a general reranker adds against a fine-tuned one, whether an LLM should be the reranker, how to rerank across Arabic and English, and how much of this an "agentic" loop makes unnecessary.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with one search; entries from before 2025 are from my own knowledge of the literature and are flagged in the sources. Verify before quoting numbers.

    1. 2009
      Reciprocal rank fusion

      Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher showed that this one-line method, with k = 60, beat more elaborate rank-combination and learning-to-rank approaches on TREC data. It has been the default ever since.

      Cormack et al., SIGIR 2009
    2. 2019
      BERT as a reranker

      Feeding the query and passage together into BERT and fine-tuning on MS MARCO gave a large jump in passage-ranking quality. This is the cross-encoder reranker. Sentence-BERT the same year gave the practical bi-encoder.

      Nogueira & Cho, arXiv 1901.04085; Reimers & Gurevych, EMNLP 2019
    3. 2020
      ColBERT and sequence-to-sequence rerankers

      Late interaction kept per-token vectors to get near-cross-encoder accuracy with precomputed documents. monoT5 used a text-to-text model to emit "true" or "false" for relevance.

      Khattab & Zaharia, SIGIR 2020; Nogueira et al., arXiv 2003.06713
    4. 2021
      BEIR: BM25 is hard to beat out of domain

      Eighteen heterogeneous datasets showed that dense retrievers trained on one domain often fall below BM25 on another, while rerankers and late-interaction models were the most robust on average.

      Thakur et al., NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks 2021
    5. 2023
      Tuned fusion, and LLMs as listwise rerankers

      Bruch and colleagues analysed fusion functions and found that a convex combination of normalised scores can beat RRF when a little in-domain tuning data exists, while RRF is more robust without it. RankGPT showed that a large general model, asked to reorder a window of passages, can match or beat supervised rerankers.

      Bruch et al., ACM TOIS 2023; Sun et al., EMNLP 2023
    6. 2024
      Multilingual and multi-granularity embedding plus reranker families

      BGE-M3 produced dense, sparse and multi-vector scores from one multilingual model; companion rerankers (bge-reranker-v2-m3) and commercial rerank APIs cover Arabic and English. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval write-up reported that adding BM25 to contextual embeddings, then a reranker, cut the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 5.7% to 1.9% on its test sets.

      Chen et al., arXiv 2402.03216; Anthropic, September 2024 (vendor-reported, internal datasets)
    7. 2026
      Financial-QA benchmarking of ten retrieval strategies

      A benchmark of 23,088 queries over 7,318 documents compared pipelines from BM25 up to corrective RAG. Adding a Cohere reranker to hybrid RRF retrieval lifted Recall@5 from 0.695 to 0.816 and MRR@3 from 0.433 to 0.605, and the authors identified reranking as the most impactful single component. One dataset and one domain, so treat the size of the gain as an example, not a constant.

      arXiv 2604.01733, 2026 (preprint)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in real RAG projects and in AI-generated designs. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Tuning the generator when retrieval is the problem

      The answers are wrong, so someone rewrites the prompt or swaps the model. The right passage was never in the context.

      Spot itFor 50 failed answers, check by hand whether the correct passage was in the retrieved set. If it was missing in most, fix retrieval first.
    • Reranking a candidate list that misses the answer

      The team adds an expensive reranker and sees little gain. The first stage returned the wrong 20.

      Spot itMeasure recall at N before the reranker. If it is below about 90% at your depth, widen the first stage, add the hybrid, fix chunking or filters.
    • Adding scores from different scales

      BM25 plus cosine, un-normalised, so one signal silently dominates. Or min-max normalising with an outlier in the list.

      Spot itPrint the fused top results with each side's contribution. If one side is nearly always the winner, the scales are off. Switch to RRF or normalise robustly.
    • Believing the default k = 60 is correct

      A constant from 2009 lists, tuned on different data. It favours consensus and buries confident single-list hits.

      Spot itLook at queries where the right answer is found by only one retriever. Sweep k and the weights on a labelled set before hard-coding.
    • Truncated passages hiding the answer

      The reranker reads only the first 512 tokens of a long chunk. The relevant sentence is at token 700 and the passage scores low.

      Spot itCheck the reranker's maximum length against your chunk length. Test with the answer sentence moved to the end of the chunk.
    • Using the reranker score as a confidence or a cut-off

      "Answer only if the score is above 0.5" with an uncalibrated model. It works for some questions and refuses others at random.

      Spot itPlot the score distribution for questions with known answers and known no-answer questions. Pick the threshold from that plot, and re-check after any model change.
    • Judging by a few impressive demos

      Five hand-picked questions work, the project goes live, and the long tail of real questions does not.

      Spot itAsk for the number of labelled questions, how they were chosen, and results by question type (identifier, paraphrase, Arabic, negation). A few hundred is a sensible start.
    • Permissions applied after retrieval

      The search ranks documents the user may not see, then the application hides them. Best passage gone, or worse, leaked into a prompt.

      Spot itCheck that access filters run inside the search query, before ranking, and that a hosted reranker or embedding service never receives restricted text it should not.
    • Stale or duplicated versions outranking the current one

      Three versions of the same policy; the old one has more keyword overlap and wins.

      Spot itSearch for a policy known to have changed. Check that effective-date filters or metadata prefer the current version and that duplicates are removed before fusion.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Staff questions come in two kinds. Some quote an exact circular or product code; some describe what they want in their own words, in Arabic or English. One search method is good at each, so we run both and merge the results. Then a second, slower model reads the best fifty candidates properly and picks the five that go to the assistant. That second step is usually where the answer quality improves most. We'll check it on a set of a few hundred real questions, and I'd show you the results by question type rather than three good demos.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “BM25 and a multilingual dense retriever each return their top 50 after access and date filters, fused with reciprocal rank fusion, then a cross-encoder reranks the top 50 to a final five. I'd tune k and the weights, the candidate depth and the chunk size on a labelled query set, reporting recall at N for the first stage and MRR and nDCG at 5 for the reranker, split by query type including Arabic and identifiers. I'd compare against BM25 alone and dense alone, and consider tuned score fusion or a fine-tuned reranker only if they beat that with intervals.”
    “Why not just use a vector database? Isn't that the modern way?”

    Embeddings handle paraphrase and cross-language, but they are unreliable on exact identifiers, rare terms and numbers, and on out-of-domain text can lose to BM25 (BEIR). Policy and compliance text is full of exact references. Hybrid costs one extra index and a small fusion step. I'd start with both and let our evaluation set show whether either side is dead weight.

    “Why not add the BM25 and similarity scores together?”

    They are on different, unbounded scales that vary by query, so one side tends to swamp the other, and normalising is sensitive to outliers. RRF uses only ranks, needs no tuning data and is stable. The downside is it ignores confidence. If we have labelled queries, a tuned weighted score blend can do better, so I'd test it as an upgrade.

    “What does the reranker actually do that the embedding search doesn't?”

    The embedding search compares a query vector to precomputed passage vectors, so a passage is summarised without knowing the question. A cross-encoder reads the question and the passage together, so it can handle conditions, negation and specifics. It is too slow for the whole corpus, so it only reorders the best few dozen candidates.

    “Can't we just give the language model 30 passages and let it sort it out?”

    Sometimes, and for low-stakes cases it may be enough. But it costs tokens on every call, adds latency, and long contexts dilute attention: facts in the middle are used less reliably. A reranker is cheaper per query than a larger prompt and gives us a measurable retrieval stage we can test and audit separately.

    “How many candidates should we rerank?”

    The smallest number at which first-stage recall has flattened, typically 20–100. I'd measure recall at several depths on our labelled questions, plot final quality against depth, and pick the knee, subject to the latency budget. Past that point cost rises and quality does not.

    “Can we send documents to a hosted rerank API?”

    That is a data-governance question before a technical one. It depends on the classification of the text, where the service processes it, contractual terms and the bank's own policy and regulator expectations, which I'd confirm with security, legal and compliance rather than assume. If it is not allowed, open rerankers can run in our own environment, and the quality gap is often smaller than people expect.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. With k = 60, a document is ranked 2nd by BM25 and 7th by the dense search. What is its RRF score?

    1/62 + 1/67 = 0.01613 + 0.01493 = 0.0311. Both retrievers contribute. A single first place in only one list would score 1/61 = 0.0164, about half as much.

    2. Document A is first in the keyword list only. Document B is 10th in both lists. Which wins at k = 60? At k = 1?

    At k = 60: A = 1/61 = 0.0164; B = 2/70 = 0.0286. B wins, consensus beats a lone first place. At k = 1: A = 1/2 = 0.5; B = 2/11 = 0.18. A wins. Small k rewards top positions, large k rewards agreement.

    3. Why does RRF need no normalisation of BM25 and cosine scores?

    It uses positions, not values. The best document gets rank 1 whether its score is 14.2 or 0.74. The cost is that it ignores how far ahead the leader was.

    4. A cross-encoder scores 100 chunks of 400 tokens each, per question. Roughly how many tokens go through the model, and what does that say about reranking a corpus?

    About 100 × 400 = 40,000 tokens plus the repeated question, per query. A corpus of a million chunks would need 400 million tokens per query, so cross-encoders cannot be the first stage. That is why they only reorder a short list.

    5. Your reranker upgrade made almost no difference. Candidate recall at 50 is 62%. What is wrong and what do you do?

    The ceiling. If the answer is in the candidate list only 62% of the time, the best the reranker can do is 62%. Improve the first stage: add the missing retriever, fix chunking or parsing, apply query rewriting, widen the lists. Then re-test the reranker.

    6. A user asks "which customers are not eligible for the fee waiver?" Why might both first-stage searches rank the "eligible customers" page first, and what fixes it?

    BM25 matches "eligible" and ignores the "not"; the embedding of "not eligible" is close to "eligible". Neither reads the question against the page together. A cross-encoder (or a good LLM reranker) can, so the page about exclusions rises. Also check that the exclusions are in a chunk that is retrieved at all.

    7. You fuse a cross-encoder score into the RRF with the other lists. Why is that usually a poor design?

    RRF only sees the reranker's rank, so you throw away its scores and combine its sharp judgement with the weaker first-stage votes. The cleaner pattern is: RRF to build the candidate list, then sort by the reranker (optionally blended with a few business features).

    8. Name three measurements you need to tune this pipeline properly.

    First-stage recall at several candidate depths; reranked quality (MRR or nDCG at 5, or the share of questions with the answer in the final five); and end-to-end answer correctness, all on a labelled question set split by query type, plus latency at each depth. Compare against BM25-only and dense-only baselines.
