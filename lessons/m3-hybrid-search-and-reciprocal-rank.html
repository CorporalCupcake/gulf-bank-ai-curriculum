    Hybrid Search, Rank Fusion and Reranking: Two Searches, Then a Careful Reader

    Why keyword search and meaning search fail on different questions, how reciprocal rank fusion merges their lists without comparing incomparable scores, and why a slower cross-encoder pass over the top candidates is usually the biggest single upgrade to a RAG system.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRun a keyword search and an embedding search because each finds what the other misses, merge their rankings by position rather than by score, then let a slower model that reads the question and each candidate together reorder a short list, and measure every stage on your own labelled questions.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take the question "What is the daily ATM cash limit on a Platinum card?" The answer sits in three places: a page titled "Platinum: daily cash limit"; a paragraph saying "the maximum you can take out of a cash machine per day is AED 20,000" (no "ATM", no "limit"); and a tariff table "FS-2207" where the only match is a product name and a number.

    • Keyword search (BM25, M3.1) finds the page and the table, because the words are there. It misses the paraphrase.
    • Embedding search (M1, F1.1) finds the page and the paraphrase, because it matches meaning. It can miss the table (a grid of numbers has little meaning to embed) and drift to generic "card limits" pages.

    Neither list is complete. Together they are. Two problems follow.

    • Merging two lists. BM25 gives 14.2, cosine gives 0.72: different scales, different meanings. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores scores and uses only each document's position in each list.
    • Getting the best five to the top. First-stage searches are fast because they never read the question and document together. A cross-encoder reranker does, which is far more accurate and far too slow for everything. So run it only on the 20-100 candidates fusion produced.

    The shape is a funnel, like a hiring process: a cheap CV screen over everyone, then careful interviews for a shortlist. Recommenders use it too (F9.5). In RAG it decides which five passages the language model reads, the biggest single influence on whether the answer is right.

    Hold onto thisRetrieval has two jobs. Recall: the right passage is somewhere in the candidates. Precision at the top: it is in the first few. Hybrid search and fusion buy recall; reranking buys precision. The most common mistake is tuning the wrong stage.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why one retriever is not enough

    The two retrievers fail on different questions. That is the whole case for combining them.

    Kind of queryKeyword (BM25)Embeddings (dense)
    Exact identifiers: "FS-2207", IBAN prefix, circular number, a nameStrong. A rare token carries weight.Weak. The model may never have seen the string.
    Paraphrase: "cash machine" for "ATM"Weak. No shared word, no score.Strong. Similar meaning, nearby vectors.
    Arabic question over English policy, or the reverseVery weak. No shared tokens.Strong with a multilingual model; weaker on dialect and transliterated names.
    Rare domain terms and acronymsStrong if spelled the same.Varies with the training data.
    Long, vague, conceptual questionsMatches common words, returns noise.Strong at topic, weaker at precise conditions.
    Negation and conditions: "who is not eligible", "after 60 days"Ignores the negation.Also weak: "eligible" and "not eligible" embed close together.

    The last row is the gap the reranker closes: both retrievers compare a query with a document summarised without knowing the query. BEIR (18 datasets) found plain BM25 a hard zero-shot baseline, and dense models can fall below it out of domain (Thakur et al., 2021). So serious systems keep both.

    Reciprocal rank fusion

    Adding scores fails because a BM25 score is unbounded and query-dependent while cosine sits in a narrow band. Normalising is fragile (Simulation 2). RRF keeps only the rank. Think of two judges at a talent show who each hand out ranked places: you combine places, not the marks they happen to give.

    score(d)=∑r∈listswrk+rankr(d)

    Each list gives a vote of w ÷ (k + rank); a list that lacks the document gives none. Usual w = 1, k = 60 (Cormack et al., 2009). Rank 1 in one list and rank 5 in the other: 1/61 + 1/65 = 0.0318.

    Reading it. The score has no unit; only the ordering matters. Appearing in both lists roughly doubles the score, so agreement is rewarded.

    What k does. With k = 0, rank 1 is worth 1 and rank 3 is worth 0.33: the top spot dominates. With k = 60, rank 1 is worth 0.0164 and rank 10 is worth 0.0143: nearly flat. Large k is a consensus vote (both lists at a decent rank beats first in one). Small k lets one retriever's confident pick through. Neither is "right"; feel the trade in the simulation.

    def rrf(rankings, k=60, weights=None):
    """rankings: list of ordered lists of doc ids, best first."""
    scores = {}
    for w, ranking in zip(weights or [1] * len(rankings), rankings):
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            scores[doc] = scores.get(doc, 0) + w / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)
    • Fusion is a recall tool. The union holds answers neither list found alone. Expect a good fused top 30, not a perfect top 3; the reranker sorts the rest.
    • RRF is a default, not an optimum. It needs no training data, which is why engines ship it. With a few hundred labelled queries, a tuned blend of normalised scores can beat it (Bruch et al., 2023), because ranks discard confidence: a rank-1 by a mile and a rank-1 by a hair score the same.
    • More than two lists work. Title search, dense on summary, dense on body are three votes. Multi-query RAG (M3.7) fuses lists from several rewrites the same way.

    In practice Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search, Qdrant and others fuse with RRF built in. You control k, the weights, results per side (50-100 typical), and filters (language, entity, date, access rights) applied before the search so restricted documents never enter either list.

    Bi-encoders, cross-encoders and why the second is better but slower

    The difference is when the query and document meet. A bi-encoder is like judging a book by its blurb, written before anyone asked a question. A cross-encoder reads the book with your question in hand.

    • Bi-encoder (the embedding model of dense search): encodes query and each document into separate vectors, documents ahead of time. Relevance is one dot product, so millions of passages search in milliseconds. The price: one vector must serve every future question and cannot know which part matters now.
    • Cross-encoder: takes [CLS] query [SEP] passage [SEP] as one input and runs the full transformer over the pair. Attention (F5.5) lets every query word see every passage word, so it sees that "not" attaches to "eligible", or that "60 days" contradicts "after 120 days". It outputs one relevance number. Fine-tuned on passage ranking, this sharply beat the best first stage of its day (Nogueira and Cho, 2019).

    The cost: nothing can be precomputed, so one query means one forward pass per candidate. 50 candidates of 300 tokens is 15,000 tokens through the model, against a few dozen for a bi-encoder query. A million passages per query is out of the question. Hence the funnel.

    from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

reranker = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3")   # multilingual, includes Arabic
pairs  = [(query, chunk.text) for chunk in candidates]   # e.g. 50 from the fused list
scores = reranker.predict(pairs)                     # one number per pair
top5   = [c for _, c in sorted(zip(scores, candidates), key=lambda t: -t[0])][:5]

    Training uses (query, relevant passage, non-relevant passage) triples with a ranking loss. The hard part is "hard negatives": plausible passages a first stage retrieves. They are what the reranker meets in production, so fine-tuning on your bank's own documents and queries can beat swapping to a bigger generic model.

    The funnel: how deep to rerank, and what it costs

    The design choice is how many candidates N to rerank.

    • Too small: the right passage is not in the list, and a reranker cannot promote what it never sees. First-stage recall at N is a hard ceiling.
    • Too large: latency and cost grow linearly, and there are more plausible wrong passages to be fooled by. Past a point quality is flat or slightly worse.

    Plot candidate recall and final top-5 quality against N and pick the smallest N where both flatten (the knee).

    What decides whether the funnel works in production:

    • Truncation. Rerankers cap input (often 512 tokens). If the cut falls before the relevant sentence, the score is wrong. Design chunk size (M3.5) and reranker window together.
    • Scores are not comparable across queries. A raw 3.1 on one question is not the same confidence as 3.1 on another. Use scores to sort; calibrate on labelled queries (F7.4) before using one as a "no good answer" cut-off.
    • Send fewer passages onward. Keep the top 3-8, not all 50. More passages cost tokens and dilute the prompt; models use the middle of a long context less reliably than the ends (Liu et al., 2023; M1.5).
    • Do not fuse the reranker back into RRF. RRF discards score magnitudes, which is what the reranker is good at. Build candidates with RRF, then sort only by reranker score, optionally blended with recency or authority.
    • Measure stages separately. Record the correct passage's rank after each stage. Candidate recall at N shows whether the first stage is the bottleneck; MRR and nDCG at 5 (F9.5) judge the reranker. MRR is the average of 1/rank of the first correct passage: 0.5 means typically second. Without labels you tune by anecdote.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in real RAG projects and in AI-generated designs. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Tuning the generator when retrieval is the problem

      The answers are wrong, so someone rewrites the prompt or swaps the model. The right passage was never in the context.

      Spot itFor 50 failed answers, check by hand whether the correct passage was in the retrieved set. If it was missing in most, fix retrieval first.
    • Reranking a candidate list that misses the answer

      The team adds an expensive reranker and sees little gain. The first stage returned the wrong 20.

      Spot itMeasure recall at N before the reranker. If it is below about 90% at your depth, widen the first stage, add the hybrid, fix chunking or filters.
    • Adding scores from different scales

      BM25 plus cosine, un-normalised, so one signal silently dominates. Or min-max normalising with an outlier in the list.

      Spot itPrint the fused top results with each side's contribution. If one side is nearly always the winner, the scales are off. Switch to RRF or normalise robustly.
    • Believing the default k = 60 is correct

      A constant from 2009 lists, tuned on different data. It favours consensus and buries confident single-list hits.

      Spot itLook at queries where the right answer is found by only one retriever. Sweep k and the weights on a labelled set before hard-coding.
    • Truncated passages hiding the answer

      The reranker reads only the first 512 tokens of a long chunk. The relevant sentence is at token 700 and the passage scores low.

      Spot itCheck the reranker's maximum length against your chunk length. Test with the answer sentence moved to the end of the chunk.
    • Using the reranker score as a confidence or a cut-off

      "Answer only if the score is above 0.5" with an uncalibrated model. It works for some questions and refuses others at random.

      Spot itPlot the score distribution for questions with known answers and known no-answer questions. Pick the threshold from that plot, and re-check after any model change.
    • Judging by a few impressive demos

      Five hand-picked questions work, the project goes live, and the long tail of real questions does not.

      Spot itAsk for the number of labelled questions, how they were chosen, and results by question type (identifier, paraphrase, Arabic, negation). A few hundred is a sensible start.
    • Permissions applied after retrieval

      The search ranks documents the user may not see, then the application hides them. Best passage gone, or worse, leaked into a prompt.

      Spot itCheck that access filters run inside the search query, before ranking, and that a hosted reranker or embedding service never receives restricted text it should not.
    • Stale or duplicated versions outranking the current one

      Three versions of the same policy; the old one has more keyword overlap and wins.

      Spot itSearch for a policy known to have changed. Check that effective-date filters or metadata prefer the current version and that duplicates are removed before fusion.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. With k = 60, a document is ranked 2nd by BM25 and 7th by the dense search. What is its RRF score?

    1/62 + 1/67 = 0.01613 + 0.01493 = 0.0311. A lone first place scores 1/61 = 0.0164, about half.

    2. Document A is first in the keyword list only. Document B is 10th in both lists. Which wins at k = 60? At k = 1?

    k = 60: A = 1/61 = 0.0164; B = 2/70 = 0.0286. B wins. k = 1: A = 1/2 = 0.5; B = 2/11 = 0.18. A wins. Small k rewards top positions, large k rewards agreement.

    3. Why does RRF need no normalisation of BM25 and cosine scores?

    It uses positions, not values: the best document is rank 1 whether its score is 14.2 or 0.74. The cost is ignoring how far ahead the leader was.

    4. A cross-encoder scores 100 chunks of 400 tokens each, per question. Roughly how many tokens go through the model, and what does that say about reranking a corpus?

    About 100 × 400 = 40,000 tokens per query. A million chunks would need 400 million, so cross-encoders cannot be the first stage; they reorder a short list.

    5. Your reranker upgrade made almost no difference. Candidate recall at 50 is 62%. What is wrong and what do you do?

    The ceiling: the answer is in the candidates only 62% of the time, so the reranker can do no better. Fix the first stage (add the missing retriever, fix chunking or parsing, query rewriting, wider lists), then re-test the reranker.

    6. A user asks "which customers are not eligible for the fee waiver?" Why might both first-stage searches rank the "eligible customers" page first, and what fixes it?

    BM25 matches "eligible" and ignores "not"; the embedding of "not eligible" sits close to "eligible". A cross-encoder (or good LLM reranker) reads question and page together, so the exclusions page rises. Also check the exclusions chunk is retrieved at all.

    7. You fuse a cross-encoder score into the RRF with the other lists. Why is that usually a poor design?

    RRF sees only the reranker's rank, discarding its scores and mixing its sharp judgement with weaker first-stage votes. Use RRF to build candidates, then sort by the reranker (optionally with a few business features).

    8. Name three measurements you need to tune this pipeline properly.

    First-stage recall at several depths; reranked quality (MRR or nDCG at 5); end-to-end answer correctness, all on labelled questions split by query type, plus latency per depth. Compare with BM25-only and dense-only baselines.
