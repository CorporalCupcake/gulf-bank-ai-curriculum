    Hybrid Search, Rank Fusion and Reranking: Two Searches, Then a Careful Reader

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Take the question "What is the daily ATM cash limit on a Platinum card?" The answer sits in three places: a page titled "Platinum: daily cash limit"; a paragraph saying "the maximum you can take out of a cash machine per day is AED 20,000" (no "ATM", no "limit"); and a tariff table "FS-2207".

    • Keyword search (BM25, M3.1) finds the page and the table, because the words are there. It misses the paraphrase.
    • Embedding search (M1, F1.1) finds the page and the paraphrase, because it matches meaning. It can miss the table, which is just numbers.

    Neither list is complete; together they are. Two problems follow. First, merging: BM25 gives 14.2 and cosine gives 0.72, so the scores cannot be compared. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores scores and uses only each document's position in each list. Second, getting the best five to the top: a cross-encoder reranker reads the question and each candidate together, which is accurate but slow, so it runs only on the 20-100 candidates fusion produced.

    The shape is a funnel, like hiring: a cheap CV screen over everyone, then careful interviews for a shortlist. In RAG it decides which five passages the language model reads.

    Hold onto thisRetrieval has two jobs. Recall: the right passage is somewhere in the candidates. Precision at the top: it is in the first few. Hybrid search and fusion buy recall; reranking buys precision. The most common mistake is tuning the wrong stage.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why one retriever is not enough

    The two retrievers fail on different questions, which is the case for combining them.

    Kind of queryKeyword (BM25)Embeddings (dense)
    Exact identifiers: "FS-2207", a circular number, a nameStrong. A rare token carries weight.Weak. The model may never have seen the string.
    Paraphrase: "cash machine" for "ATM"Weak. No shared word, no score.Strong. Similar meaning, nearby vectors.
    Arabic question over English policy, or the reverseVery weak. No shared tokens.Strong with a multilingual model.
    Negation: "who is not eligible"Ignores the negation.Also weak: "eligible" and "not eligible" embed close together.

    The last row is the gap the reranker closes. Serious systems keep both retrievers.

    Reciprocal rank fusion

    Adding scores fails because a BM25 score is unbounded and query-dependent while cosine sits in a narrow band. RRF keeps only the rank. Think of two talent-show judges: you combine their places, not the marks they happen to give.

    Each list gives a document a vote that shrinks with its rank: 1/(k + rank), with k usually 60 (Cormack et al., 2009). Votes from all lists are added; a list that lacks the document gives none. Appearing in both lists roughly doubles the score, so agreement is rewarded.

    What k does. Small k lets one retriever's confident first place win. Large k flattens the votes into a consensus vote: decent in both lists beats first in one. Neither is "right"; feel the trade in the simulation.

    • Fusion is a recall tool. The union holds answers neither list found alone. Expect a good top 30, not a perfect top 3; the reranker sorts the rest.
    • RRF is a default, not an optimum. It needs no training data, which is why engines ship it. With a few hundred labelled queries a tuned score blend can beat it (Bruch et al., 2023), because ranks discard confidence.

    Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search, Qdrant and others ship RRF built in. Apply filters (language, date, access rights) before the search so restricted documents never enter either list.

    Bi-encoders, cross-encoders and why the second is better but slower

    The difference is when the query and document meet. A bi-encoder is like judging a book by its blurb, written before anyone asked a question. A cross-encoder reads the book with your question in hand.

    • Bi-encoder (the embedding model of dense search): turns each document into a vector ahead of time, so millions of passages search in milliseconds. The price: one vector must serve every future question.
    • Cross-encoder: reads query and passage together as one input, so every query word can see every passage word. It notices that "not" attaches to "eligible", or that "60 days" contradicts "after 120 days", and outputs one relevance number.

    The cost: nothing can be precomputed, so one query means one model pass per candidate. Hence the funnel.

    reranker = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3")   # multilingual, includes Arabic
scores = reranker.predict([(query, c.text) for c in candidates])   # one number per pair

    Fine-tuning on your own documents and queries can beat swapping to a bigger generic model.

    The funnel: how deep to rerank, and what it costs

    The design choice is how many candidates N to rerank. Too small: the right passage is not in the list, and a reranker cannot promote what it never sees, so first-stage recall at N is a hard ceiling. Too large: latency and cost grow with N, and there are more plausible wrong passages to be fooled by. Pick the smallest N where quality flattens (the knee).

    What decides whether the funnel works in production:

    • Truncation. Rerankers cap input (often 512 tokens). If the cut falls before the relevant sentence, the score is wrong. Design chunk size (M3.5) and reranker window together.
    • Scores are not comparable across queries. Use them to sort; calibrate on labelled queries (F7.4) before using one as a "no good answer" cut-off.
    • Do not fuse the reranker back into RRF. RRF discards score magnitudes, which is what the reranker is good at. Build candidates with RRF, then sort only by reranker score.
    • Measure stages separately. Record the correct passage's rank after each stage. Candidate recall at N shows whether the first stage is the bottleneck; MRR (average of 1/rank of the first correct passage; 0.5 means typically second) and nDCG at 5 (F9.5) judge the reranker.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in real RAG projects and in AI-generated designs. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Tuning the generator when retrieval is the problem

      Someone rewrites the prompt, but the right passage was never in the context.

      Spot itFor 50 failed answers, check by hand whether the correct passage was retrieved. If it was missing in most, fix retrieval first.
    • Reranking a candidate list that misses the answer

      An expensive reranker gives little gain because the first stage returned the wrong 20.

      Spot itMeasure recall at N before the reranker. If it is below about 90%, widen the first stage, add the hybrid, fix chunking or filters.
    • Adding scores from different scales, or trusting k = 60

      BM25 plus cosine, un-normalised, so one signal silently dominates. Or leaving RRF at its default, which favours consensus and buries confident single-list hits.

      Spot itPrint the fused top results with each side's contribution. Look at queries where only one retriever found the answer, and sweep k and weights on a labelled set.
    • Truncated passages and uncalibrated cut-offs

      The reranker reads only the first 512 tokens, so a relevant sentence at token 700 scores low. Or "answer only if the score is above 0.5" refuses questions at random.

      Spot itCheck the reranker's maximum length against your chunk length. Pick any threshold from the score distribution of known-answer and no-answer questions, and re-check after any model change.
    • Judging by a few impressive demos

      Five hand-picked questions work, then the long tail of real questions does not.

      Spot itAsk for the number of labelled questions and results by question type. A few hundred is a sensible start.
    • Permissions and stale versions handled after retrieval

      The search ranks documents the user may not see, or an old policy version outranks the current one.

      Spot itCheck that access and effective-date filters run inside the search query, before ranking, and that duplicates are removed before fusion.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A document is ranked 2nd by BM25 and 7th by the dense search. Why does RRF score it higher than a lone first place?

    With k = 60 the two votes (about 0.016 and 0.015) add to roughly 0.031, nearly double the 0.016 of a lone first place. At the usual k, being found by both retrievers beats topping one.

    2. Document A is first in the keyword list only. Document B is 10th in both lists. Which wins at k = 60? At k = 1?

    At k = 60 B wins, because two near-equal votes beat one. At k = 1 A wins, because the top spot is worth far more than a tenth place. Small k rewards top positions, large k rewards agreement.

    3. Why does RRF need no normalisation of BM25 and cosine scores?

    It uses positions, not values: the best document is rank 1 whether its score is 14.2 or 0.74. The cost is ignoring how far ahead the leader was.

    4. Why can a cross-encoder rerank 100 chunks per question but not a million?

    Nothing can be precomputed, so every chunk needs a full model pass for every query. 100 chunks is manageable; a million per query is impossible. Cross-encoders reorder a short list and cannot be the first stage.

    5. Your reranker upgrade made almost no difference. Candidate recall at 50 is 62%. What is wrong and what do you do?

    The ceiling: the answer is in the candidates only 62% of the time, so the reranker can do no better. Fix the first stage (add the missing retriever, fix chunking or parsing, query rewriting, wider lists), then re-test the reranker.

    6. A user asks "which customers are not eligible for the fee waiver?" Why might both first-stage searches rank the "eligible customers" page first, and what fixes it?

    BM25 matches "eligible" and ignores "not"; the embedding of "not eligible" sits close to "eligible". A cross-encoder (or good LLM reranker) reads question and page together, so the exclusions page rises. Also check the exclusions chunk is retrieved at all.

    7. You fuse a cross-encoder score into the RRF with the other lists. Why is that usually a poor design?

    RRF sees only the reranker's rank, discarding its scores and mixing its sharp judgement with weaker first-stage votes. Use RRF to build candidates, then sort by the reranker (optionally with a few business features).

    8. Name three measurements you need to tune this pipeline properly.

    First-stage recall at several depths; reranked quality (MRR or nDCG at 5); end-to-end answer correctness, all on labelled questions split by query type, plus latency per depth. Compare with BM25-only and dense-only baselines.
