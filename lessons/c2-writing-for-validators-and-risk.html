    Writing for Validators, Charts That Carry One Message, and Facing Hard Questions

    How to write a model document a sceptical independent reviewer can say yes to, how to make a chart say one true thing, and how to stay useful when a senior person in the room disagrees with you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceYour readers are not trying to be convinced, they are trying to find what you did not say, so write so that every claim has its evidence next to it, every chart makes one honest point, and every hard question gets a calm answer that separates what you know, what you assumed and what you will go and check.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    You have built a model that flags suspicious account activity. Three people need to hear about it, and none of them wants the same thing.

    • The business head wants a decision: do we deploy, and what do we gain? That is the executive summary of C2.1.
    • The independent validator, a team that did not build the model and reports separately, wants to know how it could be wrong. Their job is to challenge you. A finding they raise goes on a list that the risk committee reads.
    • The risk committee sits above both. It wants to know whether the bank's exposure to this model is understood, owned and bounded, and it rarely has time to read more than a few pages.

    The same facts serve all three, but in a different order and with a different burden of proof. The business reader trusts a clear answer. The validator trusts only what they can check. The committee trusts a validator who has signed off and an owner who knows the limits.

    This lesson covers three skills that follow from that. Writing for the sceptical reader. Charts, which are the fastest way to persuade and the fastest way to mislead. And live presentation, where you cannot edit and a senior person may push back in front of colleagues.

    Hold onto thisA validator reads to find gaps, so honesty about weaknesses earns more trust than polish. A chart should carry one message you could say in a sentence. And in a live challenge, your aim is not to win the exchange, it is to leave the room with the decision made on accurate information.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C2.3 Writing for validators and risk committees

    Model risk management in banks rests on a simple idea: the people who build a model are poorly placed to judge it, so a separate team challenges it. Validators check three broad things, in the language used by the long-standing US supervisory guidance and mirrored in many other jurisdictions: whether the concept is sound, whether the implementation does what the document says, and whether outcomes are monitored. (See G3.3 for how the validation function works and G3.1 for what counts as a model at all.)

    Your document is the validator's main evidence. A good test: could a competent stranger, with your document and the data, reproduce your numbers and explain why you made each choice? If not, the validator will have to ask, which is slower and signals gaps.

    What a validator looks for

    • Intended use and limits. What decision is the model for, on what population, and where must it not be used? Say the out-of-scope uses yourself.
    • Data and definitions. Where each field comes from, the sample window, exclusions and why, how the target is defined, and checks that nothing from the future leaked in (F3.1).
    • Method and alternatives. Why this model type, what simpler benchmark you beat, and what you tried and discarded. A validator seeing only the winner suspects selection.
    • Performance with uncertainty. Out-of-time results, intervals (F2.2), segment results, and calibration where probabilities are used (F7).
    • Drivers. What the model relies on, in plain terms, with evidence (F8).
    • Limitations and monitoring. Known weaknesses, the assumptions that must hold, what is monitored, and the trigger levels that cause a review (G3.5).

    Regulators in both of the bank's home markets expect a formal framework. The UAE Central Bank issued Model Management Standards, with companion Guidance, in late 2022, covering governance, data, development, implementation, usage, monitoring and independent validation, and they include documentation and independent-validation sections. I have not read the full text of those sections for this lesson, so check the Rulebook and your bank's own model policy for what exactly is required, and for how they apply to a Saudi-regulated entity. In the US the long-standing SR 11-7 was replaced in April 2026 by revised interagency guidance (SR 26-2), which keeps the same architecture but takes a more risk-based approach; reports differ on how it treats AI models, so treat that as unsettled.

    Writing patterns that work

    WeakStrongWhy the validator prefers it
    “The model performs well.”“Out-of-time recall at 300 alerts a day is 71% (95% interval 66–76%, 410 confirmed cases), against 58% for the existing rules.”A number, a base, an interval and a benchmark: all checkable.
    “Features were selected for relevance.”“Of 240 candidates, 38 were kept. Selection used the 2023 training window only; dropped features and reasons are in Appendix B.”Shows the process and that the test data stayed untouched.
    “Limitations: none significant.”“Not validated for new-to-bank customers (under 3 months); only 1,100 such cases, recall unstable. Do not use for them.”A named limit is a limit managed. “None” invites the validator to find one.
    “We used SHAP to explain the model.”“Mean absolute SHAP shows transaction velocity and merchant risk dominate. Dependence plots show no use of nationality. Stability across quarters: Appendix D.”States what was seen, not just the tool.
    Results first, definitions in an appendix nobody reads.One-page summary up front, then numbered sections that follow the validator's checklist, with a change log.Lets them tick items off and find what changed since last review.

    Two structural habits help more than any phrasing. First, give every claim a place to be checked: a table, a notebook reference, a version of the code. Second, write the limitations section first, while you are still clear-eyed, and then check the headline claims against it. If your summary says “robust” and your limitations say “unstable on small segments”, one of them is wrong.

    For the risk committee you write a different, shorter thing, usually two to four pages: the purpose, the risk rating of the model, the validation outcome, open findings with owners and dates, residual limitations, and the decision requested. Lead with what you are asking them to approve or note. Committee members are senior and read quickly, so they remember the finding that sounded unresolved.

    C2.4 Telling a story with data and charts

    A chart is an argument. The reader takes in its shape in about two seconds and reads the labels afterwards, if ever. So the shape must already say what you want, and what it says must be true.

    The discipline is one chart, one message. Write the message as a sentence first: “Alert precision has not changed in two years.” Then choose the chart that shows exactly that, remove everything else, and put the sentence in the title. A title that says “Alert precision by month” names the topic; a title that says the finding tells the reader what to look for.

    • Choose the form for the comparison. Lines for change over time, bars for comparing amounts from zero, dots or slopes for before and after. Avoid pie charts beyond two or three parts, and avoid 3D, which distorts size.
    • Bars start at zero; lines need not, but say so. Bar length encodes the value, so truncating bars exaggerates. A line shows change, so a non-zero baseline is acceptable when the range is labelled, but it makes small movements look large.
    • Show uncertainty. A line wiggling between 62% and 68% each month may be noise. A band or interval stops the reader mistaking noise for trend (F2.1).
    • Remove ink that does not help. Heavy gridlines, legends you could replace with a direct label, ten colours. Highlight the one series that matters in a strong colour and grey the rest.
    • Pick the window honestly. Starting a line at a trough produces a rise from almost any noisy series. Show enough history to give context, and say how the window was chosen.

    The exploration side of this (F3.3) is about finding things; this is about showing one of them without distortion. Below is a simulated series of monthly alert precision that is, in truth, flat with noise. Use the controls to see how the same data can be made to tell opposite stories.

    Two things to notice. The first is that no axis choice is neutral: zoomed, the eye sees a large move; unzoomed, a small one. Your job is to pick the one that matches what the evidence supports and to label it. The second is that the start month matters as much as the axis. Choosing a window after looking at the data is a quiet form of the forking-paths problem (F2.3), and reviewers recognise it.

    Practical checks before a chart leaves your desk: read the title alone and see if it states the finding; check the axis range and units; ask whether a colleague with no context would draw the same conclusion in five seconds; and ask what the chart hides, such as a segment that behaves differently.

    For a validator or committee, the same rules apply with extra rigour. Put sample sizes on the chart or its caption, state the period, and keep figure formats consistent across the pack so differences between figures mean something.

    C2.5 Presenting live and handling hard questions

    Writing allows editing. A live room does not. What carries you is structure and a way of treating questions that works even when you are nervous.

    Structure the talk

    • Open with the answer and the ask. “I'm recommending we pilot the model on 20% of cards from March; I need your approval for that.” Context follows. This is C2.1 delivered aloud.
    • Three points, not twelve. People remember about three. Pick the three that matter for this audience and cut the rest into backup slides.
    • One idea per slide, with the sentence as the title. Keep backup slides for the questions you expect.
    • Rehearse the first minute and the close. Nerves peak at the start and you want a smooth landing.

    A routine for hard questions

    1. Listen to the end, then pause. Interrupting looks defensive; a two-second pause looks thoughtful.
    2. Restate the question. “So the concern is that fraud on this segment is higher than we modelled?” It checks you understood, buys thinking time, and shows you take it seriously.
    3. Sort it. Is this a fact I know, an assumption I made, a judgement that reasonable people differ on, or something I don't know?
    4. Answer in that category. “We measured this: 71%.” “That's an assumption; here's what happens if it's wrong.” “I don't know; I'll check the segment and send you the figure by Thursday.” Then do it.
    5. Check you answered. “Does that address your concern?”

    When a senior person disagrees, they are usually protecting something: the budget, a customer segment, their own regulatory exposure, or a past experience with a model that failed. Ask what worry sits behind the challenge. Agree on what you can (“you're right that the data is thin on new customers”) before holding firm on what you can't. If you were wrong, say so briefly and fix it; that is cheap, and it makes your other answers more believable. If you are right and the person is not persuaded, do not escalate the argument in the room. Offer to bring the evidence, name what would change your mind, and move on.

    In the Gulf context relationships and seniority carry real weight, and public contradiction of a senior person can cost more than it gains. This is a generalisation and varies a great deal by organisation, so read your own room. The usual workable route is to disagree with the claim and not the person, to defer to the senior person on the decision while being clear about the evidence, and to follow up privately or in writing (more in C3.5).

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Model development and validation cycles. A fraud, AML or credit model usually has a developer document, a validator report with findings, and a committee paper. You write the first and feed the other two.
    • IFRS 9 and credit risk packs. Heavy documentation, strong audit and regulator attention, and numbers that move provisions. Interval and calibration evidence is expected.
    • Annual reviews and monitoring reports. Charts of stability and performance over time, where honest windows and uncertainty bands prevent false alarms and false comfort.
    • Board and committee briefings. Short, live, and often with a senior member who has strong views. Backup slides and calm sorting of questions matter here.
    • Arabic and English materials. If a committee pack or chart is read in both languages, check that labels, numerals and reading direction are consistent. Do not assume a figure built for one translates untouched.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in documents, charts and rooms. Each comes with a quick check you can run on your own work.

    • Hiding limitations to look strong

      The validator finds the weak segment anyway, and now also doubts everything else.

      Spot itSearch your draft for “robust”, “significant” and “no issues”. Each needs evidence beside it, or a limit next to it.
    • Claims with no place to check them

      “Performance is stable” with no table, period, base or code version.

      Spot itPick any sentence and ask where a stranger would verify it. If you cannot point to a table or notebook, add one.
    • Numbers that disagree across the pack

      The summary says 71%, the appendix says 69%, because a version changed.

      Spot itRegenerate the figures from code before sending, and compare the summary to the appendix line by line.
    • Dumping everything on a committee

      Forty pages for a body that reads four. The one weakness gets lost, or found and resented.

      Spot itCan the paper be summarised as a decision, a rating and three open items? If not, restructure.
    • A truncated or cherry-picked chart

      A bar chart starting at 60%, or a line started at a trough, makes noise look like drama.

      Spot itCheck the axis start, the window and whether the change exceeds the noise. Add a band.
    • Too many messages in one chart

      Six lines, two axes, a legend the audience has to decode.

      Spot itWrite the one sentence the chart should say. If you need two, make two charts.
    • Defending instead of answering

      A question about a weakness gets a long justification, and the room hears fear.

      Spot itRecord a practice answer. If the first sentence is not an answer to the question, rewrite it.
    • Bluffing on something you don't know

      A confident guess that is later shown wrong ends your credibility on everything.

      Spot itBefore answering, ask which of fact, assumption, judgement or unknown it is. If unknown, say so and give a date.
    • Winning the argument, losing the room

      You correct a senior person publicly and are right, and the decision goes against you.

      Spot itAsk what outcome you want. Often it is a better decision, which a private follow-up with evidence serves best.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    Opening a committee paper · 30 seconds
    “We are asking the committee to approve the transaction-monitoring model for use on retail accounts from March. Independent validation rated it satisfactory with two open findings, both with owners and dates. The main limit is that it has not been validated on accounts under three months old, so we propose to exclude them and review after two quarters of data.”
    To a validator who has found a gap
    “Agreed, that's a real gap. We have 90 confirmed cases in that segment, so recall there has a wide interval, roughly plus or minus ten points. I've added it to the limitations, restricted use for that segment, and put a monitoring trigger in place. I can have the updated pack to you by Thursday.”
    “Why should I trust your 71% recall?”

    Because it comes from out-of-time data the model never saw, with a stated interval, on 410 confirmed cases, against a rules benchmark. The interval is 66 to 76%, so I would plan on 66. Code version and notebook are referenced in section 5 if you want to reproduce it.

    “My team says fraud in this segment is up threefold. Your model is wrong.”

    That could matter a great deal, so let me check what it means. If fraud tripled, the model may be missing a new pattern; it may also be that confirmed labels are arriving late or that the segment grew. I'd like to compare the model's alert rate and recall on that segment over the last three months, and I'll bring that to you by Thursday. Can your team share the cases?

    “Why not just use the simpler rule set?”

    We tested it as the benchmark. On the same out-of-time data the rules catch 58% at 300 alerts a day and the model 71%. If simplicity and explainability outweigh those 13 points for the committee, the rules are a defensible choice, and I'd say so in the paper.

    “Your chart looks like precision is rising. Is it?”

    Not reliably. The axis is zoomed to make the movement visible. The fitted trend is under one point a year with a band that includes zero, so I'd read it as flat with noise. I'll replace it with the full-axis version and the band.

    “I don't have time. What's the bottom line?”

    Approve a pilot on 20% of cards from March. It should catch about two thirds of the fraud we currently miss, with a review team load we can staff. The one risk is new-to-bank customers, which we exclude. I need a yes or a no on the pilot today.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a validator treat “Limitations: none significant” as a warning sign?

    Every model has limits: thin segments, assumptions, data gaps. “None” suggests the author has not looked or is not disclosing, and it invites the validator to find some. A named limit with a boundary on use shows it is under control.

    2. Rewrite “The model performs well” so it can be checked.

    Give the metric, the operating point, the data period, the sample size, an interval and a benchmark. Example: “Recall at 300 alerts a day on out-of-time data is 71% (66–76%, 410 cases), against 58% for the current rules.”

    3. A bar chart shows precision of 64% against 66%, with the axis starting at 60%. What is wrong and what do you change?

    Bar length encodes the value, so the 60% start makes the 66% bar look much more than a bit longer (6 against 4 units of length, a 50% difference for a 3% real difference). Start bars at zero, or use dots and an interval. Better, ask whether a 2-point gap exceeds noise at all.

    4. In Simulation 2, why does starting the line at month 14 change the story?

    The fixed series has a small drift and noise. A short window sits on whatever the noise did, and over a short span a few lucky months produce a visible rise, while the band is wide because there are few points. A window chosen after seeing the data is a forking path.

    5. You are asked a question and genuinely do not know. What do you say?

    Restate it, say plainly that you don't know yet, say what you'll do to find out and by when, and offer what you do know that bears on it. Then do it. This costs little and a bluff costs a lot if exposed.

    6. A senior colleague says your model is “obviously overfit”. How do you respond in the room?

    Ask what they have seen that suggests it, since the worry may come from experience. Agree overfitting is the right thing to worry about, then show the out-of-time result and the gap between training and test. If the gap is small, say so calmly; if you haven't tested it, say you will and when.

    7. Your risk committee paper is 30 pages. What do you do?

    Write a two to four page front paper with the decision requested, the rating, open findings with owners and dates, and the main limits, and move the detail to an appendix or the validator-facing document. The committee is not the same reader as the validator.
