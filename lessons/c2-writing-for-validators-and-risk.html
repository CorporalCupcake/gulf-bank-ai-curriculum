    Writing for Validators and Facing Hard Questions

    Part 1

    Three readers, three questions

    In one sentenceA validator is an independent reviewer who did not build your model and is paid to find how it could be wrong, so you write for them by showing your weaknesses and making every claim checkable.

    The running example is a model that flags suspicious account activity. Think of a house survey: the brochure says "charming", the surveyor is paid to find the damp.

    How it differs from writing for a business head

    • A business head wants a decision and the gain. That is the answer-first summary from the previous lesson.
    • A validator wants evidence. They check that the concept is sound, that the code does what the document says, and that outcomes are monitored.
    • A risk committee wants a rating, open findings with owners and dates, and the decision requested, in two to four pages.
    Part 2

    Writing for the validator

    Builders are poorly placed to judge their own model, like a student marking their own exam. Your document is the validator's main evidence. The test: could a competent stranger, with your document and the data, reproduce your numbers and explain each choice?

    What they look for:

    1. Intended use and limits. The decision, the population, and where the model must not be used. State the out-of-scope uses yourself.
    2. Data and method. Where each field comes from, how the target is defined, checks that nothing from the future leaked in, and the simpler benchmark you beat. Showing only the winner looks like selection.
    3. Performance with uncertainty. Out-of-time results (tested on a later period than training), ranges, segments, and calibration if you output probabilities.
    4. Limitations and monitoring. Known weaknesses, what is watched, and the trigger levels that force a review.

    Weak and strong sentences

    WeakStrongWhy it works
    "The model performs well.""Out-of-time recall at 300 alerts a day is 71% (95% range 66 to 76%, 410 confirmed cases), against 58% for the current rules."A number, a base, a range and a benchmark, all checkable.
    "Limitations: none significant.""Not validated for customers under 3 months old: only 1,100 cases, recall unstable. Do not use for them."A named limit is a managed limit. "None" invites the validator to find one.
    "We used SHAP to explain it.""Transaction velocity and merchant risk dominate. No use of nationality."States what was seen, not just the tool.

    Two habits beat any phrasing. Give every claim a place to be checked: a table, notebook or code version. And write the limitations first, then check the headlines against them. If the summary says "robust" and the limitations say "unstable on small segments", one is wrong.

    Both home regulators expect a formal model framework. Check your bank's model policy for the exact requirements.

    Part 3

    Charts that carry one message

    A chart is an argument. The reader gets its shape in two seconds and the labels later, if ever. The rule is one chart, one message. Write the message as a sentence and use it as the title: "Alert precision has not changed in two years", not "Alert precision by month".

    • Bars start at zero. Bar length is the value, so cutting the axis exaggerates. A 64% bar next to a 66% bar on an axis starting at 60% looks like a doubling.
    • Show uncertainty. A line wobbling between 62% and 68% may be noise. A band stops noise passing as a trend.
    • Choose the window honestly. Starting at a trough gives a rise from almost any noisy series. Picking the start after seeing the data is a quiet way of cheating.

    Check before sending: does the title alone state the finding? Would a colleague with no context reach the same conclusion in five seconds?

    Part 4

    Presenting live and facing hard questions

    Structure the talk. Open with the answer and the ask: "I recommend a pilot on 20% of cards from March, and I need your approval." Give three points, not twelve, and push the rest to backup slides. Make each slide's title its sentence.

    When a hard question comes, follow five steps.

    1. Listen to the end, then pause. Interrupting looks defensive.
    2. Restate the question. It checks you understood and buys thinking time.
    3. Sort it into one of four kinds, below.
    4. Answer in that category.
    5. Check: "Does that address your concern?"
    Kind of questionWhat it isWhat you say
    FactYou measured it"We measured this: 71%."
    AssumptionYou chose it"That is an assumption. Here is what happens if it is wrong."
    JudgementReasonable people differGive your view, your reason, and what would change it.
    Don't knowNo evidence yet"I don't know. I will send the figure by Thursday." Then do it.

    A senior person who disagrees is usually protecting something: a budget, a customer segment, or the memory of a model that failed. Ask what worry sits behind the challenge and agree where you can. If you were wrong, say so briefly and fix it. If you are right and still unpersuaded, do not escalate in the room. Offer the evidence, name what would change your mind, and follow up privately or in writing. In the Gulf, publicly contradicting a senior person can cost more than it gains. That is a generalisation, so read your own room.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Hiding limitations to look strong

      The validator finds the weak segment anyway, then doubts everything else. Search your draft for "robust" and "no issues": each needs evidence or a limit beside it.

    • Claims with no place to check them

      "Performance is stable" with no table, period or code version, or a summary that says 71% while the appendix says 69%. Regenerate figures from code and compare.

    • Dumping everything on a committee

      Forty pages for a body that reads four. If the paper cannot be said as a decision, a rating and three open items, restructure it.

    • Bluffing or winning the argument

      A confident guess later shown wrong, or publicly correcting a senior person and losing the room. Answer in the first sentence, or say you will find out.

    1. Why does a validator treat "Limitations: none significant" as a warning sign?

    Every model has limits. "None" suggests the author has not looked, or is hiding them. A named limit with a boundary on use shows control.

    2. Rewrite "The model performs well" so it can be checked.

    Give the metric, the operating point, the period, the sample size, a range and a benchmark, as in the 71% example above.

    3. A bar chart shows precision of 64% against 66%, with the axis starting at 60%. What do you change?

    Start the bars at zero, or use dots with ranges. Better still, ask whether a two-point gap exceeds noise at all.

    4. You are asked something you genuinely do not know. What do you say?

    Restate it, say plainly you do not know yet, say how and by when you will find out, and offer what you do know. Then do it.

    5. Your risk committee paper is 30 pages. What do you do?

    Write a two to four page front paper (decision requested, rating, open findings with owners and dates, main limits) and move the detail to an appendix.
