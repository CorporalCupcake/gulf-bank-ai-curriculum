    Writing for Validators, Charts That Carry One Message, and Facing Hard Questions

    How to write a model document a sceptical independent reviewer can say yes to, how to make a chart say one true thing, and how to stay useful when a senior person in the room disagrees with you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceYour readers are looking for what you did not say, so put evidence beside every claim, make each chart carry one honest point, and answer hard questions calmly by separating what you know, what you assumed and what you will check.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a house survey. The brochure says "charming"; the surveyor is paid to find the damp. Your model flags suspicious account activity, and three readers will judge it differently.

    • The business head wants a decision: deploy or not, and what do we gain? That is the executive summary of C2.1.
    • The independent validator did not build the model and looks for how it could be wrong.
    • The risk committee asks whether the bank's exposure to the model is understood, owned and bounded.

    Three skills follow: writing for the sceptic, charts, and live presentation.

    Hold onto thisHonesty about weaknesses earns more trust from a validator than polish. A chart carries one message you can say in a sentence. In a live challenge, the aim is not to win but to leave with the decision made on accurate information.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C2.3 Writing for validators and risk committees

    Builders are poorly placed to judge their own model, like a student marking their own exam. So a separate team challenges it. Validators check three things: is the concept sound, does the implementation do what the document says, and are outcomes monitored. (See G3.3 and G3.1.)

    Your document is their main evidence. The test: could a competent stranger, with your document and the data, reproduce your numbers and explain each choice?

    What a validator looks for

    • Intended use and limits. The decision, the population, and where the model must not be used. State the out-of-scope uses yourself.
    • Data and method. Source of each field, target definition, checks that nothing from the future leaked in (F3.1), and the simpler benchmark you beat. Showing only the winner looks like selection.
    • Performance with uncertainty. Out-of-time results, intervals (F2.2), segments, and calibration where probabilities are used (F7).
    • Limitations and monitoring. Known weaknesses, what is monitored, and the trigger levels that force a review (G3.5).

    Both home markets expect a formal framework. The UAE Central Bank issued Model Management Standards and Guidance in late 2022. I have not read the full documentation and validation sections, so check the Rulebook and your bank's model policy, including how they apply to a Saudi-regulated entity.

    Writing patterns that work

    WeakStrongWhy the validator prefers it
    “The model performs well.”“Out-of-time recall at 300 alerts a day is 71% (95% interval 66–76%, 410 confirmed cases), against 58% for the existing rules.”A number, a base, an interval and a benchmark: all checkable.
    “Limitations: none significant.”“Not validated for new-to-bank customers (under 3 months); only 1,100 such cases, recall unstable. Do not use for them.”A named limit is a limit managed. “None” invites the validator to find one.
    “We used SHAP to explain the model.”“Mean absolute SHAP shows transaction velocity and merchant risk dominate. Dependence plots show no use of nationality.”States what was seen, not just the tool.

    Two habits beat any phrasing. Give every claim a place to be checked: a table, notebook reference or code version. And write the limitations first, then check the headlines against them. If the summary says “robust” and the limitations say “unstable on small segments”, one is wrong.

    The risk committee gets two to four pages: purpose, risk rating, validation outcome, open findings with owners and dates, and the decision requested. Lead with the ask. Senior readers skim.

    C2.4 Telling a story with data and charts

    A chart is an argument, like a newspaper headline: the reader gets its shape in two seconds and the labels later, if ever.

    The rule is one chart, one message. Write the message as a sentence (“Alert precision has not changed in two years”) and put it in the title. “Alert precision by month” names a topic; the sentence tells the reader what to look for.

    • Bars start at zero; lines need not, but say so. Bar length is the value, so truncating exaggerates.
    • Show uncertainty. A line wobbling between 62% and 68% may be noise. A band stops noise passing as trend (F2.1).
    • Pick the window honestly. Starting at a trough gives a rise from almost any noisy series.

    Below, monthly alert precision is flat with noise. See how the same data tells opposite stories.

    No axis choice is neutral: zoomed, the eye sees a big move; unzoomed, a small one. Pick the one the evidence supports and label it. Choosing the start month after seeing the data is a quiet forking-paths problem (F2.3), and reviewers spot it.

    Before a chart leaves your desk: does the title alone state the finding? Would a colleague with no context reach the same conclusion in five seconds?

    C2.5 Presenting live and handling hard questions

    Structure the talk

    • Open with the answer and the ask. “I'm recommending a pilot on 20% of cards from March; I need your approval.” Context follows.
    • Three points, not twelve. People remember about three. Push the rest to backup slides.
    • One idea per slide, the sentence as the title.

    A routine for hard questions

    1. Listen to the end, then pause. Interrupting looks defensive.
    2. Restate the question. It checks understanding and buys thinking time.
    3. Sort it. Is it a fact you know, an assumption you made, a judgement people differ on, or something you don't know?
    4. Answer in that category. “We measured this: 71%.” “That's an assumption; here's what happens if it's wrong.” “I don't know; I'll send the figure by Thursday.” Then do it.
    5. Check. “Does that address your concern?”

    A senior person who disagrees is usually protecting something: budget, a customer segment, or the memory of a model that failed. Ask what worry sits behind the challenge and agree where you can. If you were wrong, say so briefly and fix it. If you are right and unpersuaded, do not escalate in the room: offer the evidence, name what would change your mind, move on.

    In the Gulf, publicly contradicting a senior person can cost more than it gains. This is a generalisation; read your own room. Disagree with the claim, not the person, and follow up privately or in writing (C3.5).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Hiding limitations to look strong

      The validator finds the weak segment anyway, then doubts everything else.

      Spot itSearch your draft for “robust” and “no issues”. Each needs evidence or a limit beside it.
    • Claims with no place to check them

      “Performance is stable”, with no table, period, base or code version. Or the summary says 71% and the appendix 69%.

      Spot itPick any sentence: where would a stranger verify it? Regenerate figures from code and compare summary to appendix.
    • Dumping everything on a committee

      Forty pages for a body that reads four. The one weakness gets lost, or found and resented.

      Spot itCan the paper be said as a decision, a rating and three open items? If not, restructure.
    • A truncated, cherry-picked or crowded chart

      Bars starting at 60%, a line started at a trough, or six lines and two axes. Noise looks like drama, or the message is lost.

      Spot itWrite the one sentence the chart should say. Check axis start, window, and whether the change exceeds noise.
    • Defending or bluffing instead of answering

      A long justification, or a confident guess later shown wrong. Either way the room hears fear.

      Spot itIf your first sentence does not answer the question, rewrite it. If you don't know, say so and give a date.
    • Winning the argument, losing the room

      You correct a senior person publicly, you are right, and the decision goes against you.

      Spot itAsk what outcome you want. Usually a better decision, which a private follow-up with evidence serves best.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a validator treat “Limitations: none significant” as a warning sign?

    Every model has limits. “None” suggests the author has not looked, or is hiding them. A named limit with a boundary on use shows control.

    2. Rewrite “The model performs well” so it can be checked.

    Give the metric, the operating point, the period, the sample size, an interval and a benchmark. For example: “Recall at 300 alerts a day on out-of-time data is 71% (66–76%, 410 cases), against 58% for the current rules.”

    3. A bar chart shows precision of 64% against 66%, with the axis starting at 60%. What is wrong and what do you change?

    Bar length is the value, so a truncated axis makes the second bar look far longer than a two-point gap deserves. Start bars at zero, or use dots with an interval. Better still, ask whether the gap exceeds noise at all.

    4. In Simulation 2, why does starting the line at month 14 change the story?

    A short window sits on whatever the noise did, so a few lucky months look like a rise. A window chosen after seeing the data is a forking path.

    5. You are asked a question and genuinely do not know. What do you say?

    Restate it, say plainly you don't know yet, say how and by when you'll find out, and offer what you do know. Then do it. A bluff costs far more if exposed.

    6. A senior colleague says your model is “obviously overfit”. How do you respond in the room?

    Ask what they have seen, since the worry may come from experience. Agree it is worth worrying about, then show the out-of-time result. If untested, say you will test it and when.

    7. Your risk committee paper is 30 pages. What do you do?

    Write a two to four page front paper (decision requested, rating, open findings with owners and dates, main limits) and move the detail to an appendix. The committee is a different reader.
