    Know Your Agent: When AI Agents Pay

    Part 1

    What Know Your Agent is

    In one sentenceKnow Your Agent is the set of checks that lets a bank and a merchant trust an AI agent to pay on a customer's behalf: who the agent is, who it acts for, what it may spend, and proof of all three.

    Card payments assume a person is paying, and fraud systems look for human signs such as typing rhythm. Now a customer tells an assistant, "Book me a flight to Muscat next Thursday, under AED 1,500," and the agent pays. To the merchant, that looks like a bot, and bots have always been treated as fraud.

    The picture to hold is a valet key: it starts the car but does not open the boot. An agent needs the same for money, a credential tied to a known agent and customer, with limits built in.

    Status checked 26 October 2026. This area moves fast, so re-check before relying on any detail. Items marked Live are in real use, Proposed are drafts.

    How it differs from what banks do today

    • Card-not-present payments assume a human at a screen. Agent payments have no typing, no mouse and often no human present.
    • Bot blocking treats all automation as hostile. Agents are automation you want to let through, so the question becomes which agents.
    • Know your customer (KYC) checks a person. Know Your Agent adds checks on the software and the business running it (know your business, KYB).
    RememberIdentity is not authority. Proving an agent is genuine tells you who is asking, not whether the customer wanted this purchase.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Identify the agent. The operator keeps a private key and the public key goes into a network directory after the business is vetted (KYB). Each request is signed, with a short expiry and a one-off random number (a nonce). The standard is Web Bot Auth. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol (Live) allows signatures of at most 8 minutes, and merchants block any nonce already seen, which stops replays.
    2. Bind it to the customer and card. This builds on network tokenisation, as in Apple Pay: the merchant sees a substitute token, not the real card number, plus a one-time cryptogram per payment. For agents the token is tied to that agent and that card (Mastercard's Agentic Tokens, Visa Intelligent Commerce). The customer sets it up with a passkey, the phone's fingerprint or face check, so nobody can enrol a stolen card.
    3. Write down the permission. The customer's limits become something a machine can enforce and nobody can quietly change.
    4. Pay. The issuing bank approves the token, honours the limits, and makes the final authorisation call, now with a signal that an agent is involved.
    5. Keep evidence. The signed permission, agent identity and transaction events are stored for disputes.

    How each network writes the permission

    • Visa Intelligent Commerce: the agent submits a purchase instruction carrying the customer's limits, and credentials are released only against it. Live
    • Mastercard: from August 2026 issuers attach spend controls to a token (amount limits, single use, allow or block lists), and Mastercard declines rule-breakers before the bank sees them. Live
    • Google AP2: signed mandates, each "open" (customer constraints) or "closed" (one specific purchase). Proposed

    Human present or not? If the customer approves the exact basket, the agent is a fast shopping assistant. If they said "buy the tickets when they go on sale, up to AED 400" and left, the pre-signed limits are all that stand between them and a mistake.

    Part 3

    How to think about limits and attacks

    Each layer stops different attacks. Running example: a genuine, verified agent reads a web page with hidden text telling it to buy AED 1,000 of gift cards (prompt injection).

    Identity layer
    Passes. The agent and its signature are genuine, so identity alone does not help.
    Mandate layer
    Stops it, because gift cards or that amount sit outside the customer's limits. Monitoring is the backstop.
    Replayed request
    The signature is valid because the content is identical. Only the nonce check catches it.
    Limits too tight or too loose
    Tight limits block good purchases and annoy the customer. Loose limits let a manipulated agent spend. This is the familiar fraud trade-off.

    Fraud and disputes

    • Agent account takeover: an attacker steals the customer's agent account, which already holds an approved token.
    • Rogue or front agents: a business registers an agent to launder stolen cards. KYB and passkey binding defend against this.
    • Old fraud signals vanish. A model trained on human behaviour scores good agent traffic as risky. Useful new features are the agent's track record, human present or not, and headroom under the mandate. Labels start scarce, so begin with rules and anomaly detection.
    • A new kind of dispute: "I authorised the agent, but not this purchase." As of October 2026 no network has published an agent-specific liability shift. Evidence decides, so a bank that stores the signed mandate wins disputes it would lose relying on the agent company's logs.

    Which layer does what

    Strong at: letting good agents through while keeping a cap on the damage. Weak at: standards that are still settling, and rules for who pays when things go wrong.

    In banking, the issuer is the last line: it approves the token, enforces the limits, and must keep signed evidence so that it can win disputes.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating a verified agent as an authorised purchase

      A prompt-injected agent passes identity checks. "It's verified, so we approve" is the warning sign.

    • Blocking all bot traffic as fraud

      Legacy rules decline good agent payments for looking automated. Compare agent and human decline rates.

    • Mandates so broad they mean nothing

      "Up to AED 50,000, any merchant, no expiry" still counts as a mandate. Check the limits customers actually set, and let them revoke an agent in two taps.

    • Stale or vendor-only claims

      Every claim about liability or standards needs a date and a primary source.

    1. What four questions does Know Your Agent answer?

    Who is the agent, who is it acting for, what may it do, and can we prove it. They are answered by signed requests, an agent token, mandates with limits, and signed evidence.

    2. A request has a valid signature from a registered agent, but its nonce was seen three minutes ago. What should the merchant do?

    Block it. It is a replay of a genuine request, so only the nonce check catches it.

    3. What is the difference between human-present and human-not-present purchases?

    Human present: the customer approves the exact basket. Human not present: the customer signs limits in advance and the agent buys later within them.

    4. Where do the spending limits live in Visa's design and in Mastercard's?

    Visa: in the agent's purchase instruction, which credentials are released against. Mastercard: on the token, and Mastercard declines violations before the issuer sees them.

    5. Your model declines 8% of agent payments against 1% of human ones, with similar confirmed fraud. What is likely happening?

    The model leans on behavioural features that agents lack. Segment agent traffic, add agent features and calibrate separately.
