    Know Your Agent: How AI Agents Get Permission to Pay

    What Visa and Mastercard have built so AI agents can buy things for your customers, how each layer of trust works, and what an issuing bank in the Gulf has to decide.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBefore an AI agent can spend a customer's money, every party needs to know who the agent is, who it's acting for, what it's allowed to do, and how to prove afterwards what happened. Know Your Agent is the name for answering those four questions.

    Status checked on 26 October 2026. Tags show how established each claim is: Live in real use, Pilot announced or testing, Proposed specification or draft, Inferred my reading where public detail is missing.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Card payments were designed around a person. When you pay online, the bank's fraud systems look for signs of a human: how you type, how the mouse moves, which device you're on. 3-D Secure pops up and asks you to confirm. If something goes wrong, the dispute rules ask one question: did the cardholder authorise this?

    Now a customer says to an AI assistant, “Book me a flight to Muscat for next Thursday, under AED 1,500.” The assistant searches, picks a flight and pays. No human typed the card number or saw the checkout page. To the merchant and the bank, that looks exactly like a bot, and bots have always been treated as fraud.

    Think of it like a valet key. You don't give the valet your house keys. You give them a key that starts the car, doesn't open the boot, and only goes so fast. Agentic payments need the same thing for money: a credential the agent can use, tied to a known agent and a known customer, with limits built in.

    Question 1Who is this agent?Which software, built and run by which accountable business?
    Question 2Who is it acting for?Is a real, verified customer behind it, and did they set it up?
    Question 3What may it do?Which amounts, merchants, categories and dates did the customer allow?
    Question 4Can we prove it?If there's a dispute, is there signed evidence of what was asked and what was done?

    Each question is answered by a different layer of technology. No single layer is enough, and seeing which attack each one stops is the quickest way to understand the whole design.

    Hold onto thisIdentity is not authority. Proving an agent is genuine tells you who is asking, not whether the customer wanted this purchase. That's why KYA is always identity, plus binding to the customer, plus limits, plus evidence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Agent identity: signed requests and directories

    The first layer answers who is this agent? The approach Visa and Mastercard settled on was developed with Cloudflare and is called Web Bot Auth. It's built on an existing internet standard, HTTP Message Signatures (RFC 9421).

    • The agent's operator creates a key pair. The private key stays with the agent. The public key is published in a directory run by the card network, but only after the network has vetted the business behind the agent. That vetting is the “know your business” step.
    • Every request the agent sends to a merchant carries a signature over key parts of the request: the website (@authority) and the page (@path), plus a creation time, an expiry time, a one-off random value called a nonce, and a tag saying whether the agent is browsing or paying.
    • The merchant, or its content delivery network such as Cloudflare, looks up the public key and checks the signature. If it checks out, the request came from that registered agent and hasn't been altered.

    Visa's version is called the Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP) Live. Its specification says the creation and expiry times should be no more than 8 minutes apart, and that merchants should block any request whose nonce they've already seen in that window. Together these stop replay attacks.

    Binding the agent to the customer and the card

    The second layer answers who is it acting for? Both networks build on network tokenisation, which banks already use for Apple Pay and saved cards. The real card number never leaves the network. The merchant gets a substitute token that only works in one context, plus a fresh one-time cryptogram for each payment, so a stolen token is close to useless anywhere else.

    For agents, the networks issue a token tied to that agent and that card. Mastercard calls these Agentic Tokens. Visa's agentic cards come through Visa Intelligent Commerce. When the customer sets the agent up, they confirm who they are with a passkey, the same fingerprint or face check their phone uses for sign-in. That passkey step is what stops someone enrolling a stolen card into an agent: they'd need the real cardholder's device.

    The issuing bank stays in the loop throughout. It approves or refuses each token request, just as it does when a customer adds a card to a phone wallet.

    Delegated authority: mandates and limits

    The third layer answers what may it do? This is where the protocols differ most, and where the vocabulary gets confusing. They all express the same idea: the customer's permission, written down in a form a machine can enforce and nobody can quietly change.

    • Visa Intelligent Commerce: the agent submits a purchase instruction to Visa, containing the amount limits and restrictions the customer agreed to. Visa calls these mandates. Payment credentials are released only against that instruction. Live
    • Mastercard: from August 2026, issuers can attach spend controls directly to a token. These include per-transaction, daily, monthly and lifetime limits, single use, and allow or block lists by merchant, merchant category and country. Mastercard declines anything that breaks a rule before the bank even sees it. Live
    • Mastercard Verifiable Intent, co-developed with Google: a chain of three signed layers. The bank vouches for the customer's identity, the customer signs their constraints, and the agent signs what it actually did. It's designed as dispute evidence. Pilot
    • Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), now with the FIDO Alliance: signed mandates. Version 0.1 named them the Intent, Cart and Payment mandates. The current specification uses Checkout and Payment mandates, each in an “open” form (the customer's constraints) and a “closed” form (one specific purchase). Proposed
    • Stripe shared payment tokens: a token scoped to one seller, with a maximum amount and an expiry, which can be revoked. Live, preview terms, not yet available in the Gulf

    One distinction matters everywhere: human present versus human not present. If the customer is there to approve the exact basket, the agent is just a fast shopping assistant. If the customer said “buy the tickets the moment they go on sale, up to AED 400” and walked away, the agent acts alone. Then the pre-signed limits are the only thing standing between the customer and a mistake.

    The flows, end to end

    Here are the three main designs, step by step. Setting up happens once. After that, every purchase follows the same short loop.

    What the issuing bank decides

    This is the part that lands on your desk. In every design, the issuer still approves the token, sets or honours the limits, and makes the final authorisation decision on each payment. Visa now passes new token data fields and a “token assurance” signal in the authorisation message, so the bank's systems can see that an agent is involved and how strongly it was verified.

    The bank's decisions boil down to a trade-off you'll recognise from fraud work. Tight limits stop the bad purchases and also the good ones. Loose limits let the customer's agent do its job and also let a manipulated agent spend.

    Liability and disputes

    Card disputes have always come in two main kinds: “I didn't do this” (fraud) and “I did this, but it wasn't what I paid for” (merchant disputes). Agents add a third, awkward kind: “I authorised the agent, but not this purchase.”

    • The first kind is what tokens and passkeys tackle. If the agent's token was properly provisioned and authenticated, fraud follows the existing rules, according to Worldpay's 2026 guidance.
    • The third kind is unsettled. As of October 2026 no card network has published an agent-specific liability shift that I could verify. Visa says agent disputes are handled under the same essential rules. Mastercard positions Verifiable Intent as the evidence that will decide these cases. American Express went furthest, launching purchase protection for card members against errors by registered agents in April 2026. Live

    Practically, that makes evidence the bank's best friend: the signed mandate, the agent's identity, the token assurance level and the transaction events. A bank that stores these properly will win disputes that a bank relying on the agent company's logs will lose.

    Fraud in agentic commerce

    Agents don't remove fraud. They move it. The new attack surfaces are worth knowing by name:

    • Prompt injection. Hidden text on a web page or in a product review instructs the agent to buy something else, or to send money elsewhere. The agent and its signature are genuine, so only limits and monitoring help.
    • Agent account takeover. Instead of stealing card details, the attacker steals access to the customer's agent account, which already holds an approved token.
    • Rogue or front agents. A business registers an agent specifically to launder stolen cards. KYB vetting and passkey binding are the defence.
    • Mandate gaming. Many small purchases that each sit just under the limits.
    • “The agent did it.” Customers claiming the agent made a purchase they actually wanted, to get a refund. This is the agentic version of friendly fraud.

    For your fraud models, the biggest change is that the old signals disappear. Typing rhythm, mouse movement and session behaviour are exactly what an agent lacks, so a model trained on human sessions will score good agent traffic as risky. Mastercard's merchant guidance explicitly says not to block consumer agents just because they're bots. The new features are the agent's identity and track record, whether a human was present, how much room is left under the mandate, how old the instruction is, and the token assurance signal. And because agent fraud labels are scarce at first, expect to start with rules and anomaly detection, then move to supervised models as labels arrive.

    Watch outEverything from the F7 lesson applies here with extra force. The fraud rate on agent transactions will differ from card-not-present traffic, so thresholds and calibration need to be set separately for the agent channel, not inherited from the main model.

    The Gulf picture

    The UAE is one of the most active markets outside the US. Both networks chose it as an early launch market.

    • Visa Agentic Ready, UAE Pilot: ADCB, ADIB, Al Ansari Exchange, Emirates Islamic, Emirates NBD, Mashreq, Tabby, Wio and Ziina. If you're joining one of these, agent transactions are likely already in your test environments.
    • Mastercard Agent Pay, UAE Pilot: launched with Majid Al Futtaim and the fintech Dataiera. VOX Cinemas ticket booking was announced as a use case for full rollout. No issuing bank was named.
    • Visa and Aldar Live: a voice agent paying property service charges, first done with an Emirates NBD Darna Visa card.
    • Saudi Arabia: as of this check I found no agentic payments announcement from a Saudi bank, and nothing from SAMA specific to AI agents. Watch this space. It will change.
    • Regulation: the CBUAE guidance note (February 2026) sets principles including governance, fairness, transparency, human oversight and data management, but has no agent-specific text. Law firms describe it as non-binding but a clear signal of what supervisors expect.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Treating a verified agent as an authorised purchase

      Identity checks pass on a prompt-injected agent. Verification says who, not whether the customer wanted it.

      Spot itAsk what happens when a genuine, verified agent tries something outside the customer's intent. If the answer is “it's verified, so we approve”, that's the gap.
    • Blocking all bot traffic as fraud

      Legacy fraud rules decline good agent transactions because they look automated, so customers abandon the bank's cards for agent use.

      Spot itCheck the decline rate on transactions flagged as agent-initiated against comparable human ones.
    • Reusing the human fraud model unchanged

      Behavioural features are missing or misleading for agents, so scores are miscalibrated in a channel with a different fraud rate.

      Spot itAsk whether the model has agent-specific features, and whether calibration has been checked separately on agent traffic.
    • Mandates so broad they mean nothing

      “Up to AED 50,000, any merchant, no expiry” technically counts as a mandate.

      Spot itLook at the distribution of limits customers actually set, and whether defaults are sensible.
    • Relying on the agent company's logs for disputes

      Unsigned logs held by a third party are weak evidence, and may not be available when you need them.

      Spot itAsk what signed evidence the bank itself stores for each agent transaction.
    • No way for customers to see and revoke agents

      Customers forget which agents hold their cards, so a compromised agent keeps spending.

      Spot itOpen the app. Can a customer list their agents, see the limits, and cut one off in two taps?
    • Confident claims built on stale or vendor sources

      Typical errors: describing AP2 only with its 2025 names, claiming a liability shift that hasn't been published, saying ChatGPT checkout is fully live, or calling vendor frameworks “the standard”.

      Spot itEvery claim about this area needs a date and a primary source. Check press releases and specifications, not blogs.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Visa and Mastercard are building the rails for AI assistants to pay on our customers' behalf, and the UAE is one of their first markets. The core idea is Know Your Agent: the network vets the company behind each agent, the customer approves it with a passkey, and the card it uses is a special token with limits that we and the customer set. For us that means three decisions: when we approve agent tokens, what controls we give customers, and how our fraud models treat agent payments. Liability rules aren't settled yet, so we should store strong evidence and start with sensible default limits.”
    To a technical or risk audience
    “KYA is four layers. Agent identity uses RFC 9421 message signatures verified against network key directories, with nonce and expiry to stop replays. Binding uses agent-scoped network tokens provisioned through passkey authentication, with the issuer approving provisioning. Scope comes from mandates: Visa purchase instructions, Mastercard token spend controls, or AP2-style signed mandates. Evidence means signed intent and transaction events for disputes. As an issuer, I'd tag agent transactions now, build channel-specific features and calibration, offer a consent and revoke screen, and treat prompt injection as the main residual risk, because it defeats identity but not limits.”
    “Isn't KYA just KYC for bots?”

    Partly. It includes know-your-business checks on whoever builds and runs the agent. But it also binds the agent to a verified customer, scopes what it may do, keeps signed evidence, and allows revocation. KYC asks who a person is. KYA asks who the software is, who's accountable for it, who it acts for, and what it's allowed to do.

    “If the agent is verified by Visa, why would we ever decline it?”

    Because verification proves identity, not intent. A verified agent can be manipulated by prompt injection, its owner's account can be taken over, or it can misread an instruction. The mandate limits and our own risk scoring are what catch those cases.

    “Who pays if the agent buys the wrong thing?”

    It's not settled. As of October 2026, no network has published an agent-specific liability shift that I could verify. Visa says existing dispute rules apply. Mastercard is positioning Verifiable Intent as the evidence for these cases. Amex covers its card members against registered agents' errors. Until rules settle, the bank's position depends on the evidence it holds, so we should store the signed mandate and transaction events ourselves.

    “What should we do in the next six months?”

    Five things. First, flag agent-initiated transactions in our data now, so we have labels later. Second, set a token provisioning policy for agent requests. Third, design customer controls: a list of agents, limits and a revoke button. Fourth, build agent-specific features and monitoring into the fraud stack. Fifth, write a dispute playbook for “I didn't authorise this purchase” cases. If we're in Visa's Agentic Ready programme, use the test phase to measure all of this before volumes grow.

    “Will this break our fraud models?”

    It can. The behavioural signals that separate humans from bots disappear, so good agent transactions can score as risky. The fraud rate in the agent channel will also differ. I'd segment agent traffic, add features like agent identity, human presence and mandate headroom, and set thresholds and calibration separately for that channel.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. What four questions does Know Your Agent answer, and which technology answers each?

    Who is the agent? Vetting the operator, plus signed requests checked against a network directory. Who does it act for? An agent-scoped token, provisioned after passkey verification. What may it do? Mandates and token spend controls. Can we prove it? Signed intent and transaction events. Revocation and monitoring run across all four.

    2. A merchant receives a request with a valid signature from a registered agent, but the nonce was seen three minutes ago. What should it do?

    Block it. It's a replay: someone resent a captured genuine request. The signature is valid because the content is identical, so only the nonce check catches it.

    3. A genuine, verified agent reads a web page with hidden instructions and tries to buy AED 1,000 of gift cards. Which layer stops it?

    Not identity, which passes. The mandate stops it: either the gift-card category is blocked or the amount is over the limit. Monitoring is the backstop. Signed intent evidence helps afterwards, in the dispute.

    4. In AP2, what's the difference between human-present and human-not-present purchases?

    Human present: the customer approves the exact basket and price, by signing the closed mandate. Human not present: the customer signs their constraints in advance, in the open mandate, and the agent completes the purchase later within those constraints, signing with its own key.

    5. Where do the spending limits live in Visa's design compared with Mastercard's?

    In Visa Intelligent Commerce, the agent submits a purchase instruction carrying the customer's limits, and credentials are released against it. In Mastercard's design, issuers can attach spend controls directly to the agentic token, and Mastercard declines any transaction that breaks them before it reaches the issuer.

    6. Your bank's fraud model declines 8% of agent transactions against 1% of human ones, but confirmed fraud is similar. What's likely happening?

    The model is relying on behavioural features that agents lack, so they look like bots. It's miscalibrated for this channel. Segment agent traffic, add agent-specific features, and set thresholds and calibration separately, using the methods from F7.
