    Deepfakes Against eKYC and Call Centres

    Part 1

    What a deepfake attack is

    In one sentenceA deepfake attack uses AI-generated faces or voices to pass as a real customer, either by fooling the camera check or by skipping the camera altogether.

    eKYC ("electronic know your customer") is how a bank opens an account remotely. The customer shows a national ID, takes a selfie, and a liveness check (turn your head, blink) decides whether a live person is there.

    • Presentation attack: a printed photo, a screen replay or a mask shown to a real camera.
    • Injection attack: software on the phone pretends to be the camera and feeds in generated video. A stolen photo of the victim is animated to nod and blink on command.

    Liveness models learned to spot paper edges, screen glare and flat skin. Generated video has none of those flaws, so the model says "live". It judged how the picture looked. Nobody checked where it came from.

    Voice works the same way. A few seconds of a customer's voice is enough to clone it and speak live. In early 2024 a finance employee at Arup in Hong Kong paid out roughly HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call in which several "colleagues" were deepfakes (widely reported).

    Part 2

    How it differs from older fraud

    • Stolen documents: the old attack needed a physical fake. A deepfake needs only a few photos from social media or a leaked data breach.
    • Synthetic identity fraud: a made-up person built from real and fake details. Deepfakes give that invented person a convincing face. The two are often combined.
    • Voice passwords: "my voice is my password" asks whether a voice is similar to the enrolled one. A clone is built to be similar.

    The key change is cost. Every part of the attack is cheap and repeatable, so one attacker can try thousands of accounts.

    Part 3

    How the attack and the defence work

    The attack, step by step

    1. Collect a few photos or a short voice clip of the victim.
    2. Use a face-swap or image-to-video model to make a moving, talking copy.
    3. Run a virtual camera, an emulator (a pretend phone) or a hooked app to replace the real camera feed.
    4. Pass the liveness check and open the account, or phone the call centre and ask for a payment.

    The defence: three ideas

    • Judge the content. Train a model to spot generated faces or voices. Useful, but it chases the generators and can lose most of its power on a new one it has not seen.
    • Verify the channel. Like checking a letter's postmark rather than its handwriting: confirm the frames came from a genuine camera on an unmodified phone, using hardware-backed attestation (the phone's chip vouches for itself). This ignores how good the fake looks, and it removes a whole class of attacks.
    • Ask for what a photo cannot give. Read the chip in the ID card with NFC, set an unpredictable challenge, link devices to accounts (one phone opening thirty accounts is a red flag), and send high-risk cases to a person or a branch.

    For phone calls, treat the voice as weak evidence and lean on the surroundings: is the number linked to the customer, is the request urgent or secret, is it a large payment to a new payee? Then add a step the caller cannot control, such as a callback on the registered number or an in-app approval.

    Part 4

    How to read detector results

    A detector gives each attempt a score, and you pick a threshold. Think of a smoke alarm: sensitive enough to catch every fire and it also goes off when you make toast.

    • Catch rate: the share of attacks stopped. A rate of 90% means 1 attack in 10 gets through.
    • False rejection rate: the share of genuine customers wrongly stopped. Moving the threshold trades one against the other.
    • The base rate matters most. If 1 attempt in 500 is an attack, even a 2% false rejection rate means about ten genuine customers rejected for every attack caught. "99% accurate" says nothing about this.
    • A new generator breaks the lab number. The same threshold catches far less on a deepfake tool the detector never saw, while genuine rejections stay the same.
    • A voice match is weak evidence. A clone matches about as often as a genuine caller does, so a match barely changes the odds. A failed callback changes them a lot.
    Part 5

    Which defence to use

    DefenceStopsWeak against
    Passive liveness (PAD)Printed photos, screen replayInjected video
    Injection detectionVirtual cameras, emulators, hooked appsSkilled attackers on a clean device
    Deepfake detectorKnown generatorsNew, unseen generators
    NFC chip read, device linkageFake or tampered ID, mass account openingA real customer lending their own face
    Callback, limits, second approvalFake calls, whatever the voice sounds likeAdds friction for customers
    • Strong at: layers. Cheap attacks fail cheap checks, and channel checks remove whole attack types.
    • Weak at: any single layer. A skilled attacker can beat several at once, so also cap what one approved request can do.

    In banking, assume some fakes will succeed, and set limits (cooling-off periods on new payees, second approval for large amounts) so one success cannot cause a large loss.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • A certificate for the wrong attack

      Liveness certified against presentation attacks gets called "deepfake-proof". Ask whether testing covered virtual cameras, emulators and app hooking.

    • A great benchmark, weak in production

      99% on a public dataset can collapse on a new generator. Ask for results on generators the detector has never seen.

    • Vendor-default threshold

      Either staff drown in rejections and quietly override them, or attacks slip in. Check the override rate and set the threshold from costs.

    • Voice treated as proof

      A call is approved because the voice matched. Ask what happens when the match is high but the request is unusual.

    1. A liveness model beats every printed photo and screen replay. Why can a deepfake still pass?

    It judges the image, and an injected stream skips the camera. Generated video has no paper edges or glare to trip it. The fix is to verify where the frames came from.

    2. Attacks are 1 in 500 attempts and 2% of genuine customers are flagged. What does the queue look like?

    Mostly genuine customers, about ten for each attack caught. Attacks are so rare that even a small false rejection rate swamps them.

    3. The attacker moves to an unseen generator. What happens at the same threshold?

    The catch rate drops sharply and genuine rejections stay the same. You annoy the same customers and miss most attacks.

    4. Why does "voice matches the voiceprint" carry so little weight?

    Clones are built to match, so a match is about as likely from a fraudster as from a genuine caller. Signals like a failed callback or an unknown device separate the two.

    5. Name two controls that limit the damage even when a deepfake succeeds.

    Cooling-off periods and limits on first payments to a new payee, and second approval or out-of-band confirmation (a check on a separate channel) for large or unusual requests.
