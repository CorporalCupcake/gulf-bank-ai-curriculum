    Deepfakes at the Door: eKYC, Call Centres and the AI Supply Chain

    Why a perfect liveness score no longer proves a person was there, how injected video and cloned voices get past onboarding and phone checks, what layered defence really buys you, and how the models you download can be the attack.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA face or voice that looks right no longer proves a person was there, so defences must verify the capture channel and add signals an attacker cannot synthesise; the same "trust the artefact" mistake applies to model weights you download.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a bouncer who checks your ID photo against your face, but never checks whether you walked in through the door or through a hole in the wall. That is eKYC. A new customer shows an Emirates ID or Saudi national ID, then takes a selfie with a "turn your head" check, and a model decides whether a live human is there.

    An attacker never points a camera at anything. Software on their device pretends to be the camera and feeds in video from a generative model: a stolen photo of the victim, animated to nod and blink on command. The liveness model sees a face that nods, blinks and matches the ID, and says yes. It judged the picture. Nobody checked where the picture came from.

    Phones have the same flaw. A few seconds of a customer's voice, from a video or voicemail, is enough to clone it and speak live. If the check is "does this sound like the enrolled voice?", the clone passes. In early 2024 a finance employee at Arup in Hong Kong paid out roughly HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call where several "colleagues" were deepfakes (widely reported; the firm confirmed it was a victim).

    Hold onto thisTwo attacks, two defences. A presentation attack shows a fake to the camera (printed photo, screen, mask). An injection attack bypasses the camera and feeds fake data into the software. Classic liveness was built for the first; the second is growing.
    1 LensReal camera sees the world. Presentation attacks happen here.
    2 DeviceOperating system, drivers. A virtual camera replaces the real one here.
    3 AppThe bank's SDK. Hooking tools can swap frames inside it.
    4 NetworkUpload to the server, which can only trust what it can verify.
    5 ServerFace match, liveness model, device and risk checks.

    A3.6 is the same idea one step back. Before a model sees a customer, your team downloaded it, and a model file is code and data from someone you probably cannot interview.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    Injection attacks against eKYC (A3.5)

    Every part of the attack is ordinary and cheap:

    • Source material. A few photos of the target from social media or a leaked KYC breach. Face-swap and image-to-video models turn them into a talking, blinking head.
    • The generator. A real-time face-swap tool driven by the attacker's own face, or a pre-rendered clip following challenges ("smile", "look left").
    • The injection. A virtual camera driver on a PC, an emulator or rooted phone substituting the stream, or a hooked SDK replacing frames after capture. The app sees a normal camera and frame size.
    • The document. Often a synthetic or tampered ID, linking to synthetic identity fraud (A9.2).

    Liveness models are fooled because they learned "real skin, depth cues, screen moiré, paper edges" from attacks that existed at training time. Modern generated video has none of those artefacts. Being classifiers, they do well on generators they saw and badly on new ones.

    PAD (presentation attack detection, ISO/IEC 30107) assumes the attacker holds something up to a real sensor. IAD (injection attack detection) is the newer layer; CEN/TS 18099 aims to standardise its evaluation (cited from memory; check its scope before quoting).

    Detection is an arms race, so verify the channel

    Three families of defence fail differently:

    1. Judge the content. Train a model to spot generated faces or voices (texture, frequency artefacts, lip-audio mismatch). Useful, but it chases the generators; a detector strong on benchmarks can lose most of its power on an unseen one.
    2. Verify the channel. Like checking a letter's postmark rather than its handwriting: confirm frames came from a genuine camera on an unmodified device running genuine app code. No virtual camera, emulator or root; hardware-backed attestation (Play Integrity, App Attest); signed or encrypted capture the server can verify. It does not care how good the fake looks.
    3. Add what a photo cannot supply. NFC reading of the ID chip (the state signs the face image, so a forged card image fails), an active challenge the attacker cannot predict, device and behaviour linkage (one device opening thirty accounts), and for high risk a human or branch visit.

    Layers help because attacks differ: cheap attacks fail cheap checks. But they do not multiply as neatly as Simulation 1 shows, since a skilled attacker beats several at once. The channel check matters most because it removes a whole class of attacks.

    The detector trade-off and the base rate

    A detector gives each attempt a score: genuine customers score low, attacks higher. Pick a threshold and you get a catch rate (TPR, share of attacks flagged) and a false rejection rate (FPR, share of genuine customers flagged). Think of a smoke alarm: set it sensitive enough to catch every fire and it goes off every time you make toast.

    • d' is the gap between the two score distributions in standard deviations. Around 3 is a good lab detector; 1 is nearly useless. Going from 3 to 1.35 means catching far fewer attacks at the same false rejections.
    • Base rate. If 1 attempt in 500 is an attack, a 2% false rejection rate gives about ten genuine rejections per attack caught, as in imbalanced data (F3.5). Misreading to avoid: "99% accurate" says nothing about how many rejections are real attacks.
    share of rejections that are attacks=π·TPRπ·TPR+(1−π)·FPR

    π is the share of attempts that are attacks. Same Bayes arithmetic as any rare-event problem. The new part: d' is not fixed, and falls when the attacker uses a generator the detector has not seen.

    Voice cloning against the call centre (A3.5)

    "My voice is my password" has the same flaw as the selfie. Speaker verification asks whether a voice is similar to the enrolled voiceprint, and a clone is built to be similar. A few seconds of audio is enough, and a voice-conversion tool can drive the clone live, so there is no recorded clip to detect. Phone audio is narrowband and compressed, which hides detector artefacts. The sound of a voice is weak evidence, but surrounding evidence still works:

    • whether the number or device is linked to the customer, and whether caller-ID is spoofed;
    • a synthetic-speech detector score (noisy, not a verdict);
    • the shape of the request: urgency, secrecy, a new payee, a large amount, contact details changed just before;
    • an out-of-band step the caller cannot control: callback on the registered number, in-app approval, a delay on first payments to a new payee.

    Weak signals combine as odds. Like a detective stacking clues, start from the prior odds that such a call is an impersonation and multiply by a likelihood ratio per signal. A ratio of 1 means the signal tells you nothing; 23 means it is 23 times likelier from a fraud than from a genuine caller; 0.3 means a clean result lowers suspicion a little. This is naive Bayes (F1.4). Signals from one fraudster are correlated, so the posterior is a little optimistic.

    AI supply chain risk (A3.6)

    Think of a model like a package from a stranger: you would check the seal before opening it. It reaches the bank through a chain (author, hub such as Hugging Face, data scientist's download, registry, build, deployment), and any link can be compromised. Three attacks to tell apart:

    1. Code in the file. Many formats are Python pickles, which run arbitrary code when loaded. Malicious models on public hubs have opened a reverse shell on whoever loaded them. trust_remote_code options do the same by running the author's Python. This is ordinary malware, landing on a laptop or training cluster with data access.
    2. Weights that behave. A backdoored model works normally until a trigger appears (a phrase, a pattern in an invoice image, an account prefix), like a lock that opens for any key plus one secret key. BadNets showed it for image classifiers; "sleeper agents" showed backdoors in language models can survive safety training. Reading the file will not reveal it. Only behavioural tests and provenance help.
    3. Poisoned data. Fine-tuning or retrieval data someone else influences: public datasets, scraped text, vendor labels, a shared wiki. A 2025 study found a few hundred poisoned documents (roughly a fixed number) could backdoor models of very different sizes, so "we have so much data a few bad rows won't matter" is false. See data poisoning (G1.2).

    Controls, borrowed from software engineering:

    • Safe formats. safetensors holds tensors only and cannot run code on load.
    • Provenance. Pin the exact revision by hash, prefer publisher-signed artefacts, record sources in an AI bill of materials.
    • Internal registry. Builds pull from it, not from live hubs.
    • Scanning plus sandboxed loading. Scanners are rule-based and have been evaded, so never rely on one.
    • Behavioural evaluation before promotion, and review of any code a model wants to run.
    • Data lineage (P1.3) and an untrusted-source tier for fine-tuning data.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A certificate for the wrong attack

      Liveness certified against presentation attacks gets called "deepfake-proof". Injection was never in scope.

      Spot itAsk whether independent testing covered virtual cameras, emulators and SDK hooking, on which generators.
    • Great benchmark, weak in production

      99% accuracy on a public dataset collapses on a new generator.

      Spot itAsk for results on held-out generators; track the live score distribution for drift.
    • Threshold left at the vendor default

      Either a flood of genuine rejections that staff quietly override, or a loose setting that lets attacks in.

      Spot itCheck the manual override rate and the share of rejections that are fraud. Set the threshold from costs.
    • Voiceprint treated as proof

      The call is approved because the voice matched.

      Spot itAsk what happens when the match is high and the request is unusual. There should be an out-of-band step.
    • Detection one application at a time

      Each application looks fine; thirty share a device, payee or address.

      Spot itCheck that device and network linkage runs across accounts, not per session.
    • Downloading models in production

      A job pulls a model from a public hub at start-up, with no pinned revision.

      Spot itSearch code and pipelines for hub downloads, trust_remote_code=True, and torch.load of unknown files.
    • Trusting a scanner

      "It passed the hub's malware scan."

      Spot itScanners are denylists and have been bypassed. Load unknown artefacts in a sandbox with no network or data access; prefer formats that cannot run code.
    • Fine-tuning on data nobody owns

      Public or vendor datasets and shared wikis go straight into training or retrieval.

      Spot itCan you name the source and owner of every training set, and who can edit it? If not, treat it as untrusted.
    • Believing detection is the control

      The programme spots fakes but puts no limit on what one approved call can do.

      Spot itAsk for the worst loss from one successful fake. Cap it with limits, new-payee delays and second approval.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a liveness model with a perfect record against printed photos and screen replays still be beaten by a deepfake?

    It judges the image, and an injected stream bypasses the sensor. A generated video has no paper edges or moiré, so it never trips what the model learned. Fix: verify where the frames came from, not just how they look.

    2. A detector flags 84% of attacks and 2.3% of genuine customers. Attacks are 0.2% of 10,000 attempts. How many genuine customers are rejected per attack caught?

    Attacks: 20, caught 16.8. Genuine rejected: 9,980 × 0.023 ≈ 230. So 230 ÷ 16.8 ≈ 14 genuine rejections per attack caught; only about 7% of rejections are attacks.

    3. The attacker moves to a generator the detector has not seen and d' falls from 3 to 1.35. At the same threshold of 2, what happens to the catch rate?

    Catch rate = P(Z > 2 − d'). At d' = 3: P(Z > −1) = 84%. At d' = 1.35: P(Z > 0.65) = 26%. Genuine rejections stay at 2.3%, so you reject the same genuine customers while missing about three attacks in four.

    4. Why does "voice matches the voiceprint" carry almost no weight in the odds calculation?

    Likelihood ratio = P(match | clone) ÷ P(match | genuine). Clones are built to match, so the ratio is near 1 and the odds barely move. Signals that separate the two groups carry the weight: callback, detector flag, unknown number or device.

    5. Prior odds of impersonation are 1 in 2,000. A detector flags the call (likelihood ratio 23) and the number is unregistered (ratio 8). What is the probability the call is a fraud?

    Prior odds ≈ 0.0005. Times 23 × 8 = 184 gives 0.092 odds, and 0.092 ÷ 1.092 ≈ 8.4%. Not certain, but enough to demand a callback before moving money.

    6. Why is a safetensors file safer than a PyTorch .bin file, and what does it not protect against?

    A pickle-based .bin can run arbitrary Python on load; safetensors holds only tensors and metadata. It does not protect against backdoored weights, a malicious trust_remote_code class, or malicious code elsewhere in the repository.

    7. Can we find a backdoor in a downloaded model by scanning the file?

    No. Scanners look for code-execution payloads; a trained-in backdoor is just numbers in the weights. Reduce the risk with provenance (trusted source, signature, pinned hash), behavioural tests for triggers, and limits on what the model can do.

    8. Name two controls that limit the damage even when a deepfake succeeds.

    Limits and cooling-off periods on first payments to a new payee, and second approval or out-of-band confirmation for large or unusual requests. Also device and identity linkage to spot clusters, and fast recall on instant payment rails.
