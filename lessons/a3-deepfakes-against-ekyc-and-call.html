    Deepfakes at the Door: eKYC, Call Centres and the AI Supply Chain

    Why a perfect liveness score no longer proves a person was there, how injected video and cloned voices get past onboarding and phone checks, what layered defence really buys you, and how the models you download can be the attack.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA face or voice that looks right no longer proves a person was there, so defences must verify the capture channel and add signals an attacker cannot synthesise; the same "trust the artefact" mistake applies to model weights you download.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a bouncer who checks your ID photo against your face, but never checks whether you came through the door or a hole in the wall. That is eKYC: a customer shows an Emirates ID or Saudi national ID, takes a selfie with a "turn your head" check, and a model decides whether a live human is there.

    An attacker never points a camera at anything. Software on their device pretends to be the camera and feeds in generated video: a stolen photo of the victim, animated to nod and blink on command. The liveness model says yes. It judged the picture. Nobody checked where the picture came from.

    Phones have the same flaw: a few seconds of a customer's voice is enough to clone it and speak live. In early 2024 a finance employee at Arup in Hong Kong paid out roughly HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call where several "colleagues" were deepfakes (widely reported).

    Hold onto thisTwo attacks, two defences. A presentation attack shows a fake to the camera (printed photo, screen, mask). An injection attack bypasses the camera and feeds fake data into the software. Classic liveness was built for the first; the second is growing.
    1 LensReal camera sees the world. Presentation attacks happen here.
    2 DeviceOperating system, drivers. A virtual camera replaces the real one here.
    3 AppThe bank's SDK. Hooking tools can swap frames inside it.
    4 NetworkUpload to the server, which can only trust what it can verify.
    5 ServerFace match, liveness model, device and risk checks.

    A3.6 is the same idea one step back. Before a model sees a customer, your team downloaded it, and a model file is code and data from someone you probably cannot interview.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    Injection attacks against eKYC (A3.5)

    Every part of the attack is cheap. A few photos of the target from social media or a leaked KYC breach; a face-swap or image-to-video model; then a virtual camera driver, emulator or hooked SDK that substitutes the stream. The app sees a normal camera. The ID is often synthetic or tampered, linking to synthetic identity fraud (A9.2).

    Liveness models are fooled because they learned "real skin, depth cues, screen moiré, paper edges" from attacks that existed at training time. Modern generated video has none of those artefacts. PAD (presentation attack detection) covers fakes held up to a real sensor; IAD (injection attack detection) is the newer layer.

    Detection is an arms race, so verify the channel

    Three families of defence fail differently:

    1. Judge the content. Train a model to spot generated faces or voices. Useful, but it chases the generators and can lose most of its power on an unseen one.
    2. Verify the channel. Like checking a letter's postmark rather than its handwriting: confirm frames came from a genuine camera on an unmodified device (no virtual camera, emulator or root; hardware-backed attestation). It does not care how good the fake looks.
    3. Add what a photo cannot supply. NFC reading of the ID chip, an unpredictable active challenge, device linkage (one device opening thirty accounts), and for high risk a human or branch visit.

    Layers help because cheap attacks fail cheap checks, but a skilled attacker beats several at once. The channel check matters most because it removes a whole class of attacks.

    The detector trade-off and the base rate

    A detector scores each attempt; pick a threshold and you get a catch rate and a false rejection rate. Think of a smoke alarm: sensitive enough to catch every fire and it goes off every time you make toast.

    • Separation. How far apart genuine and attack scores sit. A new generator pushes attack scores towards the genuine ones, so the same threshold catches less.
    • Base rate. If 1 attempt in 500 is an attack, even a 2% false rejection rate gives about ten genuine rejections per attack caught (see imbalanced data, F3.5).

    Voice cloning against the call centre (A3.5)

    "My voice is my password" has the same flaw as the selfie. Speaker verification asks whether a voice is similar to the enrolled voiceprint, and a clone is built to be similar. A few seconds of audio is enough, and live voice conversion means there is no recorded clip to detect. So treat the voice as weak evidence and lean on the surroundings:

    • whether the number or device is linked to the customer, and whether caller-ID is spoofed;
    • the shape of the request: urgency, secrecy, a new payee, a large amount;
    • an out-of-band step the caller cannot control: callback on the registered number, in-app approval, a delay on first payments to a new payee.

    Weak signals add up like a detective stacking clues. Each nudges the odds by how much likelier it is from a fraudster than from a genuine caller. A signal that appears equally in both groups, like a voice match, moves nothing.

    AI supply chain risk (A3.6)

    Think of a model like a package from a stranger: check the seal before opening it. It reaches the bank through a chain (author, hub, download, registry, deployment), and any link can be compromised. Three attacks:

    1. Code in the file. Many formats are Python pickles, which run arbitrary code when loaded, and trust_remote_code runs the author's Python. Malicious models on public hubs have opened a reverse shell on whoever loaded them.
    2. Weights that behave. A backdoored model works normally until a trigger appears (a phrase, a pattern in an invoice image), like a lock that also opens for one secret key. Reading the file will not reveal it; only behavioural tests and provenance help.
    3. Poisoned data. Fine-tuning or retrieval data someone else influences. A few hundred poisoned documents can backdoor models of very different sizes, so "we have so much data a few bad rows won't matter" is false. See data poisoning (G1.2).

    Controls: use safe formats (safetensors holds tensors only and cannot run code on load); pin exact revisions by hash and build from an internal registry, not live hubs; load unknown artefacts in a sandbox and run behavioural evaluation before promotion. Scanners are rule-based and have been evaded, so never rely on one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A certificate for the wrong attack

      Liveness certified against presentation attacks gets called "deepfake-proof". Injection was never in scope.

      Spot itAsk whether testing covered virtual cameras, emulators and SDK hooking.
    • Great benchmark, weak in production

      99% accuracy on a public dataset collapses on a new generator.

      Spot itAsk for results on held-out generators; watch the live score distribution.
    • Threshold left at the vendor default

      Either a flood of rejections that staff quietly override, or a loose setting that lets attacks in.

      Spot itCheck the override rate; set the threshold from costs.
    • Voiceprint treated as proof

      The call is approved because the voice matched.

      Spot itAsk what happens when the match is high and the request is unusual.
    • Downloading models in production

      A job pulls a model from a public hub at start-up, with no pinned revision.

      Spot itSearch pipelines for hub downloads, trust_remote_code=True, and torch.load of unknown files.
    • Believing detection is the control

      The programme spots fakes but puts no limit on what one approved call can do.

      Spot itAsk for the worst loss from one successful fake. Cap it with limits and second approval.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a liveness model with a perfect record against printed photos and screen replays still be beaten by a deepfake?

    It judges the image, and an injected stream bypasses the sensor. Generated video has no paper edges or moiré to trip it. Fix: verify where the frames came from, not just how they look.

    2. Attacks are 1 attempt in 500 and 2% of genuine customers get flagged. What does the rejection queue look like, and why?

    Mostly genuine customers, about ten for each attack caught. Attacks are so rare that even a small false rejection rate swamps them. "99% accurate" says nothing about this.

    3. The attacker moves to a generator the detector has not seen. At the same threshold, what happens to the catch rate and to genuine rejections?

    The catch rate drops sharply; genuine rejections stay the same. You annoy the same customers and miss most attacks. Test on held-out generators and watch the live score distribution for drift.

    4. Why does "voice matches the voiceprint" carry almost no weight when you combine signals?

    Clones are built to match, so a match is about as likely from a fraudster as from a genuine caller. The weight sits with signals that separate the two: callback, detector flag, unknown number or device.

    5. A detector flags a call and the number is unregistered, but impersonation is very rare. Is it a fraud? What do you do?

    Not certain: two signals lift a tiny starting chance to single-digit percent. That is far above normal, so you do not approve, and you do not accuse. You demand an out-of-band callback before moving money.

    6. Why is a safetensors file safer than a PyTorch .bin file, and what does it not protect against?

    A pickle-based .bin can run arbitrary Python on load; safetensors holds only tensors. It does not protect against backdoored weights, a malicious trust_remote_code class, or malicious code elsewhere in the repository.

    7. Can we find a backdoor in a downloaded model by scanning the file?

    No. A trained-in backdoor is just numbers in the weights. Use provenance (trusted source, signature, pinned hash), behavioural tests for triggers, and limits on what the model can do.

    8. Name two controls that limit the damage even when a deepfake succeeds.

    Cooling-off periods and limits on first payments to a new payee, and second approval or out-of-band confirmation for large or unusual requests.
