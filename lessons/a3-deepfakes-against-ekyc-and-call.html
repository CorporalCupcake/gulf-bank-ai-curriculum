    Deepfakes at the Door: eKYC, Call Centres and the AI Supply Chain

    Why a perfect liveness score no longer proves a person was there, how injected video and cloned voices get past onboarding and phone checks, what layered defence really buys you, and how the models you download can be the attack.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBiometrics used to prove that a person was present; generative AI means a face or voice that looks right proves nothing about where it came from, so defences must verify the capture channel and add signals the attacker cannot synthesise, and the same "trust the artefact" mistake applies to model weights you download.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new customer opens an account in a banking app. The app asks for an Emirates ID or Saudi national ID, then a selfie with a short "turn your head" check. A model compares the selfie with the ID photo and decides whether a live human is looking at the camera. It is fast, cheap and, for years, good.

    Now imagine the attacker never points a camera at anything. Software on their phone or laptop pretends to be the camera and feeds in a video that a generative model is producing frame by frame: a stolen photo of the victim, animated to nod and blink on command. The liveness model sees a face that nods, blinks and matches the ID. It says yes. It judged the picture. Nobody checked where the picture came from.

    The phone line has the same flaw. A fraudster needs a few seconds of a customer's voice, taken from a video or a voicemail, to produce a clone that can say anything in real time. If the bank's phone check is "does this voice sound like the enrolled voice?", the clone can pass. In early 2024 a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong paid out roughly HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call in which several "colleagues" were deepfakes (widely reported; the firm confirmed it was a victim). A bank is not exempt.

    Hold onto thisThere are two different attacks and two different defences. A presentation attack shows a fake to the camera (a printed photo, a screen, a mask). An injection attack bypasses the camera and feeds fake data into the software. Classic liveness was built for the first. The second is the one that is growing.
    1 LensReal camera sees the world. Presentation attacks happen here.
    2 DeviceOperating system, drivers. A virtual camera replaces the real one here.
    3 AppThe bank's SDK. Hooking tools can swap frames inside it.
    4 NetworkUpload to the server, which can only trust what it can verify.
    5 ServerFace match, liveness model, device and risk checks.

    The second half of this lesson (A3.6) is the same idea one step back. Before a model ever sees a customer, your team downloaded it. A model file is code and data from someone you probably cannot interview, and the bank's controls tend to assume it is just a file.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    Injection attacks against eKYC (A3.5)

    The pieces of an attack are all ordinary and cheap.

    • Source material. One or a few photos of the target, from social media, a leaked ID image, or a previous breach of KYC documents. Face-swap and image-to-video models turn them into a talking, blinking head.
    • The generator. A real-time face-swap or "reenactment" tool, driven by the attacker's own face and voice, or a pre-rendered clip prompted to follow challenges ("smile", "look left").
    • The injection. A virtual camera driver on a PC, an emulator or rooted phone that substitutes the camera stream, or a hooked app SDK that replaces frames after capture. The app sees a camera device and a normal frame size.
    • The document. Often a synthetic or tampered ID image, also generated, which is why this connects to synthetic identity fraud (A9.2).

    Why liveness models are fooled: they learned "real skin, depth cues, moiré from screens, paper edges", and they were trained on attacks that existed when the data was collected. Modern generated video has none of those artefacts. A learned detector is also a classifier, so it inherits the generalisation problem: it does well on generators it saw in training and badly on a new one.

    Two vocabulary points for the room. PAD (presentation attack detection) is standardised in ISO/IEC 30107; its testing assumes the attacker holds something up to a real sensor. IAD (injection attack detection) is the newer layer, and European work (CEN/TS 18099, a technical specification on biometric data injection attack detection) is trying to standardise how it is evaluated. Check the exact scope of that document before quoting it; I have cited it from memory.

    Detection is an arms race, so verify the channel

    There are three families of defence, and they fail differently.

    1. Judge the content. Train a model to spot generated faces or voices: texture, frequency artefacts, temporal inconsistencies, lip-audio mismatch. Useful, but it chases the generators. A detector with strong benchmark scores can lose most of its power on an unseen generator.
    2. Verify the channel. Check that the frames came from a genuine camera on a genuine, unmodified device running genuine app code: no virtual camera, no emulator or root, hardware-backed app attestation (Play Integrity, App Attest), and signed or encrypted capture that the server can verify. This does not care how good the fake looks.
    3. Add what cannot be synthesised from a photo. NFC reading of the ID chip (the face image is signed by the issuing state, so a forged card image fails), an active challenge unknown to the attacker in advance, device and behaviour linkage (the same device opening thirty accounts), and, for high risk, a human or a branch visit.

    Defence in depth works because attacks are not identical, but it does not multiply as neatly as Simulation 1 suggests. Cheap attacks fail on cheap checks. The skilled attacker engineers around all of them together, which is why the channel check matters most: it removes a whole class of attacks rather than chasing each fake.

    The detector trade-off and the base rate

    Treat a deepfake detector as a score. Genuine customers score low, attacks score higher. Pick a threshold, and you get a catch rate (TPR) and a false rejection rate (FPR). How well the two groups separate is summarised by d', the distance between the two score distributions in standard deviations. The base rate then does the rest, exactly as in imbalanced data (F3.5): if only 1 attempt in 500 is an attack, a 2% false rejection rate on genuine customers produces about ten genuine rejections for every attack caught.

    share of rejections that are attacks=π·TPRπ·TPR+(1−π)·FPR

    π is the share of attempts that are attacks. Same Bayes arithmetic as every rare-event problem. The new part is that d' is not fixed: it falls when the attacker switches to a generator the detector has not seen.

    Voice cloning against the call centre (A3.5)

    Voice authentication was sold as "my voice is my password". The weakness is the same as for faces. A speaker-verification model asks whether the voice is similar to an enrolled voiceprint, and a cloned voice is built to be similar. Cloning needs seconds of audio; the clone can be driven live through a voice-conversion tool, so there is no recorded-clip replay to detect. Telephone audio is also narrowband and compressed, which hides some of the artefacts a detector would use.

    The sound of a voice is therefore weak evidence, but surrounding evidence still works. A call centre has plenty:

    • whether the call comes from a number or device linked to the customer, and whether caller-ID has been spoofed;
    • a synthetic-speech detector score on the call audio (useful, but a noisy signal, not a verdict);
    • the shape of the request: urgency, secrecy, a new payee, a large amount, a change of contact details just before it;
    • an out-of-band step the caller cannot control: calling back on the registered number, an in-app approval, a push notification, a delay on first payments to a new payee.

    Because the signals are weak individually, think in odds. Start from the prior odds that such a call is an impersonation, and multiply by a likelihood ratio for each signal: a flag from the detector multiplies the odds by a large number, a clean result by a number a little below one. This is naive Bayes (F1.4) with the independence assumption stated honestly: signals from the same fraudster are correlated, so the posterior is a little optimistic.

    AI supply chain risk (A3.6)

    A model reaches the bank through a chain: a lab or community author, a hub such as Hugging Face, a download by a data scientist, a registry, a build, a deployment. An attacker can compromise any link, and three kinds of attack are different enough to know apart.

    1. Code in the file. Many model formats are Python pickles, which can execute arbitrary code when loaded. Researchers have found malicious models on public hubs whose "weights" file opens a reverse shell on the machine that loads it. The same applies to trust_remote_code options that run the model author's Python at load. This is classic malware delivered as a model, and it lands on a data scientist's laptop or a training cluster with access to data.
    2. Weights that behave. A poisoned or backdoored model works normally except when a trigger appears (a phrase, a pattern in an invoice image, a particular account prefix). The BadNets work showed this for image classifiers; the "sleeper agents" work showed that backdoors in language models can survive safety training. You cannot find this by reading the file. You can only test behaviour and know the provenance.
    3. Poisoned data. Fine-tuning or retrieval data that someone else influences: a public dataset, scraped web text, a vendor's labelled data, a shared internal wiki. Recent work found that a small, roughly fixed number of poisoned documents (on the order of a few hundred in the 2025 study) was enough to plant a backdoor in models of very different sizes, so "we train on so much data that a few bad rows won't matter" is not a safe belief. See data poisoning (G1.2).

    The controls are the ones software engineering already learned, applied to models: safe formats (safetensors stores tensors only and cannot run code on load), provenance (pin the exact revision by hash, prefer artefacts signed by their publisher, record where each model came from in an AI bill of materials), an internal registry that the build uses instead of live hub downloads, scanning plus sandboxed loading (scanners are rule-based and have been evaded, so never rely on one), behavioural evaluation before promotion, and a review of any code a model wants to run. For fine-tuning data, track lineage (P1.3) and keep an untrusted-source tier.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Digital onboarding and account recovery. New-to-bank customers, remote account opening, SIM swap followed by a password reset. Lower friction means a bigger target.
    • The contact centre. Voice biometrics for authentication, high-value payment confirmations, private-banking relationship managers who know the customer by voice.
    • Video calls and executive impersonation. A deepfaked senior leader instructing a treasury payment. Procedure beats detection: a second approver on a known channel.
    • The vendor stack. Many banks buy liveness and voice detection from vendors. Ask them for results on injection attacks and unseen generators, not only presentation-attack certificates.
    • Models and data in the bank's own platform. Open-weight models (including Arabic ones, see A14) downloaded by teams, and on-premises or sovereign deployments where the bank hosts the weights itself.

    In the Gulf, regulators expect strong customer authentication and strong governance of AI and third parties. I am not going to cite specific articles of CBUAE or SAMA rules here; read the current circulars for your institution and see A12.1.

    Where the field is now

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    The failures are mostly about trusting a score or a file beyond what it proves.

    • A certificate for the wrong attack

      The vendor's liveness is certified against presentation attacks, so it is called "deepfake-proof". Injection attacks were never in scope.

      Spot itAsk whether independent testing covered virtual cameras, emulators and SDK hooking, and on which generators.
    • Great benchmark, weak in production

      A detector with 99% accuracy on a public dataset collapses on a new generator.

      Spot itAsk for results on generators held out of training, and track the score distribution of live traffic for drift.
    • Threshold left at the vendor default

      Either a flood of genuine rejections that onboarding teams quietly override, or a loose setting that lets attacks in.

      Spot itLook at the manual override rate and the share of rejections that turn out to be fraud. Set the threshold from costs.
    • Voiceprint treated as proof

      The call is approved because the voice matched.

      Spot itAsk what happens if the match is high and the request is unusual. There should be an out-of-band step.
    • Fraud linked to one-by-one detection only

      Each application looks fine; thirty share a device, a payee or an address.

      Spot itCheck that device and network linkage runs across accounts, not per session.
    • Downloading models in production

      A job pulls a model from a public hub at start-up, with no pinned revision.

      Spot itSearch code and pipelines for hub downloads, trust_remote_code=True, and pickle-based loading (torch.load of unknown files).

    • Trusting a scanner

      "It passed the hub's malware scan."

      Spot itScanners are denylists that have been bypassed. Load unknown artefacts only in a sandbox without network or data access, and prefer formats that cannot execute code.
    • Fine-tuning on data nobody owns

      Public or vendor datasets and shared wikis go straight into training or retrieval.

      Spot itCan you name the source and owner of every training set, and who can edit it? If not, treat it as untrusted.
    • Believing detection is the control

      The programme is built around spotting fakes, with no limit on what a single approved call can do.

      Spot itAsk what the worst loss from one successful fake is. Cap it with limits, delays on new payees and second approval.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our selfie and voice checks were designed for someone holding a photo up to a camera. Today an attacker can feed generated video straight into the app or clone a customer's voice from a few seconds of audio, and the system will say it matches. We cannot out-detect every new fake, so we need to prove the image came from a real camera on a real device, add checks a fake cannot pass such as reading the ID chip and calling back on the registered number, and cap what any single approval can move. I'd want independent testing of our vendor against injection attacks, not just printed photos.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd separate presentation attacks from injection attacks and ask for evidence on both. For injection I want channel verification: virtual-camera and emulator checks, hardware attestation, signed capture. A content-based deepfake detector sits on top and is evaluated on held-out generators, with the threshold set from costs given the true attack rate. For calls I combine detector score, device and number linkage, and request risk, and use an out-of-band callback for high-risk actions. For models, safetensors only, pinned hashes through an internal registry, sandboxed loading, behavioural tests, and data lineage.”
    “Can't we just buy a deepfake detector?”

    You can and should, but it is one layer. Detectors are classifiers trained on known generators, so their quality decays when attackers change tools, and at a low attack rate even a good one rejects many genuine customers for each attacker caught. The channel check and out-of-band steps do not depend on how good the fake looks.

    “What is the difference between a presentation attack and an injection attack?”

    In a presentation attack the fake is shown to a real sensor: a photo, a screen, a mask. In an injection attack the sensor is bypassed and fake data is fed into the software, through a virtual camera, an emulator or a hooked SDK. Liveness models were tuned for the first. The second needs device and channel integrity, because the image can be perfect.

    “Should we switch off voice authentication?”

    Not necessarily, but stop treating it as sufficient for high-risk actions. Keep it as a convenience for low-value requests, and add a synthetic-speech score, number and device linkage, and an out-of-band confirmation before large payments or changes to contact details. Our own call-centre data on fraud outcomes should decide where the line sits.

    “Is it safe to download open-weight models from Hugging Face?”

    It can be, with a process. Use safetensors rather than pickle formats, pin the exact revision by hash, bring models through an internal registry, load unknown ones in a sandbox, avoid trust_remote_code unless the code was reviewed, and run behavioural tests before promotion. Provenance matters as much as scanning, because backdoored weights contain no malicious code to find.

    “Could our fine-tuning data be poisoned?”

    If anyone outside our control can influence it, yes. Research reports that a small, roughly fixed number of poisoned documents can plant a backdoor even in large models, so dataset size is not a defence. I'd track the source and owner of each dataset, keep untrusted sources out of the training set or in a lower-trust tier, and test the fine-tuned model against trigger-style probes.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can a liveness model with a perfect record against printed photos and screen replays still be beaten by a deepfake?

    Because it judges the image, and an injected stream bypasses the sensor, so the image can be free of the artefacts it was trained to find. It was never tested on that attack, and a generated video has no paper edges or moiré. The fix is to verify where the frames came from, not just what they look like.

    2. A detector flags 84% of attacks and 2.3% of genuine customers. Attacks are 0.2% of 10,000 attempts. How many genuine customers are rejected per attack caught?

    Attacks: 20, caught 16.8. Genuine: 9,980 × 0.023 ≈ 230. So about 230 ÷ 16.8 ≈ 14 genuine rejections per attack caught, and only about 7% of rejections are attacks.

    3. The attacker moves to a generator the detector has not seen and d' falls from 3 to 1.35. At the same threshold of 2, what happens to the catch rate?

    Catch rate = P(Z > 2 − d'). With d' = 3: P(Z > −1) = 84%. With d' = 1.35: P(Z > 0.65) = 26%. The genuine rejection rate does not change (still 2.3%), so the detector now rejects as many genuine customers while missing about three attacks in four.

    4. Why does "voice matches the voiceprint" carry almost no weight in the odds calculation?

    The likelihood ratio is P(match | clone) ÷ P(match | genuine). Clones are built to match, so the ratio is close to 1 and the odds hardly move. Signals that distinguish the two groups are the ones that carry weight: an out-of-band callback, a detector flag, an unknown number or device.

    5. Prior odds of impersonation are 1 in 2,000. A detector flags the call (likelihood ratio 23) and the number is unregistered (ratio 8). What is the probability the call is a fraud?

    Prior odds ≈ 0.0005. Times 23 × 8 = 184 gives 0.092 odds, which is 0.092 ÷ 1.092 ≈ 8.4%. Not certain, but high enough for a callback before moving any money.

    6. Why is a safetensors file safer than a PyTorch .bin file, and what does it not protect against?

    A pickle-based .bin can run arbitrary Python when loaded; safetensors holds only tensors and metadata, so loading cannot execute code. It does not protect against backdoored weights, a malicious trust_remote_code model class, or malicious code in other files in the same repository.

    7. Can we find a backdoor in a downloaded model by scanning the file?

    No. Scanners look for code-execution payloads in file formats. A trained-in backdoor is just numbers in the weights. You reduce the risk with provenance (trusted source, signature, pinned hash), behavioural testing for triggers, and restricting what the model can do if it misbehaves.

    8. Name two controls that limit the damage even when a deepfake succeeds.

    Limits and cooling-off periods on first payments to a new payee, and second approval or out-of-band confirmation for large or unusual requests. Also device and identity linkage to spot clusters, and rapid recall processes on instant payment rails.
