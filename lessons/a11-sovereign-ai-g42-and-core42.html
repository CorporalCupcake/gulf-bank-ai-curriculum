    Sovereign AI and Confidential Computing: Where Your Data Runs and Who Can See It

    Why Gulf states are building national AI infrastructure, what G42, Core42, Humain and Stargate UAE actually are, how data residency differs from sovereignty, and how confidential computing protects data while a GPU is using it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentence"Sovereign AI" is a question about who controls the compute, the law and the keys, not just where the building is; national platforms make in-country AI capacity available, and confidential computing is the technical tool that lets a bank use someone else's GPUs without letting them read the data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine you keep your customers' files in a safe deposit box. Three questions matter. Is the vault in your country? Whose law can force it open? And can the vault staff look inside while they move your box around? Most people only ask the first one.

    AI makes all three sharper. To get an answer from a large model, the data has to leave storage and sit, readable, in the memory of a GPU. For a bank that means customer names, balances and documents, in plaintext, on hardware it does not own, run by a company that may answer to a foreign government.

    That is the root of "sovereign AI". Gulf governments looked at the world's AI capacity, which sat largely in a few US companies' data centres, and decided that depending on it entirely was a strategic risk. They are paying to build local capacity: chips, power, data centres, Arabic and regional models, and the companies to run them. The UAE route runs mainly through G42 and its infrastructure arm Core42, plus the Stargate UAE campus. Saudi Arabia's runs through Humain, owned by the Public Investment Fund.

    For a bank the practical effect is simple. Options for running AI inside the country that did not exist two years ago now do. Rules about customer data have always limited what you may send abroad. The skill is to match each workload to the right place, and to understand the technology that narrows the trust you have to place in the host.

    Hold onto thisThree separate properties get lumped under "sovereign": residency (the bytes sit in the country), jurisdiction (whose courts and governments can compel access) and operator access (whether the people running the machines can read the data). Residency is easy to buy. The other two are where the real work is.

    Residency, sovereignty and the rules behind them

    Three layers of rule shape what a bank can do. I describe them in general terms; I am not citing article numbers, because the details change and your legal team owns the reading.

    • Data protection law. The UAE has a federal personal data law (Federal Decree-Law 45 of 2021) with rules on transferring personal data outside the country, and free zones such as DIFC and ADGM have their own regimes. Saudi Arabia has its Personal Data Protection Law with transfer regulations issued by SDAIA. In both, moving personal data abroad is allowed only on stated grounds, such as adequate protection or safeguards.
    • Financial-sector rules. The central banks (CBUAE, SAMA) set expectations on outsourcing, cloud use, operational resilience and customer data. These are often stricter than the general law for core banking and payment data. Some sector rules may require local storage of certain data or approval before use of cloud. Exactly which, and for which data class, I am not certain; confirm with your regulatory team (see A12.1 – A12.2).
    • National cyber and cloud frameworks. Both countries publish cloud security and classification frameworks that grade data and say which hosting is acceptable at each grade. Government and critical-infrastructure data is treated more tightly.

    Notice what these rules usually do not do: they rarely say "use this particular company". They set conditions on location, transfer, control and audit. Sovereign platforms exist to make meeting those conditions easy, not to be required by them.

    The foreign-law question deserves a sentence. A provider incorporated abroad can be ordered by its home courts to hand over data it controls, even if the data sits in your country. This is the reason many sovereign offerings put a local company, local staff and sometimes local key management in the middle, and why technical controls such as customer-held keys and confidential computing matter beyond the contract.

    Build, rent or go sovereign: the economics of GPUs

    A bank's first instinct when told data must stay in the country is sometimes "buy our own GPUs". Usually that is wrong. A GPU server costs the same whether it is busy or idle, so the cost of each useful hour depends on how often it is working. Renting charges only for hours used (or reserved), at a higher hourly price.

    own cost per useful hour=capex/life+running cost per year8760·u

    8,760 is the hours in a year and u is the share of them in which the GPU does useful work. Halve the utilisation and the cost per useful hour doubles. Renting is flat at the hourly price. Where the curves cross is the break-even utilisation.

    Two things the arithmetic hides. Owning at scale also needs power, cooling, networking, and people who can run a GPU cluster, which most banks do not have (P4.1 – P4.2). And GPUs are supply-constrained, so "rent" may depend on a queue. A common compromise: keep the sensitive core on bank-controlled or sovereign infrastructure, use open-weight models there (M2.5), and use external APIs only for data that is non-sensitive or de-identified.

    Confidential computing: protecting data while it is being used

    Data has three states. Encryption at rest (on disk) and in transit (over the network) are solved and routine. The third state, in use, is the gap: to compute on data, the processor must hold it decrypted in memory. Anyone with enough access to the host (a cloud administrator, a compromised hypervisor, someone with a debugger or a physical probe) could read it there.

    Confidential computing closes this with a trusted execution environment (TEE): a region of hardware where code and data run encrypted in memory and are decrypted only inside the processor package. The operating system and hypervisor underneath are treated as untrusted. CPU-based TEEs include Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP (whole confidential virtual machines) and Intel SGX (smaller enclaves). They were designed for CPUs, and AI runs on GPUs, so the data would leave the protected region on its way to the accelerator.

    Nvidia added confidential computing in its Hopper H100 GPUs, and later generations such as Blackwell extend it. A confidential VM and a confidential GPU are linked: data crossing the PCIe bus between them is encrypted (through shared "bounce buffers"), and GPU memory is protected from the host. The result is a CPU TEE plus GPU TEE that together keep a model and the prompts it processes out of the host's view.

    The part that makes this useful rather than just clever is remote attestation. The hardware produces a signed report ("measurement") of exactly what firmware and software it is running. The bank checks that report against what it expects, through the chip vendor's certificate chain or an attestation service, and only then releases the decryption key. If the host has been tampered with, the measurement differs and the key never leaves the bank's key service. The bank does not have to trust the provider's word, only the hardware vendor's signing and the code it reviewed.

    Be precise in the room about what confidential computing does not do. It does not stop the code you run from leaking data (a bad model or a prompt injection still leaks, see A3). It does not remove trust in the chip maker and its firmware. It has had real attacks: researchers have repeatedly shown side-channel and physical attacks against CPU TEEs including SGX, SEV and TDX, mostly needing substantial access, and vendors patch some and document others as out of scope. And it does not decide where the data lives or which court can order disclosure; that is still residency and jurisdiction. Performance cost is workload dependent; for large-model inference vendors report it as modest, and you should measure your own.

    Compare it with the other privacy-enhancing tools in the next lesson (A11.3 – A11.4). Differential privacy changes the output; homomorphic encryption computes on ciphertext at large cost; confidential computing computes on plaintext inside protected hardware at small cost. That trade, speed against the hardware you must trust, is why it is the first of the three to be practical for LLM serving.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Customer-service assistants. Transcripts in Arabic and English contain personal data. Host the model in-country or redact before any external call.
    • Credit and KYC document analysis. Passports, Emirates ID and salary certificates are high-sensitivity inputs; the strongest hosting and confidential computing fit here.
    • AML collaboration. Pooling data between banks or with a regulator needs a protected environment; see federated AML (A9.4).
    • Fine-tuning on internal data. The training set and resulting weights are both sensitive assets.
    • Internal productivity tools. Code assistants and meeting summarisers can leak strategy or customer names. Same classification, lower stakes.

    Where the field is now

    The shift in the last 18 months is from "should we build national AI?" to "who gets the chips, how much power exists and who can run frontier models here?" Confidential computing has moved from a niche for secure enclaves to a mainstream option on major clouds.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures come from treating one property as all three. Each comes with a quick check.

    • "It is in the UAE, so it is sovereign"

      Residency is met; jurisdiction and operator access may not be. A foreign provider's local region still answers to its home country's courts.

      Spot itAsk who owns and operates the platform, who holds the encryption keys, and which legal orders could reach the provider.
    • Believing announced capacity exists

      Plans a bank is building around: "1 GW is coming" with no delivered GPUs or contract terms.

      Spot itAsk for committed capacity, dates, price and an SLA in writing, and a fallback if delivery slips.
    • Confidential computing without attestation

      The feature is switched on but nobody checks the report, so the bank trusts the provider's word again.

      Spot itFind where the key is released. If it is released without verifying a measurement, the protection is decorative.
    • Protecting the CPU but not the GPU

      A confidential VM is used but the GPU is in normal mode, so model inputs sit in plaintext GPU memory.

      Spot itConfirm both the CPU and GPU TEE modes are enabled and attested together.
    • Assuming it solves everything

      Prompt injection, over-broad logging or a leaky tool still expose data from inside the protected environment.

      Spot itCheck logs, telemetry and outputs. Protected hardware does not protect what the application writes out.
    • Leaking through "harmless" side doors

      Prompts sent to an external service for evaluation, embeddings sent to a vector database abroad, or error reports with payloads.

      Spot itDraw the data flow end to end, including observability tools and vendor support channels.
    • Buying GPUs for a bursty workload

      A cluster at 15% utilisation costs several times a rental.

      Spot itAsk for expected utilisation, and compute the break-even as in Simulation 2.
    • Single-vendor lock-in to a national champion

      Everything sits with one provider with no portability.

      Spot itAsk how you would move in 90 days: formats, model weights, key export and contract exit terms.
    • Quoting regulation from memory

      "The regulator says all AI must be local." Usually an oversimplification of conditions on specific data classes.

      Spot itAsk which instrument, which article and which data class, and have compliance confirm it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Three things are tangled under 'sovereign': where the data sits, whose law can reach it, and whether the host's staff can read it. We should classify our data and decide for each class. The most sensitive stays in-country on infrastructure we or a local operator control, with our own keys. Where we need a provider's GPUs, confidential computing lets us use them without exposing plaintext, and we verify that before any key is released. Less sensitive work can use cheaper external models.”
    To a technical interviewer or architect
    “I'd route by data class through a gateway. For regulated inputs, an in-country open-weight model on confidential VMs with GPU TEEs, with attestation gating key release from our own KMS. I'd treat the TEE as reducing trust in the host, not removing trust in the silicon vendor, and measure the overhead on our own workload. I'd also keep portability: weights, formats and an exit plan, so we are not tied to one national platform.”
    “Why not just use a US cloud region in the UAE?”

    It may be fine for many workloads, and the regulator may allow it for those data classes. The open question is jurisdiction and operator access: a foreign provider is still subject to its home legal system. For the most sensitive data we'd want customer-held keys at minimum, and either a sovereign operator or confidential computing. I'd get compliance to confirm what the rules require.

    “Is Stargate UAE something we can use?”

    It's a very large planned cluster, with a first phase targeted for 2026. I haven't seen published terms for banks using it, so I'd treat it as future capacity to evaluate, not a plan to depend on today. What matters is committed capacity, price, data-handling terms and who operates it.

    “What exactly does confidential computing protect?”

    Data and code while they are being processed, from the host operator and the layers beneath the application. On GPUs it protects the data crossing the bus and held in GPU memory. It doesn't protect against bugs in our own application, or remove trust in the chip vendor, and it doesn't address residency or legal jurisdiction.

    “Should we buy our own GPUs?”

    Only if we expect high, steady utilisation and have the team to run them. At a rough illustration, owning breaks even with renting at around two-thirds utilisation, and bank workloads are bursty. A mixed approach is likelier: rent in-country capacity for most, and buy only for a sustained, sensitive baseline.

    “How do we know the provider isn't reading our data?”

    Contracts and audits give assurance, and attestation gives evidence: the hardware signs what it is running, we verify it, and only then release the key. If the host has been altered, the key is withheld. We still depend on the hardware vendor's signing and the code we reviewed, which is a much smaller circle than the whole provider.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. Name the three properties often mixed under "sovereign", and say which one a data centre in the UAE guarantees.

    Residency, jurisdiction and operator access. A UAE data centre guarantees only residency. A foreign-owned operator can still be subject to foreign orders, and its staff may still be able to read plaintext.

    2. Which are G42, Core42, Khazna, Humain and Stargate UAE, in one line each?

    G42: Abu Dhabi AI holding company. Core42: its sovereign cloud and AI infrastructure arm. Khazna: its data-centre builder and operator. Stargate UAE: planned 1 GW AI cluster in Abu Dhabi, built by Khazna with OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, Cisco and SoftBank. Humain: Saudi, PIF-owned AI company for data centres, models and applications.

    3. Using the Simulation 2 assumptions, what is the break-even utilisation with no premium?

    Annual cost per owned GPU = 150,000 ÷ 4 + 3.5 × 8,760 = 37,500 + 30,660 = AED 68,160. Dividing by 8,760 hours gives AED 7.78 per hour at full use. Setting 7.78 ÷ u = 12 gives u ≈ 65%.

    4. Why is a confidential VM alone not enough for LLM inference?

    The model runs on a GPU. Unless the GPU is also in confidential mode and the link between them is encrypted, prompts and activations sit in plaintext in GPU memory. You need CPU and GPU TEEs, attested together.

    5. What does remote attestation do, and what happens if the host firmware is changed?

    The hardware signs a measurement of what it is running. The bank compares it to the expected value and only then releases the key. If firmware has changed, the measurement differs and the key is withheld, so the data is never decrypted on that host.

    6. Give two things confidential computing does not protect against.

    Any of: leaks caused by the application itself (injection, logging); compromise or flaws in the chip vendor's hardware or firmware (including side channels); a legal order served on the provider about data it holds the keys to; the question of where data is stored.

    7. A workload is customer chat transcripts with names and account numbers. What is a sensible hosting order of preference?

    Redact first where possible. Then host in-country on infrastructure with customer-held keys, ideally on a sovereign or bank-controlled platform, with confidential computing if a provider's GPUs are used. An offshore public API is the last choice, and only for redacted text if rules allow.
