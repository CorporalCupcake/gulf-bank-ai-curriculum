    Sovereign AI and Confidential Computing: Where Your Data Runs and Who Can See It

    Why Gulf states are building national AI infrastructure, what G42, Core42, Humain and Stargate UAE actually are, how data residency differs from sovereignty, and how confidential computing protects data while a GPU is using it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentence"Sovereign AI" is a question about who controls the compute, the law and the keys, not just where the building is; national platforms make in-country AI capacity available, and confidential computing is the technical tool that lets a bank use someone else's GPUs without letting them read the data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a safe deposit box. Three questions matter. Is the vault in your country? Whose law can force it open? Can the vault staff look inside while they move your box? Most people ask only the first.

    AI sharpens all three. To answer, a large model needs the data readable in GPU memory. For a bank that means customer names, balances and documents, in plaintext, on hardware it does not own, run by a company that may answer to a foreign government.

    That is the root of "sovereign AI". Gulf governments saw most AI capacity sitting in a few US companies' data centres and judged full dependence a strategic risk. They are building local chips, power, data centres and Arabic models. The UAE route runs through G42, its infrastructure arm Core42 and the Stargate UAE campus. Saudi Arabia's runs through Humain, owned by the Public Investment Fund.

    For a bank, the skill is to match each workload to the right place, and to know the technology that narrows how much you must trust the host.

    Hold onto this"Sovereign" lumps three properties: residency (bytes sit in the country), jurisdiction (whose courts can compel access) and operator access (whether the people running the machines can read the data). Residency is easy to buy. The other two are the real work. Common misreading: "it is in the UAE, so it is sovereign".

    Residency, sovereignty and the rules behind them

    Three layers of rule shape what a bank can do. Described in general terms; your legal team owns the reading.

    • Data protection law. UAE Federal Decree-Law 45 of 2021 governs transfers of personal data abroad; DIFC and ADGM have their own regimes. Saudi Arabia has its Personal Data Protection Law with SDAIA transfer regulations. Both allow transfers only on stated grounds, such as adequate protection or safeguards.
    • Financial-sector rules. CBUAE and SAMA set expectations on outsourcing, cloud, resilience and customer data, often stricter than general law for core banking and payment data. Some may require local storage or approval before cloud use. Which rules, for which data class, is uncertain: confirm with regulatory teams (A12.1 – A12.2).
    • National cyber and cloud frameworks. Both countries grade data and say which hosting suits each grade. Government and critical-infrastructure data is treated most tightly.

    These rules rarely say "use this company". They set conditions on location, transfer, control and audit. Sovereign platforms make those conditions easy to meet; they are not required by them.

    Foreign law matters too. A provider incorporated abroad can be ordered by its home courts to hand over data it controls, even if the data sits in your country. That is why sovereign offerings put a local company, staff and sometimes key management in the middle, and why customer-held keys and confidential computing matter beyond the contract.

    Build, rent or go sovereign: the economics of GPUs

    "Data must stay in the country, so buy our own GPUs" is usually wrong. Think of owning a car versus taking taxis: the car costs the same parked or driven, so cost per useful hour depends on how often it works. Renting charges only for hours used, at a higher hourly price.

    own cost per useful hour=capex/life+running cost per year8760·u

    8,760 is hours in a year; u is the share of them doing useful work. Halve u and cost per useful hour doubles. Renting is flat. Where the curves cross is the break-even utilisation; above it owning wins, below it renting wins.

    The arithmetic hides two things. Owning needs power, cooling, networking and a team that can run a GPU cluster, which most banks lack (P4.1 – P4.2). And GPUs are supply-constrained, so renting may mean a queue. The usual compromise: sensitive core on bank-controlled or sovereign infrastructure with open-weight models (M2.5), external APIs only for non-sensitive or de-identified data.

    Confidential computing: protecting data while it is being used

    Data has three states. Encrypted at rest (disk) and in transit (network) are routine. In use is the gap: to compute, the processor holds data decrypted in memory, where a cloud administrator, compromised hypervisor or physical probe could read it. Like a locked file that must be opened on a shared desk to be worked on.

    Confidential computing closes the gap with a trusted execution environment (TEE): hardware where code and data stay encrypted in memory and are decrypted only inside the processor. The OS and hypervisor underneath are treated as untrusted. CPU TEEs include Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP (whole confidential VMs) and Intel SGX (smaller enclaves). But AI runs on GPUs, so data would leave the protected region on the way to the accelerator.

    Nvidia added GPU confidential computing in Hopper H100; later generations such as Blackwell extend it. Data crossing the PCIe bus between a confidential VM and GPU is encrypted (via shared "bounce buffers"), and GPU memory is shielded from the host. CPU TEE plus GPU TEE together keep the model and prompts out of the host's view.

    What makes this useful is remote attestation. Think of a tamper-evident seal you check before accepting a parcel. The hardware produces a signed "measurement" of exactly what firmware and software it runs. The bank checks it against the expected value, via the chip vendor's certificate chain or an attestation service, and only then releases the decryption key. If the host was tampered with, the measurement differs and the key never leaves the bank's key service. The bank trusts the hardware vendor's signing and code it reviewed, not the provider's word.

    What confidential computing does not do:

    • Stop your own code leaking data. A bad model or prompt injection still leaks (A3).
    • Remove trust in the chip maker and its firmware. Researchers have repeatedly shown side-channel and physical attacks on SGX, SEV and TDX, mostly needing substantial access; vendors patch some and call others out of scope.
    • Decide where data lives or which court can order disclosure. That is still residency and jurisdiction.

    Performance cost depends on the workload. Vendors report it as modest for large-model inference; measure your own.

    Against the other privacy tools in the next lesson (A11.3 – A11.4): differential privacy changes the output; homomorphic encryption computes on ciphertext at large cost; confidential computing computes on plaintext inside protected hardware at small cost. Speed traded for hardware trust is why it is first to be practical for LLM serving.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures treat one property as all three. Each has a quick check.

    • "It is in the UAE, so it is sovereign"

      Residency is met; jurisdiction and operator access may not be. A foreign provider's local region still answers to its home courts.

      Spot itAsk who owns and operates the platform, who holds the keys, and which legal orders could reach the provider.
    • Believing announced capacity exists

      Building plans on "1 GW is coming" with no delivered GPUs or terms.

      Spot itAsk for committed capacity, dates, price and an SLA in writing, plus a fallback if delivery slips.
    • Confidential computing without attestation

      The feature is on but nobody checks the report, so the bank trusts the provider's word again.

      Spot itFind where the key is released. If it is released without verifying a measurement, the protection is decorative.
    • Protecting the CPU but not the GPU

      A confidential VM with the GPU in normal mode leaves model inputs in plaintext GPU memory.

      Spot itConfirm CPU and GPU TEE modes are both enabled and attested together.
    • Assuming it solves everything

      Prompt injection, over-broad logging or a leaky tool still expose data from inside the protected environment.

      Spot itCheck logs, telemetry and outputs. Protected hardware does not protect what the application writes out.
    • Leaking through "harmless" side doors

      Prompts sent to an external evaluator, embeddings in an offshore vector database, error reports with payloads.

      Spot itDraw the data flow end to end, including observability tools and vendor support channels.
    • Buying GPUs for a bursty workload

      A cluster at 15% utilisation costs several times a rental.

      Spot itAsk for expected utilisation and compute break-even as in Simulation 2.
    • Single-vendor lock-in to a national champion

      Everything sits with one provider and nothing is portable.

      Spot itAsk how you would move in 90 days: formats, weights, key export, contract exit terms.
    • Quoting regulation from memory

      "The regulator says all AI must be local" usually oversimplifies conditions on specific data classes.

      Spot itAsk which instrument, article and data class, and have compliance confirm.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name the three properties often mixed under "sovereign", and say which one a data centre in the UAE guarantees.

    Residency, jurisdiction and operator access. Only residency. A foreign-owned operator can still face foreign orders, and its staff may read plaintext.

    2. Which are G42, Core42, Khazna, Humain and Stargate UAE, in one line each?

    G42: Abu Dhabi AI holding company. Core42: its sovereign cloud and AI infrastructure arm. Khazna: its data-centre builder and operator. Stargate UAE: planned 1 GW AI cluster in Abu Dhabi, built by Khazna with OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, Cisco and SoftBank. Humain: Saudi, PIF-owned AI company for data centres, models and applications.

    3. Using the Simulation 2 assumptions, what is the break-even utilisation with no premium?

    Annual cost per GPU = 150,000 ÷ 4 + 3.5 × 8,760 = 37,500 + 30,660 = AED 68,160, or AED 7.78 per hour at full use. Setting 7.78 ÷ u = 12 gives u ≈ 65%.

    4. Why is a confidential VM alone not enough for LLM inference?

    The model runs on a GPU. Unless the GPU is also confidential and the link encrypted, prompts and activations sit in plaintext in GPU memory. You need CPU and GPU TEEs, attested together.

    5. What does remote attestation do, and what happens if the host firmware is changed?

    The hardware signs a measurement of what it runs; the bank compares it to the expected value, then releases the key. Changed firmware changes the measurement, so the key is withheld and data is never decrypted on that host.

    6. Give two things confidential computing does not protect against.

    Any of: leaks caused by the application itself (injection, logging); compromise or flaws in the chip vendor's hardware or firmware (including side channels); a legal order served on the provider about data it holds the keys to; the question of where data is stored.

    7. A workload is customer chat transcripts with names and account numbers. What is a sensible hosting order of preference?

    Redact first. Then host in-country with customer-held keys, ideally sovereign or bank-controlled, with confidential computing if using a provider's GPUs. An offshore public API is last, and only for redacted text if rules allow.
