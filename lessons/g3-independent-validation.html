    Independent Validation: How a Model Earns Its Approval, and the Documents That Get It There

    What a validator actually does to your model (soundness, outcomes, benchmarking), why they cannot always detect a bad model with the data you have, and how to write documentation that a sceptical stranger can check without phoning you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceValidation is a second, independent team trying to find out whether the model is fit for its stated use, by checking the reasoning, the numbers it produced against what happened, and a fair rival; your documentation is the only evidence they have of your intent, so write it for a reader who cannot ask you anything.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Engineers who build a bridge do not certify it themselves. A different firm checks the load calculations, tests the steel and inspects the weld. The first team is not dishonest. It is simply too close: it knows what it meant, so it sees what it meant.

    Models are the same. The developer who built a probability-of-default model knows which variables were cleaned by hand, which quarter was dropped and why, and what the model is "really for". That knowledge is invisible in the model itself. An independent validator starts with none of it. They get the model, the data and the documentation, and they have to decide: is this fit for the purpose it will be used for?

    Notice the wording. Not "is it accurate?" and not "is it clever?". Fit for a stated use. A model that ranks customers well can still be unfit for setting provisions, because provisions need calibrated probabilities. The use defines the test.

    Validators typically work along three lines, which regulators and textbooks name in nearly the same words:

    • Conceptual soundness. Is the design reasonable? Is the method suitable for this problem, are the assumptions defensible, are the variables sensible and legitimately available at decision time? You can do this before seeing any results.
    • Outcomes analysis. What did the model predict, and what happened? Backtesting, out-of-time testing, calibration and stability, with an honest view of how much data there is.
    • Benchmarking and sensitivity. How does it compare with a simpler model or an independent rebuild, and how does it behave when inputs move, break or go missing?

    The second half of this lesson is the other side of the table. Documentation is not paperwork added at the end. It is the interface between you and the validator. If the document is vague, the validator must guess, and a guess becomes a finding.

    Hold onto thisIndependence means the validator is not reviewing their own work, has the standing to say no, and reports to someone other than the model owner. It does not mean hostile. The best validations leave the model better and the developer better prepared for the next one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What validation is for, and who does it

    The reference frame in most banks is the supervisory guidance on model risk management, issued in the US as SR 11-7 (Federal Reserve and OCC, 2011) and widely copied. It defines model risk as harm from decisions based on models that are wrong or misused, and asks for "effective challenge": critical analysis by objective, informed people who can identify limitations and get them fixed. In the UAE, the Central Bank published Model Management Standards and Guidance in December 2022, covering governance, data, development, implementation, usage, monitoring and independent validation, for all licensed banks. Check the current text on the CBUAE Rulebook before quoting it in a meeting; I have not read it clause by clause and details such as validation frequency by risk tier vary by institution.

    The usual arrangement is "three lines". The model owner uses the model and is accountable for it. The developer builds it. The validation team sits in risk, separate from both, reviews before first use and again periodically or when something material changes. Internal audit checks that the whole process works. Higher-risk models (provisioning, capital, large credit decisions, AML) get deeper validation, more often.

    Conceptual soundness: reading the design

    A validator begins with questions that need no data. They matter more than any statistic because a statistic cannot repair a flawed design.

    • Purpose and population. What decision does this support, for which customers, and which are out of scope? A retail scorecard trained on salaried nationals may be silently applied to expatriate or self-employed applicants. Same model, different population.
    • Target definition. "Default" means 90 days past due in one place and a restructure in another. A label that does not match the use creates a model that answers the wrong question (F3, leakage and point-in-time lessons).
    • Data and features. Did every feature exist at decision time? Is its quality stable? Is any feature a proxy that policy or law forbids (G2.2)? This is where point-in-time correctness is audited by someone else.
    • Method choice. Why a boosted tree and not a logistic regression? "It scored higher" is a reason; "it scored 0.4 points of AUC higher and cannot be explained to a customer who was declined" is a discussion. Validators ask for the alternatives that were tried.
    • Assumptions and limits. Stationarity, independence, the economic cycle the data covers. A model fitted on 2021–2024 has never seen a downturn; say so.
    • Explainability. Can the owner explain individual outcomes and the main drivers? Tools such as those in F8 are checked for stability, not just produced.

    Outcomes analysis: what the data can and cannot show

    Three families of tests, all asking different questions.

    • Discrimination. Does it rank risky above safe? AUC or Gini, KS, and precision/recall at the working point (F7).
    • Calibration. Do predicted probabilities match observed frequencies, by grade or score band? For credit this is the backtest: predicted PD per grade against the realised default rate.
    • Stability. Does it hold up out of time, across segments, and as inputs drift (F7 drift)? Validators insist on an out-of-time test, not a random split.

    The uncomfortable truth is statistical. A grade with a 0.5% PD and 5,000 accounts expects about 25 defaults a year. The count varies by roughly ±10 just by chance. So a true PD of 0.7%, 40% worse than predicted, is hard to tell apart from luck. For one grade the test is roughly:

    flag if|p^−p|>1.96p(1−p)n

    p is the model's predicted default rate for the grade, p̂ the observed rate, n the number of accounts. The right-hand side is the normal-approximation binomial band (F2.1). It is a rough tool: for very small p or n use an exact binomial test, and defaults in one grade are correlated through the economy, so real bands are wider. Validators know this and many prefer a one-sided test, since under-prediction is the dangerous direction.

    Three consequences for how validators behave. They pool grades or years for power. They use conservative margins where data is thin and say "inconclusive" rather than "passed". And they ask for sensitivity and stress tests to cover what the data cannot: what happens to the output if unemployment rises or a key input is missing. Absence of evidence in a small sample is not evidence that the model is right.

    Benchmarking and replication

    The third line asks: compared with what? Common benchmarks are a simple model (logistic regression, an earlier version), an independent rebuild by the validator, an external score such as a bureau score, and an expert judgement. If the validator's quickly built challenger matches your complex model within noise, the complexity has not earned its place. If it clearly beats it, you have a finding.

    For machine-learning models the validator also reruns your code. Replication, getting the same numbers from the same data and the documented steps, catches a surprising share of errors: a hidden filter, a leaked feature, a seed that was cherry-picked. It is also why pipelines must be versioned and reproducible, which links to the testing habits in F10.

    Findings: how they are rated and what happens next

    The output is a report with a verdict (approved, approved with conditions, not approved) and a list of findings, each rated by severity. Banks use their own scales, but a typical logic is: high if the model could mislead a material decision or breaks policy; medium if it weakens reliability and must be fixed on a timetable; low for housekeeping. Each finding gets an owner and a deadline. Unresolved high findings can restrict the model's use.

    Documentation that passes review

    A good model document has one test: could a competent stranger reproduce your decisions from it? Not just the final model, but why you chose it. Validators work through it in a predictable order, and every gap becomes a question, then a finding.

    SectionWhat the validator looks forWhat triggers a finding
    Purpose, scope, intended useThe decision, the population, what the model must not be used forVague use ("risk scoring"), no exclusions
    Data and sample designSources, period, lineage, exclusions with counts, target definition, point-in-time logicUnexplained drops, "cleaned data" with no rules, target not matching use
    Method and alternativesWhy this approach, what else was tried, results side by sideOne model shown; choice justified by accuracy alone
    FeaturesDefinition, rationale, treatment, expected direction of effectUnexplained transformations; features with no business logic
    PerformanceOut-of-time results, intervals, segments, calibrationOnly in-sample results; one number with no uncertainty
    Assumptions and limitationsHonest list, with compensating controls (overlays, human review)"None identified"
    ReproducibilityCode version, data snapshot, seeds, environmentCannot rerun to the same numbers
    Monitoring plan and change logMetrics, thresholds, owners, triggers for redevelopment; versions and approvalsNo thresholds ("we will watch it")

    Three habits make documents pass. Write decisions with reasons, not just descriptions: "we excluded accounts under 3 months old (1.8% of rows) because the target is undefined for them". State limitations before the validator finds them; a disclosed weakness with a control is a note, an undisclosed one is a finding. And keep one source of truth: numbers in the document should be generated by the code, not typed by hand. For ML systems a model card is a good summary page, but it does not replace this depth.

    Random splits flatter models. Validators ask for forward-in-time tests and so should you.

    F3.1 · LeakageThe most common serious finding

    Features built from information not available at decision time are the classic reason an impressive model collapses on replication.

    F8 · ExplanationsSoundness needs drivers you can state

    Validators test whether the model's drivers agree with business logic and whether explanations are stable.

    G2 · Fairness and documentationProxies and bias review

    Fairness testing is increasingly part of validation scope for lending and customer-facing models.

    B4 · PD, LGD, EADThe classic validated model

    IFRS 9 and capital models are the highest-tier validations: backtests by grade, benchmarking, overlay review.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit and IFRS 9. Deepest validation, annual cycles, challenger models, overlays reviewed by finance and risk.
    • Fraud and AML. Few confirmed positives and delayed labels make outcomes analysis weak; validators lean on conceptual soundness, sampling reviews and above-the-line/below-the-line testing of alert thresholds.
    • GenAI and copilots. No single ground truth. Validation shifts to evaluation sets, red-teaming, guardrail testing and use-scope controls. Practice is still forming; say so.
    • Vendor models. You validate what you can see, test the outputs, and document the limits of the black box (G3.6).

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Validation by the developer's friend

      The "independent" reviewer sits in the same team, shares a boss or was consulted during development.

      Spot itAsk who the validator reports to, and whether they had any input into the design.
    • Passing on thin data

      "No significant deviation" in a grade with 20 defaults. The test could not have found a problem.

      Spot itAsk for defaults per grade and the power of the test. Inconclusive should be written down as inconclusive.
    • In-sample performance presented as validation

      AUC reported on the development sample, or a random split of the same period.

      Spot itLook for an out-of-time sample with dates, and intervals.
    • Validating the number, not the use

      A model with good ranking is approved, then used for pricing or provisions that need calibrated probabilities.

      Spot itCompare the approved use in the report with how the outputs are actually consumed downstream.
    • Documentation written after the fact

      Methodology reconstructed from memory; numbers typed by hand and inconsistent with the code.

      Spot itTry to reproduce one table. If it differs, other things will too.
    • Limitations list that says "none"

      Every model has a population, period and assumption it was not tested on.

      Spot itAsk what the model has never seen: a downturn, a new product, a new customer segment.
    • Findings closed without evidence

      A high finding is "resolved" by an email.

      Spot itCheck that each closure links to changed code, data or a control, and was re-tested.
    • Model changes without revalidation

      A feature is swapped in a hotfix; the approved version no longer matches production.

      Spot itCompare the production version hash with the validated one.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Before this model is used on customers, a separate team re-checks the design, tests its predictions against what actually happened, and compares it with a simpler alternative. The honest limit is data: for our safest customers there are few defaults, so some tests can only say ‘no evidence of a problem’, not ‘proven right’. We've written down where the model should not be used, and what we'll watch once it's live.”
    To a validator or technical interviewer
    “Our document states the intended use and exclusions, the target definition with point-in-time logic, the alternatives we tried and why we chose this one. Performance is out of time with intervals, calibration by grade with defaults per grade, and a simple challenger. Limitations come with compensating controls. The code is versioned and the tables regenerate from it, so you can replicate. Thresholds and owners for monitoring are defined.”
    “Why can't the model team just validate it themselves? They know it best.”

    Because knowing it best is the problem. They see what they intended and share the blind spots that produced the design. Independence gives a view not shaped by the build, and the standing to say no. The developers' knowledge is used through documentation and Q&A, not as a substitute for challenge.

    “The backtest passed. Why isn't that enough?”

    A pass depends on data volume. With few defaults in a grade, the test cannot detect even a large error. It also tests only the past. I'd look at power, at stress and sensitivity analysis, and at whether the use fits the population the test covered.

    “How do you validate a model with only 150 confirmed cases?”

    Lean on conceptual soundness, benchmarking against a simple model, sampling-based review of alerts, and sensitivity tests. Report outcomes with wide intervals and call them inconclusive where they are. Add a plan to gather more labels, including a random review sample.

    “What makes a validator reject a document?”

    Not style. Gaps in the reasoning: no stated use, unexplained exclusions, one model shown, only in-sample numbers, no limitations, results that cannot be reproduced, and a monitoring plan with no thresholds.

    “Does an ML model need different validation from a scorecard?”

    The same three elements, but more weight on replication, leakage checks, stability of drivers and explanations, and a simpler challenger to show the complexity pays. The regulator's principles don't change; the evidence does.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. Name the three elements of validation and one question each asks.

    Conceptual soundness: is the design and method defensible for the use? Outcomes analysis: do predictions match what happened, out of time? Benchmarking and sensitivity: how does it compare with a rival, and how does it behave when inputs change?

    2. A grade has 2,000 accounts and a predicted PD of 2%. How many defaults do you expect, and roughly what is the 95% band on the observed rate?

    40 defaults. SE = √(0.02 × 0.98 ÷ 2,000) ≈ 0.31 points, so the band is 2% ± 0.61 points, i.e. 1.4% to 2.6% (28 to 52 defaults). A true PD of 2.5% would often pass.

    3. Why is the backtest weaker for the safest grade?

    Few defaults, so the count is dominated by chance. The relative band is wide even with many accounts. Pool years or grades, or rely on other evidence.

    4. A model ranks well but is used to set provisions. What is the validation issue?

    Use mismatch. Provisioning needs calibrated probabilities, which ranking tests don't establish. The approval should cover calibration, or the use should be restricted.

    5. List four things a validator needs in the documentation to replicate your work.

    Data sources and snapshot, exclusions and target definition with counts, feature definitions and transformations, and code version with seeds and environment. Also the splits used.

    6. Why disclose limitations yourself?

    A disclosed limitation with a compensating control is part of a sound design. The same limitation found by the validator is evidence of poor understanding, and rated higher.

    7. Your complex model matches the validator's simple challenger within noise. What happens?

    The complexity is not justified by performance. Expect a finding or a request for extra benefit, such as segment behaviour or stability. You might adopt the simpler model.
