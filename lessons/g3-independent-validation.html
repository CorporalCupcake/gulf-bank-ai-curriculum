    Independent Validation: How a Model Earns Its Approval, and the Documents That Get It There

    What a validator actually does to your model (soundness, outcomes, benchmarking), why they cannot always detect a bad model with the data you have, and how to write documentation that a sceptical stranger can check without phoning you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceValidation is a second, independent team checking whether the model is fit for its stated use; your documentation is their only evidence of your intent, so write it for a reader who cannot ask you anything.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bridge is not certified by the firm that built it. A second firm re-checks it. The builders are honest, just too close: they see what they meant.

    Models are the same. The developer of a probability-of-default model knows which quarter was dropped and which variables were hand-cleaned. None of that shows in the model. An independent validator gets the model, the data and the documentation, and decides: is it fit for its stated use? Not "is it accurate?". A model that ranks customers well can still be unfit for setting provisions, which need calibrated probabilities.

    Validators work along three lines:

    • Conceptual soundness. Is the design reasonable? Needs no results.
    • Outcomes analysis. Predicted versus what happened.
    • Benchmarking and sensitivity. How does it compare with a simpler rival, and how does it behave when inputs move?

    Documentation is the validator's only window into your intent. Where it is vague, they guess, and a guess becomes a finding.

    Hold onto thisIndependent means the validator is not reviewing their own work, can say no, and reports to someone other than the model owner. It does not mean hostile.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What validation is for, and who does it

    The reference frame is the US guidance SR 11-7, which asks for "effective challenge": critical analysis by objective, informed people who can get problems fixed. The Central Bank of the UAE issued Model Management Standards and Guidance in December 2022 covering the same ground. Check the current CBUAE Rulebook text before quoting it.

    The usual set-up: the owner is accountable for the model, the developer builds it, and a separate validation team reviews it before first use, periodically, and on material change. Higher-risk models (provisioning, capital, AML) get deeper validation.

    Conceptual soundness: reading the design

    These questions need no data, and matter more than any statistic: a number cannot repair a flawed design.

    • Purpose and population. A scorecard trained on salaried nationals may be silently applied to expatriates. Same model, different population.
    • Target definition. "Default" may mean 90 days past due in one place and a restructure in another. A label that doesn't match the use answers the wrong question.
    • Data and features. Did every feature exist at decision time (point-in-time correctness)? Is any a forbidden proxy (G2.2)?

    Also expect questions on method choice (show the alternatives tried), assumptions (a model fitted on 2021 to 2024 has never seen a downturn; say so) and explainability (F8).

    Outcomes analysis: what the data can and cannot show

    Three tests, three questions:

    • Discrimination. Does it rank risky above safe? AUC or Gini, precision/recall (F7). Like sorting runners by speed without knowing their times.
    • Calibration. Do predicted probabilities match what happened? A forecast of "30% rain" should see rain on about 3 days in 10.
    • Stability. Does it hold out of time and under drift? Validators want an out-of-time test, not a random split.

    The catch is statistical. A grade with PD 0.5% and 5,000 accounts expects about 25 defaults a year, varying by roughly 10 either way by chance alone. A true PD 40% worse looks like luck, like judging a coin from 10 flips. The validator draws a band around the prediction and flags the grade only if the observed rate falls outside it. Few defaults mean a wide band, and a wide band hides real problems.

    So validators pool grades or years, write "inconclusive" rather than "passed", and demand stress tests for what data cannot show. No evidence in a small sample is not evidence the model is right.

    Benchmarking and replication

    The question is "compared with what?": a simple model, the validator's own rebuild, or a bureau score. If a quick challenger matches your complex model within noise, the complexity hasn't earned its place.

    The validator also reruns your code. Replication (same numbers from the same data and documented steps) catches hidden filters, leaked features and cherry-picked seeds. Hence reproducible pipelines (F10).

    Findings: how they are rated and what happens next

    The report gives a verdict (approved, approved with conditions, not approved) and findings rated by severity: high if the model could mislead a material decision or breaks policy; medium if it weakens reliability and must be fixed on a timetable; low for housekeeping. Each finding has an owner and a deadline.

    Documentation that passes review

    One test: could a competent stranger reproduce your decisions from it? Every gap becomes a question, then a finding.

    SectionWhat the validator looks forWhat triggers a finding
    Purpose, scope, intended useThe decision, the population, what the model must not be used forVague use ("risk scoring")
    Data and sample designSources, exclusions with counts, target definitionUnexplained drops, "cleaned data"
    Method and alternativesWhy this approach, what else was triedOne model shown
    PerformanceOut-of-time results, intervals, calibrationOnly in-sample results
    Assumptions and limitationsHonest list, with compensating controls"None identified"

    Three habits help. Write decisions with reasons: "we excluded accounts under 3 months old (1.8% of rows) because the target is undefined for them". State limitations first: a disclosed weakness with a control is a note; an undisclosed one is a finding. Generate numbers from code, don't type them.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Validation by the developer's friend

      The "independent" reviewer shares a boss with the developer or had input into the design.

      Spot itAsk who the validator reports to.
    • Passing on thin data

      "No significant deviation" in a grade with 20 defaults. The test could not have found a problem.

      Spot itAsk for defaults per grade. Write inconclusive as inconclusive.
    • In-sample performance presented as validation

      AUC reported on the development sample, or a random split of the same period.

      Spot itLook for a dated out-of-time sample.
    • Validating the number, not the use

      A model with good ranking is approved, then used for provisions that need calibrated probabilities.

      Spot itCompare the approved use with how outputs are consumed.
    • Documentation written after the fact, limitations "none"

      Method reconstructed from memory, numbers that disagree with the code, and no admitted gaps.

      Spot itReproduce one table. Ask what the model has never seen: a downturn, a new segment.
    • Findings closed or models changed without evidence

      A high finding is "resolved" by an email, or a hotfix means production no longer matches the approved version.

      Spot itCheck each closure links to changed code and a re-test, and compare the production version with the validated one.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name the three elements of validation and one question each asks.

    Soundness: is the design defensible? Outcomes: do predictions match what happened? Benchmarking: how does it compare with a rival and react to input changes?

    2. A grade has 2,000 accounts and a predicted PD of 2%. You expect 40 defaults. Why might 48 not trigger a flag?

    Chance alone moves the count by roughly a dozen either way, so 48 sits inside the band. A true PD of 2.5% would often pass unnoticed.

    3. Why is the backtest weaker for the safest grade?

    Few defaults, so chance dominates and the band is wide. Pool years or grades, or use other evidence.

    4. A model ranks well but is used to set provisions. What is the validation issue?

    Use mismatch. Provisions need calibrated probabilities, which ranking tests don't establish.

    5. List four things a validator needs in the documentation to replicate your work.

    Data snapshot; exclusions and target definition with counts; feature definitions; code version and seeds.

    6. Why disclose limitations yourself?

    Disclosed with a control, it is sound design. Found by the validator, it is rated higher.

    7. Your complex model matches the validator's simple challenger within noise. What happens?

    Complexity is unjustified. Expect a finding, or a request to show extra benefit.
