    Independent Validation

    Part 1

    What independent validation is

    In one sentenceIndependent validation is a second team, separate from the builders, checking whether a model is fit for its stated use, and with the power to say no.

    A bridge is not certified by the firm that built it. A second firm re-checks it. The builders are honest, just too close: they see what they meant.

    Models are the same. Imagine a team building a probability-of-default (PD) scorecard: a model that gives each customer a chance of failing to repay. The developers know which quarter was dropped and which variables were hand-cleaned. None of that shows in the model. The validator gets the model, the data and the documents, and asks one question: is this fit for its use?

    Fit for use is not the same as accurate

    A model that ranks customers well can still be unfit for setting provisions, which need calibrated probabilities (more on that below). Validators judge the model against what it is used for.

    "Independent" does not mean hostile. It means the validator is not marking their own work and is not under the owner's pressure. The standard reference is the US guidance SR 11-7, which calls this "effective challenge". The Central Bank of the UAE issued Model Management Standards and Guidance in December 2022. Check the current CBUAE Rulebook before quoting it.

    Part 2

    How a validation works

    Validation happens before first use, on a regular cycle, and after any material change. Higher-risk models (provisioning, capital, anti-money-laundering) get deeper checks.

    1. Read the documents. They are the validator's only window into your intent. Where they are vague, the validator guesses, and a guess becomes a finding.
    2. Check the design (conceptual soundness). Needs no results. A number cannot repair a flawed design.
    3. Test the outcomes. Predictions against what actually happened.
    4. Benchmark and replicate. Compare with a simpler rival, then rerun the developer's code.
    5. Report. A verdict and rated findings, each with an owner and a deadline.

    What the design check asks

    • Purpose and population. A scorecard trained on salaried nationals may be silently used for expatriates. Same model, different people.
    • Target definition. "Default" may mean 90 days overdue in one place and a restructure in another. A label that does not match the use answers the wrong question.
    • Data at decision time. Did every input exist when the decision was made? Is any input a forbidden stand-in for a protected trait?
    • Assumptions. A model fitted on 2021 to 2024 has never seen a downturn. The documents should say so.

    What the outcome tests ask

    • Discrimination. Does it rank risky customers above safe ones? Measured with AUC or Gini. Like sorting runners by speed without knowing their times.
    • Calibration. Do predicted probabilities match reality? A forecast of "30% rain" should see rain on about 3 days in 10.
    • Stability. Does it hold on newer data? Validators want an out-of-time test (a later period), not a random split of the same period.

    The catch: few defaults means wide uncertainty

    Say a grade has a predicted PD of 0.5% and 5,000 accounts. You expect about 25 defaults a year, but chance alone moves that by roughly 10 either way. A true PD that is 40% worse can look like luck, like judging a coin from 10 flips.

    So the validator draws a band around the prediction and flags the grade only if the actual rate falls outside it. Few defaults mean a wide band, and a wide band hides real problems. The honest verdict on a thin grade is "inconclusive", not "passed". Validators then pool grades or years, and lean on stress tests for what data cannot show.

    Part 3

    How to read a validation report

    The report gives a verdict: approved, approved with conditions or not approved. Findings are rated by severity. Scales differ by bank, but the shape is usual.

    High finding
    The model could mislead a material decision, or breaks policy. Approval is usually held until it is fixed.
    Medium finding
    Weakens reliability. Must be fixed on a timetable.
    Low finding
    Housekeeping, such as wording or a missing label.
    A grade with 2,000 accounts and PD 2% shows 48 defaults, not the expected 40
    Chance alone moves the count by roughly a dozen either way, so 48 sits inside the band. No flag, but a true PD of 2.5% would often pass unnoticed too.
    A simple challenger matches your complex model, within noise
    The complexity has not earned its place. Expect a finding or a request to show the extra benefit.

    What passes documentation review

    One test: could a competent stranger reproduce your decisions from it? Each gap becomes a question, then a finding.

    • Purpose and scope. The decision, the population and what the model must not be used for. "Risk scoring" is too vague.
    • Data. Sources and exclusions with counts. "Cleaned data" triggers a finding.
    • Method. Why this approach and what else was tried.
    • Performance. Out-of-time results with intervals. In-sample results alone trigger a finding.
    • Limitations. An honest list with controls. "None identified" triggers a finding.
    • Reproducibility. Code version, data snapshot and seeds.

    Two habits help. Write decisions with reasons ("we excluded accounts under 3 months old, 1.8% of rows, because the target is undefined for them"). Generate numbers from code rather than typing them.

    Part 4

    Which check answers which question

    CheckAnswersWeak spot
    Conceptual soundnessIs the design defensible?Cannot show it works in practice
    Outcomes analysisDo predictions match what happened?Few defaults make it inconclusive
    BenchmarkingIs it better than a simpler rival?Depends on a fair rival
    ReplicationCan the numbers be reproduced?Needs a reproducible pipeline

    Strong at: catching hidden filters, leaked inputs and design flaws that no statistic can fix.

    Weak at: proving a model is right on thin data. No evidence of a problem is not evidence of no problem.

    In banking, a model that skips validation, or is validated by the developer's own team, is a finding for auditors and regulators, however good its accuracy looks.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • A validator who is the developer's friend

      They share a boss with the developer or helped design the model. Ask who the validator reports to.

    • Passing on thin data

      "No significant deviation" in a grade with 20 defaults. The test could not have found a problem, so write "inconclusive".

    • In-sample results presented as validation

      AUC on the development sample, or a random split of the same period. Look for a dated out-of-time sample.

    • Validating the number, not the use

      A model that ranks well is approved, then used for provisions that need calibrated probabilities. Compare the approved use with how outputs are really consumed.

    1. Name the elements of validation and one question each asks.

    Soundness: is the design defensible? Outcomes: do predictions match what happened? Benchmarking: how does it compare with a simpler rival?

    2. A grade has predicted PD 2% on 2,000 accounts, and 48 defaults appear against 40 expected. Why might this not be flagged?

    Chance alone moves the count by about a dozen either way, so 48 sits inside the band.

    3. Why is the backtest weaker for the safest grade?

    It has few defaults, so chance dominates and the band is wide. Pool years or grades, or use other evidence.

    4. A model ranks well but is used to set provisions. What is the issue?

    A use mismatch. Provisions need calibrated probabilities, and ranking tests do not establish those.

    5. Why should you disclose limitations yourself?

    Disclosed with a control, a limitation is sound design. Found by the validator, it is rated higher.
