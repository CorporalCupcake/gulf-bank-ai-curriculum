    A11.3 – A11.4 Edge About 40 minutes

    Privacy-Enhancing Technologies: What Is Practical Today

    Differential privacy, data clean rooms, homomorphic encryption and federated learning: what each one actually protects, what it costs in accuracy and compute, and which of them a Gulf banking group can use this year rather than in a research paper.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceThese tools each protect a different thing (the output, the join, the computation, the location of the data), none of them is a switch that makes data "anonymous", and the practical ones today are differential privacy on aggregates, clean rooms for controlled joins, secure aggregation and federated learning for training across entities, with homomorphic encryption limited to small, narrow computations.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank group operates in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and two smaller markets. A mule-account ring moves money through all of them. Each subsidiary sees a slice of the pattern, and the group model would be much better if it saw the whole. But customer data is personal data, each country has its own rules on moving it, and the subsidiaries' legal teams will not send raw tables to a central lake.

    Four families of tools exist for this situation. It helps to see them as answers to four different questions, because most confusion in meetings comes from treating them as one thing.

    QuestionToolPlain-language idea
    Can the answer I publish give away a person?Differential privacyAdd calibrated random noise so the answer would have been almost the same with or without any one person.
    Can two parties join their data without seeing each other's?Clean rooms (and private set intersection)A controlled space where only approved, aggregated results leave.
    Can someone compute on data they cannot read?Homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computationMathematics that lets a server add or multiply numbers it cannot see.
    Can I train a model without moving the data?Federated learningSend the model to the data, send back only updates, and average them.

    Notice what is missing from each answer. Differential privacy protects the output but not the raw data in the room where it is computed. A clean room controls who sees what, but is largely a governance arrangement. Encryption protects data during computation but is slow. Federated learning keeps data in place, but the updates it sends can leak. Every tool leaves a gap, and the skill is knowing which gap you can live with. The simulations below make four of those gaps concrete.

    Hold onto thisPrivacy tools buy protection by spending something: accuracy (noise), money and time (encryption), simplicity (federation) or governance effort (clean rooms). Always ask what is protected, from whom, and what it costs. If a vendor cannot say, the tool is not yet understood.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Differential privacy: the promise and the noise

    Differential privacy (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith, 2006) is a property of a procedure, not of a dataset. A randomised procedure M is ε-differentially private if, for any two datasets D and D′ that differ in one person's records, and any possible output set S:

    P[M(D)∈S]≤eε·P[M(D′)∈S]

    In words: whatever the output turns out to be, it was at most eε times more likely with you in the data than without. With ε = 0.1 that factor is 1.1, essentially no information. With ε = 10 it is 22,000, essentially none of the protection. Small ε means strong privacy and noisy answers. A common variant, (ε, δ)-DP, allows the guarantee to fail with a tiny probability δ, which makes some mechanisms (Gaussian noise) possible.

    The simplest mechanism is the one in Simulation 1. If a query changes by at most Δ when one person is added or removed (its sensitivity), add noise drawn from a Laplace distribution with scale Δ/ε. A count has Δ = 1. So the typical error is 1/ε counts, whatever the size of the dataset. That detail is the whole economics of DP: noise is fixed in absolute terms, so big groups lose almost nothing and small groups lose everything. An error of 10 on a branch segment of 1,200 is under 1%. The same error on a cell of 15 customers makes it useless, which is exactly the cell you most want to protect.

    Two things DP does that older "anonymisation" does not. It holds against an attacker with any outside knowledge, including other published datasets. And it has an arithmetic: you can add up how much privacy several releases cost. That second point is the next section. The first is why DP exists: Dinur and Nissim (2003) showed that answering enough exact or lightly perturbed aggregate queries lets an attacker reconstruct individual records, and the US Census Bureau found in its own tests that its 2010 tables could be partly reconstructed, which is why it moved to DP for 2020 (Abowd, 2018).

    Budgets, clipping and the cost of accuracy

    Privacy loss accumulates. If you answer k queries about the same people, each with εq, the total loss is at most k·εq (basic composition; advanced composition and accounting methods do better). So an organisation sets a privacy budget, say ε = 1 per quarter for a dataset, and every query spends part of it. Ten queries share it, so each gets ε = 0.1 and ten times the noise. When the budget is gone, the data is closed. This is operationally painful, which is why most real DP deployments release one fixed set of statistics rather than offering open-ended querying.

    Averages and sums bring a second problem. One customer with AED 80 million in an account can change a mean balance by a lot, so the sensitivity is unbounded. The fix is to clip: cap each value at C before averaging. Then one person can change the mean by at most C/n, and the noise scale is C/(n·ε). Pick C too high and you add needless noise. Pick it too low and you cut off the high balances and bias the answer downwards. The best C balances the two.

    A few consequences for practice. First, DP is best at statistics over large groups: dashboards, segment averages, published reports, histograms. Second, it works for model training too, as DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016): clip each example's gradient and add Gaussian noise at every step. This costs accuracy, more on rare classes. With 200 confirmed AML cases (see imbalanced data) the noise needed for a sensible ε can swamp the signal, because rare examples are exactly what clipping and noise erase. Third, ε has no universal safe value. Researchers argue about whether ε around 1 is strong and ε above 10 is largely symbolic; deployed systems have used much larger values, and NIST's 2025 guidance (SP 800-226) is mainly about asking what a claimed ε really covers: one record or one customer, one release or a lifetime.

    The "unit of privacy" is the quiet trap. A customer with 4,000 transactions is 4,000 rows. If the guarantee protects a row, it says little about the customer. Protecting the customer means clipping how much any one customer can contribute, which means more noise.

    Clean rooms and private set intersection

    A data clean room is a controlled environment where two or more parties put data that is joined on a shared key (a hashed ID, say), and each can run only approved analyses. Typical rules: no row-level output, a minimum group size (results with fewer than, for example, 50 people are suppressed), a list of allowed queries, an audit log. Cloud and warehouse vendors now ship these as products, and some add differential privacy to the outputs. A bank and a retailer use one to measure how many card customers who saw an offer later spent at the merchant.

    Be clear about what a clean room is. In most products it is access control and contract, not cryptography: the platform operator, or a bug, can in principle see the joined data. Stronger variants run inside confidential-computing hardware (trusted execution environments), which shifts trust to the chip vendor and an attestation process. The questions to ask: who operates it, who can see the unaggregated join, how are repeated queries limited (otherwise an analyst can difference two queries and isolate a person), and which legal entity is the data controller.

    A cryptographic cousin is private set intersection (PSI): two parties find which identifiers they share, and nothing about the rest. Google's Private Join and Compute (Ion et al., 2020) extends this to computing a sum over the intersection. For banks the natural uses are shared watch-lists, sanctioned-entity screening across groups, and checking whether an account on a suspected mule list also appears at a counterparty, where only the overlap, or only a count, should be learned.

    Homomorphic encryption and secure aggregation

    Homomorphic encryption (HE) lets a server compute on ciphertexts and return an encrypted result that only the data owner can open. The idea is old: textbook RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic and Paillier is additively so. The breakthrough was fully homomorphic encryption (Gentry, 2009), which supports arbitrary computation. Modern schemes come in families: BFV and BGV for exact integer arithmetic, CKKS for approximate arithmetic on real numbers (the one machine learning uses), and TFHE for fast bit-level operations.

    The price is the problem. Every ciphertext is large (thousands of times the size of the plaintext in many settings), operations are far slower than plaintext, and the noise inside a ciphertext grows with each multiplication, limiting the depth of computation unless the scheme performs costly "bootstrapping". Reported overheads for general computation are often three or more orders of magnitude; treat any single figure as workload-dependent. That rules out training large networks or running an LLM under encryption today. What it supports well:

    • Private lookups. Check a value against a large database without revealing the query. Apple uses this design for caller identification and photo landmark lookups, and open-sourced its library in 2024.
    • Sums and simple scores. Add encrypted values from many parties, or score a customer with a linear or small-tree model whose features stay encrypted.
    • Small, high-value joins where the data genuinely cannot leave its owner.

    A simpler relative does most of the practical work in banking: secret sharing, the basis of secure multi-party computation. Each party splits its number into random-looking shares that sum back to the secret, and gives one share to each other party. Any single share, or any incomplete set, is uniformly random and reveals nothing. Yet adding up the shares everyone holds gives the exact total. This is the core of secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., 2017), used to combine model updates in federated learning. Simulation 3 runs it.

    Federated learning across group entities

    In federated learning (FL) a central server holds a model. Each round it sends the current weights to the participants (here, the group's entities). Each trains on its own data for a few steps and returns the new weights or the change. The server averages them, weighting by data size, and repeats. This is FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017):

    wt+1=∑knknwkt+1

    wk is entity k's weights after training locally from the shared wt, and nk its number of examples. If every entity did exactly one gradient step, this equals ordinary training on the pooled data. The trouble starts when they do several local steps on different data.

    Two flavours matter. Cross-device FL has millions of phones (Gboard keyboard prediction was the first large deployment). Cross-silo FL has a handful of institutions with big datasets and stable connections, which is the bank-group case. Cross-silo is easier in some ways (reliable participants, real contracts) and harder in others: the entities are very different. Different countries have different customers, products, label definitions and fraud typologies. This is called non-IID data, and it is the main technical difficulty. Each entity's local optimum differs from the global one. After several local steps the models drift towards their own optima, and averaging them lands in a place that is optimal for nobody and, with enough local steps, not even where pooled training would end. Simulation 4 shows this with the simplest possible model.

    The important part is what FL does not do. Federated learning is not a privacy guarantee. The updates are functions of the training data. Gradient-inversion attacks (Zhu et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2020) can reconstruct images or text from a single shared gradient in some settings, and a participant can sometimes infer whether a particular record was in a training set (membership inference, see memorisation and private training). So a serious design stacks the tools from this lesson: secure aggregation so the server only sees the sum of updates, differential privacy on the updates so the sum itself leaks little, and sometimes confidential computing for the server. Each layer costs accuracy or engineering effort. Whether an update counts as personal data for cross-border purposes is a legal question; do not assume it does not. Ask legal and the regulator.

    Cross-border federation also needs boring agreements first: one definition of the label ("confirmed fraud" in one country might mean a customer dispute, in another a bank investigation), one feature schema, one validation process, and a decision on who owns the resulting model. Most failed federated projects fail here, not in the maths.

    # One round of FedAvg, cross-silo. Everything below runs inside each entity
# except the last line, which runs on the coordinator.
def local_update(global_w, entity_data, steps, lr):
    w = global_w.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        w -= lr * gradient(w, entity_data)       # raw data never leaves
    return w - global_w, len(entity_data)        # send the change and a count

updates = [local_update(w, d, steps=5, lr=0.1) for d in entity_datasets]
w = w + sum(n * delta for delta, n in updates) / sum(n for _, n in updates)

    How it connects

    These tools sit between privacy law, infrastructure and modelling. Here is where they meet the rest of the curriculum.

    Data residency pushes toward federation and clean rooms: if the data cannot move, the computation has to.

    B1 · Fraud detectionFraud sees only its own slice

    Mule networks cross institutions. Federated or PSI-based collaboration is the credible way to see across them.

    B2 · Sanctions and AMLShared watch-lists

    PSI and clean rooms are natural for checking overlap with another party's list without exchanging customer files.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataWhy rare classes suffer under DP

    Noise and clipping erase rare cases first. The positive count again decides what is feasible.

    P4 · Hybrid deploymentCompute placement

    Federated nodes are on-premises deployments with a coordinator; every entity needs the same pipeline and monitoring.

    G1 · Data poisoningFederation widens the attack surface

    A dishonest participant can send poisoned updates, and secure aggregation makes them harder to inspect. Robust aggregation is part of the design.

    F1.4 · ProbabilityBayes and the attacker

    DP's guarantee is a bound on how far an output can move an attacker's posterior odds: at most eε.

    Where it shows up at a Gulf bank:

    • Group-wide fraud and mule detection. Subsidiaries in different jurisdictions train a shared model or exchange overlap signals without moving customer tables.
    • Partnerships. Measuring offers with a retailer, telco or fintech through a clean room instead of swapping customer lists.
    • Internal analytics. Publishing branch-level or segment dashboards to wide audiences with DP noise, or with minimum cell sizes at the least.
    • Cloud and AI vendors. Encrypting or federating workloads when a regulator or board does not accept raw data going to a third-party region. A trusted environment inside a Gulf data centre may be simpler than any cryptography; see the sovereignty lesson.
    • Regulatory reporting. Sharing statistics with supervisors or industry bodies. Whether DP is expected or accepted here is a question for each regulator, not something this lesson can answer.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    DP and FL are mature enough for production in the right setting. HE is practical only for narrow tasks. The active questions are how to audit privacy claims, how to make cross-institution fraud sharing legally workable, and whether hardware acceleration will make encrypted computation ordinary.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are claims that go further than the tool does. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "It is differentially private" with no ε

      The phrase is a property that comes in degrees. Without ε, δ, the unit of privacy and the number of releases, it means nothing.

      Spot itAsk: what is ε, what does one unit protect (row, customer, account), and how many releases share the budget?
    • An ε so large it is symbolic

      ε = 50 on a customer-level release gives a bound of e50, which is no practical protection.

      Spot itCompute eε. If the number has more than six digits, ask what attack the guarantee actually blocks.
    • Budget leakage through repeated queries

      Analysts re-run the same query with fresh noise and average it away, or an unaccounted dashboard refreshes daily.

      Spot itFind out whether a system tracks cumulative ε per dataset, or whether noise is regenerated on each call.
    • Small cells in a clean room

      A result for "customers over 60 in this postcode with a loan" returns 3 people. Two queries differing by one filter isolate someone.

      Spot itCheck minimum thresholds, query logging and whether differencing attacks are blocked.
    • "The data never leaves, so it is anonymous"

      Federated updates are functions of the data and can be inverted or used for membership inference.

      Spot itAsk who sees individual updates, whether secure aggregation and noise are applied, and what the threat model is for a curious coordinator.
    • Non-IID drift ignored

      The federated model scores well on the largest entity and badly on the smallest, or loses to each entity's local model.

      Spot itDemand per-entity results against a local-only baseline, not one pooled number. Check the number of local steps.
    • Different labels under one name

      "Fraud" means a customer dispute in one entity and a closed investigation in another, so the model learns an average of two incompatible definitions.

      Spot itWrite each entity's label definition and delay side by side before any training.
    • Encryption oversold

      A proof of concept on 1,000 rows is described as production-ready. At scale the latency or cost is hundreds of times worse.

      Spot itAsk for throughput on production-sized data, the ciphertext size, and the multiplicative depth the model needs.
    • Collusion and the total itself

      Secure aggregation hides individual inputs, not what the total reveals. With three entities, two can solve for the third.

      Spot itAsk how many participants there are and whether the aggregate is released with noise.
    • Privacy technology treated as a legal basis

      A team assumes noise or encryption removes the need for a lawful basis, residency analysis or a regulator conversation.

      Spot itCheck that legal has reviewed the design, and that the regulator's position on the specific flow is recorded, not assumed.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our fraud teams in each country see only their own part of a cross-border pattern, and data rules stop us pooling it. There are three practical routes. First, publish only group-level statistics with a measured amount of noise. Second, join data with a partner in a controlled room that returns only approved aggregates. Third, train one shared model by sending the model to each entity and combining what comes back, with protection on those updates. None is free: each costs some accuracy or engineering effort, and legal needs to confirm what each one counts as under each country's law. I'd start with one pilot on mule-account detection and measure the gain against each entity alone.”
    To a technical interviewer or privacy reviewer
    “I'd pick the tool by threat model. For published statistics, differential privacy with a stated ε, customer-level sensitivity via clipping, and a tracked budget. For partner joins, a clean room with minimum cell sizes, query logging and ideally attested hardware. For training across entities, FedAvg with secure aggregation and noised updates, evaluated per entity against local baselines because the data is non-IID. Homomorphic encryption only for narrow lookups or small scores. I'd also say what is not protected: federated updates can leak, and a secure total can still reveal a small group's figure.”
    “Is differential privacy the same as anonymisation?”

    No. Anonymisation is a claim about a dataset. DP is a property of a procedure and comes in degrees measured by ε. It is stronger in one respect, because it holds even against an attacker with outside data, but a large ε or a row-level unit gives little protection. Whether a given DP release counts as anonymous in law is for legal and the regulator to decide.

    “What ε should we use?”

    There is no universal answer. I'd choose it by the attack I want to defeat and the accuracy I need, show the stakeholders the curve of error against ε as in the simulation, and be wary of anything much above 10. For published statistics on large groups, ε around 1 costs little accuracy. I'd also record what the guarantee covers: a customer, not a row, and how many releases share it.

    “Why not just use homomorphic encryption for everything?”

    Cost. Ciphertexts are large, computation is hundreds to thousands of times slower than plaintext for general work, and deep computations need expensive noise management. It works well for private lookup and simple scoring, not for training a gradient-boosted model or running an LLM. I'd look at confidential computing or a controlled on-premises deployment first.

    “If data stays in each country, why is federated learning not automatically compliant?”

    Because the updates are derived from personal data and can be attacked to recover it. Compliance depends on what leaves, what the coordinator can see, the lawful basis for the processing, and how each regulator treats model updates. I don't know of settled public guidance on that for the UAE or Saudi Arabia, so I'd put the question to legal and the supervisor early, with a threat model and the protections attached.

    “Would a federated model beat what each entity can build alone?”

    Often for the smaller entities and for cross-border patterns, rarely for the largest one on its home market. It can also lose if data differs a lot and local steps are many. I'd test per entity against local-only and pooled baselines in a pilot, with the same label definition, before committing to the architecture.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A count query has sensitivity 1 and you release it with ε = 0.5. What is the Laplace scale and the typical error? What is the largest factor by which one answer can change an attacker's odds?

    Scale = 1/ε = 2, so the typical absolute error is 2 counts. The odds can move by at most e0.5 ≈ 1.65.

    2. Your quarterly budget is ε = 1 and your team wants 25 equally important dashboard counts. What happens to each count's error?

    Each gets ε = 1/25 = 0.04, so the Laplace scale is 25 and the typical error is 25 counts, 25 times the error of a single count at ε = 1. Cells smaller than a few hundred become unusable. Options: fewer queries, a smaller set of important ones, or a larger budget with eyes open.

    3. You release the mean balance of 10,000 customers with clip C = AED 200,000 and ε = 1. What is the noise scale, and why not set C to AED 100 million to avoid clipping anyone?

    Scale = C/(n·ε) = 200,000 ÷ 10,000 = AED 20. At C = 100 million the scale would be AED 10,000 per release, about 500 times more noise, far more than the clipping bias it removes, in exchange for protecting a handful of rich customers' exact values.

    4. A vendor says its clean room is "privacy-safe". What four questions do you ask?

    Who operates it and can they see the joined rows? Is there confidential-computing attestation or just access control? What minimum group size and query limits stop differencing attacks? Who is the data controller for the joined output, and what does the audit log record?

    5. In Simulation 3, entities A, B and C hold 120, 85 and 40. If B and C collude, what do they learn about A, and what would reduce it?

    They learn A exactly: the published total is 245, and 245 − 85 − 40 = 120. Secure aggregation hid A's inputs from B and C individually, not what the total implies. Remedies: more participants (so colluders must be a larger fraction), adding DP noise to the total, or not publishing totals for very small groups.

    6. Your federated fraud model, after many local steps per round, has a worse pooled error than centralised training. Give two causes and two fixes.

    Causes: non-IID data causing client drift (each entity's local optimum differs), and too many local steps. Fixes: fewer local steps per round (more communication), a smaller learning rate or a drift-correcting method, aligned label definitions and features, and personalised heads per entity. Always compare against each entity's local-only model.

    7. Why is "the raw data never leaves the entity" insufficient as a privacy claim for federated learning?

    The updates are computed from the data. Gradient-inversion attacks have reconstructed inputs from them, and membership inference can detect individual records. You need secure aggregation so the server sees only the sum, DP noise so the sum leaks little, and a clear threat model for each participant and for the coordinator.
