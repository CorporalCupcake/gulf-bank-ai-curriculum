    Privacy-Enhancing Technologies: What Is Practical Today

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank group operates in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and two smaller markets. A mule-account ring moves money through all of them, and each subsidiary sees only a slice. A group model would do far better seeing the whole, but customer data is personal data and each country restricts moving it.

    Four tool families answer four different questions. Most meeting confusion comes from treating them as one thing.

    QuestionToolPlain-language idea
    Can the answer I publish give away a person?Differential privacyAdd calibrated random noise so the answer would have been almost the same with or without any one person.
    Can two parties join their data without seeing each other's?Clean rooms (and private set intersection)A controlled space where only approved, aggregated results leave.
    Can someone compute on data they cannot read?Homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computationMathematics that lets a server add or multiply numbers it cannot see.
    Can I train a model without moving the data?Federated learningSend the model to the data, send back only updates, and average them.

    Each leaves a gap: DP protects the output, not the raw data; a clean room is largely governance; encryption is slow; federated updates can leak. The skill is knowing which gap you can live with.

    Hold onto thisEvery privacy tool buys protection by spending something: accuracy, money and time, simplicity, or governance effort. Ask what is protected, from whom, and at what cost.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Differential privacy: the promise and the noise

    Think of a fogged window: you see the crowd's shape, not any one face. DP is a property of a procedure, not a dataset. It promises that the published answer would have been almost the same with or without any one person's data. The privacy dial is ε: small ε means strong privacy and noisy answers; large ε means accurate answers and little protection. Roughly, ε near 1 is strong and above 10 largely symbolic.

    The simplest mechanism is the one in Simulation 1: add random noise sized to how much one person can change the answer. A count changes by at most 1, so the noise is about 1/ε counts whatever the dataset size. That is the economics of DP: noise is fixed in absolute terms, so big groups lose almost nothing and small groups lose everything. An error of 10 on a 1,200-customer segment is under 1%. On a cell of 15 customers it makes the answer useless, and that is the cell you most want to protect.

    Unlike older "anonymisation", DP holds against an attacker with any outside knowledge. It exists because exact aggregates leak: enough of them let an attacker reconstruct individual records, which is why the US Census Bureau moved to DP for 2020.

    Budgets, clipping and the cost of accuracy

    Privacy loss accumulates, like a spending allowance. An organisation sets a privacy budget, say ε = 1 per quarter, and every query spends part of it. Ten queries get ε = 0.1 each, so ten times the noise each. When the budget is gone, the data is closed. Most real deployments therefore release one fixed set of statistics.

    Averages add a problem: one customer with AED 80 million can swing a mean balance. The fix is to clip: cap each value at C. Too high a cap adds needless noise; too low cuts off big balances and biases the answer down. The best cap balances the two.

    Consequences for practice:

    • DP is best at statistics over large groups: dashboards, segment averages, histograms. It also works for training (DP-SGD), but costs accuracy, more on rare classes. With 200 confirmed AML cases (see imbalanced data) the noise can swamp the signal.
    • There is no universally safe ε. Ask what a claimed ε covers: one record or one customer, one release or a lifetime.
    • The unit of privacy is the quiet trap. A customer with 4,000 transactions is 4,000 rows. A row-level guarantee says little about the customer. Protecting the customer means limiting each customer's contribution, which means more noise.

    Clean rooms and private set intersection

    A data clean room is a controlled space where parties put data joined on a shared key and can run only approved analyses. Typical rules: no row-level output, a minimum group size, a list of allowed queries, an audit log.

    In most products a clean room is access control and contract, not cryptography: the operator, or a bug, can in principle see the joined data. Ask who operates it, who can see the unaggregated join, how repeated queries are limited (otherwise an analyst differences two queries to isolate a person), and which legal entity is the data controller.

    Private set intersection is the cryptographic cousin: two parties learn which identifiers they share and nothing about the rest. Bank uses: shared watch-lists and checking whether an account on a mule list appears at a counterparty.

    Homomorphic encryption and secure aggregation

    Homomorphic encryption (HE) lets a server compute on data it cannot read and return an encrypted result only the owner can open. Think of a locked glovebox: the server works the gloves without seeing the contents. The catch is cost: ciphertexts are far larger and computation is orders of magnitude slower than plaintext. Training large networks or running an LLM under encryption is out today. What works:

    • Private lookups: check a value against a large database without revealing the query.
    • Sums and simple scores on encrypted values from many parties.
    • Small, high-value joins where data genuinely cannot leave its owner.

    A simpler relative does most of the practical work: secret sharing. Each party splits its number into random-looking shares and hands one to each other party. Any incomplete set is pure randomness, yet adding everyone's shares gives the exact total. This is secure aggregation, used to combine federated updates. Simulation 3 runs it.

    Federated learning across group entities

    In federated learning (FL) the model goes to the data, not the data to the model. Each round, a server sends the current model to the entities, each trains locally for a few steps and returns the change, and the server averages the changes. Repeat.

    Cross-silo FL, a few institutions with big datasets, is the bank-group case. Its main difficulty is non-IID data: different countries have different customers, products and fraud patterns, so each entity's local optimum differs from the global one. Think of three chefs each improving a shared recipe for their own diners: after several tastings alone, each pulls the recipe their way, and the average suits nobody. Simulation 4 shows this with the simplest model.

    The key point: federated learning is not a privacy guarantee. Updates are functions of the training data. Attacks can reconstruct inputs from a shared gradient, and membership inference can detect whether a record was in training (see memorisation and private training). A serious design stacks the tools: secure aggregation so the server sees only the sum, differential privacy on the updates so the sum leaks little.

    Cross-border federation also needs boring agreements first: one label definition, one feature schema, and an owner for the model. Most failed federated projects fail here, not in the maths.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are claims that go further than the tool does. Each has a quick check.

    • "It is differentially private" with no ε

      DP comes in degrees. Without ε, the unit of privacy and the number of releases, the phrase means nothing.

      Spot itAsk: what is ε, what does one unit protect (row, customer, account), how many releases share the budget?
    • Budget leakage through repeated queries

      Analysts re-run a query with fresh noise and average it away, or an unaccounted dashboard refreshes daily.

      Spot itDoes the system track cumulative ε per dataset?
    • Small cells in a clean room

      "Customers over 60 in this postcode with a loan" returns 3 people. Two queries differing by one filter isolate someone.

      Spot itCheck minimum thresholds, query logging and differencing defences.
    • "The data never leaves, so it is anonymous"

      Federated updates are functions of the data and can be inverted.

      Spot itWho sees individual updates? Are secure aggregation and noise applied?
    • Non-IID drift ignored

      The federated model scores well on the largest entity, badly on the smallest, or loses to each entity's local model.

      Spot itDemand per-entity results against a local-only baseline, not one pooled number.
    • Different labels under one name

      "Fraud" means a dispute in one entity and a closed investigation in another, so the model averages two definitions.

      Spot itWrite each entity's label definition side by side before training.
    • Encryption oversold

      A proof of concept on 1,000 rows is called production-ready. At scale, latency or cost is hundreds of times worse.

      Spot itAsk for throughput on production-sized data.
    • Collusion and the total itself

      Secure aggregation hides individual inputs, not what the total reveals. With three entities, two can solve for the third.

      Spot itHow many participants? Is the aggregate released with noise?
    • Privacy technology treated as a legal basis

      A team assumes noise or encryption removes the need for a lawful basis or a regulator conversation.

      Spot itHas legal reviewed the design?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You halve ε from 1 to 0.5 on a count. What happens to the noise, and to what an attacker can learn?

    The typical error doubles (roughly 1 count to 2), and the attacker's evidence from one answer shrinks. Stronger privacy always costs accuracy.

    2. Your quarterly budget is ε = 1 and the team wants 25 equally important dashboard counts. What happens to each count's error?

    Each gets a 25th of the budget, so each has about 25 times the noise of a single count. Cells under a few hundred become unusable. Options: fewer queries, a smaller set of important ones, or a larger budget with eyes open.

    3. You release the mean balance of 10,000 customers. Why not set the clip at AED 100 million to avoid clipping anyone?

    The noise scales with the clip, so a huge clip adds far more noise than the bias it removes, just to protect a few rich customers' exact values. Pick the clip where bias and noise balance.

    4. A vendor says its clean room is "privacy-safe". What four questions do you ask?

    Who operates it, and can they see the joined rows? Is there confidential-computing attestation or just access control? What minimum group size and query limits stop differencing attacks? Who is the data controller for the output, and what does the audit log record?

    5. In Simulation 3, if B and C collude, what do they learn about A, and what would reduce it?

    They learn A exactly, by subtracting their own figures from the total. Secure aggregation hides inputs, not what the total implies. Remedies: more participants, DP noise on the total, or no totals for very small groups.

    6. Your federated fraud model, after many local steps per round, does worse than centralised training. Give two causes and two fixes.

    Causes: non-IID data causing client drift, and too many local steps. Fixes: fewer local steps per round (more communication), aligned labels and features, personalised heads per entity. Always compare against each entity's local-only model.

    7. Why is "the raw data never leaves the entity" insufficient as a privacy claim for federated learning?

    Updates are computed from the data and can be inverted or used for membership inference. You need secure aggregation (server sees only the sum), DP noise (the sum leaks little), and a threat model for each participant and the coordinator.
