    Privacy-Enhancing Technologies: What Is Practical Today

    Differential privacy, data clean rooms, homomorphic encryption and federated learning: what each one actually protects, what it costs in accuracy and compute, and which of them a Gulf banking group can use this year rather than in a research paper.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceThese tools each protect a different thing (the output, the join, the computation, the location of the data), none of them is a switch that makes data "anonymous", and the practical ones today are differential privacy on aggregates, clean rooms for controlled joins, secure aggregation and federated learning for training across entities, with homomorphic encryption limited to small, narrow computations.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank group operates in the UAE, Saudi Arabia and two smaller markets. A mule-account ring moves money through all of them, and each subsidiary sees only a slice. The group model would be far better seeing the whole, but customer data is personal data, each country restricts moving it, and legal teams will not send raw tables to a central lake.

    Four tool families answer four different questions. Most meeting confusion comes from treating them as one thing.

    QuestionToolPlain-language idea
    Can the answer I publish give away a person?Differential privacyAdd calibrated random noise so the answer would have been almost the same with or without any one person.
    Can two parties join their data without seeing each other's?Clean rooms (and private set intersection)A controlled space where only approved, aggregated results leave.
    Can someone compute on data they cannot read?Homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computationMathematics that lets a server add or multiply numbers it cannot see.
    Can I train a model without moving the data?Federated learningSend the model to the data, send back only updates, and average them.

    Each leaves a gap. DP protects the output, not the raw data where it is computed. A clean room is largely governance. Encryption is slow. Federated updates can leak. The skill is knowing which gap you can live with. The simulations below make four of them concrete.

    Hold onto thisEvery privacy tool buys protection by spending something: accuracy (noise), money and time (encryption), simplicity (federation) or governance effort (clean rooms). Ask what is protected, from whom, and at what cost. If a vendor cannot say, the tool is not yet understood.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Differential privacy: the promise and the noise

    Think of a fogged window: you can see the crowd's shape, not any one face. Formally (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith, 2006), DP is a property of a procedure, not a dataset. A randomised procedure M is ε-differentially private if, for any datasets D and D′ differing in one person's records, and any output set S:

    P[M(D)∈S]≤eε·P[M(D′)∈S]

    Whatever the output, it was at most eε times more likely with you in the data than without. ε = 0.1 gives 1.1: essentially no information. ε = 10 gives 22,000: essentially no protection. Small ε means strong privacy and noisy answers. The variant (ε, δ)-DP lets the guarantee fail with tiny probability δ, which allows Gaussian noise.

    The simplest mechanism is the one in Simulation 1. If a query changes by at most Δ when one person is added or removed (its sensitivity), add Laplace noise with scale Δ/ε. A count has Δ = 1, so typical error is 1/ε counts whatever the dataset size. That is the economics of DP: noise is fixed in absolute terms, so big groups lose almost nothing and small groups lose everything. An error of 10 on a 1,200-customer segment is under 1%. On a cell of 15 customers it makes the answer useless, and that is the cell you most want to protect.

    DP beats older "anonymisation" in two ways. It holds against an attacker with any outside knowledge, and it has an arithmetic for adding up the privacy cost of several releases. It exists because exact aggregates leak: Dinur and Nissim (2003) showed enough of them let an attacker reconstruct individual records, and the US Census Bureau found its 2010 tables could be partly reconstructed, so it moved to DP for 2020 (Abowd, 2018).

    Budgets, clipping and the cost of accuracy

    Privacy loss accumulates, like a spending allowance. Answer k queries with εq each and total loss is at most k·εq (basic composition; better accounting exists). So an organisation sets a privacy budget, say ε = 1 per quarter, and every query spends part of it. Ten queries get ε = 0.1 each, so ten times the noise. When the budget is gone, the data is closed. This is painful, so most real deployments release one fixed set of statistics instead of open-ended querying.

    Averages and sums add a problem: one customer with AED 80 million can swing a mean balance, so sensitivity is unbounded. The fix is to clip: cap each value at C. Then one person moves the mean by at most C/n, and noise scale is C/(n·ε). C too high adds needless noise; too low cuts off high balances and biases the answer down. The best C balances the two.

    Consequences for practice:

    • DP is best at statistics over large groups: dashboards, segment averages, histograms.
    • It works for training too as DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016): clip each example's gradient and add Gaussian noise every step. This costs accuracy, more on rare classes. With 200 confirmed AML cases (see imbalanced data) the noise for a sensible ε can swamp the signal.
    • There is no universally safe ε. Roughly, ε near 1 is strong and above 10 largely symbolic, though deployed systems have used larger values. NIST's 2025 guidance (SP 800-226) asks what a claimed ε covers: one record or one customer, one release or a lifetime.
    • The unit of privacy is the quiet trap. A customer with 4,000 transactions is 4,000 rows. A row-level guarantee says little about the customer. Protecting the customer means clipping each customer's contribution, which means more noise.

    Clean rooms and private set intersection

    A data clean room is a controlled space where parties put data joined on a shared key (a hashed ID) and can run only approved analyses. Typical rules: no row-level output, a minimum group size (say, results under 50 people suppressed), a list of allowed queries, an audit log. Example: a bank and a retailer measure how many card customers who saw an offer later spent at the merchant. Cloud and warehouse vendors ship these as products, some adding DP to outputs.

    In most products a clean room is access control and contract, not cryptography: the operator, or a bug, can in principle see the joined data. Stronger variants run in confidential-computing hardware (trusted execution environments), shifting trust to the chip vendor and attestation. Ask: who operates it, who can see the unaggregated join, how are repeated queries limited (otherwise an analyst differences two queries to isolate a person), and which legal entity is the data controller.

    Private set intersection (PSI) is the cryptographic cousin: two parties learn which identifiers they share and nothing about the rest. Google's Private Join and Compute (Ion et al., 2020) also computes a sum over the overlap. Bank uses: shared watch-lists, sanctions screening across groups, checking whether an account on a mule list appears at a counterparty, learning only the overlap or a count.

    Homomorphic encryption and secure aggregation

    Homomorphic encryption (HE) lets a server compute on ciphertexts and return an encrypted result only the data owner can open. Think of a locked glovebox: the server works the gloves inside without seeing or holding the contents. Textbook RSA (multiplicative) and Paillier (additive) are early examples; fully homomorphic encryption (Gentry, 2009) allows arbitrary computation. Families: BFV and BGV (exact integers), CKKS (approximate real numbers, the one ML uses), TFHE (fast bit operations).

    The cost is the catch. Ciphertexts are thousands of times larger, operations far slower than plaintext, and noise grows with each multiplication, limiting depth unless costly "bootstrapping" is used. Reported overheads for general computation are often three or more orders of magnitude, and depend on workload. So training large networks or running an LLM under encryption is out today. What works:

    • Private lookups. Check a value against a large database without revealing the query. Apple uses this for caller identification and photo landmark lookups, and open-sourced its library in 2024.
    • Sums and simple scores. Add encrypted values from many parties, or score with a linear or small-tree model on encrypted features.
    • Small, high-value joins where data genuinely cannot leave its owner.

    A simpler relative does most of the practical work in banking: secret sharing, the basis of secure multi-party computation. Each party splits its number into random-looking shares that sum back to the secret and hands one to each other party. Any incomplete set is uniformly random and reveals nothing, yet adding everyone's shares gives the exact total. This is secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., 2017), used to combine federated updates. Simulation 3 runs it.

    Federated learning across group entities

    In federated learning (FL) the model goes to the data, not the data to the model. Each round, a central server sends the current weights to the entities. Each trains locally for a few steps and returns the change. The server averages them, weighted by data size, and repeats. This is FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017):

    wt+1=∑knknwkt+1

    wk is entity k's weights after local training from the shared wt; nk is its example count. With one gradient step each, this equals ordinary pooled training. Trouble starts with several local steps on different data.

    Two flavours. Cross-device FL has millions of phones (Gboard was the first large deployment). Cross-silo FL has a few institutions with big datasets and stable connections: the bank-group case. Its main difficulty is non-IID data: different countries have different customers, products, label definitions and fraud typologies, so each entity's local optimum differs from the global one. Think of three chefs each improving a shared recipe for their own diners: after several tastings alone, each pulls the recipe their way, and the average suits nobody. With enough local steps the average even lands away from where pooled training would end. Simulation 4 shows this with the simplest model.

    The key point: federated learning is not a privacy guarantee. Updates are functions of the training data. Gradient-inversion attacks (Zhu et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2020) can reconstruct images or text from one shared gradient, and membership inference can detect whether a record was in training (see memorisation and private training). A serious design stacks the tools: secure aggregation so the server sees only the sum, differential privacy on the updates so the sum leaks little, sometimes confidential computing for the server. Each layer costs accuracy or effort. Whether an update counts as personal data across borders is a legal question; ask legal and the regulator.

    Cross-border federation also needs boring agreements first: one label definition ("confirmed fraud" may mean a customer dispute in one country and a bank investigation in another), one feature schema, one validation process, and an owner for the model. Most failed federated projects fail here, not in the maths.

    # One round of FedAvg, cross-silo. Everything below runs inside each entity
# except the last line, which runs on the coordinator.
def local_update(global_w, entity_data, steps, lr):
    w = global_w.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        w -= lr * gradient(w, entity_data)       # raw data never leaves
    return w - global_w, len(entity_data)        # send the change and a count

updates = [local_update(w, d, steps=5, lr=0.1) for d in entity_datasets]
w = w + sum(n * delta for delta, n in updates) / sum(n for _, n in updates)
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are claims that go further than the tool does. Each has a quick check.

    • "It is differentially private" with no ε

      DP comes in degrees. Without ε, δ, the unit of privacy and the number of releases, the phrase means nothing.

      Spot itAsk: what is ε, what does one unit protect (row, customer, account), how many releases share the budget?
    • An ε so large it is symbolic

      ε = 50 gives a bound of e50: no practical protection.

      Spot itCompute eε. If it has more than six digits, ask what attack the guarantee blocks.
    • Budget leakage through repeated queries

      Analysts re-run a query with fresh noise and average it away, or an unaccounted dashboard refreshes daily.

      Spot itDoes the system track cumulative ε per dataset, or regenerate noise on each call?
    • Small cells in a clean room

      "Customers over 60 in this postcode with a loan" returns 3 people. Two queries differing by one filter isolate someone.

      Spot itCheck minimum thresholds, query logging and differencing defences.
    • "The data never leaves, so it is anonymous"

      Federated updates are functions of the data and can be inverted or used for membership inference.

      Spot itWho sees individual updates? Are secure aggregation and noise applied? What is the threat model for a curious coordinator?
    • Non-IID drift ignored

      The federated model scores well on the largest entity, badly on the smallest, or loses to each entity's local model.

      Spot itDemand per-entity results against a local-only baseline, not one pooled number. Check local steps.
    • Different labels under one name

      "Fraud" means a customer dispute in one entity and a closed investigation in another, so the model averages two definitions.

      Spot itWrite each entity's label definition and delay side by side before training.
    • Encryption oversold

      A proof of concept on 1,000 rows is called production-ready. At scale, latency or cost is hundreds of times worse.

      Spot itAsk for throughput on production-sized data, ciphertext size and the multiplicative depth needed.
    • Collusion and the total itself

      Secure aggregation hides individual inputs, not what the total reveals. With three entities, two can solve for the third.

      Spot itHow many participants? Is the aggregate released with noise?
    • Privacy technology treated as a legal basis

      A team assumes noise or encryption removes the need for a lawful basis, residency analysis or a regulator conversation.

      Spot itHas legal reviewed the design, and is the regulator's position on the flow recorded, not assumed?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A count query has sensitivity 1 and you release it with ε = 0.5. What is the Laplace scale and typical error? By what factor can one answer change an attacker's odds?

    Scale = 1/ε = 2, so the typical error is 2 counts. Odds move by at most e0.5 ≈ 1.65.

    2. Your quarterly budget is ε = 1 and the team wants 25 equally important dashboard counts. What happens to each count's error?

    Each gets ε = 0.04, so the scale is 25: 25 times the error of a single count at ε = 1. Cells under a few hundred become unusable. Options: fewer queries, a smaller set of important ones, or a larger budget with eyes open.

    3. You release the mean balance of 10,000 customers with clip C = AED 200,000 and ε = 1. What is the noise scale, and why not set C to AED 100 million to avoid clipping anyone?

    Scale = C/(n·ε) = 200,000 ÷ 10,000 = AED 20. At C = 100 million it would be AED 10,000, about 500 times more noise, far more than the bias it removes, just to protect a few rich customers' exact values.

    4. A vendor says its clean room is "privacy-safe". What four questions do you ask?

    Who operates it, and can they see the joined rows? Is there confidential-computing attestation or just access control? What minimum group size and query limits stop differencing attacks? Who is the data controller for the output, and what does the audit log record?

    5. In Simulation 3, entities A, B and C hold 120, 85 and 40. If B and C collude, what do they learn about A, and what would reduce it?

    They learn A exactly: 245 − 85 − 40 = 120. Secure aggregation hides inputs, not what the total implies. Remedies: more participants, DP noise on the total, or no totals for very small groups.

    6. Your federated fraud model, after many local steps per round, has a worse pooled error than centralised training. Give two causes and two fixes.

    Causes: non-IID data causing client drift, and too many local steps. Fixes: fewer local steps per round (more communication), a smaller learning rate or drift-correcting method, aligned labels and features, personalised heads per entity. Always compare against each entity's local-only model.

    7. Why is "the raw data never leaves the entity" insufficient as a privacy claim for federated learning?

    Updates are computed from the data. Gradient inversion has reconstructed inputs from them, and membership inference can detect records. You need secure aggregation (server sees only the sum), DP noise (the sum leaks little), and a threat model for each participant and the coordinator.
