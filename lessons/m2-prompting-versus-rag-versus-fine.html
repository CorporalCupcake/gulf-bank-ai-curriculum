    Prompting, RAG or Fine-Tuning?

    Part 1

    Three ways to adapt a model

    In one sentenceYou can change what a language model does in three ways: prompting (brief it before each call), RAG (let it look things up) or fine-tuning (retrain it). Pick by asking whether it lacks information or a behaviour.

    Picture a new hire at a bank's contact centre answering customer questions. She can be briefed before each call, look things up in the policy library mid-call, or take a six-week training course. Those are the three levers.

    How they differ

    • Prompting is cheap, instant and reversible. It goes further than people expect.
    • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) gives current facts and can cite the page they came from.
    • Fine-tuning builds habits and style. It is poor at memorising a manual that changes every quarter.
    • Long context means pasting the whole library in every time. It needs no pipeline but costs a lot per question.

    So "fine-tune the model on our documents" is the classic confusion. The model absorbs facts patchily, cannot say where they came from, and is out of date the day a rate card changes. Use retrieval for facts. Use fine-tuning when you want the model to do something differently every time: return strict JSON, write in the bank's Arabic register, or sort complaints into your 40 internal categories.

    Part 2

    How to choose: the ladder

    Try the cheap test first and climb only when measured results demand it, like a doctor ordering tests. Our running example is a customer-support assistant for a bank.

    1. Write an evaluation set first. 50 to 200 real cases with agreed good answers. Without it, every comparison is an opinion.
    2. Prompt well. Clear instructions, a few examples, the output format spelled out.
    3. Add retrieval if the failures are missing or stale facts. Fix search first: most RAG failures are retrieval failures.
    4. Try a bigger or better model. A newer model often removes the failure.
    5. Fine-tune if what remains is behaviour, format or cost at volume, and you have labelled examples.

    The mature pattern combines all three: a tuned small model, retrieval for facts and a good prompt.

    How to read the cost

    Think of a taxi versus a leased car. Prompting and RAG pay per call. Fine-tuning pays up front plus fixed hosting, then little per call. So the best choice depends on volume.

    • Long context is the expensive one. Pasting a 400,000-token policy library into every call sends about 270 times more input than a 1,500-token prompt. It only works at low volume, or with caching, which bills a repeated prefix at a fraction of the price.
    • At low volume, cost is trivial next to engineering time, so choose for quality.
    • At very high volume on a narrow task, a small fine-tuned model wins, because each call is cheap.
    • Prices move often. Treat any crossover point as a snapshot and check current prices.
    Part 3

    Why retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts

    Memorising a few hundred practice answers does not make you know the subject. That is fine-tuning on facts. Research (Ovadia et al., 2023) found retrieval beat fine-tuning for adding knowledge. Training on facts the model did not already know can even make it more likely to hallucinate (Gekhman et al., 2024).

    • No citation. A tuned model cannot tell you which page a fact came from.
    • No removal. You cannot delete a withdrawn product from its weights.
    • No permissions. You cannot restrict who sees what. In a bank, all three are requirements.

    The strongest hybrid fine-tunes the skill of using retrieved text: answer only from the passages, ignore distractors, say "not in the documents" (RAFT, Zhang et al., 2024). Facts still come from retrieval. Training teaches reading habits.

    Part 4

    What fine-tuning does, and LoRA

    Supervised fine-tuning is practice with an answer key. You train on pairs of (input, ideal output) and the model's weights shift so the ideal output becomes more likely.

    • Quality beats quantity. A few hundred to a few thousand clean, consistent examples often suffice for format and style. Noisy labels teach the noise.
    • Hold out test examples from the start. They are the only honest measure.
    • Three costs: forgetting (narrow tuning can weaken general ability), lock-in (the tuning belongs to one base model, and you retrain when it is retired) and governance (customer data in training is still customer data, and the model can leak it).

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation) makes tuning cheap. Full fine-tuning updates every weight: for a 7-billion-parameter model, roughly 100 GB of GPU memory and a new 13 GB copy per task. LoRA rests on one observation: the change a task needs is usually simple. So it freezes the original weights and trains a tiny add-on that captures the change.

    Picture a mixing desk with millions of dials. Full fine-tuning turns every dial. LoRA moves a few master faders, each shifting many dials at once. The rank is the number of faders: higher means more capacity and a bigger add-on, still a small fraction of the model.

    • Small files. An adapter is tens of megabytes, and one base model can serve many adapters. Merging one in adds no delay.
    • Less memory. QLoRA holds the frozen base in 4-bit numbers, so a very large model fits on one GPU.
    • Not free. On hard domains like code and maths, a low-rank adapter can lag full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024), though it forgets less.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    LeverWhat changesStrong atWeak at
    PromptingThe contextTone, format, a few examples; minutes to tryLong prompts cost tokens on every call
    RAGThe context, filled from your dataBig or changing knowledge, citations, permissionsNeeds a search system; only as good as retrieval
    Fine-tuningThe weightsStyle, strict format, narrow tasks at volumePoor at facts; goes stale; needs labelled data
    Long contextThe context, very largeOne-off analysis of big documentsExpensive at volume; buried facts get missed

    In practice:

    • Rate card changes monthly and answers must cite it: RAG.
    • 3 million emails a month into 40 categories, with 20,000 labelled examples: fine-tune a small model, once a prompted baseline falls short.
    • Small, stable knowledge: prompting alone.
    Show the LoRA size formula

    Adapter size = rank × (inputs + outputs) for each weight matrix you adapt. A rank of 8 on a 7-billion-parameter model is about 20 million trainable numbers, roughly 0.3% of the model.

    In banking, retrieval is usually the right answer for policy and product questions, because it can cite the source, drop withdrawn documents and respect who may see what.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Fine-tuning to add knowledge

      "We trained it on our manuals." It answers fluently and wrongly about a rate it half-remembers. Ask about something that changed last month and ask for the source.

    • Jumping to fine-tuning before a baseline

      Weeks of training when a better prompt would have fixed it. No prompted baseline, no case.

    • Evaluating on training data

      The tuned model scores 96% because the examples were in its training set. Check how the test set was held out.

    • Blaming the model for a retrieval failure

      "It hallucinates" often means the right passage was never retrieved. Log the passages: if the answer is not there, fix search.

    1. Is the model missing information or a behaviour? Which lever for each?

    Information: show it, through prompting or retrieval. Behaviour: instruct it first, and fine-tune only if instructions fail.

    2. Why not fine-tune on the rate card?

    It goes stale when rates change and cannot cite its source. Retrieval does both.

    3. Why can LoRA train on under 1% of a model's numbers and still change its behaviour?

    The change a task needs is usually simple, so a few well-chosen directions capture most of it. The original weights stay frozen.

    4. A colleague says a fine-tune scored 94% against 78% for the prompt. What do you check?

    That the test set was held out, that the baseline prompt was properly engineered, how many test cases there were, and that general and safety behaviour did not degrade.

    5. When does a fixed-cost tuned model beat a per-call prompted one?

    When monthly per-call cost exceeds fixed hosting plus training spread over time. Work both out with your real token counts and current prices.
