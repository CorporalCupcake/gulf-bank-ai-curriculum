    M2.3 · M2.4 Day one ★ About 42 minutes

    Prompting, RAG or Fine-Tuning? Choosing How to Adapt a Model, and Doing It Cheaply with LoRA

    What each lever changes inside the system, why "teach it our documents" is usually the wrong brief, how the costs cross over, and what low-rank adapters really do when you do need to change the model.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePrompting and retrieval change what the model is shown, fine-tuning changes how it behaves; so use retrieval for facts that change or need citing, fine-tuning for style, format and narrow skills at volume, start with the cheapest lever, and let an evaluation set, not enthusiasm, justify moving to the next.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new hire at the bank's contact centre can do three things to get good at answering customers. She can be briefed before each call: here is the customer, here is the script, here are today's rules. She can look things up during the call in the policy library. Or she can go on a six-week training course until the right tone and the right habits are second nature.

    Those are the three levers on a language model. Briefing is prompting. Looking things up is retrieval-augmented generation, RAG: fetch the relevant passages and put them in front of the model. Training is fine-tuning: change the model's weights with examples.

    The mistake that costs banks months is mixing up what each is good for. Training courses are good at habits and style, poor at memorising a 300-page product manual that changes every quarter. A policy library is good for facts and for pointing at the page ("see section 4.2"), but it will not teach her to sound like the bank. The brief is cheap, instant and reversible, and it goes further than people expect.

    A request such as "fine-tune the model on our documents so it knows our products" is the classic confusion. Fine-tuning on documents is a poor way to inject facts: the model absorbs them patchily, cannot say where they came from, and is out of date the day a rate card changes. Retrieval is the right tool for that job. Fine-tuning is the right tool when you want the model to do something differently, every time, such as always returning a strict JSON record, writing in the bank's Arabic register, or classifying complaints into your 40 internal categories as well as a model ten times its size.

    Hold onto thisAsk two questions about any gap you see. Is the model missing information (then show it, with prompting or retrieval) or missing a behaviour (then instruct it, and only if instructions fail, train it)? Start at the cheapest lever and move up only when a measured evaluation says the cheaper one is not enough.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What each lever actually changes

    A deployed model call is a function: output = model(weights, context). The weights were fixed by pretraining and post-training (M1). The context is the text you send, bounded by the window (context windows). Everything you can do falls into changing one of those two.

    Notice what is not a lever: telling the model "be accurate" or "do not hallucinate". Reliability comes from what you show it, how you check it (M5) and what you do when it is wrong.

    The ladder: start cheap, climb on evidence

    The reliable sequence is a ladder, and you only climb when a failing evaluation tells you to.

    1. Write an evaluation set first: 50 to 200 real questions or cases with agreed good answers. Without it, every comparison below is an opinion.
    2. Prompt well. Clear instructions, a handful of examples, the output format spelled out. Measure. Many "we need fine-tuning" requests end here.
    3. Add retrieval if failures are missing or stale facts. Fix the search before touching the model: most RAG failures are retrieval failures (agentic RAG extends this).
    4. Try a bigger or better model (model choice). A newer model often removes a failure that you were about to fine-tune away.
    5. Fine-tune if the remaining failures are about behaviour, format, or cost at volume, and you have the labelled examples.
    6. Combine. The mature pattern is a fine-tuned small model for the narrow behaviour, retrieval for the facts, and a good prompt tying them together.

    The ladder is cheapest-first, not best-first. There are legitimate reasons to jump: a 5-million-a-month classification task justifies fine-tuning a small model almost from the start, because a large prompted model costs too much per call.

    Where the money crosses over

    Each option has a different cost shape. Prompting and RAG pay per call: tokens in, tokens out. Fine-tuning pays up front and in fixed hosting and then little per call. Long context pays per call, in proportion to the corpus. So the right choice depends on volume.

    For one call, the arithmetic is simple: cost = (input tokens × input price) + (output tokens × output price). Prompting a 1,500-token prompt costs a few fils. Pasting a 400,000-token policy library into every call costs about 270 times more input, which only makes sense at low volume or with caching, where re-reading an already-seen prefix is billed at a fraction of the normal price.

    Three lessons from the shape of those curves. First, at low volume the cost differences are irrelevant next to engineering time, so choose for quality and speed of delivery. Second, at very high volume of a narrow task, a small tuned model wins on cost and also on latency. Third, long context is a tool for small corpora and one-off jobs; as a standing production design for a large library it rarely survives a finance review. Prompt caching narrows the gap and the economics move every few months (model choice, cost and latency goes deeper), so treat any specific crossover figure as a snapshot.

    Why retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts

    The evidence is consistent. Ovadia et al. (2023) compared the two for injecting knowledge and found retrieval outperformed unsupervised fine-tuning, both for facts seen in training and for new ones; models struggled to learn new facts from fine-tuning alone. Gekhman et al. (2024) found something worse: when fine-tuning examples contain facts the model did not already know, the model learns them slowly, and as they are learned it becomes more prone to hallucinate. The model is being trained to produce confident answers about things it has no grounding for.

    The mechanism is intuitive. Pretraining took trillions of tokens to store knowledge. A few thousand examples at a small learning rate nudge behaviour; they do not reliably write facts into the weights. And even if they did, you could not point to the source, remove a withdrawn product, or restrict who sees what. In a regulated bank those are requirements, not niceties: an answer about a fee needs a link to the current schedule, and a document a branch employee may not read must not influence what they are told.

    The strongest hybrid is to fine-tune for the skill of using retrieved context: answering only from the passages, ignoring distractors, saying "not in the documents" when it is not there. Zhang et al. (2024) called this retrieval-augmented fine-tuning (RAFT). The facts still come from retrieval; the training only teaches good reading habits.

    What fine-tuning really does

    Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) continues training on pairs of (input, ideal output). The loss is the same next-token loss as pretraining (loss functions), computed only on the answer tokens. Each step nudges every weight a little in the direction that makes the ideal output more likely. After a few epochs the model produces that style of output without being told to.

    What you get depends heavily on data quality. A few hundred to a few thousand clean, consistent examples are often enough for format and style; noisy, inconsistent labels teach the model the inconsistency. Data quality beats quantity, and the labelling guide you write matters as much as the training run (annotator agreement). Held-out examples, kept from the very start, are your only honest measure of whether it worked, and overfitting (F6) is a live risk with small sets.

    Variants you will hear named: instruction tuning (teach it to follow a general format of tasks), preference tuning such as DPO (teach it which of two answers is better, see RLHF to DPO), and distillation (train a small model to copy a large model's answers on your task, a route to cheap high-volume inference, see quantization, pruning and distillation). Also reinforcement fine-tuning against a verifiable reward, where the correct answer can be checked automatically.

    Three costs that are easy to forget. Forgetting: pushing hard on a narrow task can degrade general ability, so you must evaluate on things you did not train on. Staleness and lock-in: the adapter belongs to one base model; when the vendor retires it, you retrain. Governance: training data from customers is still customer data, and a model trained on it can leak it (memorisation), so the data goes through the same privacy review as any dataset (privacy basics).

    LoRA: changing a model by adding a tiny matrix

    Full fine-tuning updates every weight, which for a 7-billion-parameter model means storing, besides the weights, a gradient and two optimiser statistics for each one: roughly 16 bytes per parameter in common mixed-precision setups, so about 108 GB for a 7B model before counting activations. That needs several expensive GPUs, and each task produces a whole new 13 GB copy of the model.

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation, Hu et al., 2021) rests on one observation: the change that fine-tuning makes to a weight matrix tends to be simple, well described by a low-rank matrix. So freeze the original matrix W and learn the change as the product of two thin matrices:

    W′=W+αr·BA

    W is a frozen dout × din matrix. A is r × din and B is dout × r, where the rank r is small (8, 16, 64). Only A and B are trained: r(din + dout) numbers instead of din × dout. For a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix at r = 8, that is 65,536 numbers instead of 16.8 million, 0.4%. α is a scaling constant. B starts at zero so the model begins exactly as it was.

    Think of W as a large mixing desk with 16 million dials. Full fine-tuning lets you turn every dial. LoRA says the adjustment you want can be made by moving a few master faders (the rank r), each of which moves many dials together in a fixed pattern. If the change you need really is that simple, you lose almost nothing.

    Practical consequences that matter in a meeting:

    • Small artefacts. An adapter is tens of megabytes. One base model can serve many adapters (one per business line or language), swapped per request. Merging BA into W afterwards removes any inference overhead.
    • Memory. No gradients or optimiser state for the frozen weights. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) goes further: hold the frozen base in 4-bit, train adapters in higher precision, and a 65B model fits on a single 48 GB GPU. Quantization is covered in P3.
    • Which matrices. The original paper adapted only attention projections. Recent evidence says adapting the MLP layers matters more; Thinking Machines (2025) found MLP-only LoRA matched LoRA on all layers while attention-only did clearly worse.
    • It is not free. "LoRA learns less and forgets less" (Biderman et al., 2024): on hard domains such as code and maths, LoRA lagged full fine-tuning at low rank, though it preserved general ability better.

    Other parameter-efficient methods are variants on the same theme: adapters (small bottleneck layers inserted between existing ones, Houlsby et al., 2019), prefix and prompt tuning (learn a few "virtual tokens" prepended to the input), and refinements such as DoRA, which splits the update into magnitude and direction. They matter less than the question that precedes them: do you need to change the weights at all?

    Part 3

    How it connects

    M2 · Prompting and context engineeringThe first rung

    Everything on the ladder begins here. A well-built prompt with a few examples removes a surprising share of "we need fine-tuning" requests.

    M2 · Model choice, cost and latencyThe other cost lever

    Swapping to a larger or newer model, or a smaller routed one, is often cheaper than fine-tuning and belongs on the ladder before it.

    M3 · RetrievalWhat RAG is made of

    RAG is a search system. Chunking, query rewriting and parsing PDFs decide most of its quality.

    M1 · Pretraining, post-training, context windowsWhere the weights come from

    Fine-tuning is a small continuation of post-training. Knowing the scale of pretraining explains why a few thousand examples cannot rewrite what the model knows.

    F6 · Loss and overfittingSame training, smaller scale

    Fine-tuning is gradient descent on next-token loss. Held-out data, early stopping and learning rates matter exactly as they did in F6.

    P3 · Quantization and distillationMaking the tuned model cheap

    QLoRA relies on 4-bit quantization; distilling a large model into a small tuned one is the main route to cheap high-volume inference.

    M5 · EvaluationThe only referee

    Every move up the ladder must be justified by a measured gain on a fixed evaluation set. See also groundedness checks.

    P2 · Prompt versioning and tracingOperating the choice

    Prompts, retrieval indices and adapters are all versioned artefacts. Trace which version produced which answer.

    A14 · Arabic LLMsWhen the base model is weak in your language

    For Gulf Arabic dialects, fine-tuning (or choosing a stronger Arabic base model) is a more realistic lever than for English.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Staff policy and product assistants (internal copilots). Facts change, answers need citations, access depends on role. This is RAG territory; fine-tuning, if used at all, shapes tone and refusal behaviour.
    • Complaint and email triage in Arabic and English. A closed set of categories, millions of items and thousands of labelled examples: the textbook case for fine-tuning a small model, perhaps distilled from a large one.
    • Structured extraction from trade documents and statements (document AI). Start with prompting and a schema; fine-tune if the volume and error pattern justify it.
    • Credit memo drafting. Retrieval of the customer's file plus prompting for the house template. Fine-tuning for the bank's writing style is possible but needs careful validation (LLMs for credit memos).
    • Data residency and sovereignty. Fine-tuning an open-weights model on-premises or in a sovereign cloud can be a requirement rather than a preference (hybrid deployment, sovereign AI). Check CBUAE or SAMA rules on outsourcing and model governance with compliance; I will not state specifics here (A12).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The big debates have moved. "RAG versus fine-tuning" is no longer the question; the live ones are how far long contexts and caching have eaten into RAG's territory, how good LoRA is compared with full tuning, and whether hosted fine-tuning is worth it given how fast base models improve.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Fine-tuning to add knowledge

      "We trained it on our manuals." It answers fluently and wrongly about a rate it half-remembers, with no source.

      Spot itAsk a question whose answer changed last month. Ask for the source of an answer. If it cannot do either, the facts belong in retrieval.
    • Jumping to fine-tuning before a baseline

      Weeks spent tuning when a better prompt or a newer model would have fixed it.

      Spot itAsk for the score of the best prompted baseline on the same evaluation set. No baseline, no case.
    • Evaluating on training data

      The tuned model scores 96%. The examples were in its training set.

      Spot itAsk how the test set was held out, and whether near-duplicates (the same complaint templated many times) cross the split.
    • Retrieval blamed on the model

      "The model hallucinates." Really, the right passage was never retrieved.

      Spot itLog the retrieved passages. If the answer is not in them, fix search. If it is in them and the model ignored it, that is a model or prompt problem.
    • Catastrophic forgetting and narrow gains

      Excellent on the target task, worse at everything else, including safety behaviour.

      Spot itRun a general and a safety regression set before and after tuning.
    • Stale adapters on replaced base models

      The vendor retires the model; the adapter cannot be moved; the data pipeline to retrain does not exist.

      Spot itAsk whether training data, config and evaluation are versioned and can be rerun on a new base within days.
    • Long context everywhere

      Pasting whole libraries into each call. The prototype is lovely; the invoice is not, and answers about mid-document facts quietly degrade.

      Spot itCompute tokens per call times calls per month. Test questions whose answers sit in the middle of the context.
    • Sensitive data in training sets

      Customer names, account numbers or private conversations used for tuning without review.

      Spot itAsk for the data protection assessment and the redaction method. Probe the tuned model for verbatim recall of training records.
    • Believing LoRA is always equal to full tuning

      A rank-8 adapter on a large, hard dataset underperforms and nobody checks.

      Spot itCompare ranks on the evaluation set; if the score keeps rising with rank, capacity is the limit.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There are three ways to make the AI useful for our business. Give it clear instructions, let it look things up in our own documents, or retrain it. For answering questions about products and policy, looking things up is better, because the answers are current and we can show the source. Retraining makes sense for a narrow, high-volume job like sorting complaints, where a small tuned model is cheaper and more consistent. We try the cheap options first and only move up when a test on real cases shows we must.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd define a fixed evaluation set first, then climb: a strong prompt with examples, then retrieval if the failures are factual, a stronger base model, and only then fine-tuning for behaviour or unit cost. Facts go through retrieval because fine-tuning injects them poorly, can't cite, and goes stale. If we tune, I'd use LoRA or QLoRA on MLP and attention layers, compare ranks, keep a held-out set and a general regression set, and version data, adapter and base model together.”
    “Can we fine-tune the model on our product documents so it knows them?”

    We can, but it is the weaker route. Fine-tuning teaches style and behaviour; facts are absorbed unreliably, cannot be cited and age quickly. Studies find retrieval beats fine-tuning for new knowledge, and fine-tuning on facts the model doesn't know can increase hallucination. I'd build retrieval with citations, and consider fine-tuning only to improve how it answers from the retrieved text.

    “Why not just put everything in the prompt now that context windows are huge?”

    For a small corpus or one-off analysis, we should. At volume, cost grows with corpus size on every call, caching only partly helps, accuracy on mid-context facts is weaker, and we can't restrict by user permission. Past a few hundred pages, retrieval is cheaper and more controllable. We should measure both on our own questions.

    “What is LoRA and why does it matter?”

    It freezes the model and trains two thin matrices per layer whose product is the weight update. That is under 1% of the parameters, so tuning fits on one GPU, adapters are tens of megabytes, and we can serve many adapters over one base. Recent work shows it matches full tuning on small-to-medium datasets and falls behind when the data is large or the task hard.

    “When would you choose fine-tuning over RAG?”

    When the gap is behaviour, not information: strict output formats, a consistent voice, narrow classification or extraction at high volume, or a dialect the base model handles poorly. Also when a small tuned model can replace a large prompted one at far lower cost per call. And often both: tuned for behaviour, retrieval for facts.

    “How do we know the fine-tune actually helped?”

    Compare it, on a held-out evaluation set that was fixed beforehand, against the best prompted baseline, with intervals, and check that general and safety behaviour did not regress. Then run it in shadow mode on live traffic before it replaces anything, and compare cost and latency, not just accuracy.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A rate card changes every month and agents must quote it with the document name. Which lever, and why not the others?

    Retrieval. Facts change monthly, so fine-tuning would be stale and cannot cite. Prompting alone does not scale beyond a few pages. Long context could work if the library is small, but permissions and cost at volume argue for retrieval.

    2. The bank sorts 3 million customer emails a month into 40 categories and has 20,000 labelled examples. Which lever, and why?

    Fine-tune a small model (after confirming a prompted baseline is not good enough). The task is narrow, high-volume and well labelled, so a tuned small model gives lower cost, lower latency and more consistent output than prompting a large one on every email.

    3. A 4,096 × 11,008 matrix gets a LoRA adapter of rank 16. How many trainable numbers, and what share of the matrix?

    16 × (4,096 + 11,008) = 16 × 15,104 = 241,664. The full matrix has 45,088,768 entries, so the adapter is about 0.54%.

    4. Why can a LoRA adapter be served with no extra latency?

    After training, the product BA (scaled) has the same shape as W, so it can be added into W once. The merged model is the same size and speed as the original. Alternatively, keep adapters separate to swap them per request, at a small cost.

    5. A colleague says a fine-tune scored 94% against 78% for the prompt baseline. What do you check before believing it?

    That the test set was held out from training and has no near-duplicates; that the baseline prompt was properly engineered, not a weak first try; the number of test cases and an interval; and that general and safety behaviour did not degrade.

    6. At about what volume does a fixed-cost tuned model beat a per-call prompted model? How would you find out for your case?

    When monthly per-call cost (tokens × price × calls) exceeds the fixed hosting plus amortised training cost. Compute both for your actual token counts and a current price list; the crossover moves with prices, prompt length and model size. Remember the tuned model must also match quality on your evaluation set.

    7. What does RAFT add to plain RAG, and does it put facts in the weights?

    It fine-tunes the model on examples that include retrieved documents, some irrelevant, so it learns to answer from the relevant ones and ignore distractors. Facts still come from retrieval at query time; the weights learn reading habits, not the content.
