    M2.3 · M2.4 Day one ★ About 42 minutes

    Prompting, RAG or Fine-Tuning? Choosing How to Adapt a Model, and Doing It Cheaply with LoRA

    What each lever changes inside the system, why "teach it our documents" is usually the wrong brief, how the costs cross over, and what low-rank adapters really do when you do need to change the model.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePrompting and retrieval change what the model is shown, fine-tuning changes how it behaves; so use retrieval for facts that change or need citing, fine-tuning for style, format and narrow skills at volume, start with the cheapest lever, and let an evaluation set, not enthusiasm, justify moving to the next.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new hire at the bank's contact centre has three ways to get good at answering customers. She can be briefed before each call (the customer, the script, today's rules). She can look things up in the policy library mid-call. Or she can take a six-week training course until the right tone is habit.

    These are the three levers on a language model. Prompting is the brief. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the library: fetch the relevant passages and put them in front of the model. Fine-tuning is the course: change the model's weights using examples.

    Each has a different strength. The course builds habits and style but is poor at memorising a 300-page manual that changes every quarter. The library gives current facts and can point at the page ("see section 4.2"), but will not teach her to sound like the bank. The brief is cheap, instant and reversible, and goes further than people expect.

    So "fine-tune the model on our documents so it knows our products" is the classic confusion. The model absorbs the facts patchily, cannot say where they came from, and is out of date the day a rate card changes. Use retrieval for that. Use fine-tuning when you want the model to do something differently every time: return strict JSON, write in the bank's Arabic register, or sort complaints into your 40 internal categories as well as a model ten times its size.

    Hold onto thisAsk whether the model is missing information (show it, with prompting or retrieval) or a behaviour (instruct it; train it only if instructions fail). Start at the cheapest lever and move up only when a measured evaluation says it is not enough.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What each lever actually changes

    A model call is a function: output = model(weights, context). The weights are fixed by pretraining and post-training (M1). The context is the text you send, bounded by the window (context windows). Every lever changes one of the two.

    "Be accurate" and "do not hallucinate" are not levers. Reliability comes from what you show the model, how you check it (M5) and what you do when it is wrong.

    The ladder: start cheap, climb on evidence

    Think of a doctor: try the cheap test first, and escalate only when results demand it. Here the results are a failing evaluation.

    1. Write an evaluation set first: 50 to 200 real cases with agreed good answers. Without it, every comparison is an opinion.
    2. Prompt well. Clear instructions, a few examples, the output format spelled out. Many "we need fine-tuning" requests end here.
    3. Add retrieval if failures are missing or stale facts. Fix search before touching the model: most RAG failures are retrieval failures (agentic RAG extends this).
    4. Try a bigger or better model (model choice). A newer model often removes the failure you were about to tune away.
    5. Fine-tune if what remains is behaviour, format or cost at volume, and you have labelled examples.
    6. Combine. The mature pattern: a tuned small model for the narrow behaviour, retrieval for facts, a good prompt tying them together.

    The ladder is cheapest-first, not best-first. Jumping is fine when the numbers say so: a 5-million-a-month classification task justifies a tuned small model almost from the start.

    Where the money crosses over

    Think of a taxi versus a leased car. Prompting and RAG pay per call (tokens in, tokens out). Fine-tuning pays up front plus fixed hosting, then little per call. Long context pays per call in proportion to the corpus. So the best choice depends on volume.

    One call costs (input tokens × input price) + (output tokens × output price). A 1,500-token prompt costs a few fils. Pasting a 400,000-token policy library into every call costs about 270 times more input. That only works at low volume or with caching, which bills a re-read prefix at a fraction of the normal price.

    Three lessons from the curves. At low volume, cost differences are trivial next to engineering time, so choose for quality and delivery speed. At very high volume of a narrow task, a small tuned model wins on cost and latency. Long context suits small corpora and one-off jobs; as a standing design for a large library it rarely survives a finance review. Prices move every few months (model choice, cost and latency), so treat any crossover figure as a snapshot.

    Why retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts

    Studying for an exam by memorising a few hundred practice answers does not make you know the subject. That is fine-tuning on facts. Ovadia et al. (2023) found retrieval beat unsupervised fine-tuning for injecting knowledge, for facts seen in training and new ones. Gekhman et al. (2024) found worse: when fine-tuning examples contain facts the model did not already know, they are learned slowly, and the model becomes more prone to hallucinate, because it is trained to answer confidently without grounding.

    The reason: pretraining took trillions of tokens to store knowledge. A few thousand examples at a small learning rate nudge behaviour; they do not reliably write facts into the weights. Even if they did, you could not cite the source, remove a withdrawn product, or restrict who sees what. In a bank those are requirements: a fee answer needs a link to the current schedule, and a document a branch employee may not read must not shape what they are told.

    The strongest hybrid fine-tunes the skill of using retrieved context: answer only from the passages, ignore distractors, say "not in the documents". This is retrieval-augmented fine-tuning (RAFT, Zhang et al., 2024). Facts still come from retrieval; training teaches reading habits.

    What fine-tuning really does

    Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is practice with an answer key. Train on pairs of (input, ideal output) using the same next-token loss as pretraining (loss functions), computed only on the answer tokens. Each step nudges every weight so the ideal output becomes more likely. After a few epochs the model produces that style unprompted.

    Data quality beats quantity. A few hundred to a few thousand clean, consistent examples often suffice for format and style; noisy labels teach the noise. The labelling guide matters as much as the run (annotator agreement). Held-out examples, set aside from the start, are the only honest measure, and overfitting (F6) is a live risk with small sets.

    Variants:

    • Instruction tuning: teach it to follow a general format of tasks.
    • Preference tuning (DPO): teach it which of two answers is better (RLHF to DPO).
    • Distillation: train a small model to copy a large one on your task, a route to cheap high-volume inference (quantization, pruning and distillation).
    • Reinforcement fine-tuning: train against a reward that can be checked automatically.

    Three costs to remember:

    • Forgetting. Pushing hard on a narrow task can degrade general ability, so evaluate on things you did not train on.
    • Staleness and lock-in. The adapter belongs to one base model; when the vendor retires it, you retrain.
    • Governance. Customer data in training is still customer data, and the model can leak it (memorisation). Put it through the same privacy review (privacy basics).

    LoRA: changing a model by adding a tiny matrix

    Full fine-tuning updates every weight. For a 7-billion-parameter model that means storing a gradient and two optimiser statistics per weight: roughly 16 bytes per parameter in common mixed-precision setups, about 108 GB before activations. That needs several expensive GPUs, and every task yields a new 13 GB copy of the model.

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation, Hu et al., 2021) rests on one observation: the change fine-tuning makes to a weight matrix is usually simple, well described by a low-rank matrix. So freeze the original matrix W and learn the change as the product of two thin matrices:

    W′=W+αr·BA

    W is a frozen dout × din matrix. A is r × din and B is dout × r, where the rank r is small (8, 16, 64). Only A and B are trained: r(din + dout) numbers instead of din × dout. For a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix at r = 8, that is 65,536 numbers instead of 16.8 million, 0.4%. α is a scaling constant. B starts at zero so the model begins exactly as it was.

    Picture W as a mixing desk with 16 million dials. Full fine-tuning turns every dial. LoRA moves a few master faders (the rank r), each shifting many dials in a fixed pattern. If the change you need is that simple, you lose almost nothing. Higher r means more faders: more capacity, bigger adapter. Going from r = 8 to 16 doubles the adapter, not the model.

    What matters in a meeting:

    • Small artefacts. An adapter is tens of megabytes. One base model can serve many adapters (per business line or language), swapped per request. Merging BA into W removes any inference overhead.
    • Memory. No gradients or optimiser state for frozen weights. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) holds the frozen base in 4-bit and trains adapters in higher precision, so a 65B model fits on one 48 GB GPU (P3).
    • Which matrices. The original paper adapted only attention projections. Thinking Machines (2025) found MLP-only LoRA matched all-layer LoRA, while attention-only did clearly worse.
    • Not free. "LoRA learns less and forgets less" (Biderman et al., 2024): on hard domains such as code and maths, low-rank LoRA lagged full fine-tuning, though it preserved general ability better.

    Related methods: adapters (small bottleneck layers inserted between existing ones, Houlsby et al., 2019), prefix and prompt tuning (learn a few "virtual tokens" prepended to the input) and DoRA (splits the update into magnitude and direction). They matter less than the question before them: do you need to change the weights at all?

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Fine-tuning to add knowledge

      "We trained it on our manuals." It answers fluently and wrongly about a rate it half-remembers, with no source.

      Spot itAsk a question whose answer changed last month. Ask for the source of an answer. If it cannot do either, the facts belong in retrieval.
    • Jumping to fine-tuning before a baseline

      Weeks spent tuning when a better prompt or a newer model would have fixed it.

      Spot itAsk for the score of the best prompted baseline on the same evaluation set. No baseline, no case.
    • Evaluating on training data

      The tuned model scores 96%. The examples were in its training set.

      Spot itAsk how the test set was held out, and whether near-duplicates (the same complaint templated many times) cross the split.
    • Retrieval blamed on the model

      "The model hallucinates." Really, the right passage was never retrieved.

      Spot itLog the retrieved passages. If the answer is not in them, fix search. If it is in them and the model ignored it, that is a model or prompt problem.
    • Catastrophic forgetting and narrow gains

      Excellent on the target task, worse at everything else, including safety behaviour.

      Spot itRun a general and a safety regression set before and after tuning.
    • Stale adapters on replaced base models

      The vendor retires the model; the adapter cannot be moved; the data pipeline to retrain does not exist.

      Spot itAsk whether training data, config and evaluation are versioned and can be rerun on a new base within days.
    • Long context everywhere

      Pasting whole libraries into each call. The prototype is lovely; the invoice is not, and answers about mid-document facts quietly degrade.

      Spot itCompute tokens per call times calls per month. Test questions whose answers sit in the middle of the context.
    • Sensitive data in training sets

      Customer names, account numbers or private conversations used for tuning without review.

      Spot itAsk for the data protection assessment and the redaction method. Probe the tuned model for verbatim recall of training records.
    • Believing LoRA is always equal to full tuning

      A rank-8 adapter on a large, hard dataset underperforms and nobody checks.

      Spot itCompare ranks on the evaluation set; if the score keeps rising with rank, capacity is the limit.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A rate card changes every month and agents must quote it with the document name. Which lever, and why not the others?

    Retrieval. Monthly changes make fine-tuning stale, and it cannot cite. Prompting alone does not scale past a few pages. Long context might work for a small library, but permissions and cost at volume favour retrieval.

    2. The bank sorts 3 million customer emails a month into 40 categories and has 20,000 labelled examples. Which lever, and why?

    Fine-tune a small model, after confirming a prompted baseline falls short. The task is narrow, high-volume and well labelled, so a tuned small model is cheaper, faster and more consistent than prompting a large one on every email.

    3. A 4,096 × 11,008 matrix gets a LoRA adapter of rank 16. How many trainable numbers, and what share of the matrix?

    16 × (4,096 + 11,008) = 241,664. The full matrix has 45,088,768 entries, so the adapter is about 0.54%.

    4. Why can a LoRA adapter be served with no extra latency?

    The scaled product BA has the same shape as W, so it can be added into W once. The merged model is the same size and speed as the original. Or keep adapters separate to swap per request, at a small cost.

    5. A colleague says a fine-tune scored 94% against 78% for the prompt baseline. What do you check before believing it?

    That the test set was held out, with no near-duplicates in training; that the baseline prompt was properly engineered, not a weak first try; the number of test cases and an interval; and that general and safety behaviour did not degrade.

    6. At about what volume does a fixed-cost tuned model beat a per-call prompted model? How would you find out for your case?

    When monthly per-call cost (tokens × price × calls) exceeds fixed hosting plus amortised training. Compute both with your real token counts and a current price list; the crossover moves with prices, prompt length and model size. The tuned model must also match quality on your evaluation set.

    7. What does RAFT add to plain RAG, and does it put facts in the weights?

    It fine-tunes on examples that include retrieved documents, some irrelevant, so the model learns to answer from the relevant ones and ignore distractors. Facts still come from retrieval; the weights learn reading habits, not content.
