    Prompting, RAG or Fine-Tuning? Choosing How to Adapt a Model, and Doing It Cheaply with LoRA

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new hire at the bank's contact centre has three ways to get good at answering customers. She can be briefed before each call. She can look things up in the policy library mid-call. Or she can take a six-week training course until the right tone is habit.

    These are the three levers on a language model. Prompting is the brief. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the library: fetch the relevant passages and put them in front of the model. Fine-tuning is the course: change the model's weights using examples. The course builds habits and style but is poor at memorising a manual that changes every quarter. The library gives current facts and can cite the page. The brief is cheap and reversible, and goes further than people expect.

    So "fine-tune the model on our documents" is the classic confusion. The model absorbs facts patchily, cannot say where they came from, and is out of date the day a rate card changes. Use retrieval for that. Use fine-tuning when you want the model to do something differently every time: return strict JSON, write in the bank's Arabic register, or sort complaints into your 40 internal categories.

    Hold onto thisIs the model missing information (show it, via prompting or retrieval) or a behaviour (instruct it; train only if instructions fail)? Start at the cheapest lever and climb only when a measured evaluation says so.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What each lever actually changes

    The model's behaviour depends on two things: its weights (fixed by training, see M1) and the context you send (bounded by the window). Every lever changes one of the two.

    LeverWhat changesGood forCost and speedWeakness
    Prompting (context engineering)The context.Framing, tone, format, a few examples.Minutes to try. Tokens on every call.Long prompts get expensive and partly ignored.
    Retrieval (RAG) (search, chunking, hybrid ranking)The context, filled from your data at query time.Big or changing knowledge, citations, permissions.Weeks to build well. A search system to run.Only as good as retrieval.

    "Be accurate" is not a lever. Reliability comes from what you show the model and how you check it (M5).

    The ladder: start cheap, climb on evidence

    Like a doctor, try the cheap test first and escalate only when results demand it.

    1. Write an evaluation set first: 50 to 200 real cases with agreed good answers. Without it, every comparison is an opinion.
    2. Prompt well. Clear instructions, a few examples, the output format spelled out.
    3. Add retrieval if failures are missing or stale facts. Fix search first: most RAG failures are retrieval failures.
    4. Try a bigger or better model (model choice). A newer model often removes the failure.
    5. Fine-tune if what remains is behaviour, format or cost at volume, and you have labelled examples. The mature pattern combines a tuned small model, retrieval for facts and a good prompt.

    Where the money crosses over

    Think of a taxi versus a leased car. Prompting and RAG pay per call (tokens in, tokens out). Fine-tuning pays up front plus fixed hosting, then little per call. Long context pays per call in proportion to the corpus, so the best choice depends on volume.

    Pasting a 400,000-token policy library into every call costs about 270 times more input than a 1,500-token prompt. That only works at low volume or with caching, which bills a re-read prefix at a fraction of the price.

    At low volume, cost is trivial next to engineering time, so choose for quality. At very high volume of a narrow task, a small tuned model wins. Long context rarely survives a finance review as a standing design. Prices move often (model choice, cost and latency), so treat any crossover as a snapshot.

    Why retrieval beats fine-tuning for facts

    Memorising a few hundred practice answers does not make you know the subject. That is fine-tuning on facts. Research (Ovadia et al., 2023) found retrieval beat fine-tuning for injecting knowledge. Worse, facts the model did not already know can make it more prone to hallucinate (Gekhman et al., 2024).

    A few thousand examples nudge behaviour; they do not reliably write facts into the weights. And you could not cite the source, remove a withdrawn product, or restrict who sees what. In a bank those are requirements.

    The strongest hybrid fine-tunes the skill of using retrieved context: answer only from the passages, ignore distractors, say "not in the documents" (RAFT, Zhang et al., 2024). Facts still come from retrieval; training teaches reading habits.

    What fine-tuning really does

    Supervised fine-tuning is practice with an answer key. Train on pairs of (input, ideal output) and the model's weights shift so the ideal output becomes more likely.

    Data quality beats quantity. A few hundred to a few thousand clean, consistent examples often suffice for format and style; noisy labels teach the noise. Hold out test examples from the start: they are the only honest measure, and overfitting (F6) is a risk with small sets.

    Two common variants: preference tuning (DPO) teaches which of two answers is better (RLHF to DPO), and distillation trains a small model to copy a large one (distillation). Three costs to remember:

    • Forgetting. Narrow tuning can degrade general ability, so test on things you did not train on.
    • Staleness and lock-in. Tuning belongs to one base model; when it is retired, you retrain.
    • Governance. Customer data in training is still customer data, and the model can leak it (memorisation).

    LoRA: changing a model by adding a tiny matrix

    Full fine-tuning updates every weight: for a 7-billion-parameter model, roughly 100 GB of GPU memory and a new 13 GB copy per task.

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation) rests on one observation: the change fine-tuning makes is usually simple. So freeze the original weights and train a tiny add-on that captures the change. Picture a mixing desk with 16 million dials. Full fine-tuning turns every dial. LoRA moves a few master faders, each shifting many dials at once. If the change is that simple, you lose almost nothing.

    The rank is the number of faders. Higher rank means more capacity and a bigger adapter, still a small fraction of the model. What matters in a meeting:

    • Small artefacts. An adapter is tens of megabytes. One base model can serve many adapters, and merging one in adds no inference overhead.
    • Memory. QLoRA holds the frozen base in 4-bit and trains the adapter in higher precision, so a very large model fits on one GPU.
    • Not free. On hard domains such as code and maths, low-rank LoRA can lag full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024), though it forgets less.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Fine-tuning to add knowledge

      "We trained it on our manuals." It answers fluently and wrongly about a rate it half-remembers, with no source.

      Spot itAsk about something that changed last month, and for the source. If it fails, use retrieval.
    • Jumping to fine-tuning before a baseline

      Weeks spent tuning when a better prompt would have fixed it.

      Spot itAsk for the best prompted baseline on the same evaluation set. No baseline, no case.
    • Evaluating on training data

      The tuned model scores 96%. The examples were in its training set.

      Spot itAsk how the test set was held out, and whether near-duplicates cross the split.
    • Retrieval blamed on the model

      "The model hallucinates." Really, the right passage was never retrieved.

      Spot itLog the retrieved passages. If the answer is not in them, fix search. If it is, the model or prompt is at fault.
    • Forgetting, stale adapters and LoRA limits

      Excellent on the target task, worse elsewhere, tied to a base model the vendor will retire. A small-rank adapter can also underperform on a hard, broad task.

      Spot itRun general and safety regression sets before and after. Ask whether training can be rerun on a new base within days. Compare ranks: if the score keeps rising, capacity is the limit.
    • Long context everywhere, or sensitive data in training

      Whole libraries pasted into each call: the prototype is lovely, the invoice is not, and mid-document facts quietly degrade. Or customer records used for tuning without review.

      Spot itMultiply tokens per call by calls per month. Ask for the data protection assessment and probe the tuned model for verbatim recall of training records.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A rate card changes every month and agents must quote it with the document name. Which lever, and why not the others?

    Retrieval. Fine-tuning goes stale and cannot cite. Prompting alone does not scale past a few pages. Long context hits permission and cost limits.

    2. The bank sorts 3 million customer emails a month into 40 categories and has 20,000 labelled examples. Which lever, and why?

    Fine-tune a small model, after confirming a prompted baseline falls short. The task is narrow, high-volume and well labelled, so a tuned small model is cheaper and more consistent.

    3. Why can a LoRA adapter train on well under 1% of a model's numbers and still change its behaviour?

    Because the change a task needs is usually simple, so a few well-chosen directions capture most of it. The original weights stay frozen and only the small adapter is trained.

    4. Why can a LoRA adapter be served with no extra latency?

    The adapter's change has the same shape as the original matrix, so it can be added in once. The merged model is the same size and speed as the original.

    5. A colleague says a fine-tune scored 94% against 78% for the prompt baseline. What do you check before believing it?

    That the test set was held out; that the baseline prompt was properly engineered; how many test cases there were; and that general and safety behaviour did not degrade.

    6. When does a fixed-cost tuned model beat a per-call prompted model? How would you find out for your case?

    When monthly per-call cost exceeds fixed hosting plus amortised training. Work both out with your real token counts and a current price list, and check the tuned model matches quality on your evaluation set.

    7. What does RAFT add to plain RAG, and does it put facts in the weights?

    It trains on examples with retrieved documents, some irrelevant, so the model learns to answer from the right ones. Facts still come from retrieval; the weights learn reading habits, not content.
