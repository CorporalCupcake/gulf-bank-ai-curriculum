    How Wrong Might This Number Be? Standard Errors, Intervals and the Bootstrap

    Every default rate, AUC and complaint share you report is one draw from a lottery of numbers you could have got. This lesson is about measuring how wide that lottery is, with a formula when one exists and with the bootstrap when it doesn't.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA metric without an interval is a rumour: the standard error says how much the number would move if you could rerun the data, and the two things that most often make it wrong are rare events and counting the same customer many times.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new personal-finance product launched nine months ago. The credit team pulls the first 200 loans that have had time to go bad, and 10 have defaulted. The slide says "default rate: 5.0%". The chief risk officer asks one question: is it really 5%?

    The honest answer is "probably somewhere between about 3% and 9%". Those 200 loans are one batch out of many the bank could have booked. A different 200 customers, with the same product and the same underwriting, would have given 7 defaults, or 14. The 5.0% is a single draw from a spread of possible results, and the standard error measures how wide that spread is.

    You never see the spread directly, because you only have one sample. The simulation lets you cheat: it knows the true default rate, and it lets 100 analysts each draw their own sample and build their own 95% interval. Watch how many of them are wrong.

    Three things should stand out.

    • About 5 in 100 intervals miss, and you can't tell which. That's what "95%" means. It's a promise about the method across many samples. Any single interval either contains the truth or it doesn't, and the analyst holding it has no way of knowing.
    • Width shrinks with the square root of the sample. Go from 200 loans to 800 and the intervals get half as wide, not a quarter. Precision is expensive.
    • The textbook formula breaks on rare events. With a 1% default rate and 100 loans, about 37% of samples contain no defaults at all. The Wald interval then says "0% to 0%", which is perfect confidence in a wrong answer. Wilson's interval stays honest: zero defaults out of 100 still allows a rate up to about 3.7%.
    Hold onto thisNever report a metric alone. Report it with an interval, say which method made the interval, and say what the unit of sampling was: loans, customers, branches or questions. Most bad intervals in banking come from rare events or from counting the wrong unit.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Population, sample and the sampling distribution

    Three words do all the work here, and mixing them up causes most of the confusion.

    • The population is everything you want to make a statement about: every loan this product will book under this policy, or every card transaction next quarter. Usually you can't see all of it, either because it hasn't happened yet or because labels are expensive.
    • The sample is what you actually have: 200 loans, 5,000 test transactions, 400 complaints that a reviewer read.
    • The sampling distribution is the spread of results you would get if you could repeat the sampling many times. It's imaginary, since you only ever get one sample, but it's the thing every interval is trying to describe.

    One subtlety matters in banks. Even when you have all of this year's transactions, there's still uncertainty if the question is about the process: what next year's fraud rate will be, or whether model B is better than model A in general. Statisticians call this treating the data as a draw from a super-population. Anthropic's guidance on LLM evaluations uses exactly this framing: the 1,000 benchmark questions are a sample from all the questions that could have been written. If you only want to describe this year's book exactly, there's no sampling error. You'll almost never want only that.

    Standard error and the square-root law

    The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution: the typical distance between your estimate and the truth. For an average of n independent values it has a simple form.

    SE(x¯)=σnSE(p^)=p^(1−p^)n

    Left: the SE of an average is the spread of individual values, σ, divided by the square root of the sample size. Right: a proportion is an average of zeros and ones, so the same rule gives the SE of a default rate, approval rate or precision. For 10 defaults in 200 loans: √(0.05 × 0.95 / 200) ≈ 0.0154, or 1.5 percentage points.

    Two consequences follow, and both come up in meetings.

    • The √n law. Halving the SE needs four times the data. Going from ±3 points to ±1 point needs nine times the data. When someone asks for "a more precise estimate", this tells you the cost.
    • Population size barely matters. The formula has n in it but not the population size. A survey of 1,000 customers is about as precise for a bank with 500,000 customers as for one with 5 million. The exception is when you sample a large share of the population, covered below.

    Why can we treat the estimate as roughly normal, and so use ±1.96 standard errors for 95%? That's the central limit theorem from F1.5: averages of many independent values are approximately normal whatever the shape of the individual values. The word "approximately" carries a lot of weight. For skewed or heavy-tailed data such as loss amounts (F1.5's heavy tails), the approximation can need thousands of observations rather than the folklore "30". For models fitted by maximum likelihood, the SE comes from the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak (F1.6): a sharp peak means the data pin the parameter down tightly.

    What a 95% interval actually means

    estimate±1.96×SE

    The Wald interval: the simplest 95% confidence interval. For the new product: 5.0% ± 1.96 × 1.54 points = 2.0% to 8.0%.

    Here is the precise meaning, and it's worth getting exactly right because it's the most common statistical misstatement in business. A 95% confidence interval comes from a procedure that, over many repeated samples, captures the true value 95% of the time. That's what Simulation 1 shows: the truth is fixed and the intervals move.

    So "there's a 95% chance the true default rate is between 2% and 8%" is, strictly, wrong. Once you've computed it, this interval either contains the truth or it doesn't. The probability statement belongs to the method. A useful plain-English version that is correct: "the data are consistent with a default rate anywhere from 2% to 8%, using a method that is right 19 times in 20."

    Does the distinction matter in practice? With large samples and no strong prior knowledge, a Bayesian credible interval, which does carry the "95% probability" reading, is numerically almost identical, so the loose phrasing does little harm. It matters with small samples, rare events, or when you have real prior information, such as a PD model's prediction for a rating grade. Then the two can differ a lot, and you should say which one you mean.

    Two more confusions to head off:

    • A confidence interval is not a range for individual outcomes. "Average fraud loss: AED 3,300 (2,600 to 4,100)" is about the average. A single fraud loss can easily be AED 40,000. A range for the next individual case is a prediction interval, which is much wider. Conformal prediction (R8.1) is the modern way to build those for any model.
    • The interval only covers sampling noise. It says nothing about biased labels, leakage, or a population that has drifted since the data were collected. A tight interval around a biased number is precisely wrong.

    Intervals for proportions, and why rare events break the textbook

    Banks are full of proportions with small counts: defaults per rating grade, fraud among reviewed alerts, escalations among sampled complaints, precision among the top 50 alerts. The Wald interval fails on these in four ways that Simulation 1 makes visible:

    • With zero events it has zero width, claiming certainty.
    • It's symmetric, but near 0% the real uncertainty is lopsided: the rate can't go below zero, yet could easily be double.
    • It can extend below 0%. One default in 100 gives −0.95% to 2.95%, an impossible lower end.
    • Its coverage is erratic even for large samples. Brown, Cai and DasGupta (2001) showed it oscillates and can sit well below 95% at sample sizes most people would call large. At a 0.2% rate with 1,000 cases, it covers only about 86% of the time.

    The Wilson score interval fixes nearly all of this. The trick is to stop plugging the estimate into the SE. Instead it asks: which true rates p would make what I saw unsurprising, using the SE that p itself implies? Solving that gives:

    p^+z22n±zp^(1−p^)n+z24n21+z2n

    z = 1.96 for 95%. In words: pull the centre slightly towards 50%, and add a little width that matters only when n is small. For 10 defaults in 200 it gives 2.7% to 9.0%, shifted upwards compared with Wald's 2.0% to 8.0%, because the real uncertainty is lopsided.

    The other intervals you'll meet:

    • Clopper–Pearson ("exact"). Guaranteed to cover at least 95% for every true rate, which makes it conservative: usually wider than needed. Auditors like the guarantee.
    • Jeffreys. A Bayesian interval from a Beta(k + ½, n − k + ½) distribution, with very good frequentist coverage. The ECB's instructions for validating internal ratings-based models use a Jeffreys test to back-test PDs per rating grade. Example: a grade of 400 customers with a 1% PD expects 4 defaults. Seeing 7 gives a one-sided p-value of about 0.075; seeing 8 gives about 0.033. So 7 isn't strong evidence that the PD is too low, at the usual 5% level, but 8 is.
    • The rule of three. If you see zero events in n trials, the 95% upper bound is about 3/n. An AML quality-assurance team reviews 500 closed alerts and finds no wrongly closed cases. That doesn't mean "zero misses". It means the miss rate is plausibly anything up to about 0.6%.

    Precision at k (F7) is a proportion too. If 37 of the top 50 alerts are fraud, precision is 74%, with a Wilson interval of 60% to 84%. That's a wide range to set staffing on.

    Rule of thumbUse Wilson by default for proportions. Use Clopper–Pearson when you need a guarantee for an auditor, and Jeffreys when you are following ECB-style PD back-testing. Never use Wald when the count of events is below about 10.

    Intervals for differences

    Most decisions are comparisons: new scorecard against old, branch A against branch B, model B against model A. What you need is an interval for the difference, and its standard error depends on whether the two estimates move together.

    SE(A−B)=SEA2+SEB2−2Cov(A,B)

    For independent samples, such as two separate groups of customers, the covariance is zero and the SEs combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle. For paired measurements, such as two models scored on the same test set, the covariance is usually large and positive, and the difference is much more precise than either number alone.

    A worked example. An approval-policy test sends 5,000 applicants down each path. Default rates come out at 4.0% and 4.6%. The SEs are 0.28 and 0.30 points, so the SE of the difference is √(0.28² + 0.30²) ≈ 0.41 points, and the 95% interval for the 0.6-point gap is −0.2 to +1.4 points. The new policy might be worse, or might be fine.

    Two traps here:

    • Don't compare overlapping intervals. Two 95% intervals can overlap while the difference is clearly real. With equal SEs, "no overlap" corresponds to a difference of 3.92 SEs of either estimate, when only 2.77 (that's 1.96 × √2) are needed. Always build the interval for the difference itself.
    • Use the pairing when you have it. If both models scored the same transactions, compare them case by case. Simulation 4 shows how much this tightens the answer.

    Many bank metrics are ratios of two random totals, such as average spend per transaction when customers are the sampled unit, or loss rate per dirham lent. Their SE comes from the delta method, a first-order expansion of the ratio, or simply from the bootstrap.

    Clustered data: count customers, not transactions

    Every formula so far assumes the observations are independent. Bank data almost never are. A customer's transactions resemble each other: someone who taps their card to pay taps almost every time, a customer who complains once tends to complain again, and the calls handled by one call-centre agent share that agent's habits. Twenty transactions from one customer carry much less information than twenty transactions from twenty customers.

    The cost is captured by the design effect, from survey sampling:

    Deff=1+(m−1)ρneff=nDeff

    m is the number of observations per cluster (transactions per customer), ρ the within-cluster correlation (how alike one customer's transactions are, from 0 to 1). The naive SE is too small by a factor of √Deff. With 100 customers, 20 transactions each and ρ = 0.3: Deff = 6.7, so 2,000 transactions are worth about 300 independent ones.

    Notice the ceiling. As transactions per customer grow, the effective sample size approaches K/ρ (customers divided by correlation) and stops. With 100 customers and ρ = 0.3 you can never do better than about 333 independent observations, however many transactions you pull. The information is in the customers.

    What to do about it:

    • Cluster-robust standard errors (the "sandwich" estimator, from Liang and Zeger's 1986 work on correlated data) estimate the SE from the variation between clusters rather than between rows. In practice, one argument in the regression call: cluster by customer. With equal cluster sizes it reduces to "the SD of the customer averages, divided by √K".
    • The cluster bootstrap resamples whole customers, covered below.
    • Few clusters is its own problem. With fewer than roughly 30–50 clusters, say 12 branches or 8 regions, cluster-robust SEs are themselves unreliable and too small. The wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller, 2008) is the standard fix.
    • Cluster at the level the randomness came from. Abadie, Athey, Imbens and Wooldridge (2023) argue clustering is a design question: cluster at the level where units were sampled or where a treatment was assigned. If a pilot was rolled out branch by branch, cluster by branch, even if the outcome is measured per customer.

    LLM evaluations have exactly the same structure. Reading-comprehension benchmarks ask several questions about the same passage. Anthropic's 2024 analysis found clustered SEs on one such benchmark (DROP) were about 3 times the naive ones. If your bank's internal test set for a complaints assistant has 20 questions about each of 30 policy documents, you have 30 clusters, not 600 independent questions.

    Finite populations and survey weights

    Two refinements, briefly, because they come up in audit and quality-assurance sampling.

    Finite population correction. When you sample a large share of a finite population, there's less left to be uncertain about. Multiply the SE by √((N − n)/(N − 1)). Reviewing 400 of the 1,000 suspicious activity reports filed this year shrinks the SE by a factor of about 0.77. Under 5% of the population, ignore it.

    Weights. QA and survey samples are often deliberately unbalanced. A bank might review high-risk alerts at 1 in 2 and the rest at 1 in 18, or oversample Arabic-language complaints so that both languages have enough cases. Every case must then be weighted by the inverse of its sampling rate, or the high-risk segment dominates the estimate. Weighting removes the bias but costs precision. Kish's effective sample size measures the cost:

    neff=(∑wi)2∑wi2

    With 500 cases at weight 2 and 500 at weight 18, 1,000 reviewed alerts behave like about 610 unweighted ones. Very uneven weights, such as a few cases carrying huge weights, can wipe out most of a sample.

    The bootstrap: let the sample play the population

    Formulas exist for averages and proportions. They don't exist, or are horrible, for most things a bank actually reports: the median fraud loss, the AUC of a model, the Gini coefficient, precision at 50, the 95th percentile of call handling time. Bradley Efron's 1979 answer was the bootstrap, and it's one of the most useful ideas in modern statistics.

    The logic is a substitution. You'd like to draw many fresh samples from the population and see how much your statistic moves, but you can't. Your sample is your best picture of the population, so draw fresh samples from the sample:

    1. From your n observations, draw n at random with replacement. Some appear twice or more, some not at all.
    2. Compute the statistic on this resample.
    3. Repeat 1,000 to 10,000 times. The spread of the results approximates the sampling distribution.

    The SD of the bootstrap results is the bootstrap standard error. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles form the simplest 95% interval, the percentile interval. A resample of size n leaves out a share (1 − 1/n)n of the original points, which is about 1/e ≈ 37% for any reasonable n. That's why resamples differ enough from each other to show you the wobble.

    The machine's results, which you can reproduce with the test button (σ = 1, the default):

    • Median: the percentile interval covers about 94%. The bootstrap is at its best for middle-of-the-distribution statistics, where no simple formula exists.
    • Mean: about 85%, falling to about 70% at σ = 2. With 30 skewed losses, the sample rarely contains the giant losses that drive the true mean, and the bootstrap can only reshuffle what the sample has.
    • 99th percentile: about 20–30%. Thirty losses tell you almost nothing about the 1-in-100 tail.

    Percentile, BCa and the other intervals

    The percentile interval is simple, but it assumes the bootstrap distribution is centred correctly and that its shape doesn't depend on where the truth is. For skewed statistics neither holds. The better options:

    • BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated, Efron 1987) shifts which percentiles you read off. A bias correction measures how far the bootstrap distribution's middle sits from your estimate. An acceleration, estimated by leaving out one observation at a time (the jackknife), measures how fast the spread changes with the value. It's the default in most good software, and it improves the mean's coverage in Simulation 3 by a few points. It can't create information the sample lacks.
    • Bootstrap-t (studentised) bootstraps the estimate divided by its own SE. It's accurate when you have a decent SE formula for each resample.
    • The basic interval reflects the percentiles around the estimate. It's rarely better than the others and is mentioned only so you recognise it.

    How many resamples? For a standard error, a few hundred is enough. For a 95% interval, use at least 1,000, and 10,000 if the result is going into a validation report: the tails of the bootstrap distribution are noisy. Fix the random seed so the numbers are reproducible.

    Bootstrapping model metrics: AUC, Gini, precision at k, median loss

    For a model metric, the "observations" are rows of the test set. Resample rows with replacement, recompute the metric, repeat. Three details make the difference between a correct and an incorrect answer:

    • Stratify rare classes. Resample frauds from the frauds and genuine cases from the genuine ones, so every resample has the same fraud count. Otherwise some resamples have noticeably fewer frauds, purely by chance, and the interval is wider than it should be.
    • Know what drives the uncertainty. AUC precision is limited by the rarer class. An AUC of 0.85 measured on 100 frauds has an SE of about 0.024, so it's roughly 0.85 ± 0.05, whether there are 4,000 genuine transactions or 4 million. Gini, the credit-risk name for 2 × AUC − 1, has exactly double the uncertainty: 0.70 ± 0.10.
    • Pair the comparison. To compare two models, apply the same resample to both and record the difference each time. Cases that are hard for one model are usually hard for the other, so the difference is far more stable than either AUC.

    DeLong's 1988 method gives an analytic SE for AUC differences on paired data, and you'll see it in validation reports. The paired bootstrap gives essentially the same answer and works unchanged for precision at k, recall at a fixed alert volume, or expected cost.

    The lesson for model governance: an AUC gain of 0.01, or 2 Gini points, on a test set with 100 frauds is not evidence of anything. Ask for the paired interval on the difference before approving a challenger model. If the test set can't separate them, the honest answer is to keep the incumbent, or to get more frauds, usually by extending the out-of-time window.

    Dependent data: cluster and block bootstraps

    The plain bootstrap resamples rows as if they were independent. If they aren't, it makes the same mistake as the naive SE in Simulation 2. The fix is to resample whatever is independent.

    • Cluster bootstrap. Resample customers (or branches, or documents in an LLM test set) with replacement, and take all their rows with them. This automatically respects any correlation inside the cluster.
    • Block bootstrap for time series. Daily fraud losses, call volumes and market returns are correlated over time: bad days come in runs. Shuffling days destroys that and understates the uncertainty. The moving block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989) resamples blocks of consecutive days, say 10 at a time. The stationary bootstrap (Politis and Romano, 1994) uses blocks of random length. The block length should be longer than the memory in the series. Software can pick it from the data.

    When the bootstrap fails

    The bootstrap's one assumption is that the sample is a good stand-in for the population. It fails exactly where that's false.

    • Tiny samples. With 10 observations there are only 10 values to reshuffle. The bootstrap distribution is too narrow, and intervals undercover. Below about 20–30 observations, prefer an exact or Bayesian method if one exists.
    • Extremes. The maximum, the 99.9th percentile, or a 1-in-1,000-day loss. A resample can't contain a value larger than the sample's maximum, and about 63% of resamples contain the maximum itself, so the bootstrap distribution piles up against a wall (Bickel and Freedman, 1981). For tail quantities, use extreme value theory, such as fitting a generalised Pareto distribution to losses above a high threshold, or the m-out-of-n bootstrap, which resamples fewer than n points.
    • Heavy tails. If the variance is infinite, as with some Pareto-type operational losses, the bootstrap of the mean is simply invalid (Athreya, 1987). Even with finite variance, heavy tails make it converge slowly, as the σ slider shows. Report medians or trimmed means, or model the tail.
    • The wrong unit. Resampling transactions when customers are the independent unit, or days when the series has memory.
    • A sample that isn't random. The bootstrap measures sampling noise in the data you have. If those data are biased (only investigated alerts get labels, or the test set leaks the future), it will give you a confident interval around the wrong number.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    Uncertainty sits underneath every number in the curriculum. These are the places you'll meet this lesson's ideas again.

    F1.5 · Distributions and the CLTWhy ±1.96 works, and when it doesn't

    The central limit theorem makes averages normal. Heavy tails slow it down, which is why the mean's interval slipped in Simulation 3.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodCurvature is precision

    The standard errors printed beside every regression coefficient come from the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak.

    F2.3 · F2.7 · Tests and powerAn interval is a test turned inside out

    A 95% interval holds every value a 5% test wouldn't reject. Power analysis is the √n law run backwards: how big must n be for the interval to exclude zero?

    F2.4 · Multiple comparisonsTwenty intervals, one will miss

    Slice results by 20 segments and about one 95% interval will exclude the truth by chance. Picking that segment is how false findings are made.

    F3.6 · A/B testingThe difference interval is the result

    Experiments randomised by branch or by customer must cluster at that level. Ratio metrics need the delta method or a bootstrap.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, calibrationEvery metric there needs an interval

    Precision at k is a proportion with a small denominator. A calibration check per score band is a set of small-count proportions too.

    B4.1 · B4.3 · PD and IFRS 9Back-testing is interval estimation

    Does the observed default rate in each rating grade fall inside what the PD predicts? Few defaults per grade means Jeffreys or exact intervals, not Wald.

    M5.2 · R7.4 · LLM evaluationAn eval score is an estimate

    Benchmark scores need clustered SEs and paired comparisons. LLM-as-judge labels can be combined with a few human labels using prediction-powered inference.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionThe cousin: intervals for predictions

    Confidence intervals cover a parameter. Conformal prediction covers the next individual outcome, with a guarantee that holds for any model.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The classical theory is settled, and banks have used it for decades in survey and audit sampling and in model validation. The live work is in machine learning evaluation, which spent years reporting single numbers. It's now rediscovering clustered SEs, paired tests and small-sample intervals, and inventing new ways to combine scarce human labels with plentiful model labels.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you'll see in model reviews, management reporting and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • A metric with no interval

      “The challenger's AUC is 0.871 against 0.866, so we recommend switching.” With 150 frauds in the test set, that gap is well inside the noise.

      Spot itAsk: how many frauds (or defaults) are in the test set, and what's the paired interval on the difference?
    • “0 out of 300, so the error rate is zero”

      A QA review finds no errors and reports 0% (0% to 0%). That's a Wald interval collapsing.

      Spot itApply the rule of three: the upper bound is about 3/300 = 1%. Ask for a Wilson or exact interval.
    • Rows counted as independent

      Two million transactions from 3,000 customers, and an interval so tight it's printed to three decimals.

      Spot itAsk what the independent unit is. If customers, the SE must be clustered by customer, or the bootstrap must resample customers.
    • Comparing overlapping intervals

      “The intervals overlap, so there's no difference.” Or two models' separate intervals are compared when both were scored on the same test set.

      Spot itAsk for one interval on the difference, computed with pairing if the cases are shared.
    • Misreading what 95% means

      “There's a 95% chance the true rate is in this range”, or worse, treating the interval for an average as a range for individual customers.

      Spot itAsk whether the range is for the average or for the next case. If it's for the next case, you need a prediction interval, which is much wider.
    • Bootstrapping an extreme

      A 99.9% loss quantile, or a worst-case day, with a bootstrap interval from 250 days of data. The interval can't go above the worst day observed.

      Spot itCheck whether the interval's upper end equals the sample maximum. For tails, ask for an extreme value model.
    • Shuffling time

      Daily loss or volume data bootstrapped day by day, which ignores that bad days come in runs. The interval is too narrow.

      Spot itAsk whether the series is autocorrelated, and whether a block bootstrap was used.
    • The interval treated as the whole error

      A tight interval around a number computed on leaked features, biased labels or a pre-drift period.

      Spot itAsk what the interval doesn't include: label quality, selection, drift. Ask for out-of-time results as well as the interval.
    • Picking the best of many, then quoting its interval

      Twenty models or segments are tried and the winner is reported with an ordinary 95% interval. The selection itself inflates the result.

      Spot itAsk how many things were compared. See F2.4 on multiple comparisons.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't hold together

      Typical signs: “±” with no method or no stated confidence level; an SE computed with n = rows when the data are per customer; an AUC interval that goes above 1; a proportion's interval that goes below 0%; 50 bootstrap resamples behind a 95% interval; no random seed, so nothing reproduces.

      Spot itRecompute one SE by hand from the formula in Part 2. If the code resamples rows, check that rows really are the independent unit.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The 5% default rate is based on 200 loans, so the honest reading is ‘somewhere between about 3% and 9%’. That's too wide to reprice on yet. Every month adds about 25 matured loans, so by year-end the range will be roughly half as wide. If we need an answer sooner, the decision should allow for the top of the range, not the middle.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I report every metric with a 95% interval and state the method. For proportions I use Wilson, or Jeffreys for PD back-testing. I cluster at the level the randomness came from, usually the customer, and I check the design effect. For metrics without a formula, like AUC, precision at k or a median loss, I use a stratified bootstrap with BCa intervals and a fixed seed. Model comparisons are paired, on the same resamples. I don't bootstrap tail quantiles. For those I'd fit an extreme value model.”
    “We have two million transactions. Surely sampling error is negligible?”

    It depends on what's independent. If those transactions come from 5,000 customers and behaviour is customer-driven, the effective sample size could be closer to 5,000 than two million. And for rare outcomes, what matters is the number of events: two million transactions with 400 frauds gives a fraud-related metric about as precise as 400 observations allow. So I'd cluster by customer and look at the count of events, not the count of rows.

    “The challenger model's Gini is two points higher. Can we switch?”

    Two Gini points is 0.01 AUC. On a test set with a hundred or so defaults, the paired interval on that difference is usually wider than ±0.02 AUC, so the test set can't separate the models. I'd show the paired bootstrap or DeLong interval. If it includes zero, I'd keep the incumbent or extend the test window to get more defaults, and look at other criteria such as stability, calibration and explainability.

    “What does the 95% actually mean?”

    If we repeated the whole exercise many times with fresh samples, intervals built this way would capture the true value about 95% of the time. For this particular interval, the practical reading is that the data are consistent with any value in the range. If you want a direct probability statement about where the truth lies, that's a Bayesian credible interval. With this much data the two would be almost identical.

    “Why not just bootstrap everything?”

    Mostly we can, and it's often the simplest route. Three exceptions: very small samples, where it understates uncertainty; extremes like a 99.9% loss quantile, where it can't see beyond the largest value in the data; and dependent data, where you must resample customers or blocks of time rather than rows. And where a good formula exists, as for a simple proportion, the formula is faster, reproducible and easier to explain to an auditor.

    “The LLM assistant scored 82% on our 300-question test. Is that good enough to launch?”

    First, 82% of 300 has a 95% interval of roughly 77% to 86%. If the questions come in groups about the same policy document, the real interval is wider, because the groups are the independent units. Second, compare it to the current process on the same questions, paired. Third, if the grading was done by an LLM judge, the judge's errors add uncertainty. A small set of human-graded answers, combined using prediction-powered inference, can correct for that without regrading everything.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A reviewer samples 400 complaints and finds 12 were wrongly closed. What are the estimate, the SE and the Wald 95% interval? How many complaints would halve the width?

    p̂ = 3%. SE = √(0.03 × 0.97 / 400) ≈ 0.85 points. Wald interval: 3% ± 1.67 points ≈ 1.3% to 4.7%. Wilson gives 1.7% to 5.2%, shifted upwards. Halving the width needs four times the sample: 1,600 complaints.

    2. A KYC refresh team checks 300 files and finds no errors. What can you say?

    Not “the error rate is zero”. By the rule of three, the 95% upper bound is about 3/300 = 1%. The data are consistent with an error rate anywhere from 0% to about 1%.

    3. True or false: “there's a 95% probability that the true rate lies in this particular 95% confidence interval.”

    False, strictly. The 95% describes the procedure over repeated samples. A particular interval either contains the truth or doesn't. A Bayesian credible interval supports the probability statement, and with large samples and vague priors the two are numerically close.

    4. A campaign study covers 50 branches with 200 customers each. Outcomes within a branch have correlation 0.05. What's the effective sample size?

    Deff = 1 + (200 − 1) × 0.05 = 10.95. Effective n = 10,000 / 10.95 ≈ 910. A correlation that sounds tiny cuts the information by a factor of 11, because the clusters are large. And with only 50 clusters, a wild cluster bootstrap is worth considering.

    5. Someone bootstraps 250 daily operational losses to put an interval on the 99.9th percentile. What's wrong?

    Two things. The 99.9th percentile is beyond the data: 250 days contain on average a quarter of a 1-in-1,000-day event. And no resample can exceed the largest loss observed, so the interval's top is capped at the sample maximum. Days also aren't independent. Use an extreme value model for the tail, with its own uncertainty, and a block bootstrap if you bootstrap at all.

    6. Models A and B are scored on the same 5,000 transactions. Their separate 95% intervals for AUC overlap a lot. Can you conclude there's no difference?

    No. Overlap of separate intervals is the wrong test, and it ignores that both models scored the same cases. Their errors are positively correlated, so the SE of the difference is much smaller than the separate intervals suggest. Compute a paired bootstrap (or DeLong) interval for AUC(B) − AUC(A).

    7. Your bootstrap resample of 30 losses contains the largest loss in the sample. How unusual is that?

    Not unusual at all. The chance a resample omits a given point is (29/30)30 ≈ 36%, so about 64% of resamples include the maximum, often more than once. That's why bootstrap distributions of extremes pile up at the sample maximum.
