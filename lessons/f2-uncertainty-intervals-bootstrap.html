    Standard Error and Confidence Intervals: How Wrong Might This Number Be?

    Part 1

    What standard error is

    In one sentenceThe standard error (SE) is how much your number would wobble if you redid the whole thing with a fresh sample. Small SE: trust the number. Big SE: it could easily have come out quite different.

    Say you ask 100 people how long they sleep, and the average is 7.1 hours. Ask a different 100 people and you might get 6.9. Another 100: 7.2. The averages bounce around the true answer, because each time you happened to pick different people.

    The standard error is the typical size of that bounce.

    In real life you only run the survey once. The SE is worked out from that single sample (how spread out the answers were, and how many people you asked), and it tells you how much the average would bounce if you could repeat it.

    Part 2

    How to read a standard error

    Rule of thumbThe true value is usually within 2 SEs of your estimate. About two times in three it is within 1 SE.
    Average 7.1 h, SE 0.2
    The true average is probably between 6.7 and 7.5 (7.1 ± 2 × 0.2). A fairly precise answer.
    SE of 2
    If you reran this, you'd typically land about 2 away from today's number. Landing 4 away wouldn't be surprising. Landing 6 away would be.
    Compare the SE with the number itself
    SE 2 on an average of 100 is tight: roughly 96 to 104. SE 2 on an average of 3 is almost useless: anywhere from about −1 to 7.
    A difference of 5 between two groups, SE of the difference 2
    5 is 2.5 SEs away from zero, so the gap is unlikely to be just luck. If the difference were 1 (half an SE), it could easily be noise.

    What makes it big or small

    • More data makes it smaller, but slowly. To halve the SE you need four times as much data.
    • Messier data makes it bigger. If people's answers vary wildly, any one sample's average can drift further.

    SE is not the same as SD

    • Standard deviation (SD) is how spread out the individual people are. Some sleep 5 hours, some 9. Collecting more data doesn't shrink it, because people really do differ.
    • Standard error (SE) is how spread out the average is across repeat samples. It shrinks as you collect more data.
    Part 3

    Confidence intervals

    In one sentenceA 95% confidence interval is your estimate ± about 2 SEs: a range of believable values for the truth.

    Example: "average sleep 7.1 hours, 95% CI 6.7 to 7.5".

    • Inside the range: believable values for the true average.
    • Outside: unlikely, given this data.
    • Wide interval: you don't know much yet. Collect more data before deciding anything.
    • For a difference between groups, does it include 0? If yes, you can't rule out "no real difference".

    The precise meaning: if you repeated the study many times and built an interval each time, 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. For day-to-day work, "a range of believable values" is the right way to think about it.

    Part 4

    The bootstrap

    In one sentenceWhen there's no simple SE formula, reshuffle your own data many times and see how much your number wobbles.

    There are easy formulas for the SE of an average or a percentage. But what about a median, a ratio, or a model's accuracy score? The bootstrap works for almost anything.

    1. Take your sample, say 500 rows.
    2. Draw 500 rows from it at random, with replacement: the same row can be picked twice, and some rows not at all.
    3. Work out your number (median, accuracy, whatever) on this new sample.
    4. Repeat 1,000 times.
    5. The spread of those 1,000 numbers is your SE. The middle 95% of them is your confidence interval.

    Why it works: your sample is your best picture of the whole population. Resampling from it imitates "go and collect another sample".

    Part 5

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    ToolAnswersUse it when
    SDHow different are individuals?Describing the data itself
    SEHow precise is my estimate?Reporting any average, rate or difference
    Confidence intervalWhat range is believable?Communicating results to anyone. Easier to read than an SE.
    BootstrapSE or interval for anythingNo formula exists: medians, ratios, model scores

    When the bootstrap breaks:

    • Tiny samples (under about 30): your sample is a poor picture of the population.
    • Extremes such as the maximum: resampling can never produce a value bigger than one you already have.
    • Linked rows, such as many rows per person or data over time: resample whole people or whole time blocks, not single rows.
    Show the formulas

    SE of an average = SD ÷ √(number of people). The square root is why you need 4× the data to halve it.

    SE of a percentage = √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n), with p as a fraction. For 30% of 400 people: √(0.3 × 0.7 ÷ 400) ≈ 0.023, i.e. 2.3 percentage points.

    95% CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE.

    In banking, every reported rate (default rate, fraud rate, uplift from a campaign) should come with an interval, so nobody acts on a difference that is just noise.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Mixing up SD and SE

      SD is about people, SE is about the estimate. Reporting the SD as "uncertainty" makes results look far less precise than they are.

    • Judging by overlapping intervals

      Two intervals can overlap a little and the difference can still be real. Compute the interval for the difference itself.

    • Forgetting that rows are linked

      Ten rows from the same customer are not ten independent pieces of evidence. Treating them as such makes the SE far too small.

    • Thinking SE covers every kind of error

      It only covers the luck of which people you happened to sample. A biased sample (say, only surveying night-shift workers) is wrong no matter how small the SE is.

    1. An average is 50 with an SE of 5. Roughly what range is believable for the truth?

    About 40 to 60 (50 ± 2 × 5).

    2. You want to halve your SE. How much more data do you need?

    Four times as much.

    3. You collect much more data. What happens to the SD and to the SE?

    The SD stays about the same, because people still differ. The SE shrinks, because the average gets more precise.

    4. The 95% CI for "new design minus old design" is −1% to +4%. What do you conclude?

    It includes 0, so you can't rule out no difference. The effect might be positive, but you need more data to be sure.

    5. You need an interval for a median. What do you use?

    The bootstrap.
