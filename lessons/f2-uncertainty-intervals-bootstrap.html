    How Wrong Might This Number Be? Standard Errors, Intervals and the Bootstrap

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new loan product has 200 matured loans and 10 defaults. The slide says "5.0%". Honestly, it is somewhere between about 3% and 9%: another 200 customers might show 7 defaults, or 14.

    The standard error (SE) measures that wobble. Below, 100 analysts each build a 95% interval around a known truth. Count the wrong ones.

    • About 5 in 100 intervals miss, and nobody can tell which. "95%" describes the method, not one interval.
    • The textbook (Wald) formula breaks on rare events. It can say "0% to 0%".
    Hold onto thisNever report a metric alone. Give the interval, the method, and the unit of sampling (loans, customers, branches).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Population, sample and the sampling distribution

    The population is everything you care about, such as every loan the product will ever book. The sample is the spoonful you tasted. The sampling distribution is the imaginary spread of results if you repeated the sampling.

    Standard error and the square-root law

    The standard error says how much your number wobbles from sample to sample. It shrinks with the square root of the sample size, so halving it needs four times the data. A rough 95% interval is the estimate plus or minus two SEs.

    What a 95% interval actually means

    A 95% confidence interval comes from a method that captures the truth in 95% of repeated samples, like throwing a net at a fixed fish. So "95% chance the rate is in this range" is strictly wrong. Say instead: "the data are consistent with 2% to 8%, using a method right 19 times in 20."

    It describes the average, not individual cases, and covers sampling noise only: a tight interval around a biased number is precisely wrong.

    Intervals for proportions, and why rare events break the textbook

    Banks are full of small-count proportions, such as defaults per rating grade. The textbook (Wald) interval fails on them: zero events gives zero width. The Wilson interval stays honest. With zero events in n trials, the rule of three puts the upper bound near three divided by n.

    Rule of thumbUse Wilson by default. Use Clopper–Pearson when an auditor wants a guarantee. Never use Wald with fewer than about 10 events.

    Intervals for differences

    Build the interval for the difference, not for each number. Overlapping intervals can still hide a real gap. When two models score the same cases, their errors move together, so a paired comparison is far more precise.

    Clustered data: count customers, not transactions

    Twenty transactions from one customer are like asking one person the same question twenty times. With 100 customers and similar behaviour within each, 2,000 transactions are worth about 300 independent ones.

    More transactions per customer cannot beat the ceiling set by the number of customers. Cluster SEs by customer, or resample whole customers. LLM evaluations are the same: 20 questions on each of 30 documents is 30 clusters, not 600 questions.

    Finite populations and survey weights

    Sampling a large share of a small population leaves less uncertainty. QA samples are often deliberately unbalanced, with high-risk alerts reviewed more often, so weight each case by the inverse of its sampling rate.

    The bootstrap: let the sample play the population

    Formulas are awkward for a median loss or AUC. The bootstrap draws fresh samples from your own sample, like re-dealing from the deck you were dealt. Resample with replacement, recompute, repeat thousands of times. The spread of results is your uncertainty.

    It works for the median, less well for heavy-tailed means, and badly for extreme percentiles.

    Percentile, BCa and the other intervals

    BCa is a refined bootstrap interval that corrects for skew and bias. It cannot create information the sample lacks. Use at least 1,000 resamples.

    Bootstrapping model metrics: AUC, Gini, precision at k, median loss

    Resample test-set rows and recompute. Stratify, so each resample keeps the same number of frauds. AUC precision is limited by the rarer class. Compare two models on the same resamples.

    A 0.01 AUC gain on 100 frauds is not evidence. Ask for the paired interval before approving a challenger.

    Dependent data: cluster and block bootstraps

    Resample whatever is independent: whole customers, or blocks of consecutive days for time series, since bad days come in runs.

    When the bootstrap fails

    It fails on tiny samples, extremes (a resample cannot exceed the sample maximum), the wrong unit, and non-random samples.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • No proper interval on a comparison

      “AUC 0.871 against 0.866, so switch”, with 150 frauds.

      Spot itAsk how many frauds there are, and for a paired interval.
    • “0 out of 300, so the error rate is zero”

      A collapsed interval reported as 0% to 0%.

      Spot itRule of three: the upper bound is about 1%.
    • Rows counted as independent

      Two million transactions from 3,000 customers, interval printed to three decimals.

      Spot itAsk what the independent unit is.
    • Misreading what 95% means

      An average's interval treated as a range for individual customers.

      Spot itAsk what it covers and leaves out.
    • Bootstrapping an extreme

      A 99.9% loss quantile from 250 days of data.

      Spot itIs the upper end the sample maximum?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A team checks 300 files and finds no errors. What can you say?

    Not “zero”. The upper bound is about 1%.

    2. Is “95% probability the true rate lies in this interval” correct?

    Strictly, no. The 95% describes the method, not this interval.

    3. 50 branches of 200 customers, slightly alike within a branch. Are those 10,000 independent customers?

    No. Large clusters make even a small correlation cut the information a lot.

    4. Why can't you bootstrap the 99.9th percentile from 250 days?

    No resample exceeds the largest loss seen. Use an extreme value model.

    5. Two models' separate AUC intervals overlap. No difference?

    No. Compute a paired interval on the difference.
