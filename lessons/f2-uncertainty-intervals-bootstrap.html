    How Wrong Might This Number Be? Standard Errors, Intervals and the Bootstrap

    Every default rate, AUC and complaint share you report is one draw from a lottery of numbers you could have got. This lesson is about measuring how wide that lottery is, with a formula when one exists and with the bootstrap when it doesn't.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA metric without an interval is a rumour: the standard error says how much the number would move if you could rerun the data, and the two things that most often make it wrong are rare events and counting the same customer many times.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new personal-finance product has 200 matured loans, and 10 defaulted. The slide says "default rate: 5.0%". The chief risk officer asks: is it really 5%?

    The honest answer is "somewhere between about 3% and 9%". Think of a poll: ask 200 people and you get 52% yes, ask another 200 and you get 47%. Likewise, a different 200 customers on the same product would have shown 7 defaults, or 14. The 5.0% is one draw from a spread of possible results. The standard error (SE) measures how wide that spread is: the typical distance between your number and the truth.

    You only ever see one sample, so the simulation cheats: it knows the true rate and lets 100 analysts each build a 95% interval. Count how many are wrong.

    • About 5 in 100 intervals miss, and nobody can tell which. "95%" is a promise about the method across many samples, like a weather forecaster who is right 19 days in 20. Any single interval either holds the truth or not.
    • Width shrinks with the square root of the sample. 200 loans to 800 halves the width, not quarters it. Precision is expensive.
    • The textbook (Wald) formula breaks on rare events. At a 1% default rate with 100 loans, about 37% of samples contain no defaults. Wald then says "0% to 0%": perfect confidence in a wrong answer. Wilson stays honest: zero defaults in 100 still allows a rate up to about 3.7%.
    Hold onto thisNever report a metric alone. Give the interval, the method, and the unit of sampling (loans, customers, branches, questions). Most bad intervals come from rare events or counting the wrong unit.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Population, sample and the sampling distribution

    A soup taster tastes one spoonful to judge the pot. The pot is the population, the spoonful is the sample.

    • The population is everything you want to make a statement about: every loan this product will book under this policy, or every card transaction next quarter. Often it hasn't happened yet.
    • The sample is what you have: 200 loans, 5,000 test transactions, 400 reviewed complaints.
    • The sampling distribution is the spread of results you would get by repeating the sampling many times. It's imaginary, but every interval tries to describe it.

    Even with all of this year's transactions, uncertainty remains if the question is about the process: next year's fraud rate, or whether model B beats model A in general. This is the super-population view, and it's the same framing Anthropic's LLM-evaluation guidance uses: 1,000 benchmark questions are a sample of all questions that could be written. You only escape sampling error if you describe this year's book and nothing else.

    Standard error and the square-root law

    The standard error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. A bigger SE means your number wobbles more from sample to sample. For an average of n independent values:

    SE(x¯)=σnSE(p^)=p^(1−p^)n

    Left: the SE of an average is the spread of individual values, σ, divided by the square root of the sample size. Right: a proportion is an average of zeros and ones, so the same rule gives the SE of a default rate, approval rate or precision. For 10 defaults in 200 loans: √(0.05 × 0.95 / 200) ≈ 0.0154, or 1.5 percentage points.

    • The √n law. Halving the SE needs four times the data; going from ±3 points to ±1 needs nine times. This is the price of "a more precise estimate".
    • Population size barely matters. The formula has n but not the population size, like a poll of 1,000 that is as good for a country of 5 million as for 50 million. The exception is sampling a large share of the population (see finite populations below).
    • Why ±1.96? The central limit theorem (F1.5) says averages of many independent values are roughly normal, and 95% of a normal sits within 1.96 SEs. "Roughly" matters: for skewed or heavy-tailed data such as loss amounts (F1.5's heavy tails), thousands of observations may be needed, not the folklore "30".
    • Models: for maximum-likelihood fits, the SE comes from the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak (F1.6). A sharp peak means the data pin the parameter down.

    What a 95% interval actually means

    estimate±1.96×SE

    The Wald interval: the simplest 95% confidence interval. For the new product: 5.0% ± 1.96 × 1.54 points = 2.0% to 8.0%.

    A 95% confidence interval comes from a procedure that, over many repeated samples, captures the true value 95% of the time. Simulation 1 shows it: the truth is fixed and the intervals move. It's like a net thrown 100 times at a fixed fish: 95 throws catch it, and you can't tell whether this throw did.

    So "there's a 95% chance the true default rate is between 2% and 8%" is strictly wrong; the probability belongs to the method. A correct plain version: "the data are consistent with a default rate anywhere from 2% to 8%, using a method that is right 19 times in 20." A Bayesian credible interval does carry the "95% probability" reading. With large samples and vague priors it is almost identical; with small samples, rare events or real prior information (such as a PD model's prediction for a rating grade) it can differ a lot, so say which you mean.

    Two misreadings to avoid:

    • It isn't a range for individual outcomes. "Average fraud loss: AED 3,300 (2,600 to 4,100)" is about the average; a single loss can be AED 40,000. A range for the next case is a prediction interval, much wider. Conformal prediction (R8.1) builds those for any model.
    • It covers sampling noise only. Biased labels, leakage or drift are not in it. A tight interval around a biased number is precisely wrong.

    Intervals for proportions, and why rare events break the textbook

    Banks are full of small-count proportions: defaults per rating grade, fraud among reviewed alerts, precision among the top 50 alerts. Wald fails on them in four ways:

    • Zero events gives zero width, claiming certainty.
    • It's symmetric, but near 0% the uncertainty is lopsided: the rate can't go below zero yet could easily be double.
    • It can go below 0%. One default in 100 gives −0.95% to 2.95%.
    • Coverage is erratic even for large samples (Brown, Cai and DasGupta, 2001). At a 0.2% rate with 1,000 cases it covers only about 86%.

    The Wilson score interval fixes this by asking which true rates p would make what I saw unsurprising, using the SE that p itself implies. Solving gives:

    p^+z22n±zp^(1−p^)n+z24n21+z2n

    z = 1.96 for 95%. In words: pull the centre slightly towards 50%, and add a little width that matters only when n is small. For 10 defaults in 200 it gives 2.7% to 9.0%, shifted upwards compared with Wald's 2.0% to 8.0%, because the real uncertainty is lopsided.

    Other intervals you'll meet:

    • Clopper–Pearson ("exact"). Guaranteed to cover at least 95% for every true rate, so usually wider than needed. Auditors like the guarantee.
    • Jeffreys. A Bayesian interval from Beta(k + ½, n − k + ½) with very good frequentist coverage. The ECB's IRB validation instructions use a Jeffreys test to back-test PDs per rating grade. A grade of 400 customers with a 1% PD expects 4 defaults. Seeing 7 gives a one-sided p-value of about 0.075 (not strong evidence at 5%); seeing 8 gives about 0.033 (strong).
    • Rule of three. Zero events in n trials gives a 95% upper bound of about 3/n. An AML team reviews 500 closed alerts and finds no wrongly closed cases. That means the miss rate is plausibly anything up to about 0.6%, not zero.

    Precision at k (F7) is a proportion too. 37 of the top 50 alerts being fraud is 74% precision, with a Wilson interval of 60% to 84%: wide for setting staffing.

    Rule of thumbUse Wilson by default for proportions. Use Clopper–Pearson when you need a guarantee for an auditor, and Jeffreys when you are following ECB-style PD back-testing. Never use Wald when the count of events is below about 10.

    Intervals for differences

    Most decisions compare things: new scorecard against old, branch A against branch B. Build the interval for the difference. Its SE depends on whether the two estimates move together.

    SE(A−B)=SEA2+SEB2−2Cov(A,B)

    For independent samples, such as two separate groups of customers, the covariance is zero and the SEs combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle. For paired measurements, such as two models scored on the same test set, the covariance is usually large and positive, and the difference is much more precise than either number alone.

    Example: an approval-policy test sends 5,000 applicants down each path. Default rates are 4.0% and 4.6%, with SEs of 0.28 and 0.30 points. The SE of the difference is √(0.28² + 0.30²) ≈ 0.41, so the 95% interval for the 0.6-point gap is −0.2 to +1.4 points. The new policy might be worse, or might be fine.

    • Don't compare overlapping intervals. Two 95% intervals can overlap while the difference is real. With equal SEs, "no overlap" needs a gap of 3.92 SEs, when only 2.77 (1.96 × √2) are needed. Build the interval for the difference.
    • Use pairing when you have it. If both models scored the same transactions, compare case by case (Simulation 4). It's like comparing two runners' times on the same day, rather than across different weather.
    • Ratios of random totals (spend per transaction, loss per dirham lent) get their SE from the delta method (a first-order expansion) or the bootstrap.

    Clustered data: count customers, not transactions

    Every formula so far assumes independent observations. Bank data rarely are. Twenty transactions from one customer are like asking one person the same question twenty times: far less information than twenty people. Customers who tap to pay tap almost always, complainers complain again, and an agent's calls share the agent's habits. The cost is the design effect:

    Deff=1+(m−1)ρneff=nDeff

    m is the number of observations per cluster (transactions per customer), ρ the within-cluster correlation (how alike one customer's transactions are, from 0 to 1). The naive SE is too small by a factor of √Deff. With 100 customers, 20 transactions each and ρ = 0.3: Deff = 6.7, so 2,000 transactions are worth about 300 independent ones.

    There's a ceiling. As transactions per customer grow, the effective sample size approaches K/ρ (customers over correlation). With 100 customers and ρ = 0.3 you can never beat about 333 independent observations. The information is in the customers.

    Fixes:

    • Cluster-robust SEs (the "sandwich" estimator, Liang and Zeger, 1986) use variation between clusters, not rows. Usually one argument in the regression call: cluster by customer. With equal clusters it's the SD of customer averages divided by √K.
    • The cluster bootstrap resamples whole customers (below).
    • Few clusters (under roughly 30–50, such as 12 branches) make cluster-robust SEs too small. Use the wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller, 2008).
    • Cluster where the randomness came from (Abadie, Athey, Imbens and Wooldridge, 2023). If a pilot rolled out branch by branch, cluster by branch even if outcomes are per customer.

    LLM evaluations have the same structure: several questions share a passage. Anthropic's 2024 analysis found clustered SEs on one benchmark (DROP) about 3 times the naive ones. A complaints-assistant test with 20 questions on each of 30 policy documents has 30 clusters, not 600 questions.

    Finite populations and survey weights

    Finite population correction. Sampling a large share of a finite population leaves less to be uncertain about. Multiply the SE by √((N − n)/(N − 1)). Reviewing 400 of 1,000 suspicious activity reports shrinks the SE by about 0.77. Under 5% of the population, ignore it.

    Weights. QA samples are often deliberately unbalanced: high-risk alerts reviewed at 1 in 2, the rest at 1 in 18, or Arabic-language complaints oversampled. Weight each case by the inverse of its sampling rate, or the high-risk segment dominates. This removes bias but costs precision, which Kish's effective sample size measures:

    neff=(∑wi)2∑wi2

    With 500 cases at weight 2 and 500 at weight 18, 1,000 reviewed alerts behave like about 610 unweighted ones. Very uneven weights, such as a few cases carrying huge weights, can wipe out most of a sample.

    The bootstrap: let the sample play the population

    Formulas exist for averages and proportions, but not (or are horrible) for the median fraud loss, AUC, Gini, precision at 50, or the 95th percentile of call time. Efron's 1979 bootstrap substitutes: you can't draw fresh samples from the population, so draw fresh samples from your sample, like re-dealing a hand from the same deck of cards you were dealt.

    1. Draw n of your n observations at random with replacement. Some appear twice, some not at all.
    2. Compute the statistic on this resample.
    3. Repeat 1,000 to 10,000 times. The spread approximates the sampling distribution.

    The SD of the results is the bootstrap SE. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give the percentile interval. Each resample leaves out about 1/e ≈ 37% of the original points (the share is (1 − 1/n)n), which is why resamples differ enough to show the wobble.

    Test results at σ = 1:

    • Median: about 94% coverage. The bootstrap is best for middle-of-the-distribution statistics, where no simple formula exists.
    • Mean: about 85%, falling to about 70% at σ = 2. Thirty skewed losses rarely include the giants that drive the true mean, and the bootstrap can only reshuffle what's there.
    • 99th percentile: about 20–30%. Thirty losses say almost nothing about the 1-in-100 tail.

    Percentile, BCa and the other intervals

    The percentile interval assumes the bootstrap distribution is centred correctly and its shape doesn't depend on the truth. For skewed statistics neither holds. Better options:

    • BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated, Efron 1987) shifts which percentiles you read. The bias correction measures how far the bootstrap's middle sits from your estimate; the acceleration, found by leaving out one observation at a time (the jackknife), measures how fast the spread changes with the value. It's the default in good software and improves the mean's coverage by a few points. It can't create information the sample lacks.
    • Bootstrap-t bootstraps the estimate divided by its own SE. Accurate when each resample has a decent SE formula.
    • Basic interval: reflects percentiles around the estimate. Rarely better; recognise it only.

    Resamples: a few hundred for an SE; at least 1,000 for a 95% interval, 10,000 for a validation report (tails are noisy). Fix the seed.

    Bootstrapping model metrics: AUC, Gini, precision at k, median loss

    The "observations" are test-set rows: resample rows, recompute the metric, repeat. Three details matter:

    • Stratify rare classes. Resample frauds from frauds and genuine from genuine, so every resample has the same fraud count. Otherwise the interval is wider than it should be.
    • Know what drives uncertainty. AUC precision is limited by the rarer class. An AUC of 0.85 on 100 frauds has an SE of about 0.024, so roughly 0.85 ± 0.05, whether there are 4,000 genuine transactions or 4 million. Gini (2 × AUC − 1) has double the uncertainty: 0.70 ± 0.10.
    • Pair the comparison. Apply the same resample to both models and record the difference. Cases hard for one are usually hard for the other, so the difference is far steadier than either AUC.

    DeLong's 1988 method gives an analytic SE for paired AUC differences and appears in validation reports. The paired bootstrap gives essentially the same answer and also works for precision at k, recall at a fixed alert volume, or expected cost.

    For model governance: an AUC gain of 0.01 (2 Gini points) on a test set with 100 frauds is not evidence. Ask for the paired interval on the difference before approving a challenger. If it can't separate them, keep the incumbent or get more frauds, usually by extending the out-of-time window.

    Dependent data: cluster and block bootstraps

    The plain bootstrap treats rows as independent and repeats Simulation 2's mistake if they aren't. Resample whatever is independent.

    • Cluster bootstrap. Resample customers (or branches, or documents) with all their rows. This respects within-cluster correlation automatically.
    • Block bootstrap for time series. Bad days come in runs, like weather, and shuffling days destroys that. The moving block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989) resamples blocks of consecutive days, say 10. The stationary bootstrap (Politis and Romano, 1994) uses random block lengths. Blocks should be longer than the series' memory; software can pick it.

    When the bootstrap fails

    The bootstrap assumes the sample stands in for the population. It fails where that's false.

    • Tiny samples. Ten observations give ten values to reshuffle; intervals are too narrow. Below about 20–30, prefer an exact or Bayesian method.
    • Extremes. A resample can't exceed the sample maximum, and about 63% of resamples contain it, so the distribution piles against a wall (Bickel and Freedman, 1981). For tails use extreme value theory (a generalised Pareto fit above a high threshold) or the m-out-of-n bootstrap, which resamples fewer than n points.
    • Heavy tails. With infinite variance (some Pareto-type operational losses) the bootstrap of the mean is invalid (Athreya, 1987); with finite variance it converges slowly. Report medians or trimmed means, or model the tail.
    • The wrong unit. Resampling transactions when customers are independent, or days when the series has memory.
    • A non-random sample. If labels exist only for investigated alerts, or the test set leaks the future, you get a confident interval around the wrong number.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes seen in model reviews, management reports and AI-generated analyses, each with the quickest catch.

    • A metric with no interval

      “The challenger's AUC is 0.871 against 0.866, so we recommend switching.” With 150 frauds in the test set, that gap is well inside the noise.

      Spot itAsk: how many frauds (or defaults) are in the test set, and what's the paired interval on the difference?
    • “0 out of 300, so the error rate is zero”

      A QA review finds no errors and reports 0% (0% to 0%). That's a Wald interval collapsing.

      Spot itApply the rule of three: the upper bound is about 3/300 = 1%. Ask for a Wilson or exact interval.
    • Rows counted as independent

      Two million transactions from 3,000 customers, and an interval so tight it's printed to three decimals.

      Spot itAsk what the independent unit is. If customers, the SE must be clustered by customer, or the bootstrap must resample customers.
    • Comparing overlapping intervals

      “The intervals overlap, so there's no difference.” Or two models' separate intervals are compared when both were scored on the same test set.

      Spot itAsk for one interval on the difference, computed with pairing if the cases are shared.
    • Misreading what 95% means

      “There's a 95% chance the true rate is in this range”, or worse, treating the interval for an average as a range for individual customers.

      Spot itAsk whether the range is for the average or for the next case. If it's for the next case, you need a prediction interval, which is much wider.
    • Bootstrapping an extreme

      A 99.9% loss quantile, or a worst-case day, with a bootstrap interval from 250 days of data. The interval can't go above the worst day observed.

      Spot itCheck whether the interval's upper end equals the sample maximum. For tails, ask for an extreme value model.
    • Shuffling time

      Daily loss or volume data bootstrapped day by day, which ignores that bad days come in runs. The interval is too narrow.

      Spot itAsk whether the series is autocorrelated, and whether a block bootstrap was used.
    • The interval treated as the whole error

      A tight interval around a number computed on leaked features, biased labels or a pre-drift period.

      Spot itAsk what the interval doesn't include: label quality, selection, drift. Ask for out-of-time results as well as the interval.
    • Picking the best of many, then quoting its interval

      Twenty models or segments are tried and the winner is reported with an ordinary 95% interval. The selection itself inflates the result.

      Spot itAsk how many things were compared. See F2.4 on multiple comparisons.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't hold together

      Typical signs: “±” with no method or no stated confidence level; an SE computed with n = rows when the data are per customer; an AUC interval that goes above 1; a proportion's interval that goes below 0%; 50 bootstrap resamples behind a 95% interval; no random seed, so nothing reproduces.

      Spot itRecompute one SE by hand from the formula in Part 2. If the code resamples rows, check that rows really are the independent unit.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the Part 2 formulas.

    1. A reviewer samples 400 complaints and finds 12 were wrongly closed. What are the estimate, the SE and the Wald 95% interval? How many complaints would halve the width?

    p̂ = 3%. SE = √(0.03 × 0.97 / 400) ≈ 0.85 points. Wald interval: 3% ± 1.67 points ≈ 1.3% to 4.7%. Wilson gives 1.7% to 5.2%, shifted upwards. Halving the width needs four times the sample: 1,600 complaints.

    2. A KYC refresh team checks 300 files and finds no errors. What can you say?

    Not “the error rate is zero”. By the rule of three, the 95% upper bound is about 3/300 = 1%. The data are consistent with an error rate anywhere from 0% to about 1%.

    3. True or false: “there's a 95% probability that the true rate lies in this particular 95% confidence interval.”

    False, strictly. The 95% describes the procedure over repeated samples. A particular interval either contains the truth or doesn't. A Bayesian credible interval supports the probability statement, and with large samples and vague priors the two are numerically close.

    4. A campaign study covers 50 branches with 200 customers each. Outcomes within a branch have correlation 0.05. What's the effective sample size?

    Deff = 1 + (200 − 1) × 0.05 = 10.95. Effective n = 10,000 / 10.95 ≈ 910. A correlation that sounds tiny cuts the information by a factor of 11, because the clusters are large. And with only 50 clusters, a wild cluster bootstrap is worth considering.

    5. Someone bootstraps 250 daily operational losses to put an interval on the 99.9th percentile. What's wrong?

    Two things. The 99.9th percentile is beyond the data: 250 days contain on average a quarter of a 1-in-1,000-day event. And no resample can exceed the largest loss observed, so the interval's top is capped at the sample maximum. Days also aren't independent. Use an extreme value model for the tail, with its own uncertainty, and a block bootstrap if you bootstrap at all.

    6. Models A and B are scored on the same 5,000 transactions. Their separate 95% intervals for AUC overlap a lot. Can you conclude there's no difference?

    No. Overlap of separate intervals is the wrong test, and it ignores that both models scored the same cases. Their errors are positively correlated, so the SE of the difference is much smaller than the separate intervals suggest. Compute a paired bootstrap (or DeLong) interval for AUC(B) − AUC(A).

    7. Your bootstrap resample of 30 losses contains the largest loss in the sample. How unusual is that?

    Not unusual at all. The chance a resample omits a given point is (29/30)30 ≈ 36%, so about 64% of resamples include the maximum, often more than once. That's why bootstrap distributions of extremes pile up at the sample maximum.
