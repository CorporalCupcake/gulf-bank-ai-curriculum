    Baselines, and Knowing When Not to Use ML

    Every model result means nothing until you say what it beats. How to build the baseline that keeps you honest, how to tell whether the gain over it is real and worth its running cost, and when the right answer is a rule, a report or a fix to the process.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model's value is the gap between it and the best thing you could have done without it, measured with an interval and set against what it costs to own, so build the cheapest credible alternative first and use ML only where that gap clearly pays.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist presents a new card-fraud model: AUC 0.93, recall 64% at the agreed alert volume. The room is impressed. Then a risk manager asks: "What does the current rule catch at the same alert volume?" Nobody knows. It turns out the old rule catches 35%, and a simple logistic scorecard built in an afternoon catches 58%. The gradient-boosted model's real contribution is six points over the scorecard, not twenty-nine over the rule.

    That question is the whole of C1.3. A result is a difference, never a number on its own. "64% recall" is a fact about the model. "Six points better than the scorecard we could have shipped in a week" is a fact about the decision. The second is the one the bank pays for.

    C1.4 is the sibling idea. If the gap between the best simple approach and the model is small, or the model needs data you do not have, or nobody will act on its output, then the right answer is not a better model. It is a rule, a dashboard or a fixed process. Choosing that is not a failure of ambition; it is the work of someone who is paid for outcomes.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Most of the value usually sits on the lower rungs. Going from nothing to a decent rule, then to a scorecard, captures the large steps. The fancy model fights over what remains.
    • A small gain needs a lot of evidence. With few positives, a six-point difference is inside the noise. That is the same lesson as F2.7 and F3.5, now used to decide whether to ship.
    • Even a real gain has to pay for itself. A model needs monitoring, retraining, validation and someone on call. Those costs are real and continuous.
    Hold onto thisNever accept, or present, a model number without the baseline beside it, the interval around the gap, and the cost of ownership. If you do not know the baseline, building it is the first task, not an afterthought.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What counts as a baseline, and what makes a fair one

    A baseline is whatever the decision-maker would do if your model did not exist. There is more than one, and you should name them in order of ambition:

    RungExampleWhat it tells you
    Do nothingApprove everything, flag nothing, predict no churn.The size of the problem and the accuracy paradox (F3.5). Often free to compute.
    Naive rule of thumbForecast: same as last week. Collections: call the largest balance first.How much structure exists at all. For time series (F9) "same as last year" is brutal to beat.
    The incumbentThe existing fraud rules, the current credit policy, the analyst's own triage.What the bank actually does today. The only baseline whose gap equals real benefit.
    Simple modelLogistic regression with ten sensible features, a shallow tree.How much a few hours of modelling buys. Interpretable, easy to validate (F4.2).
    Strong standard modelTuned gradient boosting on the full features (F4.4).What mainstream tooling achieves. The bar a neural net or an LLM has to clear on tabular data.
    Human or vendorA senior analyst's judgement; a vendor model already licensed.Whether to build at all, and a ceiling check on label quality.

    A baseline is only fair if it plays by the same rules as the model. Five checks:

    • Same data and same time cut. Both see the same point-in-time features (F3.1) and are tested on the same forward-in-time window.
    • Same operating point. Compare recall at the same alert volume, or alerts at the same recall. Comparing a model at its best threshold with a rule at an arbitrary one is rigged.
    • Equal effort. If the model got a month of tuning and the baseline got a default setting, you measured effort, not method. Give the simple model the same feature work and a tuned threshold.
    • The incumbent as it really runs. Rules are often applied with analyst overrides and exceptions. Measure what happens in practice, not the policy document.
    • The same intervals. Report the gap with an interval (F2.1, F2.2), not two point estimates.

    The quantity to report is the incremental value:

    value of model=(benefitmodel−benefitbaseline)−extra cost of owning the model

    Benefit is measured in the bank's currency (frauds caught × loss avoided, minus review cost). The extra cost covers build, validation, monitoring, retraining and the people who handle exceptions. The model is worth shipping only if this is positive with reasonable confidence, not if it is merely positive in the point estimate. Simulation 1 puts an interval on the first bracket.

    How big a gap can you actually see?

    The uncertainty in a comparison is governed by the number of cases that decide it, usually the positives. For two independent recall estimates the standard error of the difference is

    SE(Δ)≈r1(1−r1)n+r2(1−r2)n

    r1 and r2 are the two recalls, n the number of frauds in the test set. At 58% and 64% with n = 500: √(0.000487 + 0.000461) = 3.1 points, so a 95% interval of about ±6 points around a 6-point gap. It touches zero. At n = 5,000 the interval shrinks to ±2 points and the gap is clear. Because both models score the same cases, a paired test (F2.2, bootstrap the cases and recompute the difference) is tighter than this formula, but the lesson holds: small gaps need many positives.

    Two practical rules follow. First, decide in advance how large a gap would change the decision (the minimum worthwhile gain), and check the interval against it rather than against zero. Second, if the interval is too wide to separate the options, say so. "These models are indistinguishable on 200 cases; I would ship the simpler one" is a legitimate, strong conclusion.

    The cost of owning a model

    A rule is a few lines that someone reads once a year. A model is a system. Sculley and colleagues called the extra burden hidden technical debt: data pipelines that must stay in step, features that silently change, feedback loops where the model's own decisions shape tomorrow's training data, and glue code around it. In a bank add model-risk management: documentation, independent validation, periodic review, monitoring thresholds, an owner who answers a regulator. I cannot tell you the exact rules for your bank; ask your model-risk team how the policy classifies a new model versus a rule change, because the effort differs.

    This is why the value calculation has two sides. Simulation 2 turns it into a break-even: how many decisions a month, and how much gain per decision, before a model's annual running cost is covered.

    Notice what the break-even says about where ML pays. It pays where decisions are frequent (millions of card transactions, tens of thousands of calls), or where each decision is worth a lot (a large corporate credit line), and where a baseline leaves real room. It rarely pays for a decision made twenty times a month with a small gain each, however clever the model.

    When not to use ML

    Go through these questions before any modelling. A "no" on most of them points elsewhere.

    QuestionIf the answer is no
    Can a precise rule state the answer?If the logic is known and exact (a policy cap, a legal list match, an arithmetic definition), write the rule. ML approximates things you cannot specify; do not approximate something you can.
    Is there an action someone will take on the output?A churn score with no approved retention offer, or an alert queue nobody works, produces nothing. Fix the action first.
    Is the pain really the process?Long statement waits caused by three manual approvals are removed by changing the approvals. A model that predicts the wait does not shorten it.
    Do you have enough labelled history?Thirty defaults in a niche portfolio cannot train or validate a model (F3.5). Use expert judgement, a simple scorecard built on policy, or a monitored rule.
    Is the future like the past?New products, new regulation or a regime shift break learned patterns. Rules and scenarios may be safer than a model trained on a world that has ended.
    Must you explain every decision exactly?Where the bank must give a precise reason for a decision, a transparent method may be required or at least far easier to defend (F8, G3). Check the actual requirement rather than assuming.
    Does the question need an answer, not a prediction?"How many dormant accounts do we have?" is a query. "Which branch had the most complaints?" is a report. Counting is not learning.
    Does the gain beat the cost, with an interval?Simulations 1 and 2. If it does not, ship the baseline and spend the effort elsewhere.

    None of this is anti-ML. Often the right design is a hybrid: rules for what is certain and mandated, a model to rank or triage what remains, and a human for the exceptions. Transaction monitoring works this way (B2.1): hard rules for typologies the regulator names, a model to prioritise alerts. And a baseline can be a stepping stone: the rule you ship today produces the labelled outcomes that make a model possible next year.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Baselines and the decision to use ML run through almost every other lesson. These are the closest links.

    C1.1 · C1.2 · Framing a requestBefore the baseline comes the question

    A baseline needs a clear target, decision and success measure. Framing gives you those; this lesson tests whether ML clears them.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataDo-nothing wins on accuracy

    The accuracy paradox is the simplest baseline lesson: the do-nothing model scores 99.5%. Compare at a stated operating point instead.

    F2.1 · F2.2 · F2.7 · Intervals and powerIs the gap real?

    The gap over a baseline is an estimate with an interval. Power tells you how many positives you needed to see it.

    F4.2 · F4.4 · Classical modelsThe strong simple baselines

    Regularised logistic regression and tuned boosted trees are the baselines that more complex models must beat on tabular data.

    F3.1 · Point-in-time correctnessA leaked model beats every baseline

    An implausibly large gain over a baseline is the first symptom of leakage. Check it before celebrating.

    B2.1 · Rules versus ML in monitoringThe incumbent is a rule set

    In AML and fraud the baseline is a live rule engine, and the realistic design is hybrid.

    B1.1 · Card fraudGains at the same alert volume

    Fraud model comparisons only mean something at the review capacity the team really has.

    F7 · Evaluation and monitoringChoosing the operating point

    A fair comparison needs the same threshold logic for both sides, derived from the cost of errors.

    C2 · Communicating resultsLead with the comparison

    The executive summary opens with the decision and the gap over the baseline, not the model's score.

    C2.3 · Writing for validatorsBenchmarks in validation

    Validators expect a documented challenger and benchmark. A good baseline section answers their first question.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Fraud and AML. The incumbent rules are the baseline, and lift over them at fixed analyst capacity is the number that matters.
    • Credit. The existing scorecard or policy rules, and in a regulated setting the simple model that supervisors already understand. A complex model has to justify its extra validation burden.
    • Customer service and complaints. A keyword rule for routing Arabic and English complaints is a legitimate baseline for a text classifier or an LLM, and sometimes the winner.
    • Forecasting. Seasonal naive (same day last week or last year) for branch cash demand and call volumes. Many complex forecasters barely beat it.
    • Reporting and management information. Plenty of "AI requests" are dashboards. Spotting that early saves everyone months.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The principle is old and keeps being re-learned. The current debates are whether new tabular foundation models and LLMs change which baseline is "strong", and whether reported gains in published work survive honest baselines.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with one web search; older items are cited from memory and should be verified before you quote them in a formal document.

    1. 2006
      The illusion of progress

      David Hand argued that in supervised classification, simple methods capture most of the achievable gain, and that the extra performance of sophisticated methods is often swamped by data issues, drift and the way the comparison was set up.

      Hand, Statistical Science, 2006
    2. 2015
      Hidden technical debt

      The ownership cost of ML systems (entanglement, pipeline jungles, feedback loops) was laid out by Google engineers and remains the standard reference for why a model is more than its accuracy.

      Sculley et al., NeurIPS, 2015
    3. 2017+
      "Don't be afraid to launch without machine learning"

      Google's Rules of Machine Learning put it as rule one: ship a heuristic first, because it gives you data, a product and a baseline.

      Zinkevich, Rules of Machine Learning, Google
    4. 2019
      No benefit over logistic regression in clinical prediction

      A systematic review of 71 studies found no performance benefit of ML over logistic regression where the comparison was at low risk of bias. The medical setting differs from banking; the pattern of inflated gains over weak baselines is the same.

      Christodoulou et al., J Clin Epidemiol, 2019
    5. 2019
      Interpretable by design

      Cynthia Rudin argued that for high-stakes decisions, one should use inherently interpretable models, noting that on structured data with meaningful features simple models often match black boxes.

      Rudin, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019
    6. 2022
      Trees still beat deep learning on tabular data

      A large benchmark found tree-based models remained state of the art on medium-sized tabular data, even with tuning budgets given to neural nets. The strong baseline is boosted trees.

      Grinsztajn, Oyallon, Varoquaux, NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2022
    7. 2023
      Leakage inflates the gain over baselines

      A survey across scientific fields found data leakage in hundreds of papers, in many cases making ML appear to beat simpler baselines that it did not beat once the leak was fixed.

      Kapoor & Narayanan, Patterns, 2023
    8. 2025–26
      Tabular foundation models: a new baseline to know

      TabPFN, a pretrained transformer for tabular data, was reported in Nature to beat tuned tree ensembles on small datasets (up to about 10,000 rows) with no per-dataset training. Its developers' later report (TabPFN-2.5) claims leading results on the TabArena benchmark; that is vendor-reported. An independent 2026 comparison on twelve clinical binary tasks found it beat the best conventional model in only about 17% of tasks, with most AUROC differences within 0.02, at higher compute cost. The honest reading: a promising extra baseline for small data, not a reason to skip gradient boosting.

      Hollmann et al., Nature, 2025; Prior Labs, arXiv 2511.08667; BMC Med Inform Decis Mak, 2026 (as reported in search results; not read in full)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in bank projects and in AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • No baseline at all

      "The model achieves 91% accuracy" with nothing to compare it to.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what, at what operating point? If the answer is silence, the result is not yet a result.
    • A straw-man baseline

      The comparison is a default-setting model or an old rule never meant for this task, while the new model had weeks of tuning.

      Spot itAsk who tuned each and for how long. Ask what a senior analyst with a spreadsheet would have built.
    • Comparing at different operating points

      Model recall at 5,000 alerts against rule recall at 2,800.

      Spot itCheck the alert volume or threshold on both sides. Put them on the same curve.
    • Point estimates for a small gap

      "Model B beats A by 2 points" on 150 positives.

      Spot itAsk for the interval. If it includes zero, the honest statement is "no demonstrated difference".
    • A gap too good to be true

      AUC 0.99 against a baseline of 0.75 on a hard problem.

      Spot itSuspect leakage or a label that contains the answer before suspecting brilliance (F3.1). Check the most important feature.
    • Ignoring the cost of ownership

      The business case counts the gain and omits monitoring, validation, retraining and support.

      Spot itAsk who owns it in year two, what it costs to run, and what the validation effort is.
    • ML because it is the brief

      A team is asked for "an AI model" for a problem a query or a policy change would solve.

      Spot itAsk what decision changes, who acts, and what they would do without the model. Walk the questions in Part 2.
    • A baseline measured on the model's blind spot

      The existing rules generated the labels, so on historic data the rules look excellent or the model looks excellent by copying them (F2.6).

      Spot itAsk how labels were produced. Prefer a randomly sampled review set, or a live shadow run.
    • Ignoring the baseline that improves too

      The comparison uses last year's rules, while the incumbent has since been tightened.

      Spot itCheck the baseline's dates and version.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The new model catches 64% of fraud at the alert volume the team can handle. The current rules catch 35%, and a simple scorecard we could ship quickly catches 58%. So most of the benefit comes from the simple step, and the extra from the advanced model is about six points, which I can't yet separate from noise. I'd ship the scorecard now, run the advanced model in shadow for a quarter to get the evidence, and decide on the cost of running it then.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I benchmark against the incumbent as it really operates and against a tuned regularised logistic model, on the same point-in-time features, forward-in-time split and operating point set from the cost ratio. I report the gap with a paired bootstrap interval and decide against a minimum worthwhile gain agreed beforehand. I add the cost of ownership to the business case, and I treat a very large gain over a baseline as a leakage warning until shown otherwise.”
    “Why are we building a model when a rule would do?”

    Maybe we shouldn't be. Let me check three things: does the rule reach the recall we need at our review capacity, does it keep up as patterns change, and what does the model add with an interval around it. If the rule is close and the model costs a lot to own, we ship the rule and keep collecting labels.

    “Is a 3-point improvement worth deploying?”

    It depends on volume and value per decision, and on whether we can show it is real. Three points of recall on 2,000 monthly frauds at AED 400 is about AED 24,000 a month. If the model costs more than that to run, no. If the interval on 3 points includes zero, we haven't shown it exists. Show me the cost and the interval.

    “The vendor says their AI beats our rules by 40%.”

    Beats them on whose data, at what alert volume, against which version of our rules? I'd ask for a shadow run on our live traffic with our analysts' outcomes, a comparison at our capacity, and the interval. Vendor numbers tend to be measured on easier data against weaker baselines.

    “Can't we just use an LLM for this?”

    Perhaps, as one candidate. For text tasks such as complaint routing, a prompted LLM is a fair baseline next to a keyword rule and a trained classifier. We compare on a labelled sample in Arabic and English, with cost per item and latency, and choose the cheapest option that meets the accuracy bar. For tabular risk scoring, boosted trees remain the baseline to beat.

    “We have only 30 defaults. Should we say ML isn't possible?”

    A trained model, no: we couldn't validate it. But we can still build an expert scorecard from policy and domain knowledge, monitor it, and capture the outcomes in a way that lets us train a model when there's enough history. The answer is a staged one, not a refusal.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. List the baselines you would build for a new fraud model, in order, and say which one the business actually cares about.

    Do nothing, a naive rule, the incumbent rule set, a simple tuned logistic model, a strong tuned boosted model. The incumbent as it really runs is the one whose gap equals real benefit; the simple model tells you whether complexity is needed.

    2. A model catches 640 of 1,000 frauds with 4,000 alerts. A rule catches 350 with 2,800 alerts. At AED 400 per fraud and AED 40 per alert, what is the monthly gain? (Use these as the month's totals.)

    Model: 640 × 400 − 4,000 × 40 = 256,000 − 160,000 = 96,000. Rule: 350 × 400 − 2,800 × 40 = 140,000 − 112,000 = 28,000. Gain = AED 68,000. The model also needs more alerts, so compare at the same volume if capacity is limited.

    3. Two models have recall 0.58 and 0.64 on 500 frauds. Is the gap real?

    SE of the difference ≈ √(0.58×0.42/500 + 0.64×0.36/500) ≈ 3.1 points, so the 95% interval is about 6 ± 6 points. It touches zero: not demonstrated. A paired bootstrap would be somewhat tighter, but you would still want more positives.

    4. A model saves AED 8 per decision and there are 6,000 decisions a month. It costs AED 500,000 a year to own. Does it pay? What is the break-even volume?

    Value = 6,000 × 12 × 8 = AED 576,000, against 500,000: net +76,000, ratio 1.15. Break-even volume = 500,000 ÷ (12 × 8) ≈ 5,208 decisions a month. It pays, but with little margin; a modest fall in gain or volume erases it.

    5. A new model has AUC 0.995 against a rule's 0.78 on a hard AML problem. Your first reaction?

    Suspect leakage or a label that encodes the answer (for example features created after the alert was raised, or labels generated by the rules themselves). Check the top features, the split, and whether the test set is forward in time before believing it.

    6. Give three reasons you might decline to build a model.

    Any three of: an exact rule exists; no one can act on the output; the real problem is the process; too few labelled events; the gain over the baseline is small relative to the cost; the future will not resemble the past; the decision requires an exact stated reason and a simpler method is safer.

    7. Why can "ship the rule now" be the best route to an eventual model?

    The rule delivers value immediately, becomes the baseline, and generates the outcomes and labels (ideally with a random review sample to avoid selection bias) that a later model needs. It also surfaces the data and process problems early.
