    Baselines, and Knowing When Not to Use ML

    A model result means nothing until you say what it beats. Build the baseline, test whether the gain is real and worth its running cost, and know when the answer is a rule, a report or a process fix.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model's value is the gap between it and the best thing you could have done without it, measured with an interval and set against what it costs to own, so build the cheapest credible alternative first and use ML only where that gap clearly pays.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A 10k time of 45 minutes means nothing until you know your last one. A model result is the same: it is a difference, never a number on its own.

    Example: a card-fraud model shows AUC 0.93 and 64% recall at the agreed alert volume. A risk manager asks what the current rule catches at the same volume. It catches 35%, and a scorecard built in an afternoon catches 58%. The model's real contribution is six points over the scorecard, not twenty-nine over the rule. "64% recall" is a fact about the model; "six points better than what we could ship in a week" is a fact about the decision, and the bank pays for the second.

    The sibling idea (C1.4): if the gap over the best simple approach is small, the data is missing, or nobody will act on the output, the answer is a rule, a dashboard or a process fix, not a better model.

    • Most value sits on the lower rungs. Nothing to a decent rule to a scorecard captures the big steps. The fancy model fights over the rest.
    • Small gains need lots of evidence. With few positives, six points is inside the noise (F2.7, F3.5).
    • Real gains must pay for themselves. Monitoring, retraining, validation and on-call support are continuous costs.
    Hold onto thisNever present a model number without the baseline beside it, an interval on the gap, and the cost of ownership. If you do not know the baseline, building it is the first task.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What counts as a baseline, and what makes one fair

    A baseline is what the decision-maker would do without your model. Name the rungs in order of ambition:

    RungExampleWhat it tells you
    Do nothingApprove everything, predict no churn.Size of the problem; the accuracy paradox (F3.5).
    Naive ruleForecast: same as last week. Collections: largest balance first.How much structure exists. "Same as last year" is brutal to beat in time series (F9).
    The incumbentExisting fraud rules, current credit policy, analyst triage.What the bank does today. The only baseline whose gap equals real benefit.
    Simple modelLogistic regression on ten sensible features, shallow tree.What a few hours of modelling buys. Easy to validate (F4.2).
    Strong standard modelTuned gradient boosting (F4.4).The bar a neural net or LLM must clear on tabular data.
    Human or vendorSenior analyst; a licensed vendor model.Whether to build at all; a ceiling check on label quality.

    A fair baseline plays by the model's rules, like racing two runners on the same track:

    • Same data and time cut. Same point-in-time features (F3.1), same forward-in-time test window.
    • Same operating point. Compare recall at equal alert volume, or alerts at equal recall.
    • Equal effort. If the model got a month of tuning and the baseline defaults, you measured effort, not method.
    • The incumbent as it really runs, with analyst overrides, not the policy document.
    • An interval on the gap (F2.1, F2.2), not two point estimates.

    Report the incremental value. Read it as: what the bank gains over doing the simple thing, after paying for the machine.

    value of model=(benefitmodel−benefitbaseline)−extra cost of owning the model

    Benefit is in AED (frauds caught × loss avoided, minus review cost). Ownership cost covers build, validation, monitoring, retraining and exception handling. Ship only if this is positive with reasonable confidence, not just in the point estimate.

    How big a gap can you actually see?

    Like telling two similar coins apart, you need many flips. Uncertainty is set by the cases that decide the comparison, usually the positives. For two independent recalls:

    SE(Δ)≈r1(1−r1)n+r2(1−r2)n

    r1, r2 are the two recalls; n is the number of frauds in the test set. At 58% and 64% with n = 500: √(0.000487 + 0.000461) = 3.1 points, so a 95% interval of about ±6 around a 6-point gap. It touches zero. At n = 5,000 it shrinks to ±2 and the gap is clear. A paired bootstrap (F2.2) on the same cases is tighter, but small gaps still need many positives.

    • Decide the minimum worthwhile gain in advance and check the interval against it, not just against zero.
    • If the interval cannot separate the options, say so. "Indistinguishable on 200 cases; I would ship the simpler one" is a strong conclusion.

    The cost of owning a model

    A rule is a few lines read once a year. A model is a system, like owning a car versus taking a taxi: the purchase price is the small part. Sculley et al. called it hidden technical debt: pipelines that must stay in step, features that silently change, feedback loops where the model's decisions shape tomorrow's training data. In a bank add model-risk management: documentation, independent validation, periodic review, monitoring thresholds, an owner who answers to a regulator. Ask your model-risk team how policy treats a new model versus a rule change; the effort differs.

    Simulation 2 turns this into a break-even: how many decisions, and how much gain per decision, before the annual running cost is covered.

    ML pays where decisions are frequent (millions of transactions) or each is worth a lot (a large corporate credit line), and the baseline leaves room. It rarely pays for twenty decisions a month with a small gain each, however clever the model.

    When not to use ML

    Ask these before modelling. A "no" on most points elsewhere.

    QuestionIf the answer is no
    Can a precise rule state the answer?Write the rule (a policy cap, a legal list match). ML approximates what you cannot specify.
    Will someone act on the output?A churn score with no retention offer produces nothing. Fix the action first.
    Is the pain really the process?Slow statements caused by three manual approvals are fixed by removing approvals, not by predicting the wait.
    Enough labelled history?Thirty defaults cannot train or validate a model (F3.5). Use expert judgement, a policy scorecard, or a monitored rule.
    Is the future like the past?New products, regulation or regime shifts break learned patterns. Rules and scenarios may be safer.
    Must you explain every decision exactly?A transparent method may be required or easier to defend (F8, G3). Check the actual requirement.
    Do you need an answer, not a prediction?"How many dormant accounts?" is a query; "Which branch had most complaints?" is a report. Counting is not learning.
    Does the gain beat the cost, with an interval?Simulations 1 and 2. If not, ship the baseline.

    This is not anti-ML. Often the best design is a hybrid: rules for what is certain and mandated, a model to triage the rest, a human for exceptions. Transaction monitoring works this way (B2.1). A shipped rule also produces the labelled outcomes that make a model possible next year.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • No baseline at all

      "The model achieves 91% accuracy", compared with nothing.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what, at what operating point? Silence means no result yet.
    • A straw-man baseline

      A default-setting model or old rule, against weeks of tuning on the new model.

      Spot itAsk who tuned each, and what a senior analyst with a spreadsheet would build.
    • Different operating points

      Model recall at 5,000 alerts against rule recall at 2,800.

      Spot itCheck alert volume or threshold on both sides; put them on the same curve.
    • Point estimates for a small gap

      "B beats A by 2 points" on 150 positives.

      Spot itAsk for the interval. If it includes zero, say "no demonstrated difference".
    • A gap too good to be true

      AUC 0.99 against 0.75 on a hard problem.

      Spot itSuspect leakage or a label containing the answer before brilliance (F3.1). Check the top feature.
    • Ignoring the cost of ownership

      The case counts gain and omits monitoring, validation, retraining and support.

      Spot itAsk who owns it in year two and what it costs to run and validate.
    • ML because it is the brief

      "An AI model" requested for something a query or policy change would solve.

      Spot itAsk what decision changes, who acts, and what they do without the model. Walk the Part 2 questions.
    • Labels made by the baseline

      The existing rules generated the labels, so the rules look excellent, or the model does by copying them (F2.6).

      Spot itAsk how labels were produced. Prefer a random review sample or a live shadow run.
    • A stale baseline

      Comparison uses last year's rules, but the incumbent has since been tightened.

      Spot itCheck the baseline's date and version.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. List the baselines for a new fraud model, in order, and say which the business cares about.

    Do nothing, naive rule, incumbent rules, simple tuned logistic model, strong tuned boosted model. The incumbent as it really runs: its gap equals real benefit. The simple model shows whether complexity is needed.

    2. A model catches 640 of 1,000 frauds with 4,000 alerts. A rule catches 350 with 2,800 alerts. At AED 400 per fraud and AED 40 per alert, what is the monthly gain? (Treat these as the month's totals.)

    Model: 640 × 400 − 4,000 × 40 = 96,000. Rule: 350 × 400 − 2,800 × 40 = 28,000. Gain = AED 68,000. The model uses more alerts, so compare at equal volume if capacity is limited.

    3. Two models have recall 0.58 and 0.64 on 500 frauds. Is the gap real?

    SE ≈ √(0.58×0.42/500 + 0.64×0.36/500) ≈ 3.1 points, so the 95% interval is about 6 ± 6. It touches zero: not demonstrated. A paired bootstrap is tighter, but you still want more positives.

    4. A model saves AED 8 per decision, 6,000 decisions a month, and costs AED 500,000 a year to own. Does it pay? What is the break-even volume?

    Value = 6,000 × 12 × 8 = AED 576,000 against 500,000: net +76,000, ratio 1.15. Break-even = 500,000 ÷ (12 × 8) ≈ 5,208 decisions a month. It pays, but a small fall in gain or volume erases it.

    5. A new model has AUC 0.995 against a rule's 0.78 on a hard AML problem. First reaction?

    Suspect leakage or a label that encodes the answer (features created after the alert, or labels generated by the rules). Check top features, the split, and that the test set is forward in time.

    6. Give three reasons to decline to build a model.

    Any three: an exact rule exists; no one can act on the output; the problem is the process; too few labelled events; small gain relative to cost; the future will not resemble the past; an exact stated reason is required and a simpler method is safer.

    7. Why can "ship the rule now" be the best route to an eventual model?

    The rule delivers value now, becomes the baseline, and generates outcomes and labels (ideally with a random review sample to avoid selection bias). It also surfaces data and process problems early.
