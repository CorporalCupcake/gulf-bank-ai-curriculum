    Baselines, and When Not to Use ML

    Part 1

    What a baseline is

    In one sentenceA baseline is what would happen without your model: the current process, or the simplest thing you could build quickly. Your model's value is the gap above it.

    "The model catches 64%" is a fact about the model. "It catches six points more than a scorecard we could ship in a week" is a fact about the decision. A 10k time of 45 minutes means nothing until you know your last one.

    How it differs from similar things

    • A baseline is the thing you would otherwise do. A benchmark is a public score on a standard dataset. Only the baseline tells you if your project is worth doing.
    • A metric such as accuracy is one number. A baseline gives it a meaning, because "91%" is poor if doing nothing scores 90%.
    Part 2

    How to compare fairly

    Climb the ladder in this order. Most of the value usually sits on the lower rungs.

    1. Do nothing. Approve everything, predict no churn. This shows the size of the problem.
    2. Naive rule. "Same as last week" or "largest balance first". Often hard to beat for forecasts.
    3. The incumbent. Today's fraud rules or credit policy. Its gap to your model is the real benefit.
    4. Simple model. A logistic regression on ten sensible features. This is what a few hours of modelling buys.
    5. Strong standard model. Tuned gradient boosting, the usual bar on tabular data.

    Then race the two on the same track:

    • Same data and time cut, so neither sees the future.
    • Same operating point. Compare recall at equal alert volume, or put both in money (frauds caught × loss avoided, minus alerts × review cost).
    • Equal effort. Run the incumbent as it really runs, or you measured effort, not method.

    How to read the gap

    Two things can make a gap look bigger than it is: too few positives, and ignoring running costs.

    • Few positives mean a wide margin. Recall of 58% against 64% on 500 frauds is uncertain by about 6 points either way, so the gap could be zero. On 5,000 frauds it shrinks to about 2 points and the gap is clear.
    • If the interval touches zero, say "no demonstrated difference" and ship the simpler option. Decide the smallest worthwhile gain in advance.
    • A model is a system, not a file. Pipelines, monitoring, retraining, and in a bank independent validation and an owner who answers to the regulator. A rule is a few lines read once a year.
    • Volume matters. Saving AED 8 per decision over 6,000 decisions a month is about AED 576,000 a year. Against AED 500,000 a year to own it, that is marginal, not a clear yes.

    Too good to be true? An AUC of 0.99 against a rule's 0.75 usually means leakage, or labels produced by the old rules themselves. Check the top feature and how labels were made.

    Show the formula

    Value of the model = (benefit of model − benefit of baseline) − extra cost of owning the model. Ship only if this is positive with reasonable confidence, not just in the best guess.

    Part 3

    When not to use ML

    Ask these before you model anything. A "no" points somewhere else.

    QuestionIf the answer is no
    Can a precise rule state the answer?Write the rule. ML is for what you cannot specify.
    Will someone act on the output?A churn score with no retention offer produces nothing. Fix the action first.
    Is the real pain the process?Slow statements caused by manual approvals are fixed by removing approvals.
    Is there enough labelled history?Thirty defaults cannot train a model. Use expert judgement.
    Do you need an answer, not a prediction?"How many dormant accounts?" is a query. Counting is not learning.
    Does the gain beat the cost, with an interval?Ship the baseline.

    This is not anti-ML. Often the best design is a hybrid: rules for what is mandated, a model to triage the rest, a human for exceptions. Shipping the rule now can also be the best route to a model later: it delivers value, becomes the baseline, and creates the labels a model needs.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    OptionStrong atWeak at
    Rule or queryCheap, exact, easy to explain and auditCannot capture patterns you cannot write down
    Simple model (scorecard, logistic regression)Most of the gain for little effort and costMisses complex interactions
    Boosted trees or deep modelSqueezes out the last points when volume is highCostly to own; gain may sit inside the noise
    Fix the processRemoves the cause, not the symptomNeeds people to change how they work

    In banking, a model that sits on a model-risk register needs documentation, independent validation and monitoring, so its gain over a simple rule has to pay for all of that.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • No baseline, or a weak one

      "91% accuracy" compared with nothing, or with an untuned, out-of-date rule. Ask: compared with what, tuned by whom?

    • Different operating points

      Model recall at 5,000 alerts against rule recall at 2,800. Check alert volume on both sides.

    • Point estimates for small gaps

      "B beats A by 2 points" on 150 positives. Ask for the interval.

    • Ignoring the cost of ownership

      The case counts gain and omits monitoring, validation and retraining. Ask who owns it in year two.

    1. List the baselines for a new fraud model, in order. Which matters most to the business?

    Do nothing, naive rule, incumbent, simple model, strong model. The incumbent: its gap is the real benefit.

    2. A model catches more fraud than the rule but sends more alerts. How do you tell if it is better?

    Put both in money (frauds caught × loss avoided, minus alerts × review cost), or compare at the same alert volume.

    3. Two models have recall 0.58 and 0.64 on 500 frauds. Is the gap real?

    Not demonstrated. The uncertainty is about 6 points either way, so the interval touches zero. You need more positives.

    4. A new model has AUC 0.995 against a rule's 0.78. First reaction?

    Suspect leakage or a label that encodes the answer. Check the top features, the split, and that the test set is later in time.

    5. Give three reasons to decline to build a model.

    Any three: an exact rule exists, nobody can act on the output, the problem is the process, too few labelled events, or the gain is small relative to cost.
