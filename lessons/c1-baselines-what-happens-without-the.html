    Baselines, and Knowing When Not to Use ML

    A model result means nothing until you say what it beats. Build the baseline, test whether the gain is real and worth its running cost, and know when the answer is a rule, a report or a process fix.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model's value is the gap between it and the best thing you could have done without it, measured with an interval and set against what it costs to own, so build the cheapest credible alternative first and use ML only where that gap clearly pays.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A 10k time of 45 minutes means nothing until you know your last one. A model result is the same: it is a difference, never a number on its own.

    Example: a fraud model catches 64% at the agreed alert volume. The current rule catches 35%; a scorecard built in an afternoon catches 58%. The model's real contribution is six points, not twenty-nine. "64%" is a fact about the model; "six points better than what we could ship in a week" is a fact about the decision.

    The sibling idea (C1.4): if the gap is small, the data is missing, or nobody will act on the output, the answer is a rule, a report or a process fix.

    • Most value sits on the lower rungs. A decent rule and a scorecard capture the big steps; the fancy model fights over the rest.
    • Small gains need lots of evidence. With few positives, six points is inside the noise (F2.7, F3.5).
    • Real gains must pay for themselves. Monitoring, retraining and validation are continuous costs.
    Hold onto thisNever present a model number without the baseline beside it, an interval on the gap, and the cost of ownership. If you do not know the baseline, building it is the first task.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What counts as a baseline, and what makes one fair

    A baseline is what the decision-maker would do without your model. The rungs, in order of ambition:

    RungExampleWhat it tells you
    Do nothingApprove everything, predict no churn.Size of the problem.
    Naive ruleSame as last week; largest balance first.How much structure exists. Often brutal to beat in time series (F9).
    The incumbentExisting fraud rules, credit policy.The only baseline whose gap equals real benefit.
    Simple modelLogistic regression on ten sensible features.What a few hours of modelling buys.
    Strong standard modelTuned gradient boosting (F4.4).The bar a neural net must clear on tabular data.

    A fair baseline plays by the model's rules, like racing two runners on the same track:

    • Same data and time cut (F3.1).
    • Same operating point: recall at equal alert volume.
    • Equal effort, with the incumbent as it really runs. Otherwise you measured effort, not method.

    Report the incremental value over the simple thing, after paying for the machine, with an interval (F2.1).

    value of model=(benefitmodel−benefitbaseline)−extra cost of owning the model

    Ship only if this is positive with reasonable confidence, not just in the point estimate.

    How big a gap can you actually see?

    Like telling two similar coins apart, you need many flips. What limits you is the number of positives (the frauds, the defaults). Recalls of 58% and 64% on 500 frauds leave the gap uncertain by about 6 points either way, so it touches zero. On 5,000 frauds that shrinks to about 2 and the gap is clear.

    • Decide the minimum worthwhile gain in advance and check the interval against it, not just zero.
    • If the interval cannot separate the options, say so and ship the simpler one.

    The cost of owning a model

    A rule is a few lines read once a year. A model is a system, like owning a car versus taking a taxi: the purchase price is the small part. Pipelines must stay in step, features change silently, and in a bank you add model-risk management: documentation, independent validation, monitoring and an owner who answers to a regulator.

    Simulation 2 finds the break-even: how many decisions, at what gain each, cover the annual cost.

    ML pays where decisions are frequent or each is worth a lot, and the baseline leaves room. It rarely pays for twenty decisions a month with a small gain each, however clever the model.

    When not to use ML

    Ask these before modelling. A "no" points elsewhere.

    QuestionIf the answer is no
    Can a precise rule state the answer?Write the rule. ML approximates what you cannot specify.
    Will someone act on the output?A churn score with no retention offer produces nothing. Fix the action first.
    Is the pain really the process?Slow statements caused by manual approvals are fixed by removing approvals.
    Enough labelled history?Thirty defaults cannot train a model (F3.5). Use expert judgement.
    Do you need an answer, not a prediction?"How many dormant accounts?" is a query. Counting is not learning.
    Does the gain beat the cost, with an interval?Ship the baseline (Simulations 1, 2).

    This is not anti-ML. Often the best design is a hybrid: rules for what is mandated, a model to triage the rest, a human for exceptions (B2.1).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • No baseline, or a weak one

      "91% accuracy" compared with nothing, or with an untuned or out-of-date rule.

      Spot itAsk: compared with what, tuned by whom, as of which version?
    • Different operating points

      Model recall at 5,000 alerts against rule recall at 2,800.

      Spot itCheck alert volume on both sides.
    • Point estimates for a small gap

      "B beats A by 2 points" on 150 positives.

      Spot itAsk for the interval. If it includes zero, say "no demonstrated difference".
    • A gap too good to be true, or circular

      AUC 0.99 against 0.75, or labels produced by the existing rules themselves.

      Spot itSuspect leakage or a label containing the answer (F3.1, F2.6). Check the top feature and how labels were made.
    • Ignoring the cost of ownership

      The case counts gain and omits monitoring, validation, retraining and support.

      Spot itAsk who owns it in year two.
    • ML because it is the brief

      "An AI model" requested for something a query or policy change would solve.

      Spot itAsk what decision changes, who acts, and what they do without the model.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. List the baselines for a new fraud model, in order, and say which the business cares about.

    Do nothing, naive rule, incumbent, simple model, strong model. The incumbent matters most: its gap is the real benefit.

    2. A model catches more fraud than the rule but sends more alerts. How do you tell whether it is really better?

    Put both in money: frauds caught times the loss avoided, minus alerts times the review cost. Or compare them at the same alert volume if review capacity is fixed.

    3. Two models have recall 0.58 and 0.64 on 500 frauds. Is the gap real?

    Not demonstrated. With 500 positives the uncertainty on the gap is about 6 points either way, so the interval touches zero. You need many more positives.

    4. A model saves AED 8 per decision, 6,000 decisions a month, and costs AED 500,000 a year to own. Does it pay, and how comfortable should you be?

    It pays: about AED 576,000 of value against 500,000 of cost. But the margin is thin, so a small fall in gain or volume erases it, and the gain itself is uncertain. Treat it as marginal, not a clear yes.

    5. A new model has AUC 0.995 against a rule's 0.78 on a hard AML problem. First reaction?

    Suspect leakage or a label that encodes the answer. Check the top features, the split, and that the test set is forward in time.

    6. Give three reasons to decline to build a model.

    Any three: an exact rule exists; no one can act on the output; the problem is the process; too few labelled events; the gain is small relative to cost; the future will not resemble the past.

    7. Why can "ship the rule now" be the best route to an eventual model?

    The rule delivers value now, becomes the baseline, and generates the outcomes and labels a model needs later. It also surfaces data and process problems early.
