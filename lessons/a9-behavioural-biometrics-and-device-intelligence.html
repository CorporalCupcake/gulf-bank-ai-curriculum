    Behavioural Biometrics and Device Intelligence

    Part 1

    What it is

    In one sentenceBehavioural biometrics and device intelligence check who is holding the phone, by quietly measuring how the app is used and what the device is, instead of relying on passwords alone.

    The problem it solves: a customer in Dubai logs in at 9 pm and sends AED 48,000 to a new payee. The username, password and one-time code are all correct. Either a scammer coached the customer, or a thief with a stolen phone did it alone. A password cannot tell the difference, because it does not know who is typing.

    How it differs from other checks

    • Passwords and codes prove you know something. Behavioural signals look at how you act, and the customer does nothing extra.
    • Fingerprint or face ID checks once, at the door. Behavioural checks run through the whole session.
    • Transaction rules look at the payment (amount, payee). These look at the person and device making it. The two are used together.
    Part 2

    How it works

    Think of a bank teller who notices that you are not your usual self: wrong pace, unfamiliar phone. The system does the same, in six steps.

    1. Collect. The app records signals passively while the customer uses it.
    2. Compare. Each signal is compared with the customer's own past sessions, or with a population model for new customers who have no history.
    3. Judge each signal. Ask: how much more likely is this signal in a takeover than in a genuine session? That ratio is the likelihood ratio (LR). An LR of 30 means thirty times more likely.
    4. Combine. Multiply the odds by each LR, starting from the base rate (how common takeover is in this kind of session).
    5. Score and route. The result is a probability that sets the response.
    6. Learn. Confirmed cases feed back into the models, though confirmed labels are few and arrive late.
    Part 3

    How to read the score

    One signal is a whisper. A single keystroke gap tells you almost nothing, because the customer and a stranger overlap heavily, just as one coin flip cannot show a coin is rigged but a hundred can. Signals only become useful in numbers and in combination.

    • More events help, with diminishing returns. Four times the keystrokes buys only twice the separation between customer and imposter.
    • A login has few events. A PIN is six keystrokes, so systems score the whole session, not one screen.
    • Different kinds of evidence combine. One muddy footprint proves nothing. Footprint plus motive plus no alibi adds up.

    Reading it

    • 3% looks tiny but is enough to act. It is 150 times the base rate. It justifies an extra step, not a block.
    • Typical bands (examples): below 1% allow, 1% to 20% step up (ask for more proof), above 20% hold for review. A bank sets these from costs and review capacity.
    • Route, do not block. A step-up is cheap and recoverable. A wrongly blocked customer is not.
    • Beware overlap. A remote-access tool, a pasted payee and long pauses are three views of one event: a stranger coaching the customer. Multiplying all three counts one clue three times and overstates the risk.
    Show the formula

    Posterior odds = prior odds × LR1 × LR2 × … × LRk. The prior odds are the base rate expressed as odds (1 in 5,000 is about 0.0002). Each LR is how much more common a signal is among takeovers than among genuine sessions. An LR below 1, such as a long-bound device, lowers the odds. Convert back with probability = odds ÷ (1 + odds). The multiplication is exact only for independent signals.

    Part 4

    When it works and when it does not

    ApproachChecksStrong atWeak at
    Password + codeWhat you knowCheap, familiarStolen or coached credentials
    Biometric loginWho you are, onceStopping a thief at the doorNothing after login
    Behavioural + deviceHow you act, all sessionSilent, continuous, many signalsWeak alone; privacy; adaptive attackers
    Transaction rulesThe payment itselfExplainable limitsCannot see who is typing

    Treat behavioural signals as a smoke detector, not a lock.

    • Adversaries adapt. Emulator farms, spoofing kits and replayed timings attack any published feature. Layer signals and use hardware attestation. Do not rely on secrecy.
    • Authorised scams are hard. When the real customer pays under pressure, their typing is genuine. Only context separates it: pauses, coaching, remote tools, a new payee.
    • False alarms hit real people. Injury, a new phone, travel or age can all trigger a flag. Test flag rates across customer groups.
    • It is personal data. Involve legal and privacy teams early, and store derived features, not raw sensor traces.

    In banking, these signals mainly decide whether a payment goes through, gets a step-up, or goes to a person, so they sit beside transaction rules, not instead of them.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating a score as proof

      A 3% takeover probability means 97 in 100 are genuine. Use it to route, not to block.

    • Multiplying overlapping clues

      Three signals that reflect one cause count as one clue. Counting them separately overstates certainty.

    • Ignoring the base rate

      Takeover is rare, so even strong evidence starts from a very low point.

    • Trusting a signal because it is secret

      Attackers probe and copy features. Layer signals instead.

    1. Why can a password not tell a coached victim from the real customer?

    Because the real customer does type it. A password proves knowledge, not intent or who is in control.

    2. Base rate 1 in 5,000. A new device (LR 5) and a behaviour mismatch (LR 30) appear. Roughly what is the risk?

    The odds multiply by 150, giving about 3%. Enough to step up, not to block.

    3. Why is it risky to multiply the LRs of remote access, pasted payee and long pauses?

    They are overlapping views of one cause, a coached victim. Multiplying counts one event three times.

    4. Why do systems score a whole session rather than one keystroke?

    One event barely separates customer from imposter. Many events add up, with diminishing returns.

    5. Why can a flag be wrong about a genuine customer?

    Injury, a new phone, travel or age change behaviour. That is why flags lead to a step-up, and flag rates should be tested across groups.
