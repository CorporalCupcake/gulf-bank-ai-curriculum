    Credit Risk: PD, LGD, EAD and Scorecards

    Part 1

    What credit risk is

    In one sentenceCredit risk is the chance a borrower does not repay, and banks measure it with three numbers multiplied together: how likely (PD), how much is owed (EAD) and how much is lost (LGD).

    The bank cannot know whether one customer will repay. It needs the average across thousands of similar loans, because that average sets the price, the provision (money set aside for losses) and the capital held.

    • PD (probability of default): how likely the borrower is to stop paying within a set period, usually 12 months.
    • EAD (exposure at default): what they will owe at that moment. A card owes whatever has been drawn by then, often more than today's balance.
    • LGD (loss given default): the share of that amount never recovered, after selling collateral (assets pledged against the loan) and paying costs.
    • Expected loss (EL): the average loss to plan for. It is a cost of doing business, like rent.

    Our running example: a bank lends AED 200,000 to a salaried customer, with PD 2% and LGD 45%. Every AED 100 lent carries 90 fils of expected loss, and the price must cover it. One loan usually loses nothing or a lot, never the average.

    Part 2

    How it differs from other models

    • Expected loss is not risk. The danger is a bad year far above average, because defaults bunch when the economy turns. Pricing and provisions cover expected loss. Capital covers the unexpected part.
    • Three models, not one. PD, LGD and EAD are separate models, often owned by different teams.
    Part 3

    How each part works

    PD: what is being predicted

    A PD of 2% means about 2 in 100 similar borrowers default within the horizon. Fix three choices first, or the number means nothing.

    • Definition of default: Basel (the international banking rulebook) uses more than 90 days past due, or the bank judging the borrower unlikely to pay. A model trained on "30 days" predicts something different.
    • Horizon: next 12 months, or the whole remaining life of the loan.
    • View: point-in-time reflects today's conditions and rises in a downturn. Through-the-cycle averages good and bad years. Think of today's forecast versus the climate average.

    EAD and LGD

    • EAD is what is drawn today plus a share of the undrawn limit, called the credit conversion factor (CCF), because borrowers draw more as trouble nears. A card with AED 30,000 drawn on a AED 50,000 limit and a CCF of 60% has an EAD of AED 42,000.
    • LGD is the share lost after recoveries. A secured mortgage sits low and an unsecured card sits high.
    • LGD is awkward for ML: outcomes are either near-full recovery or near-total loss, recent defaults are still being collected, and property values fall just as defaults rise.

    The scorecard

    Most retail PD models still start from a scorecard: a logistic regression (a simple model that outputs a probability) on grouped variables, turned into points that add up to a score. Think of a driving-test marking sheet.

    1. Bin each variable: cut it into bands, such as card utilisation of 0-30%, 30-60%, 60-90%, over 90%.
    2. Score each bin with weight of evidence (WOE): how much safer or riskier than average the bin is.
    3. Screen variables with information value (IV): one number for how well a variable separates good from bad.
    4. Fit the regression on the WOE values, then scale to points, cut-offs and rating grades.
    Part 4

    How to read the numbers

    • PD: a PD of 4% is twice the risk of 2%. Always ask for its definition and horizon.
    • WOE: zero is average, positive is safer, negative is riskier. A bin holding 10% of all good customers but only 5% of the bad ones has positive WOE (about 0.69).
    • IV: below 0.02 unusable, 0.02 to 0.1 weak, 0.1 to 0.3 medium, 0.3 to 0.5 strong. These are rules of thumb. Above 0.5 is suspicious: it is usually leakage, meaning the variable includes information from after the outcome, such as a collections flag.
    • Score points: a bank picks a reference score (say 600 at 50:1 good-to-bad odds) and the points that double the odds (say 20). Then 620 means 100:1 (PD about 1%) and 640 means 200:1 (about 0.5%). A score means nothing without its scaling. The table of score bands and PDs is the master scale.
    • Few defaults, wide doubt: a PD's precision depends on the number of defaults, not borrowers. A grade of 1,000 borrowers with zero defaults does not have a PD of zero. At 95% confidence the data fit any PD up to about 0.3%.

    Two separate virtues are easy to confuse. Discrimination asks whether the model ranks bad borrowers above good ones (measured by AUC or Gini). Calibration asks whether a group scored at 2% really defaults at 2%. Ranking drives approvals and calibration drives provisions, so credit needs both.

    Show the formulas

    Expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD. For the running example, 0.02 × 0.45 × 200,000 = AED 1,800.

    EAD = drawn + CCF × undrawn.

    • Scorecards are strong at: transparency (reasons for decline fall out directly), small samples, cheap monitoring and regulator trust.
    • Scorecards are weak at: binning throws away detail, interactions must be added by hand, and tidy bins can hide proxies for protected traits.

    In banking, the question is not "which model has the higher Gini?" but "does the gain survive stability, explainability and model risk review?"

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Ranking without calibration

      A lovely Gini, but predicted PDs twice the observed rates. Plot predicted against observed default rates by grade.

    • Mixing definitions

      A through-the-cycle PD used for provisioning, or a 30-day label for a 90-day policy. Ask of every PD: which default definition, horizon and view?

    • Leakage

      Features measured after the snapshot date leak the outcome. An IV above 0.5 is usually this.

    • Training only on the approved

      Repayment is seen only for people you lent to, so the model never learns about declined applicants. Ask how the sample was formed.

    1. A fully drawn AED 200,000 loan has PD 2% and LGD 45%. Is the AED 1,800 expected loss what this loan will lose?

    No. It is the average cost across many similar loans. The margin must cover it plus funding and running costs. Any single loan loses zero or a large sum.

    2. A line has AED 400,000 drawn of a AED 1,000,000 limit and a CCF of 50%. What is the EAD?

    400,000 + half of the undrawn 600,000 = AED 700,000. Using today's balance understates exposure and expected loss.

    3. A scorecard puts 600 at 50:1 odds with 20 points to double. Is a 640 PD higher or lower than at 600?

    Lower. 640 is two doublings (200:1), so the PD falls from about 2% to about 0.5%.

    4. A variable has IV 0.9 on a retail loan application. Good news?

    More likely a warning: leakage, or a proxy for the outcome. Check what the variable is and when it is created.

    5. What is the difference between discrimination and calibration?

    Discrimination is whether the model ranks bad borrowers above good ones. Calibration is whether the predicted PD matches the observed default rate.
