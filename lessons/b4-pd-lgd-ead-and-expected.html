    B4.1 · B4.2 Day one ★ About 45 minutes

    PD, LGD, EAD and the Scorecard: How a Bank Prices What Might Go Wrong

    Expected loss is three numbers multiplied together. Where each one comes from, why a PD is harder to know than it looks, and how the traditional scorecard (weight of evidence, information value, points) turns data into the number your model will be compared with.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank expects to lose probability of default times loss given default times exposure at default, every one of those is an estimate with its own failure modes, and the logistic-regression scorecard built on binned variables is the transparent, regulator-trusted baseline any new credit model has to beat on more than AUC.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 200,000 to a salaried customer. Will it be repaid? Nobody knows. But the bank does not need to know about this one customer. It needs to know what happens on average across ten thousand such customers, because that average decides the interest rate, the provision it books and the capital it holds.

    Credit risk breaks the question into three, because they have different causes and different owners.

    • PD, probability of default. How likely is the borrower to stop paying within a stated horizon, usually 12 months? This is about the borrower: income, behaviour, sector, the economy.
    • EAD, exposure at default. If they do default, how much will they owe at that moment? A fully drawn term loan owes what is left. A credit card or overdraft owes whatever the customer has drawn by then, often more than today's balance.
    • LGD, loss given default. Of what they owe, how much do we never get back after selling collateral, collecting and paying costs? This is about the loan: security, seniority, legal process, time.

    Multiply them and you get the expected loss: the average amount the bank should assume it will lose on this exposure over the horizon. It is a cost of doing business, like rent. A bank that prices its loans above their expected loss makes money; one that does not is slowly giving it away.

    Two things to take from it.

    • Expected loss is not the risk. It is the average. The real danger is that losses in a bad year are far above average, and that defaults bunch together when the economy turns. The bank covers expected loss with pricing and provisions, and unexpected loss with capital. IFRS 9 provisioning is the topic of the next lesson; this one builds the ingredients.
    • The three parts are separate models. A data scientist hired into credit risk will usually own one of them, and the hardest conversations are about the handoffs: a PD that is too low for the way LGD was calibrated, or an EAD built on a different definition of default.
    Hold onto thisA PD is a probability over a stated horizon and a stated definition of default. An LGD is a share of the exposure, not of the limit. An EAD may be larger than today's balance. If any of those three conditions is left implicit, the multiplication gives a precise-looking wrong number.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss

    The identity is simple. For one exposure over a horizon:

    EL=PD×LGD×EAD

    Read it as: the chance of default, times the share of the balance we lose if it happens, times the balance at that moment. At portfolio level you sum it over exposures. It assumes the three pieces can be treated as independent, which is a simplification we revisit in Part 5.

    PD: what is being predicted

    Before any model, fix the definition of default, because the label is the whole game (F3). The Basel standard uses two triggers: the borrower is more than 90 days past due on a material amount, or the bank judges they are unlikely to pay without it seizing collateral. Local regulators and the bank's own policy add details such as materiality thresholds and how long a defaulted account must perform before it is cured. A model trained on "30 days past due" and one trained on "90 days" are predicting different things, and their PDs are not interchangeable.

    Then fix the horizon. A 12-month PD is the chance of default in the next year. A lifetime PD covers the remaining life of the loan; it is built from a term structure of default hazard, which is survival analysis (F2.8). And fix the view:

    • Point-in-time (PIT) PD reflects today's conditions. It rises in a downturn. IFRS 9 wants this kind, adjusted for forward-looking information.
    • Through-the-cycle (TTC) PD averages across good and bad years, so it is more stable. Capital rules have traditionally leaned towards this kind, with a downturn flavour.

    The same borrower can legitimately have both. People get into trouble when a TTC rating is used where a PIT estimate is needed, or the reverse, and nobody states which.

    Technically, a PD model is a classifier with a calibrated probability output, usually logistic regression (F2.5) or, increasingly, gradient boosting (F4). Discrimination, ranking good borrowers above bad ones, is measured by AUC or the Gini coefficient (Gini = 2 × AUC − 1) and the KS statistic. Calibration, whether a group scored at 2% really defaults at 2%, is measured separately (F7.4). A credit model needs both. A rank-ordering model with biased levels is useless for provisioning, and the reverse is useless for choosing whom to approve.

    Two practical wrinkles. Defaults are rare for good borrowers, so PD estimation lives in the imbalanced-data world of F3.5, including its warning that rebalanced training data must be re-calibrated before the number is used as a probability. And labels need time to mature: a loan approved last month cannot show a 12-month default yet, so the training window is always a year or more behind today.

    The precision of a PD depends on the number of defaults, not the number of borrowers: the same lesson as positives in F3.5. The standard error of an observed default rate is roughly √(PD/n), so the relative error is about 1/√(defaults). To pin a PD within ±20% you need about 100 defaults. For a 0.1% grade that is 100,000 borrower-years. Banks with few defaults (large corporates, banks, sovereigns, specialised lending) have low-default portfolios where this is impossible, so they lean on external ratings, expert judgement, pooling across years and conservative upper-bound methods. A regulator-facing model must say which of those it did.

    EAD: what is owed when it goes wrong

    For a term loan, EAD is mostly the scheduled outstanding balance plus accrued interest. For anything revolving or committed (credit cards, overdrafts, working-capital lines, guarantees, letters of credit), the borrower can draw more as the credit deteriorates, because they need cash and their other lenders are pulling back. So

    EAD=drawn+CCF×(limit−drawn)

    CCF is the credit conversion factor: the share of the currently undrawn limit that will have been drawn by the time of default. It is estimated from past defaulters by looking back 12 months before each default and asking how much of the then-undrawn limit they had used. A CCF of 100% means "assume it all gets drawn". For a card with AED 30,000 drawn on a AED 50,000 limit and CCF 60%: 30,000 + 0.6 × 20,000 = AED 42,000.

    EAD is a regression-like problem on a small, selected sample (only defaulters), with outcomes that are bounded and clumped at the limit, so mean-squared-error models are a poor fit. Data science work here is mostly careful definition of the snapshot date, and honest treatment of the fact that defaulters in a benign year behave differently from defaulters in a crisis.

    LGD: what is lost after recovery

    LGD is not "one minus the sale price of the collateral". It is the economic loss after everything the bank gets back, discounted to the default date, minus the costs of getting it:

    LGD=1−PV of recoveries−PV of costsEAD

    PV means present value. A property worth AED 2 million that takes four years of court process to sell is worth less than one sold next month. LGD therefore depends on collateral type and loan-to-value, seniority, jurisdiction, the legal and enforcement process, and how long recovery takes.

    Three features make LGD awkward for ML. First, the distribution is often bimodal: many defaults are cured or fully recovered (LGD near 0), many lose nearly everything (near 100%), few land in the middle. A single regression to the mean predicts a value almost nobody gets. Second, incomplete workouts: recent defaults are still being collected, so recoveries are censored in exactly the way labels were delayed in F3.5 and survival models handle in F2.8. Dropping them biases LGD downward because quick, easy cases finish first. Third, downturn dependence: collateral values and recovery speed fall when defaults rise, so LGD and PD are positively correlated, and the independence assumption in the multiplication understates losses in a bad year. Regulatory capital approaches ask for a downturn LGD, while IFRS 9 wants a forward-looking, unbiased one.

    For Gulf banks, a few local features are worth knowing: recoveries often go through the court and enforcement process of the relevant jurisdiction, which varies in speed; real-estate collateral values are cyclical; and credit bureau coverage (Al Etihad Credit Bureau in the UAE, SIMAH in Saudi Arabia) shapes what behavioural data a PD model can see. In Islamic financing the legal form differs (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq and so on), the bank often owns the asset during the contract, and late-payment charges are typically restricted. PD, LGD and EAD still apply as concepts, but the recovery and exposure definitions need to follow the contract structure. Check the bank's own policy rather than assuming.

    Scorecards, WOE and IV

    Most retail and SME credit PD models still start from a scorecard: a logistic regression on binned variables whose coefficients are converted into integer points that sum to a score. This is the baseline your gradient-boosted model will be compared with, so it pays to understand it as well as the people who built it.

    The ingredients, in order:

    1. Pick a development sample and a good/bad definition. Typically accounts observed over 12–24 months after the application or snapshot, with "bad" meaning default as defined above. "Indeterminate" accounts (for example, in light arrears) are sometimes excluded.
    2. Bin each variable (fine classing, then coarse classing): continuous variables are cut into bands and categories grouped, so each bin has enough bads, the pattern is sensible, and the bins are stable.
    3. Score each bin with weight of evidence, which says how much better or worse than average the bin is.
    4. Screen variables with information value, a single number for how well a variable separates good from bad.
    5. Fit a logistic regression on the WOE-transformed variables, check signs, correlation and stability.
    6. Scale to points and set cut-offs and rating grades.
    WOEi=lnshare of all goods in bin ishare of all bads in bin iIV=∑i(gi−bi)×WOEi

    Here g and b are the bin's share of all good and all bad accounts. A bin holding 10% of goods and 5% of bads has WOE = ln 2 = 0.69: its odds of being good are twice the portfolio's. WOE is zero for an average bin, positive for safer and negative for riskier. IV adds up each bin's WOE weighted by how far apart its good and bad shares are; it is zero when the variable tells you nothing and grows with separation. WOE is exactly the log of the odds ratio between the bin and the whole, which is why a logistic regression on WOE is so natural: log-odds are linear in WOE by construction.

    The common rules of thumb for IV are below 0.02 unusable, 0.02–0.1 weak, 0.1–0.3 medium, 0.3–0.5 strong, and above 0.5 suspicious (probably leakage or a target proxy). They are folklore from practitioner books, not laws, and IV grows mechanically with the number of bins, which the next simulation shows.

    In code the whole transformation is a few lines, which is why Claude Code can draft it quickly; the judgement is in the bins.

    import numpy as np, pandas as pd

def woe_iv(df, bin_col, bad_col):
    t = df.groupby(bin_col)[bad_col].agg(bads="sum", n="count")
    t["goods"] = t["n"] - t["bads"]
    # a small smoothing constant avoids log(0) in empty bins
    g = (t["goods"] + 0.5) / (t["goods"].sum() + 0.5 * len(t))
    b = (t["bads"]  + 0.5) / (t["bads"].sum()  + 0.5 * len(t))
    t["woe"] = np.log(g / b)
    t["iv"]  = (g - b) * t["woe"]
    return t, t["iv"].sum()

    Why this has lasted so long

    A scorecard is a lookup table. A credit officer, an auditor and a customer can each see that "utilisation above 90%" costs 45 points. Coefficients carry the expected sign by construction (monotone bins), reasons for decline fall out directly (the variables where the applicant lost the most points), missing values get their own bin and own WOE, and outliers are tamed by binning. It is robust on small samples, cheap to monitor with PSI (F7.7), simple to validate, and regulators and validators have decades of experience with it. This is the standard a challenger model has to beat and be explained against.

    Its weaknesses are real too. Binning discards information inside a bin. It is additive, so it cannot capture interactions unless you add them by hand. Fine classing needs analyst effort and judgement. And WOE-based IV selection can mislead when variables are correlated. Gradient boosting often gains a few points of Gini on the same data, mostly by finding interactions and non-linearities automatically; the open question is whether that gain survives stability, explainability and the model risk process (Part 4).

    From log-odds to points to rating grades

    The regression outputs log-odds. Users want a score where bigger is better and a fixed number of points always means the same change in risk. The convention is to choose three numbers: a reference score (say 600), the good:bad odds at that score (say 50:1), and the points to double the odds (PDO, say 20). Then

    score=offset+factor×ln(good:bad odds)factor=PDOln2

    Every PDO points the odds of being good double. With 600 at 50:1 and PDO 20: a score of 620 means 100:1 (PD = 1/101 = 0.99%), 640 means 200:1 (0.50%), 580 means 25:1 (3.8%). The offset is chosen so the reference score lands on the reference odds. The table of score bands and their PDs is the master scale; grades are the bands.

    That last stat is the link back to the beginning: a score is a PD in disguise, a PD times LGD times EAD is an expected loss, and the master scale is what lets the credit committee set cut-offs, risk-based prices and provisions in one language. The data-science question underneath is always the same two: does the ranking separate good from bad (Gini, KS), and is each grade's PD actually right (calibration, back-testing against realised default rates)?

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Credit risk is where most of the foundations in this curriculum get used at once: probability, logistic regression, calibration, survival, selection bias, drift and governance.

    B4.3 · IFRS 9 staging and expected credit lossWhere these three numbers get booked

    IFRS 9 turns PD, LGD and EAD into provisions: 12-month ECL in stage 1, lifetime ECL when risk has risen significantly. Same ingredients, forward-looking and discounted.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsThe scorecard is logistic regression

    The model behind the points. The coefficient on a WOE variable is a log-odds change with a clear reading.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisLifetime PD and censored workouts

    A loan that has not defaulted yet is not a good one, and a recovery that is not finished is not a final LGD. Hazard models handle both.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataPDs are rare-event probabilities

    Rebalancing shifts probabilities, which matters more here than anywhere because the PD is used as a number.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Metrics and calibrationGini, KS and calibration

    Discrimination and calibration are different virtues. Credit needs both, evaluated on time-ordered data.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringPSI was born in scorecards

    The population stability index compares today's score distribution to the development sample. It is the standard early warning for a scorecard.

    F2.6 · Selection biasReject inference

    You only see repayment for people you approved. A model trained on them never learns what the declined would have done.

    F4 · Trees and boostingThe challenger model

    Gradient boosting on the same variables is the usual challenger. Monotone constraints bring it closer to scorecard behaviour.

    F8 · ExplainabilityReasons for decline

    A scorecard explains itself. A boosted model needs SHAP-style explanations, and you must check they are faithful and stable.

    G3 · Model riskValidation of PD models

    PD, LGD and EAD models are the textbook case for independent validation, back-testing and periodic review.

    G2 · FairnessCredit decisions and protected groups

    Lending is a high-stakes decision where fairness definitions bite. Bins and variables can act as proxies.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Origination. Application scorecards for personal loans, cards, auto and mortgage decisions, with cut-offs from the master scale and affordability rules on top.
    • Behavioural scoring and early warning. Monthly re-scoring of existing customers to set limits, trigger collections and feed IFRS 9 staging.
    • Pricing. Risk-based pricing adds expected loss, funding cost, operating cost and a capital charge. EL is the first term.
    • Provisioning and capital. ECL for the financial statements; PD, LGD and EAD for regulatory capital under approaches the central bank permits.
    • Corporate and SME. Internal rating models combining financial ratios and expert judgement, with few defaults and heavy reliance on overrides and external ratings.
    • Stress testing. Shock the macroeconomic inputs and watch PD and LGD move together. This is where the independence shortcut is exposed.

    Where the field is now

    The framework is old and stable. What is moving is the model inside it: whether machine learning can replace the scorecard for PD and under what conditions, how LGD and EAD are modelled, and how local regulators adopt the international capital standards.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Credit models fail slowly: the damage shows up in the next downturn, long after the model was approved. Each failure below comes with the quickest check.

    • Ranking without calibration

      A model with a lovely Gini whose predicted PDs are twice the observed default rates. Provisions and prices are wrong although approvals look sensible.

      Spot itPlot predicted against observed default rate by grade on time-ordered data. Compare the portfolio average predicted PD with the realised rate.
    • Mixing definitions of default, horizon or view

      A TTC PD used for IFRS 9, a 30-day label for a 90-day policy, or a lifetime PD put into a 12-month formula.

      Spot itAsk three questions of every PD: which default definition, which horizon, point-in-time or through-the-cycle?
    • Leakage through the label window

      Features measured after the observation date, such as a collections flag or current arrears, leak the outcome. An IV above 0.5 or a Gini above 90% on retail credit is usually this.

      Spot itCheck each feature's timestamp against the snapshot date (F3.1). Look at the top-IV variables first.
    • Training only on the approved

      Repayment is observed only for those you lent to, so the model never sees what declined applicants would have done. Variables the old policy used heavily look more predictive, or less, than they are.

      Spot itAsk how the development sample was formed and whether reject inference or a randomised exception sample was used.
    • Overbinned or unstable bins

      Many thin bins give high IV in development, reversed signs in validation and a PSI that moves for no business reason.

      Spot itCount bads per bin (aim for dozens at least). Re-calculate WOE on an out-of-time sample and check the pattern holds.
    • Treating a small default count as precise

      Reporting a 0.12% PD for a grade with three defaults.

      Spot itAsk for the number of defaults and an interval. The relative error is roughly one over the square root of the defaults.
    • Unfinished workouts in LGD

      Dropping open recovery cases, or valuing unrecovered collateral at the appraisal, understates LGD. Early finishers are the easy cases.

      Spot itCheck how open cases and the discount rate are treated, and compare LGD by default vintage: recent vintages should not look suspiciously better.
    • EAD from today's balance on revolving lines

      A card's exposure at default is higher than its balance a year before. Ignoring the CCF understates both exposure and loss.

      Spot itCompare balance 12 months before default with balance at default for past defaulters.
    • Independence between PD and LGD

      In a downturn, defaults rise and collateral values fall at the same time. Multiplying averages understates the loss in the bad year.

      Spot itAsk for a stress test where PD and LGD move together, and whether the LGD is a downturn one where the rules require it.
    • A proxy that encodes a protected attribute

      Postcode, employer or nationality-correlated variables can act as proxies. Binning can hide this because it looks tidy.

      Spot itReview each variable's WOE pattern against group outcomes, and involve the fairness and legal teams early (G2).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a credit executive · 30 seconds
    “Expected loss is three numbers multiplied: how likely they are to default, how much they will owe when they do, and how much of that we lose. A new model can improve any of them, but the first test isn't how clever it is. It's whether it ranks borrowers better than the scorecard we run now and whether its predicted default rates match what actually happens, on recent data. I'd show you both, with the number of defaults behind each figure, and what it does to approvals and provisions in AED.”
    To a model validator or technical interviewer
    “I'd define default, horizon and point-in-time or through-the-cycle first, so the PD means one thing. The baseline is a WOE-binned logistic scorecard with monotone bins, evaluated out of time on Gini, KS and grade-level calibration. The challenger is gradient boosting on the same features, with monotone constraints and a stability and explanation pack. I'd report the incremental gain with bootstrap intervals, the PSI of inputs, and the effect on grade migration, because a few Gini points that don't survive the next quarter aren't worth the added model risk.”
    “Why do we still use scorecards when XGBoost is more accurate?”

    Because accuracy is not the only requirement. A scorecard is transparent, its reasons for decline are exact, it is stable on small samples, it is monotone by design and the validation practice is mature. Boosting typically adds a few Gini points, and that is worth having when it survives out-of-time tests and can be explained. Many banks use boosting as a challenger, for early warning, or inside a constrained setup, and keep the scorecard where explanation and regulation weigh most.

    “What is the difference between PD and the score?”

    The score is a ranking number on an arbitrary scale; the PD is a calibrated probability. The master scale maps one to the other using the reference score, odds and points-to-double-odds. The mapping must be checked against observed default rates, and it can drift even when the ranking holds.

    “Our top grade has had two defaults in five years. What is its PD?”

    Not the observed rate, which is built on two events. I'd report an interval, pool with neighbouring grades or years, and use external or expert anchors where the policy allows. For a grade with zero defaults, the 'rule of three' says the 95% upper bound is about 3 divided by the number of borrower-years. I'd say clearly that this is a low-default portfolio and state the conservatism applied.

    “The new model has a Gini of 0.72 against 0.65. Do we switch?”

    I'd first ask where the gain comes from and whether it holds out of time. Then calibration by grade, stability (PSI, bin or feature importance shifts), explainability for decline reasons, fairness across groups, and what it does to approvals and expected loss in AED at the planned cut-off. A 7-point Gini gain is attractive; leakage, selection and an unexplainable model are the ways it turns out to be worthless.

    “Why is LGD so hard to model?”

    The outcome is bimodal, recoveries take years, so recent defaults are incomplete, and it moves with the economy: collateral values fall just as defaults rise. So I'd model the cure or full-recovery probability separately from loss severity, treat open workouts as censored, and build a downturn view for capital while keeping an unbiased forward-looking one for IFRS 9.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A loan of AED 200,000 is fully drawn, with PD 2% and LGD 45%. What is the 12-month expected loss, and what does it mean for pricing?

    EL = 0.02 × 0.45 × 200,000 = AED 1,800, or 0.9% of the balance. The loan's margin must cover this, plus funding, operating costs and a capital charge, before it earns anything. It is an average: on any single loan the loss is either zero or a large figure.

    2. A working-capital line has a AED 1,000,000 limit, AED 400,000 drawn, CCF 50%, PD 1%, LGD 40%. EAD and EL?

    EAD = 400,000 + 0.5 × 600,000 = AED 700,000. EL = 0.01 × 0.40 × 700,000 = AED 2,800. Using today's balance alone would give 1,600.

    3. A grade has 1,000 borrowers and zero defaults in a year. What can you say about its PD?

    Not that it is zero. The probability of zero defaults at PD p is (1 − p)1000. Setting that to 5% gives p ≈ 0.30%, the rule-of-three bound (3 ÷ 1000). So the data are consistent with any PD up to about 0.3% at 95% confidence.

    4. A bin has 10% of all goods and 5% of all bads. WOE, and its contribution to IV?

    WOE = ln(0.10 ÷ 0.05) = ln 2 = 0.69. Contribution = (0.10 − 0.05) × 0.69 = 0.035. Positive WOE means safer than average. The other bins must carry the rest of the IV.

    5. Scaling: score 600 at 50:1 odds, PDO 20. What PD does a score of 640 imply? A score of 560?

    640 is two PDOs above: odds 50 × 4 = 200:1, PD = 1/201 ≈ 0.50%. 560 is two below: odds 50 ÷ 4 = 12.5:1, PD = 1/13.5 ≈ 7.4%.

    6. The IV of a variable is 0.9 on a retail loan application. Is that great news?

    More likely a warning. Very high IV in retail credit usually signals leakage (a field recorded after the outcome, a collections flag) or a proxy for the target, such as an internal rating already derived from past behaviour. Check what the variable is and when it is created.

    7. Why can an IFRS 9 PD and a regulatory-capital PD for the same borrower differ, and both be right?

    They answer different questions. IFRS 9 wants an unbiased, point-in-time estimate that uses forward-looking information. Capital rules lean on through-the-cycle, conservative or floored parameters, and a downturn LGD. The skill is stating which one is in play and reconciling the two.

    8. Name two reasons a boosted model with a higher Gini might still lose to the scorecard in the model risk committee.

    Instability (feature importance and PSI shift between samples), unexplainable or non-monotone behaviour for decline reasons, poor calibration by grade, a gain that disappears out of time, fairness concerns, or too little default data to support the extra complexity. Any one can be decisive.
