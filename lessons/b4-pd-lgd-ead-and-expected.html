    PD, LGD, EAD and the Scorecard: How a Bank Prices What Might Go Wrong

    Expected loss is three numbers multiplied together. Where each one comes from, why a PD is harder to know than it looks, and how the traditional scorecard (weight of evidence, information value, points) turns data into the number your model will be compared with.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank expects to lose probability of default times loss given default times exposure at default, every one of those is an estimate with its own failure modes, and the logistic-regression scorecard built on binned variables is the transparent, regulator-trusted baseline any new credit model has to beat on more than AUC.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 200,000 to a salaried customer. Nobody knows whether this one will repay, and the bank does not need to. It needs the average over ten thousand such customers, because that average sets the interest rate, the provision and the capital held.

    An insurer prices car cover the same way: chance of a crash, times the repair bill, times how much the insurer cannot recover. Credit risk splits the question into three parts with different causes and owners.

    • PD, probability of default. How likely is the borrower to stop paying within a horizon, usually 12 months? About the borrower: income, behaviour, sector, the economy.
    • EAD, exposure at default. How much will they owe at that moment? A term loan owes what is left. A card or overdraft owes whatever has been drawn by then, often more than today's balance.
    • LGD, loss given default. Of that, how much is never recovered after selling collateral, collecting and paying costs? About the loan: security, seniority, legal process, time.

    Multiply them for expected loss (EL): the average loss to assume on this exposure over the horizon. It is a cost of doing business, like rent. PD 2% × LGD 45% gives EL of 0.9% of the balance: every AED 100 lent carries 90 fils of expected loss, and pricing must cover it. A bank pricing above EL makes money; below it, it gives money away. EL is not the loss you will see on one loan, which is usually zero or large.

    Two points to take away.

    • Expected loss is the average, not the risk. The danger is a bad year with losses far above average, because defaults bunch when the economy turns. Pricing and provisions cover expected loss; capital covers unexpected loss. IFRS 9 provisioning is the next lesson.
    • The three parts are separate models. A data scientist in credit risk usually owns one. The hard conversations are at the handoffs: a PD too low for how LGD was calibrated, or an EAD built on a different default definition.
    Hold onto thisA PD is a probability over a stated horizon and default definition. An LGD is a share of the exposure, not the limit. An EAD may exceed today's balance. Leave any of these implicit and the multiplication gives a precise-looking wrong number.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss

    For one exposure over a horizon:

    EL=PD×LGD×EAD

    The chance of default, times the share of the balance lost if it happens, times the balance at that moment. At portfolio level, sum over exposures. It treats the three pieces as independent, a simplification revisited in Part 3.

    PD: what is being predicted

    PD answers "how likely is this borrower to default within the horizon?" A PD of 2% means about 2 in 100 similar borrowers default in the period; going from 2% to 4% means the risk has doubled. Three choices must be fixed first, or the number is meaningless.

    • Definition of default (the label is the whole game, F3). Basel uses two triggers: more than 90 days past due on a material amount, or the bank judges the borrower unlikely to pay without seizing collateral. Local rules add materiality thresholds and cure periods. A model trained on "30 days past due" predicts something different from one trained on "90 days".
    • Horizon. A 12-month PD is the chance of default in the next year. A lifetime PD covers the loan's remaining life, built from a term structure of default hazard (survival analysis, F2.8).
    • View. Point-in-time (PIT) reflects today's conditions and rises in a downturn; IFRS 9 wants this, with forward-looking adjustment. Through-the-cycle (TTC) averages good and bad years, so it is steadier; capital rules have leaned this way, with a downturn flavour. Like today's forecast versus the climate average. Trouble starts when one is used for the other and nobody says so.

    Technically, a PD model is a classifier with calibrated probability output: logistic regression (F2.5) or, increasingly, gradient boosting (F4). Two separate virtues:

    • Discrimination: does it rank bad borrowers above good ones? Measured by AUC, Gini (2 × AUC − 1) and KS. Gini 0.65 versus 0.72 is a better ranking, nothing more.
    • Calibration: does a group scored at 2% really default at 2%? (F7.4.) A ranker with biased levels is useless for provisioning; a calibrated model that ranks poorly is useless for deciding whom to approve. Credit needs both.

    Two wrinkles. Defaults are rare for good borrowers, so PD work lives in the imbalanced-data world of F3.5: rebalanced training data must be re-calibrated before the output is used as a probability. And labels need time to mature, so the training window always lags today by a year or more.

    A PD's precision depends on the number of defaults, not borrowers (the same lesson as positives in F3.5). The relative error is about 1/√defaults, so ±20% needs about 100 defaults. For a 0.1% grade that is 100,000 borrower-years. Low-default portfolios (large corporates, banks, sovereigns, specialised lending) cannot reach that, so they lean on external ratings, expert judgement, pooling across years and conservative upper bounds. A regulator-facing model must say which it used.

    EAD: what is owed when it goes wrong

    A term loan's EAD is mostly the scheduled balance plus accrued interest. Anything revolving or committed (cards, overdrafts, working-capital lines, guarantees) can be drawn further as credit deteriorates: like someone who maxes out their cards as the bills pile up. So

    EAD=drawn+CCF×(limit−drawn)

    CCF is the credit conversion factor: the share of today's undrawn limit that will be drawn by default. It is estimated from past defaulters by looking 12 months before each default and asking how much of the then-undrawn limit they used. CCF 100% means "assume it all gets drawn". Example: a card with AED 30,000 drawn on a AED 50,000 limit, CCF 60%: 30,000 + 0.6 × 20,000 = AED 42,000.

    EAD is a small-sample problem (defaulters only) with bounded outcomes that clump at the limit, so mean-squared-error models fit poorly. The work is mostly careful definition of the snapshot date, and honest treatment of the fact that defaulters in a benign year behave differently from those in a crisis.

    LGD: what is lost after recovery

    LGD is the economic loss after everything recovered, discounted to the default date, minus recovery costs:

    LGD=1−PV of recoveries−PV of costsEAD

    PV is present value: AED 2 million of property that takes four years of court process to sell is worth less than one sold next month. LGD depends on collateral type and loan-to-value, seniority, jurisdiction, enforcement process and time to recover. Read it as a share of EAD: LGD 45% means the bank loses 45 fils of every dirham owed. A secured mortgage sits low; an unsecured card sits high. Misreading to avoid: LGD is not "one minus the collateral's sale price".

    LGD is awkward for ML for three reasons.

    • Bimodal outcomes. Many defaults cure or recover fully (LGD near 0), many lose nearly everything (near 100%), few land between. A regression to the mean predicts a value almost nobody gets, like forecasting the average of heads and tails.
    • Incomplete workouts. Recent defaults are still being collected, so recoveries are censored (F3.5, F2.8). Dropping them biases LGD downward, because quick, easy cases finish first.
    • Downturn dependence. Collateral values and recovery speed fall as defaults rise, so PD and LGD are positively correlated and the independence assumption understates bad-year loss. Capital rules ask for a downturn LGD; IFRS 9 wants a forward-looking, unbiased one.

    Gulf specifics. Recoveries run through the relevant jurisdiction's court and enforcement process, which varies in speed. Real-estate values are cyclical. Credit bureau coverage (Al Etihad Credit Bureau in the UAE, SIMAH in Saudi Arabia) shapes the behavioural data a PD model can see. In Islamic financing (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq) the legal form differs, the bank often owns the asset during the contract, and late-payment charges are typically restricted. PD, LGD and EAD still apply, but exposure and recovery definitions must follow the contract structure. Check the bank's own policy.

    Scorecards, WOE and IV

    Most retail and SME PD models still start from a scorecard: a logistic regression on binned variables, with coefficients converted into integer points that sum to a score. It is the baseline your gradient-boosted model will be compared with. Think of a driving-test marking sheet: each answer earns or loses points, and the total decides the outcome. Steps:

    1. Sample and good/bad definition. Accounts observed 12–24 months after application or snapshot; "bad" is default as defined above. "Indeterminate" accounts (light arrears) are sometimes excluded.
    2. Bin each variable (fine, then coarse classing): cut continuous variables into bands and group categories so each bin has enough bads, a sensible pattern and stability.
    3. Score each bin with weight of evidence (WOE): how much better or worse than average the bin is.
    4. Screen variables with information value (IV): one number for how well a variable separates good from bad.
    5. Fit logistic regression on the WOE variables; check signs, correlation, stability.
    6. Scale to points; set cut-offs and rating grades.
    WOEi=lnshare of all goods in bin ishare of all bads in bin iIV=∑i(gi−bi)×WOEi

    g and b are the bin's shares of all good and all bad accounts. A bin with 10% of goods and 5% of bads has WOE = ln 2 = 0.69: its odds of being good are twice the portfolio's. WOE is 0 for an average bin, positive for safer, negative for riskier. IV sums each bin's WOE weighted by the gap between its good and bad shares: 0 when the variable tells you nothing, rising with separation. Because WOE is a log odds ratio, log-odds are linear in WOE by construction, which is why logistic regression on WOE fits so naturally.

    Reading IV: below 0.02 unusable, 0.02–0.1 weak, 0.1–0.3 medium, 0.3–0.5 strong, above 0.5 suspicious (probably leakage or a target proxy). These are practitioner folklore, not laws, and IV grows mechanically with the number of bins, as the next simulation shows.

    The code is short, so Claude Code can draft it quickly. The judgement is in the bins.

    import numpy as np, pandas as pd

def woe_iv(df, bin_col, bad_col):
    t = df.groupby(bin_col)[bad_col].agg(bads="sum", n="count")
    t["goods"] = t["n"] - t["bads"]
    # a small smoothing constant avoids log(0) in empty bins
    g = (t["goods"] + 0.5) / (t["goods"].sum() + 0.5 * len(t))
    b = (t["bads"]  + 0.5) / (t["bads"].sum()  + 0.5 * len(t))
    t["woe"] = np.log(g / b)
    t["iv"]  = (g - b) * t["woe"]
    return t, t["iv"].sum()

    Why scorecards have lasted

    A scorecard is a lookup table, like a restaurant bill: an officer, an auditor and a customer can all see that "utilisation above 90%" costs 45 points.

    • Signs are right by construction (monotone bins), and reasons for decline fall out directly (where the applicant lost most points).
    • Missing values get their own bin and WOE; outliers are tamed by binning.
    • Robust on small samples, cheap to monitor with PSI (F7.7), simple to validate, with decades of regulator experience.

    Weaknesses: binning discards information inside a bin; it is additive, so interactions must be added by hand; fine classing needs analyst judgement; IV selection can mislead when variables are correlated. Gradient boosting often gains a few Gini points on the same data by finding interactions automatically. The question is whether that gain survives stability, explainability and model risk review. A challenger must beat the scorecard and be explained against it.

    From log-odds to points to rating grades

    A regression outputs log-odds, but users want a score where higher is better and a fixed number of points always means the same change in risk. Like a thermometer, the scale is a convention: pick a reference score (say 600), the good:bad odds there (say 50:1) and the points to double the odds (PDO, say 20). Then

    score=offset+factor×ln(good:bad odds)factor=PDOln2

    Every PDO points, the odds of being good double. With 600 at 50:1 and PDO 20: 620 means 100:1 (PD 0.99%), 640 means 200:1 (0.50%), 580 means 25:1 (3.8%). The offset makes the reference score land on the reference odds. The table of score bands and their PDs is the master scale; the bands are grades. A score of 640 means nothing without its scaling.

    A score is a PD in disguise; PD × LGD × EAD is an expected loss; the master scale lets the credit committee set cut-offs, prices and provisions in one language. Two questions always sit underneath: does the ranking separate good from bad (Gini, KS), and is each grade's PD right (calibration, back-testing against realised defaults)?

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Credit models fail slowly: the damage shows in the next downturn. Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Ranking without calibration

      A lovely Gini, but predicted PDs twice the observed rates. Approvals look sensible; provisions and prices are wrong.

      Spot itPlot predicted against observed default rate by grade on time-ordered data. Compare average predicted PD with the realised rate.
    • Mixing default definition, horizon or view

      A TTC PD used for IFRS 9, a 30-day label for a 90-day policy, a lifetime PD in a 12-month formula.

      Spot itAsk of every PD: which default definition, which horizon, point-in-time or through-the-cycle?
    • Leakage through the label window

      Features measured after the observation date (a collections flag, current arrears) leak the outcome. IV above 0.5 or Gini above 90% on retail credit is usually this.

      Spot itCheck each feature's timestamp against the snapshot date (F3.1). Start with the top-IV variables.
    • Training only on the approved

      Repayment is seen only for people you lent to, so the model never learns what declined applicants would have done. Variables the old policy used heavily look more or less predictive than they are.

      Spot itAsk how the sample was formed and whether reject inference or a randomised exception sample was used.
    • Overbinned or unstable bins

      Many thin bins: high IV in development, reversed signs in validation, PSI that moves for no business reason.

      Spot itCount bads per bin (dozens at least). Recalculate WOE out of time and check the pattern holds.
    • Treating a small default count as precise

      Reporting a 0.12% PD for a grade with three defaults.

      Spot itAsk for the number of defaults and an interval. Relative error is about 1/√defaults.
    • Unfinished workouts in LGD

      Dropping open recovery cases, or valuing unrecovered collateral at appraisal, understates LGD. Early finishers are the easy cases.

      Spot itCheck how open cases and the discount rate are treated. Compare LGD by default vintage: recent vintages should not look suspiciously better.
    • EAD from today's balance on revolving lines

      A card's exposure at default exceeds its balance a year earlier. Ignoring the CCF understates exposure and loss.

      Spot itCompare balance 12 months before default with balance at default for past defaulters.
    • Independence between PD and LGD

      In a downturn defaults rise as collateral values fall. Multiplying averages understates the bad-year loss.

      Spot itAsk for a stress test where PD and LGD move together, and for a downturn LGD where rules require it.
    • A proxy that encodes a protected attribute

      Postcode, employer or nationality-correlated variables can act as proxies. Tidy binning can hide this.

      Spot itReview each variable's WOE pattern against group outcomes; involve fairness and legal teams early (G2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the Part 2 formulas.

    1. A loan of AED 200,000 is fully drawn, PD 2%, LGD 45%. What is the 12-month EL, and what does it mean for pricing?

    EL = 0.02 × 0.45 × 200,000 = AED 1,800, or 0.9% of the balance. The margin must cover this plus funding, operating costs and a capital charge. It is an average: any single loan loses zero or a large sum.

    2. A line has a AED 1,000,000 limit, AED 400,000 drawn, CCF 50%, PD 1%, LGD 40%. EAD and EL?

    EAD = 400,000 + 0.5 × 600,000 = AED 700,000. EL = 0.01 × 0.40 × 700,000 = AED 2,800. Today's balance alone would give 1,600.

    3. A grade has 1,000 borrowers and zero defaults in a year. What can you say about its PD?

    Not that it is zero. P(zero defaults) = (1 − p)1000; setting that to 5% gives p ≈ 0.30%, the rule-of-three bound (3 ÷ 1000). The data fit any PD up to about 0.3% at 95% confidence.

    4. A bin has 10% of all goods and 5% of all bads. WOE, and its IV contribution?

    WOE = ln(0.10 ÷ 0.05) = ln 2 = 0.69. Contribution = (0.10 − 0.05) × 0.69 = 0.035. Positive WOE means safer than average.

    5. Score 600 at 50:1 odds, PDO 20. What PD does 640 imply? And 560?

    640 is two PDOs up: odds 200:1, PD = 1/201 ≈ 0.50%. 560 is two down: odds 12.5:1, PD = 1/13.5 ≈ 7.4%.

    6. A variable has IV 0.9 on a retail loan application. Great news?

    More likely a warning: leakage (a field recorded after the outcome, such as a collections flag) or a target proxy (an internal rating already built from past behaviour). Check what the variable is and when it is created.

    7. Why can an IFRS 9 PD and a regulatory-capital PD for the same borrower differ, and both be right?

    They answer different questions. IFRS 9 wants an unbiased, point-in-time, forward-looking estimate. Capital rules lean on through-the-cycle, conservative or floored parameters and a downturn LGD. State which is in play and reconcile the two.

    8. Name two reasons a boosted model with a higher Gini might still lose to the scorecard at the model risk committee.

    Instability (feature importance or PSI shifts), non-monotone or unexplainable behaviour for decline reasons, poor calibration by grade, a gain that vanishes out of time, fairness concerns, or too few defaults to support the complexity. Any one can be decisive.
