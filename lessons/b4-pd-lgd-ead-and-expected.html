    PD, LGD, EAD and the Scorecard: How a Bank Prices What Might Go Wrong

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 200,000 to a salaried customer. Nobody knows whether this one will repay, and the bank does not need to. It needs the average over ten thousand such customers, because that average sets the price, the provision and the capital held.

    An insurer prices car cover the same way: chance of a crash, times the repair bill. Credit risk splits the question into three parts.

    • PD, probability of default. How likely is the borrower to stop paying within a horizon, usually 12 months?
    • EAD, exposure at default. How much will they owe at that moment? A card owes whatever has been drawn by then, often more than today's balance.
    • LGD, loss given default. Of that, how much is never recovered after selling collateral and paying costs?

    Multiply them for expected loss (EL): the average loss to assume over the horizon. It is a cost of doing business, like rent. PD 2% × LGD 45% is 0.9% of the balance: every AED 100 lent carries 90 fils of expected loss, and pricing must cover it. One loan usually loses zero or a lot, never the average.

    Expected loss is the average, not the risk. The danger is a bad year far above average, because defaults bunch when the economy turns. Pricing and provisions cover expected loss; capital covers unexpected loss. The three parts are separate models, often owned by different people. IFRS 9 provisioning is the next lesson.

    Hold onto thisA PD needs a stated horizon and default definition. An LGD is a share of the exposure, not the limit. An EAD may exceed today's balance. Leave any of these implicit and you get a precise-looking wrong number.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss

    Expected loss for one exposure over a horizon is:

    EL=PD×LGD×EAD

    Chance of default, times the share lost, times the balance at that moment. Sum over exposures for a portfolio.

    PD: what is being predicted

    PD answers "how likely is this borrower to default within the horizon?" A PD of 2% means about 2 in 100 similar borrowers default; going from 2% to 4% means the risk has doubled. Three choices must be fixed first, or the number is meaningless.

    • Definition of default. Basel uses more than 90 days past due, or the bank judging the borrower unlikely to pay. A model trained on "30 days" predicts something different from one trained on "90 days".
    • Horizon. A 12-month PD covers the next year; a lifetime PD covers the loan's remaining life (F2.8).
    • View. Point-in-time reflects today's conditions and rises in a downturn (IFRS 9). Through-the-cycle averages good and bad years and is steadier (capital rules). Like today's forecast versus the climate average; trouble starts when one is used for the other.

    A PD model is a classifier with calibrated probability output. Two virtues, easily confused:

    • Discrimination: does it rank bad borrowers above good ones? Measured by AUC, Gini and KS. A higher Gini is a better ranking, nothing more.
    • Calibration: does a group scored at 2% really default at 2%? (F7.4.) Biased levels ruin provisioning; poor ranking ruins approvals. Credit needs both.

    Defaults are rare, so PD work lives in imbalanced data: rebalanced training data must be re-calibrated before the output is read as a probability.

    A PD's precision depends on the number of defaults, not borrowers. Roughly, a PD known to within 20% needs about 100 defaults, which for a 0.1% grade is 100,000 borrower-years. Low-default portfolios (large corporates, banks, sovereigns) cannot get there, so they lean on external ratings, expert judgement and conservative bounds.

    EAD: what is owed when it goes wrong

    A term loan's EAD is mostly the scheduled balance. Cards, overdrafts and committed lines can be drawn further as credit deteriorates, like someone maxing out their cards as the bills pile up. So EAD is what is drawn today plus a share of the undrawn limit, called the credit conversion factor (CCF), estimated from how past defaulters behaved before default. A card with AED 30,000 drawn on a AED 50,000 limit and a CCF of 60% has an EAD of AED 42,000. Using today's balance alone understates exposure.

    LGD: what is lost after recovery

    LGD is the share of the exposure lost after everything recovered, discounted to the default date, less recovery costs. LGD 45% means the bank loses 45 fils of every dirham owed. A secured mortgage sits low; an unsecured card sits high. Time matters: property that takes four years of court process to sell is worth less than one sold next month.

    LGD is awkward for ML:

    • Bimodal outcomes. Many defaults recover fully, many lose nearly everything, few land between. A prediction at the average is a value almost nobody gets, like forecasting the average of heads and tails.
    • Incomplete workouts. Recent defaults are still being collected. Dropping them biases LGD downward, because easy cases finish first.
    • Downturn dependence. Collateral values fall just as defaults rise, so PD and LGD move together and multiplying averages understates a bad year. Capital rules ask for a downturn LGD.

    Gulf specifics. Recovery speed depends on local court and enforcement processes, and real-estate values are cyclical. Bureau coverage (Al Etihad in the UAE, SIMAH in Saudi Arabia) shapes what a PD model can see. In Islamic financing (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq) exposure and recovery definitions must follow the contract structure.

    Scorecards, WOE and IV

    Most retail and SME PD models still start from a scorecard: a logistic regression on binned variables, with coefficients turned into points that add up to a score. Think of a driving-test marking sheet. It is the baseline your gradient-boosted model will be compared with.

    1. Bin each variable: cut continuous variables into bands, each with enough bads and a sensible pattern.
    2. Score each bin with weight of evidence (WOE): how much safer or riskier than average the bin is. Zero is average, positive is safer, negative is riskier.
    3. Screen variables with information value (IV): one number for how well a variable separates good from bad.
    4. Fit a logistic regression on the WOE variables, then scale to points, cut-offs and grades.

    Reading IV: below 0.02 unusable, 0.02 to 0.1 weak, 0.1 to 0.3 medium, 0.3 to 0.5 strong, above 0.5 suspicious (probably leakage). These are folklore, and IV grows mechanically with the number of bins.

    Why scorecards have lasted

    A scorecard is a lookup table, like a restaurant bill: an officer, an auditor and a customer can all see that "utilisation above 90%" costs 45 points.

    • Signs are right by construction, and reasons for decline fall out directly.
    • Missing values get their own bin; outliers are tamed.
    • Robust on small samples, cheap to monitor (F7.7), simple to validate, trusted by regulators.

    Weaknesses: binning discards detail, interactions must be added by hand, and tidy binning can hide proxies for protected attributes (involve fairness and legal early, G2). Gradient boosting often gains a few Gini points by finding interactions. The question is whether that gain survives stability, explainability and model risk review.

    From log-odds to points to rating grades

    A regression outputs log-odds, but users want a score where higher is better and a fixed number of points always means the same change in risk. So pick a reference score (say 600), the odds there (say 50:1) and the points to double the odds (PDO, say 20). Every 20 points up, the odds of being good double: 620 means 100:1 (PD about 1%), 640 means 200:1 (about 0.5%). The table of score bands and PDs is the master scale; the bands are grades. A score of 640 means nothing without its scaling.

    A score is a PD in disguise; PD × LGD × EAD is an expected loss; the master scale lets the credit committee set cut-offs, prices and provisions in one language. Underneath sit two questions: does the ranking separate good from bad, and is each grade's PD right?

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Credit models fail slowly: the damage shows in the next downturn. Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Ranking without calibration

      A lovely Gini, but predicted PDs twice the observed rates. Provisions and prices are wrong.

      Spot itPlot predicted against observed default rate by grade on time-ordered data.
    • Mixing default definition, horizon or view

      A TTC PD used for IFRS 9, a 30-day label for a 90-day policy, a lifetime PD in a 12-month formula.

      Spot itAsk of every PD: which default definition, which horizon, point-in-time or through-the-cycle?
    • Leakage through the label window

      Features measured after the observation date (a collections flag, current arrears) leak the outcome. IV above 0.5 or Gini above 90% on retail credit is usually this.

      Spot itCheck feature timestamps against the snapshot date (F3.1), starting with top-IV variables.
    • Training only on the approved

      Repayment is seen only for people you lent to, so the model never learns what declined applicants would have done.

      Spot itAsk how the sample was formed and whether reject inference or a randomised exception sample was used.
    • Thin bins and small default counts

      Many thin bins give high IV in development and reversed signs in validation. A 0.12% PD for a grade with three defaults is the same mistake.

      Spot itCount bads per bin (dozens at least) and ask for the number of defaults behind any PD. Recalculate WOE out of time.
    • Optimistic LGD and EAD

      Dropping unfinished recoveries understates LGD. Today's balance on revolving lines understates EAD. Multiplying average PD by average LGD understates a bad year.

      Spot itCompare LGD by default vintage, balance before and at default, and ask for a stress test where PD and LGD move together.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A fully drawn AED 200,000 loan has PD 2% and LGD 45%, so EL is AED 1,800. What does that number mean for pricing, and is it what this loan will lose?

    It is the average cost per loan across many similar loans (0.9% of the balance). The margin must cover it plus funding, operating costs and a capital charge. Any single loan loses zero or a large sum, never 1,800.

    2. A credit line has AED 400,000 drawn of a AED 1,000,000 limit. Why might its EAD be well above 400,000, and what goes wrong if you ignore that?

    Borrowers draw more as trouble nears. With a CCF of 50%, EAD is 400,000 + half the undrawn 600,000 = AED 700,000. Using today's balance understates both exposure and expected loss.

    3. A grade has 1,000 borrowers and zero defaults in a year. What can you say about its PD?

    Not that it is zero. The data fit any PD up to about 0.3% at 95% confidence (the rule of three: 3 divided by the number of borrowers). Quote an upper bound, not zero.

    4. A bin holds 10% of all goods but only 5% of all bads. Is it safer or riskier than average, and what is the WOE of an average bin?

    Safer: its good-to-bad odds are twice the portfolio's, so WOE is positive (about 0.69). An average bin has WOE 0, and a riskier one is negative.

    5. A scorecard puts 600 at 50:1 odds with PDO 20. Does 640 imply a higher or lower PD than 600, and roughly by how much?

    Lower. 640 is two doublings of the odds (200:1), so the PD falls from about 2% to about 0.5%, roughly a quarter.

    6. A variable has IV 0.9 on a retail loan application. Great news?

    More likely a warning: leakage (a field recorded after the outcome, such as a collections flag) or a target proxy (an internal rating already built from past behaviour). Check what the variable is and when it is created.

    7. Why can an IFRS 9 PD and a regulatory-capital PD for the same borrower differ, and both be right?

    They answer different questions. IFRS 9 wants an unbiased, point-in-time, forward-looking estimate. Capital rules lean on through-the-cycle, conservative or floored parameters and a downturn LGD. State which is in play and reconcile the two.

    8. Name two reasons a boosted model with a higher Gini might still lose to the scorecard at the model risk committee.

    Instability, non-monotone or unexplainable behaviour for decline reasons, poor calibration by grade, a gain that vanishes out of time, or fairness concerns. Any one can be decisive.
