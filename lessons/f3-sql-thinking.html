    F3.3 Day one About 45 minutes

    SQL Thinking: Joins, Windows and Aggregation

    Bank data lives in warehouses, and most wrong numbers in a bank are born in a query that ran without error. How to read a query by its grain, spot a join that multiplied rows, and know what a window function really does.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA SQL result is a claim about what one row means; nearly every wrong number comes from a join or filter that quietly changed the grain, or silently dropped the rows you meant to keep.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A product head asks: "What is the combined monthly salary of our salary-transfer customers who hold an account with us?" An analyst writes a query that joins the customers table to the accounts table and sums the salary column. It runs in four seconds. It returns a number. The number is nearly double the truth.

    Nothing failed. There was no error, no warning, no NULL. The reason is simple: a customer with three accounts becomes three rows after the join, and the salary is copied onto each of them. The sum then counts that salary three times. A customer with no account disappeared from an inner join, so they were counted zero times. One query, both mistakes at once.

    Play with the toy version below. Five customers, a known true total, and a join you can change.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Every table has a grain, the thing one row stands for. Customers are one row per customer. Accounts are one row per account. A salary lives at the customer grain, so it can only be summed safely at the customer grain.
    • A join changes the grain of the result to the finest of the tables it touches. After joining accounts, a row is an account, and every customer-level column is now repeated. Summing a repeated column is the commonest silent error in banking analytics.
    • The join type decides who survives. An inner join drops customers with no match. That is a form of selection (F2.6) that you chose without noticing.
    Hold onto thisBefore you read or write any query, say in one sentence what one row of each input is, and what one row of the output is. If you can't, you can't yet trust the number.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Grain, keys and fan-out

    In dimensional modelling the first design decision is the grain: the precise meaning of one row. Kimball's rule is to declare it before anything else, because every column added afterwards must be true at that grain. A salary is true per customer. A balance is true per account per day. A transaction amount is true per transaction.

    TableOne row isSafe to SUM hereDangerous to SUM after joining down to
    customersOne customer, as of nowSalary, declared income, risk scoreAccounts, cards, transactions
    accountsOne accountCredit limit, product countTransactions, daily balances
    account_daily_balanceOne account on one dateBalance across accounts on a single date. Never across dates.Anything finer
    transactionsOne posted transactionAmount in AEDAlerts raised, if one transaction can raise several
    aml_alertsOne alert (not one customer)Count of alertsCustomers: use COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)

    The balance row deserves a second look. Balances are semi-additive: you can add them across accounts, but adding them across days is meaningless. The sum of thirty daily balances is not "the balance". It is thirty times the balance. Use the average or the closing value across time.

    A join connects two tables on a key. Whether it is safe depends on the relationship:

    • One-to-one on a key that is unique in both tables. The grain is unchanged. Safe.
    • Many-to-one, such as accounts to customers, where the key is unique on the right. Safe for the left table's columns. The left grain is kept.
    • One-to-many, such as customers to accounts, with a unique key on the left. The result takes the finer grain, and left-hand columns are repeated. This is the fan-out.
    • Many-to-many, where neither side is unique. Rows multiply per key. Two joins of one-to-many type off the same parent, as in Simulation 1 with accounts and cards, behave like this: each customer's accounts are paired with each of their cards.

    The size of the damage is easy to state. Let customer c have ac accounts and bc cards.

    rows=∑cac·bcSUM(salary)=∑csc·ac·bc

    Each customer's salary sc is counted once for every pairing of an account and a card, not once. With one account and one card each, the product is 1 and nothing goes wrong, which is exactly why the bug hides in small test samples. Note the multiplication: adding a second one-to-many join doesn't add rows, it multiplies them. In Simulation 1, a customer with 3 accounts and 2 cards appears 6 times.

    How to defend yourself:

    • Test the key before joining. SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) FROM accounts GROUP BY 1 HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 tells you at once whether accounts is one-per-customer.
    • Compare row counts before and after each join. A left join that returns more rows than the left table has fanned out. An inner join that returns fewer has dropped something.
    • Aggregate the many-side first, down to the grain you want to keep, then join. That's the third button in Simulation 1.
    • Know that COUNT(DISTINCT) and SELECT DISTINCT are patches. They repair counts of keys but not sums of copied values. SUM(DISTINCT salary) is worse: it removes two customers who happen to earn the same amount.

    Inner and left joins: who survives

    Think of a join as a filter on pairs. An inner join keeps only left rows that found a partner. A left join keeps every left row and fills the right side with NULL when no partner exists. Right and full outer joins are the same ideas mirrored or combined; most production SQL uses inner and left only, and treats a right join as a sign the query could be reordered.

    The practical rule is about what you are counting. If the question is "what happens to our customers", the customers table is the spine and everything else is left-joined onto it. An inner join to a reporting table that only contains customers with activity gives you "customers with activity", a smaller population. Churn rates, cure rates and default rates computed on it are selected samples, which is the lesson of F2.6 arriving through a query.

    Two cousins are worth knowing. A semi-join asks "does a match exist?" without multiplying rows, written with EXISTS. An anti-join asks "does no match exist?", written with NOT EXISTS or a left join filtered to NULL on the right key. Anti-joins find customers with no card, accounts never reviewed, or alerts without a case. They are also the safe way to test for membership, as the NULL section below explains.

    The subtlest join bug is where you put a filter on the right-hand table:

    -- A: filter in ON. Keeps every customer; October activity if any
SELECT c.customer_id, SUM(t.amount_aed)
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN transactions t
  ON t.customer_id = c.customer_id
 AND t.txn_month = '2026-10'
GROUP BY c.customer_id;

-- B: filter in WHERE. Silently becomes an inner join
SELECT c.customer_id, SUM(t.amount_aed)
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN transactions t ON t.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE t.txn_month = '2026-10'
GROUP BY c.customer_id;

    In query B, a customer with no transactions has t.txn_month = NULL after the join. NULL = '2026-10' is not true, so WHERE throws the row away. The "left join" now behaves like an inner join, and the customers who did nothing in October, often the ones you most wanted to see, vanish. The rule: a condition that describes which partners count goes in ON. A condition that describes which final rows to keep goes in WHERE. For a left-joined table, that is almost always ON.

    Aggregation, and the mean of means

    GROUP BY collapses many rows into one per group key. Every column in the output must then be either a group key or wrapped in an aggregate. That is not pedantry: it says the output grain is the group key, and anything else has no single value to show.

    Aggregates differ in how they treat NULL, and the differences matter:

    • COUNT(*) counts rows. COUNT(col) counts rows where col is not NULL. They differ whenever the column has gaps.
    • SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX skip NULLs. So AVG(income) is the average over customers who have an income on file, not over all customers. If missing incomes are not random, this is another selection effect.
    • SUM of an all-NULL group is NULL, not zero. Wrap in COALESCE(SUM(x), 0) when a missing total should read as zero.

    The other classic trap is averaging averages. Branch A has 100 customers with an average balance of AED 10,000. Branch B has 900 with AED 40,000. The average of the two averages is AED 25,000. The true average is (100 × 10,000 + 900 × 40,000) / 1,000 = AED 37,000. Whenever you re-aggregate, carry the sums and counts, not the averages, then divide at the end. The same goes for rates: a bank-wide default rate is total defaults over total loans, not the mean of branch rates.

    WHERE filters rows before grouping. HAVING filters groups after, so it is the only place that can refer to an aggregate: HAVING COUNT(*) > 1.

    NULL: three-valued logic

    NULL means "unknown", not "empty" and not "zero". SQL therefore has three truth values, TRUE, FALSE and UNKNOWN. Any comparison with NULL, including NULL = NULL, is UNKNOWN. NOT UNKNOWN is still UNKNOWN. And a WHERE clause keeps a row only if the condition is TRUE. UNKNOWN is thrown away along with FALSE.

    The consequence is awkward: a predicate and its negation don't cover the table. Some rows are neither. In KYC data, the fields that are NULL are rarely a random sample. They are the customers whose onboarding was incomplete, who came through a legacy channel, or whose status wasn't migrated. A filter that drops them has made a decision about those customers without saying so.

    Four habits cover most of the risk.

    • Test for NULL with IS NULL, never with = NULL.
    • Prefer IS DISTINCT FROM (or an explicit COALESCE) for "not equal" when the column may be NULL.
    • Prefer NOT EXISTS to NOT IN. If the subquery returns even one NULL, x NOT IN (subquery) is never true, so the whole filter returns nothing. A single missing customer ID in a blacklist table can silently turn a screening query into one that clears everyone.
    • Count your NULLs in every column you filter or join on. COUNT(*) - COUNT(col) is the fastest way. NULL join keys never match anything, including each other, so those rows vanish from an inner join and sit unmatched in a left one.

    Window functions: aggregate without collapsing

    GROUP BY turns many rows into one. A window function computes something across a set of related rows and puts the answer on every row, keeping them all. That distinction is the whole idea. The window is defined by three parts:

    • PARTITION BY: which rows belong together, like a GROUP BY that doesn't collapse. Per customer, per account. Without it, the whole table is one partition.
    • ORDER BY: the order of rows inside the partition. It makes "previous", "running" and "latest" meaningful.
    • The frame: for each row, which rows inside the partition feed the calculation. ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW is the current row and the two before it. Without an explicit frame, a window with ORDER BY defaults to everything from the start up to the current row's peers.

    That last default hides a trap. Rows with the same ORDER BY value are peers. The default frame (RANGE) includes all peers, so two transactions on the same date both show the same running total, one that already includes the other. Add ROWS and a tiebreaker and each row gets its own step. Try it below.

    The functions you will meet most:

    • Aggregates as windows: SUM, AVG, COUNT over a window. Used for running totals, rolling averages, and "share of the customer's total" (amount divided by the partition sum).
    • Ranking: ROW_NUMBER() numbers rows 1, 2, 3 with no ties. RANK() gives ties the same number and then skips (1, 1, 3). DENSE_RANK() doesn't skip (1, 1, 2).
    • Offsets: LAG(x, 1) is the previous row's value, LEAD the next. The basis of "days since last transaction" and "change in balance from last month".
    • Distribution: NTILE(10) for deciles, PERCENT_RANK(). Useful for score bands.

    The pattern you will write or review most is latest record per entity: customer address, risk rating, account status. It is a ROW_NUMBER ordered by recency, descending, keeping number 1:

    WITH ranked AS (
  SELECT *,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
           PARTITION BY customer_id
           ORDER BY updated_at DESC, record_id DESC   -- tiebreaker
         ) AS rn
  FROM kyc_profile_history
)
SELECT * FROM ranked WHERE rn = 1;

    Note the tiebreaker. If two records share the latest timestamp, ROW_NUMBER picks one arbitrarily, and the choice can change between runs. RANK would keep both and reintroduce duplicates. Either way, an unstable "latest record" will give you a feature that flickers from day to day for no real reason. Some warehouses offer QUALIFY to filter on a window result directly, saving the wrapper.

    Latest record, and as-of joins

    "Latest record" is fine for dashboards about today. For a model, it is a trap. Suppose you build a training set of past loan applications and attach each customer's bureau score. If you attach the latest score, an application from March receives a score from October, which already reflects the missed payments that followed. The model learns from the future. This is leakage (F3.2), and a join is the commonest way it enters.

    The right operation is an as-of join: for each event, find the most recent snapshot at or before the event. History tables usually come in one of two shapes:

    • Snapshots: one row per customer per date (monthly bureau pulls).
    • Slowly changing dimensions (type 2): one row per version, with valid_from and valid_to. The match is event_time >= valid_from AND event_time < valid_to.

    Many engines now have a native ASOF JOIN (DuckDB, Snowflake). In any other engine you can write the same thing with a window function or a correlated subquery, ordered by snapshot time and limited to those not later than the event.

    There is a second clock, and it's the one people forget. A snapshot dated 31 October may only have reached the warehouse on 12 November. A bureau record reflects what the bureau knew at its date, but your bank could not use it before delivery. An honest as-of join uses the availability time (when the system could have known), not just the event time (when it happened). Simulation 4 puts the two side by side.

    Logical query order: how to read any query

    SQL is written in one order and evaluated in another. Written: SELECT … FROM … WHERE … GROUP BY …. Logically, the database works through it like this:

    1. FROM / JOINbuild the row set; grain is fixed here
    2. WHEREfilter rows (TRUE only)
    3. GROUP BYcollapse to groups
    4. HAVINGfilter groups
    5. Window functionsrun over what survived
    6. SELECTcompute outputs and aliases
    7. DISTINCTremove duplicate rows
    8. ORDER BY, LIMITsort, then truncate

    (Real optimisers reorder things for speed, but they must give the same answer as this order.) Several rules follow:

    • You can't use a window function or a SELECT alias in WHERE, because those don't exist yet. That's why "latest record" needs a wrapper or QUALIFY.
    • A window function runs after GROUP BY, so it can work on aggregated results: rank customers by their monthly spend total, in one query.
    • Filtering rows in WHERE changes what a window or aggregate sees. A running total with a WHERE on date will start from zero at the filter, not from the start of history.
    • LIMIT without ORDER BY returns an arbitrary set. Never sample with it.

    A reading checklist for any query, yours, a colleague's, or one an LLM wrote:

    1. What is one row of each input table? What is one row of the output?
    2. For each join: which key, is it unique on each side, inner or left, and what happens to unmatched rows?
    3. Where do the filters sit, and could a NULL slip through or be lost?
    4. Are any summed columns repeated from a coarser grain?
    5. For time: is each value as-of the event, or as-of today?
    6. Do the row counts reconcile with a number you already trust?
    Part 3

    How it connects

    SQL is where the statistics and modelling in the rest of the curriculum get their data. A mistake here passes into every later step unseen, because later steps assume the table is right.

    F2.6 · Selection biasAn inner join is a selection filter

    Dropping customers with no matching row is a choice about who is in the sample. Left-join from the population you care about, then look at what the NULLs say.

    F3.2 · Point-in-time correctnessLeakage enters through joins

    An as-of join with an availability clock is the mechanical fix for features that quietly peek into the future. Part 2 builds the machinery; F3.2 explains why it matters for every model.

    F3.4 · Behavioural featuresRecency, frequency, velocity

    Almost every behavioural feature is a window function: counts in a trailing frame, LAG for gaps between events, MAX of dates for recency.

    F3.1 · Exploratory analysisReconcile first

    Broken joins and duplicates are the first thing to hunt. Row-count reconciliation and key uniqueness checks are exploratory analysis done in SQL.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · MetricsFan-out poisons precision and recall

    If an alert table is joined to a table with several rows per alert, the same alert is counted multiple times and precision and recall are computed on the wrong denominator.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisEvent tables are SQL work

    Time-to-event data needs the right start date, the right end date and honest censoring, all built with date arithmetic and as-of logic before any model sees it.

    F2.1 · UncertaintyDuplicates break standard errors

    Repeated rows look like extra independent data, shrinking intervals and flattering significance. Check the grain before computing any interval.

    Bank useRegulatory and board numbers

    Customer counts, exposure totals, AML alert volumes and complaint statistics are SQL outputs. A wrong join is a misreported figure, and the question after it will be "how was this calculated?"

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core of SQL is old and stable. What is changing is the tooling around it: engines that make as-of joins a built-in, conveniences that shorten the awkward patterns, and language models that now write much of the SQL. Reading it critically has become more important, not less.

    Searches run on 1 November 2026. Benchmark figures below come from the cited papers and may have been overtaken on live leaderboards.

    1. 1970
      The relational model

      Codd's paper defines data as relations (tables) manipulated by set operations. Joins, keys and the logic of "which rows survive" all start here.

      Codd, Communications of the ACM, 1970
    2. 2003
      Window functions standardised

      The SQL standard adds OVER (PARTITION BY … ORDER BY … frame). Analytic work that once needed self-joins or cursors becomes a single clause. Documentation for every major engine still follows this shape.

      PostgreSQL documentation, window function tutorial
    3. 2011
      Temporal tables enter the standard

      System-versioned tables keep history automatically and allow queries "as of" a past time, the database-level version of the as-of join in Part 2.

      SQL:2011; see Kulkarni & Michels, SIGMOD Record, 2012
    4. 2020s
      Native ASOF JOIN

      DuckDB and Snowflake document a built-in ASOF JOIN that matches each row to the closest earlier row on a time column. Feature stores use the same operation to build point-in-time-correct training sets. Support differs between engines, so check yours before relying on it.

      DuckDB and Snowflake documentation; Hopsworks glossary on point-in-time joins
    5. 2020s
      Shorter patterns

      Several warehouses support QUALIFY (filter on a window result without a wrapper) and GROUP BY ALL. They shorten the queries you will read most often. Neither is in the standard yet, so dialects vary.

      Snowflake and DuckDB documentation
    6. 2023–26
      Open table formats and time travel

      Table formats such as Apache Iceberg let a query read the table as it was at an earlier snapshot or timestamp. That makes reproducing last quarter's training set feasible, provided the snapshots are retained.

      Apache Iceberg documentation
    7. 2025
      Text-to-SQL meets the enterprise

      Spider 2.0 tests language models on real enterprise-scale workflows with large schemas, multiple dialects and long queries. In the paper, the best agent framework solved about 21% of tasks, where models score above 90% on the older Spider 1.0 and 73% on BIRD. Clean benchmark schemas flatter the models. Real warehouses, with their ambiguous grains and half-documented columns, are much harder.

      Lei et al., Spider 2.0, ICLR 2025 (arXiv 2411.07763)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you will meet in warehouse queries and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Summing a column repeated by a join

      Customer salary, credit limit or risk-weighted exposure summed after joining to a finer table.

      Spot itCompare the total with the same sum on the source table alone. If the joined version is larger, you've fanned out.
    • A second one-to-many join that multiplies

      Accounts and cards joined to the same customer. Rows are products, not sums.

      Spot itRow count after the join exceeds either child table's count for the same customers. Aggregate each child first.
    • Patching duplicates with DISTINCT

      SELECT DISTINCT or COUNT(DISTINCT) added until the number "looks right".

      Spot itAsk why duplicates exist. If the answer isn't a clear grain statement, the fix is hiding a bug. SUM(DISTINCT amount) is nearly always wrong.
    • The left join that became an inner join

      A WHERE filter on a right-hand column removes the unmatched, NULL rows.

      Spot itAny WHERE that touches a left-joined table. Check whether the row count equals the left table's. Move the condition into ON if you meant to keep the spine.
    • Inner join to an activity table

      "Churn rate among customers" computed only over customers who appear in the transactions table.

      Spot itAsk what the denominator is, and whether it equals the whole customer population.
    • NULLs dropped by a comparison

      status <> 'closed' loses every account whose status is NULL. NOT IN with a NULL in the list returns nothing.

      Spot itRun the filter and its opposite; if the counts don't sum to the total, NULLs are in the gap. Search for NOT IN.
    • Averaging averages

      Branch means averaged, monthly rates averaged, or a rate of rates.

      Spot itLook for AVG applied to a column that is already an average or ratio. Recompute from sums and counts.
    • Latest record without a tiebreaker

      Two records share a timestamp and the "latest" one changes between runs.

      Spot itRerun the query twice, or check whether ORDER BY inside the window is unique per partition.
    • Latest values on a historical training set

      Today's risk rating, bureau score or segment joined to past events. The model looks wonderful in testing, then falls apart live.

      Spot itFor each feature ask: as of when? If it comes from a "current" table, it is leaking. Check for an availability lag too.
    • Summing balances over time

      A semi-additive measure summed across days: "total deposits" that depend on how many days are in the range.

      Spot itThe figure changes when the date range changes though the balance didn't. Use the closing value or the average per day.
    • AI-generated SQL that runs and looks plausible

      Typical signs: joins on the wrong key because two columns have similar names, a join to a history table without a date condition, DISTINCT sprinkled in, filters in WHERE on left-joined tables.

      Spot itApply the checklist in Part 2, and reconcile to a known figure. Language models write fluent SQL and don't know which of your tables are one-per-customer.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The earlier figure counted customers with several accounts several times, so it was too high. I've rebuilt it so each customer is counted once and the customers without an account are shown, not hidden. It now reconciles to the core banking total. I'd suggest we agree the definition of the number in writing, one row per customer, so it can't drift again.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I begin by declaring the grain of every input and the output, then check key uniqueness before each join and reconcile row counts after it. I aggregate the many-side to the output grain before joining, left-join from the population of interest, and watch for NULLs in filters. For anything time-dependent I use an as-of join against availability time so features don't leak. And I reconcile the final figure to a source I trust.”
    “The query ran and the number looks reasonable. Why not just use it?”

    Because the common errors don't fail and don't look absurd. A fanned-out sum is usually off by a factor that seems plausible. I'd check two things first: the row count after each join, and whether the total reconciles to a figure from a source we already trust. It takes five minutes and finds most problems.

    “Can't we just add DISTINCT to remove the duplicates?”

    It makes the row count look right while hiding why there were duplicates. For counts of keys it may be fine. For sums it is dangerous, because distinct removes legitimate rows with equal values and doesn't fix the repeated values. The proper fix is to join at the right grain or aggregate before joining.

    “Why did our customer count drop when we added the product table?”

    Most likely an inner join. Customers with no matching product row were removed. If we want all customers, with product details where they exist, it should be a left join from customers. The customers who were lost are worth a look in their own right: they are often the interesting ones.

    “Why not use the latest customer segment for the whole training set? It's simpler.”

    Because the segment today may be a result of what happened after the event we're predicting. A customer labelled high-risk now might have been labelled so because they later defaulted. Using it gives the model the answer. We need the segment as it was on each application date, and, if the data arrives late, as the bank actually knew it then.

    “Could an LLM write our SQL and save the analysts' time?”

    It can draft well and save a lot of typing, and I'd use it that way. The risk is in the checks it can't do for us: which tables are one row per customer, what a NULL means in this field, which clock a date column uses. Benchmarks on realistic enterprise warehouses show far lower accuracy than on clean ones. So an analyst should review the grain, joins and filters, and reconcile the result before anyone uses the number.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones can be done in your head.

    1. Five customers earn AED 20,000, 15,000, 30,000, 10,000 and 25,000 and hold 3, 1, 2, 0 and 2 accounts. What does SUM(salary) return after an inner join to accounts? After a left join? What is the true total?

    True total: AED 100,000. Inner join: 3 × 20,000 + 15,000 + 2 × 30,000 + 2 × 25,000 = AED 185,000, and Yusuf (no account) is missing. Left join: the same plus Yusuf's one NULL row, 185,000 + 10,000 = AED 195,000. Both wrong; the fix is to aggregate accounts to one row per customer before joining, or sum salary from the customers table alone.

    2. A customer has transactions of AED 400 (1 Oct), then AED 150 and AED 250 both on 2 Oct. With SUM(amount) OVER (ORDER BY date) and no frame, what running totals do the 2 Oct rows show? What if the frame is ROWS?

    Default frame is RANGE, so peers (same date) are included: both 2 Oct rows show 800. With ROWS and a tiebreaker, the first shows 550 and the second 800.

    3. Of 10 accounts, 5 are active, 2 dormant, 1 closed and 2 have NULL status. How many rows does status <> 'active' return? How many does status NOT IN ('closed', NULL) return?

    The first returns 3 (dormant and closed); the NULL rows give UNKNOWN and are dropped, so 5 + 3 = 8, not 10. The second returns 0: for every row that isn't closed, the comparison with NULL makes the result UNKNOWN, and for the closed row it is FALSE.

    4. You left-join customers to October transactions and add WHERE t.txn_month = '2026-10'. What have you actually written, and how do you fix it?

    An inner join in disguise: customers with no October transaction have NULL in t.txn_month, the comparison is UNKNOWN and the row is dropped. Move the condition into the ON clause (or write t.txn_month = '2026-10' OR t.txn_month IS NULL if you must filter after).

    5. Bureau scores are dated days 10, 80 and 150 and reach the warehouse 30 days later. An application is made on day 100. Which score does an as-of join on snapshot date pick? Which should it pick?

    On snapshot date it picks the day-80 snapshot, but that only arrived on day 110, ten days after the application, so it is a leak. Using availability time, the scores available by day 100 are those dated day 10 (available day 40). That is the correct one.

    6. Your "latest address" query uses ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY updated_at DESC), and next week's results differ for a few customers. Why?

    Ties on updated_at. When two rows share the latest timestamp, ROW_NUMBER chooses between them arbitrarily. Add a unique tiebreaker, such as the record id, to the ORDER BY.

    7. Branch A has 100 customers with average balance AED 10,000, and branch B has 900 with AED 40,000. A dashboard shows the bank-wide average as AED 25,000. What is the real average, and what went wrong?

    (100 × 10,000 + 900 × 40,000) / 1,000 = AED 37,000. The dashboard averaged the two branch averages, giving each branch equal weight despite a nine-to-one difference in size. Carry sums and counts, divide at the end.
