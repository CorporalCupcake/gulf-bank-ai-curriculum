    F3.3 Day one About 45 minutes

    SQL Thinking: Joins, Windows and Aggregation

    Bank data lives in warehouses, and most wrong numbers in a bank are born in a query that ran without error. How to read a query by its grain, spot a join that multiplied rows, and know what a window function really does.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA SQL result is a claim about what one row means; nearly every wrong number comes from a join or filter that quietly changed the grain, or silently dropped the rows you meant to keep.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A product head asks for the combined monthly salary of salary-transfer customers who hold an account. An analyst joins customers to accounts and sums salary. It runs in four seconds and returns a number nearly double the truth.

    Nothing failed. A customer with three accounts becomes three rows, with the salary copied onto each, so it is counted three times. A customer with no account vanished from the inner join, so was counted zero times. It is like a restaurant bill split by plates instead of by diners: the total depends on the wrong unit. Play with the toy version below.

    • Every table has a grain: what one row stands for. A salary lives at the customer grain, so it is only safe to sum there.
    • A join changes the grain to the finest table it touches. After joining accounts, a row is an account and every customer column is repeated. Summing a repeated column is the commonest silent error in banking analytics.
    • The join type decides who survives. An inner join drops customers with no match. That is selection (F2.6) you chose without noticing.
    Hold onto thisBefore reading or writing a query, say in one sentence what one row of each input is, and what one row of the output is. If you can't, you can't yet trust the number.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Grain, keys and fan-out

    Declare the grain (the precise meaning of one row) first, as Kimball's dimensional modelling advises. Every column must be true at that grain: salary per customer, balance per account per day, amount per transaction.

    TableOne row isSafe to SUM hereDangerous to SUM after joining down to
    customersOne customer, as of nowSalary, declared income, risk scoreAccounts, cards, transactions
    accountsOne accountCredit limit, product countTransactions, daily balances
    account_daily_balanceOne account on one dateBalance across accounts on a single date, never across datesAnything finer
    transactionsOne posted transactionAmount in AEDAlerts raised, if one transaction can raise several
    aml_alertsOne alert (not one customer)Count of alertsCustomers: use COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)

    Balances are semi-additive: add them across accounts, never across days. Thirty daily balances sum to thirty times the balance, like adding up your speedometer readings to get your speed. Use the average or closing value over time.

    A join connects tables on a key. Safety depends on the relationship:

    • One-to-one (key unique on both sides): grain unchanged. Safe.
    • Many-to-one (accounts to customers, unique on the right): left grain kept. Safe.
    • One-to-many (customers to accounts): result takes the finer grain and left columns repeat. This is fan-out.
    • Many-to-many (neither side unique): rows multiply per key. Two one-to-many joins off the same parent, like accounts and cards, behave this way.
    rows=∑cac·bcSUM(salary)=∑csc·ac·bc

    Customer c has ac accounts, bc cards and salary sc. The salary is counted once per account-card pairing. A second one-to-many join multiplies rows, it doesn't add them: 3 accounts and 2 cards give 6 rows. With one of each the product is 1, which is why the bug hides in small test samples.

    Defences:

    • Test the key before joining. SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) FROM accounts GROUP BY 1 HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 shows whether accounts is one-per-customer.
    • Compare row counts before and after each join. A left join returning more rows than the left table has fanned out; an inner join returning fewer has dropped something.
    • Aggregate the many-side first to the grain you want, then join (the third button in Simulation 1).
    • DISTINCT is a patch. COUNT(DISTINCT) repairs counts of keys, not sums of copied values. SUM(DISTINCT salary) is worse: it drops two customers who happen to earn the same.

    Inner and left joins: who survives

    An inner join keeps only left rows that found a partner, like a guest list at a door: no match, no entry. A left join keeps every left row and fills the right side with NULL when nothing matches. Right and full joins are mirrors or combinations; production SQL mostly uses inner and left.

    If the question is "what happens to our customers", customers is the spine and everything else is left-joined onto it. An inner join to a table holding only active customers answers a different question about a smaller population, so churn, cure and default rates on it are selected samples (F2.6).

    Two cousins: a semi-join (EXISTS) asks "does a match exist?" without multiplying rows. An anti-join (NOT EXISTS, or a left join filtered to NULL on the right key) asks "does none exist?", which finds customers with no card or alerts with no case.

    The subtlest bug is where a filter on the right-hand table sits:

    -- A: filter in ON. Keeps every customer; October activity if any
SELECT c.customer_id, SUM(t.amount_aed)
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN transactions t
  ON t.customer_id = c.customer_id
 AND t.txn_month = '2026-10'
GROUP BY c.customer_id;

-- B: filter in WHERE. Silently becomes an inner join
SELECT c.customer_id, SUM(t.amount_aed)
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN transactions t ON t.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE t.txn_month = '2026-10'
GROUP BY c.customer_id;

    In B, a customer with no transactions has t.txn_month = NULL after the join. NULL = '2026-10' is not true, so WHERE discards the row and the left join behaves like an inner join. The customers who did nothing in October, often the ones you wanted, vanish. Rule: a condition on which partners count goes in ON; a condition on which final rows to keep goes in WHERE. For a left-joined table that is almost always ON.

    Aggregation, and the mean of means

    GROUP BY collapses rows to one per group key, so every output column must be a key or an aggregate: the output grain is the key.

    • COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(col) counts non-NULL values. They differ whenever the column has gaps.
    • SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX skip NULLs. AVG(income) averages customers who have an income on file, a selection effect if gaps aren't random.
    • SUM of an all-NULL group is NULL, not zero. Use COALESCE(SUM(x), 0) if you want zero.

    Averaging averages is the classic trap. Branch A: 100 customers, average AED 10,000. Branch B: 900 customers, AED 40,000. The mean of the two is 25,000, but the true average is (100 × 10,000 + 900 × 40,000) / 1,000 = AED 37,000, just as a class average isn't the mean of two classes of different sizes. Carry sums and counts, divide last. A bank-wide default rate is total defaults over total loans, not the mean of branch rates.

    WHERE filters rows before grouping. HAVING filters groups after, so only it can use an aggregate: HAVING COUNT(*) > 1.

    NULL: three-valued logic

    NULL means "unknown", not empty and not zero. Think of a sealed envelope: you can't say it equals anything, even another sealed envelope. So SQL has TRUE, FALSE and UNKNOWN. Any comparison with NULL, including NULL = NULL, is UNKNOWN; NOT UNKNOWN is UNKNOWN; and WHERE keeps a row only if the condition is TRUE.

    So a predicate and its negation don't cover the table. In KYC data the NULLs are rarely random: incomplete onboarding, legacy channels, unmigrated statuses. A filter that drops them has decided about those customers silently.

    • Test with IS NULL, never = NULL.
    • Use IS DISTINCT FROM (or COALESCE) for "not equal" when NULLs are possible.
    • Prefer NOT EXISTS to NOT IN. One NULL in the subquery makes x NOT IN (subquery) never true, so one missing ID in a blacklist table can make a screening query clear everyone.
    • Count NULLs in every filter or join column: COUNT(*) - COUNT(col). NULL join keys match nothing, not even each other.

    Window functions: aggregate without collapsing

    GROUP BY turns many rows into one. A window function computes across a set of related rows and puts the answer on every row, keeping them all. It is like a class report that prints each pupil's mark next to the class average. Three parts define the window:

    • PARTITION BY: which rows belong together (per customer, per account). Omitted, the whole table is one partition.
    • ORDER BY: row order inside the partition, which makes "previous", "running" and "latest" meaningful.
    • The frame: which rows feed each row's calculation. ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW is the row and the two before. With ORDER BY and no explicit frame, the default is everything from the start up to the current row's peers.

    Rows with the same ORDER BY value are peers. The default RANGE frame includes all peers, so two same-day transactions show the same running total, each already including the other. Use ROWS plus a tiebreaker for one step per row.

    • Aggregates as windows: running totals, rolling averages, share of the customer's total (amount over partition sum).
    • Ranking: ROW_NUMBER() gives 1, 2, 3 with no ties. RANK() shares ties then skips (1, 1, 3). DENSE_RANK() doesn't skip (1, 1, 2).
    • Offsets: LAG(x, 1) is the previous row, LEAD the next. Basis of "days since last transaction".
    • Distribution: NTILE(10) for deciles, PERCENT_RANK(). Useful for score bands.

    The pattern you will write most is latest record per entity (address, risk rating, account status): ROW_NUMBER ordered by recency, keep number 1.

    WITH ranked AS (
  SELECT *,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
           PARTITION BY customer_id
           ORDER BY updated_at DESC, record_id DESC   -- tiebreaker
         ) AS rn
  FROM kyc_profile_history
)
SELECT * FROM ranked WHERE rn = 1;

    The tiebreaker matters. If two records share the latest timestamp, ROW_NUMBER picks one arbitrarily and can change between runs, giving a feature that flickers day to day. RANK would keep both and bring duplicates back. Some warehouses offer QUALIFY to filter on a window result without the wrapper.

    Latest record, and as-of joins

    "Latest record" suits dashboards about today. For a model it is a trap. Attach each customer's latest bureau score to a March loan application and it carries an October score that already reflects the missed payments that followed. The model learns from the future. This is leakage (F3.2), and a join is the commonest way in.

    The fix is an as-of join: for each event, take the most recent snapshot at or before it. History tables come in two shapes:

    • Snapshots: one row per customer per date (monthly bureau pulls).
    • Slowly changing dimensions (type 2): one row per version with valid_from and valid_to. Match on event_time >= valid_from AND event_time < valid_to.

    DuckDB and Snowflake have a native ASOF JOIN. Elsewhere use a window function or correlated subquery limited to snapshots not later than the event.

    There is a second clock. A snapshot dated 31 October may only have reached the warehouse on 12 November, like a letter postmarked Monday and read on Thursday. Use availability time (when the bank could have known), not just event time. Simulation 4 compares them.

    Logical query order: how to read any query

    SQL is written in one order and evaluated in another. Logically, the database works like this (optimisers reorder for speed but must give the same answer):

    1. FROM / JOINbuild the row set; grain is fixed here
    2. WHEREfilter rows (TRUE only)
    3. GROUP BYcollapse to groups
    4. HAVINGfilter groups
    5. Window functionsrun over what survived
    6. SELECTcompute outputs and aliases
    7. DISTINCTremove duplicate rows
    8. ORDER BY, LIMITsort, then truncate
    • Window functions and SELECT aliases don't exist yet in WHERE. That is why "latest record" needs a wrapper or QUALIFY.
    • Windows run after GROUP BY, so you can rank customers by monthly spend total in one query.
    • A WHERE changes what a window or aggregate sees: a running total filtered by date starts from zero at the filter.
    • LIMIT without ORDER BY returns an arbitrary set. Never sample with it.

    Checklist for any query, yours, a colleague's or an LLM's:

    1. What is one row of each input? Of the output?
    2. For each join: which key, unique on each side, inner or left, what happens to unmatched rows?
    3. Where do filters sit, and could a NULL slip through or be lost?
    4. Are any summed columns repeated from a coarser grain?
    5. For time: is each value as-of the event, or as-of today?
    6. Do row counts reconcile with a number you already trust?
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Summing a column repeated by a join

      Salary, credit limit or exposure summed after joining to a finer table.

      Spot itCompare with the same sum on the source table alone. Larger means fan-out.
    • A second one-to-many join that multiplies

      Accounts and cards joined to the same customer.

      Spot itRow count exceeds either child table's count for those customers. Aggregate each child first.
    • Patching duplicates with DISTINCT

      SELECT DISTINCT or COUNT(DISTINCT) added until the number "looks right".

      Spot itAsk why duplicates exist. No clear grain statement means a hidden bug. SUM(DISTINCT amount) is nearly always wrong.
    • The left join that became an inner join

      A WHERE filter on a right-hand column removes the NULL rows.

      Spot itAny WHERE touching a left-joined table. Does the row count equal the left table's? Move the condition into ON.
    • Inner join to an activity table

      "Churn rate among customers" computed only over customers in the transactions table.

      Spot itAsk what the denominator is, and whether it is the whole population.
    • NULLs dropped by a comparison

      status <> 'closed' loses NULL-status accounts. NOT IN with a NULL returns nothing.

      Spot itRun the filter and its opposite; if counts don't sum to the total, NULLs are in the gap. Search for NOT IN.
    • Averaging averages

      Branch means, monthly rates or rates of rates averaged.

      Spot itAVG on a column that is already an average or ratio. Recompute from sums and counts.
    • Latest record without a tiebreaker

      Two records share a timestamp and "latest" changes between runs.

      Spot itRerun twice, or check the window ORDER BY is unique per partition.
    • Latest values on a historical training set

      Today's risk rating, bureau score or segment joined to past events. Wonderful in testing, broken live.

      Spot itFor each feature ask: as of when? A "current" table means leakage. Check for availability lag too.
    • Summing balances over time

      A semi-additive measure summed across days.

      Spot itThe figure changes with the date range though the balance didn't. Use closing value or daily average.
    • AI-generated SQL that runs and looks plausible

      Joins on a similarly named wrong key, history tables joined without a date condition, DISTINCT sprinkled in, left-joined filters in WHERE.

      Spot itApply the Part 2 checklist and reconcile to a known figure. LLMs write fluent SQL but don't know which tables are one-per-customer.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones can be done in your head.

    1. Five customers earn AED 20,000, 15,000, 30,000, 10,000 and 25,000 and hold 3, 1, 2, 0 and 2 accounts. What does SUM(salary) return after an inner join to accounts? After a left join? What is the true total?

    True: AED 100,000. Inner: 3 × 20,000 + 15,000 + 2 × 30,000 + 2 × 25,000 = AED 185,000, with Yusuf (no account) missing. Left: plus Yusuf's NULL row, 195,000. Both wrong. Aggregate accounts to one row per customer first, or sum from customers alone.

    2. A customer has AED 400 (1 Oct), then AED 150 and AED 250 both on 2 Oct. With SUM(amount) OVER (ORDER BY date) and no frame, what running totals do the 2 Oct rows show? With ROWS?

    Default RANGE includes peers: both 2 Oct rows show 800. With ROWS and a tiebreaker: 550, then 800.

    3. Of 10 accounts, 5 are active, 2 dormant, 1 closed and 2 have NULL status. How many rows does status <> 'active' return? How many does status NOT IN ('closed', NULL) return?

    The first returns 3 (dormant and closed). NULL rows are UNKNOWN and dropped, so 5 + 3 = 8, not 10. The second returns 0: non-closed rows compare with NULL and give UNKNOWN, and the closed row is FALSE.

    4. You left-join customers to October transactions and add WHERE t.txn_month = '2026-10'. What have you written, and how do you fix it?

    An inner join in disguise: customers with no October transaction have NULL there, the comparison is UNKNOWN and the row is dropped. Move the condition into ON.

    5. Bureau scores are dated days 10, 80 and 150 and reach the warehouse 30 days later. An application is made on day 100. Which score does an as-of join on snapshot date pick? Which should it pick?

    Snapshot date picks day 80, but that arrived on day 110, after the application: a leak. By availability time, only the day-10 score (available day 40) was known. That is correct.

    6. Your "latest address" query uses ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY updated_at DESC) and next week's results differ for a few customers. Why?

    Ties on updated_at: ROW_NUMBER picks arbitrarily between them. Add a unique tiebreaker such as the record id.

    7. Branch A has 100 customers averaging AED 10,000, branch B has 900 averaging AED 40,000. A dashboard shows AED 25,000 bank-wide. What is the real average, and what went wrong?

    (100 × 10,000 + 900 × 40,000) / 1,000 = AED 37,000. The dashboard averaged the two averages, weighting the branches equally despite a nine-to-one size difference. Carry sums and counts, divide at the end.
