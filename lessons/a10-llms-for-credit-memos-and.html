    Credit Memos, Uplift and Climate Risk: Three Frontier Tools for the Credit Desk

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    The memo. A model drafts in a minute what takes an analyst two days. The risk is that it reads well, and one of its forty claims is a number that appears in no document.

    The phone call. A collections team can afford to call 3,000 of 10,000 late customers. The usual approach calls those most likely to pay, but many would have paid anyway. Like a flu jab, a call only adds value where it changes the outcome.

    The weather. The central bank asks how the book fares under a carbon price or a coastal flood. No history of such a world exists, so the answer is scenarios laid over models the bank already has.

    The common thread: the quantity you can easily compute is not the one you care about. Fluent text is not accurate text. Likelihood to pay is not the effect of a call. Last year's default rate is not next decade's.

    Hold onto thisAsk three questions of any of these tools. What counts as correct (a claim, a customer, a scenario)? What must it beat (an analyst, a random call list, today's stress test)? Who can reproduce the number? Without all three, it is a demo, not a control.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    LLMs for credit memos and covenant monitoring

    Most of a credit memo is summarising facts already in documents, which a language model does well. Split the job like a kitchen, with each station doing what it is good at:

    • Extraction turns PDFs and scans into fields. Show each value beside its source page (document AI, parsing PDFs).
    • Calculation is ordinary code. A model doing arithmetic in its head will sometimes be wrong, confidently.
    • Drafting is where the model writes, only from the facts it is handed, with a citation for every claim.
    • Review stays with a human who signs (guardrails and approval gates).

    Validating a drafted memo

    Validate per claim, not per memo. For each statement ask: supported by a cited passage, contradicted by the source, or simply not in it? The third, a plausible sentence with no support, is the classic hallucination and the hardest to catch because it sounds right (see groundedness checking). Try it by hand.

    Errors compound. Like a chain with 30 links, one weak link breaks it. A model that is right on 99% of claims still produces a flawed 30-claim memo about one time in four. Hence a citation for every claim, and human review of the cited passages, not the prose.

    Proving a low error rate takes many claims. Claiming "below 1%" takes about 300 clean claims. A wrong covenant threshold matters more than a wrong adjective, so test each claim type separately (statistics for evals).

    Covenant monitoring

    A financial covenant is a test in the loan agreement, such as "total debt ÷ EBITDA must not exceed 3.5x". A breach lets the lender act. The model reads the agreement; a deterministic calculator runs the test.

    The numbers are not the hard part. Definitions are. "Debt" may or may not include leases. "EBITDA" often allows negotiated add-backs. Later waivers change earlier terms. Two readings of one clause can flip a pass into a breach.

    For the build: extract the covenant definition as well as the threshold, keep the clause text attached, make the calculator show which definition it used, and flag any clause touched by a later amendment. Measure on your own agreements with a lawyer-labelled test set.

    Uplift for collections and retention

    A normal payment or churn model predicts who will pay (or stay). A campaign needs who will pay (or stay) because we acted. Think of an umbrella seller: the people carrying umbrellas are the ones who would have stayed dry anyway.

    Uplift is the extra chance of paying because a customer was called. +0.45 means 45 more payers per 100 calls; 0 means the call changes nothing; negative means it backfires. You never see it for one person, so it is estimated from groups and needs a comparison group (causal inference).

    Customers fall into four types:

    • Persuadables pay only if called. Large positive uplift. The target.
    • Sure things pay either way. The AED 30 call is wasted.
    • Lost causes pay neither way. Also wasted.
    • Sleeping dogs are less likely to pay if contacted: a call prompts a dispute, complaint or exit. Negative uplift.

    A propensity model puts sure things at the top. That looks like success and is not. Retention works the same way: do not discount for a customer who was staying anyway (churn and retention).

    At 30% calls the difference is stark. The uplift list spends the calls on persuadables and wins about 920 extra payers (roughly AED 5.5m). The likelihood list calls sure things and wins about 60. Same cost, about fifteen times less benefit.

    How uplift is estimated

    • Randomise. Call a random 50% and hold back the rest. Past calls chosen by agents or rules give confounded data (confounding and selection).
    • Two-model approach: one model on the called group, one on the uncalled, subtract. Simple, but the two models' noise adds up. Uplift trees and causal forests are the more refined standard.
    • Evaluation: the Qini curve. Sort by predicted uplift and plot cumulative extra payers, called against held-back. It is the chart above, estimated from a randomised holdout. Effects are small and noisy, so ask for intervals (bootstrap).

    Collections also carries conduct rules on contact and vulnerable customers. "Do not call this person" is usually safe. "Call this person more" for someone in difficulty needs policy sign-off.

    Climate and ESG risk modelling

    Climate risk reaches a bank through two channels.

    • Physical risk: heat and flooding damage collateral and interrupt income. Coastal real estate matters in the Gulf.
    • Transition risk: carbon prices and shifting demand squeeze emissions-heavy sectors.

    No history of a 2040 climate exists, so the method is scenarios laid over existing credit models, like a fire drill for a fire you have never had. Define a scenario, translate it into shocks to revenue, costs or collateral by sector and location, push those through the PD and LGD models (PD, LGD, EAD) and compare expected loss with base. Where it feeds provisioning, it touches IFRS 9 forward-looking information and stress testing.

    The hard part is data: borrower emissions are often estimated from sector averages, with large error, and ESG ratings from different vendors disagree widely. Disclose that.

    A scenario is a disciplined "what if", not a forecast. Its use is to find concentrations and test whether strategy survives. Climate effects build over decades while loan books reprice in years, so supervisors ask banks to explain that mismatch.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The plausible unsupported sentence

      "Management has a strong record of refinancing on time." Nothing in the file says so.

      Spot itRequire a citation per claim and check the passage supports it.
    • Omission

      The draft is accurate but leaves out a related-party loan, pending litigation or a breach.

      Spot itSeed test memos with known risks and see how often the draft surfaces them.
    • Covenant definition misread or superseded

      The model reads the original agreement and misses a waiver letter that changed the test.

      Spot itLink every covenant to its clause and amendment history.
    • Calling the propensity model an uplift model

      It ranks by chance of paying, yet is sold as "uplift".

      Spot itAsk what the control group was. No randomised held-back group means no measured uplift.
    • Noise sold as signal

      The Qini curve looks great on one split, but the interval spans zero.

      Spot itAsk for bootstrap intervals and a plain-model baseline.
    • Climate numbers with false precision

      "Climate adds AED 67.2m of expected loss", built on estimated emissions.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity to the main assumptions and how much data is estimated.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A memo tool is right on 99.5% of claims. Why is that still not safe to ship without review?

    Errors add up across claims. With 40 claims, about one memo in five still contains an error, so citations and review stay.

    2. You check 200 claims and none are wrong. Can you say the tool is error-free?

    No. You can say the error rate is probably low. Showing it is below 1% takes roughly 300 clean claims, and lower takes many more.

    3. Why can the same borrower pass or breach a leverage covenant with no change in the business?

    The definitions decide the numbers. Counting leases as debt shrinks the cushion, and add-backs widen it. In the simulation, leases cut the tolerable EBITDA fall from 20% to under 9%.

    4. Why is a propensity-to-pay model a poor way to choose whom to call?

    It ranks by chance of paying after a call, so "sure things" sit at the top and sleeping dogs are ignored. The target is the difference in payment chance with and without a call.

    5. Why can calling everyone do worse than calling the right half?

    Sleeping dogs have negative uplift. Calling them costs the call and loses payments that would have come anyway, so they pull the total down.

    6. How do you evaluate an uplift model when you never see one customer both called and not called?

    Use a randomised holdout. Rank by predicted uplift, then compare treated against control within each rank band (the Qini curve). Give bootstrap intervals, because effects are small.

    7. Why are climate scenarios not forecasts, and what do they do for a bank?

    No-one knows the carbon price path or hazard timing. Scenarios stress the book to expose concentrations and test strategy and capital. They need stated assumptions and sensitivity ranges, not one number.
