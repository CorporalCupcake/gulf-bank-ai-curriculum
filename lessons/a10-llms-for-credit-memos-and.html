    LLMs on the Credit Desk: Memos, Uplift and Climate Risk

    Part 1

    Three tools, one idea

    In one sentenceThree newer tools help the credit desk: a language model that drafts credit memos, an uplift model that picks collections calls that change the outcome, and climate scenarios laid over existing credit models.

    Each has an easy number that is not the one you care about: fluent text is not accurate text, and likelihood to pay is not the effect of a call.

    Ask three questions of any of them. What counts as correct (a claim, a customer, a scenario)? What must it beat (an analyst, a random call list, today's stress test)? Who can reproduce the number? Without all three answers, it is a demo, not a control.

    Terms: PD is probability of default, LGD is loss given default (the share lost if a borrower defaults), and expected loss is exposure × PD × LGD.

    Part 2

    Credit memos and covenants

    A credit memo is the analyst's written case for a loan. An analyst takes about two days; a model drafts it in a minute. The risk is that it reads well, and one of its forty claims is a number that appears in no document.

    The fix is to split the job like a kitchen, so each station does what it is good at.

    • Extract: turn PDFs and scans into fields, each shown next to its source page.
    • Calculate: ordinary code does the arithmetic. A model doing sums "in its head" is sometimes confidently wrong.
    • Draft: the model writes only from the facts it is handed, with a citation for every claim.
    • Review: a human reads the cited passages, not just the prose, and signs.

    How to check a drafted memo

    Check per claim, not per memo. For each statement ask: is it supported by the cited passage, contradicted by it, or simply not in the source? The third is the classic hallucination ("Management has a strong record of refinancing on time", when nothing says so).

    • Claim support rate: share of claims whose cited passage really supports them, judged by a human sample, by claim type.
    • Omission rate: seed test memos with a known risk (a covenant breach, a related-party loan) and see whether the draft mentions it.

    Errors compound: a model right on 99% of claims still produces a flawed 30-claim memo about one time in four. Test each claim type separately, since a wrong covenant threshold matters more than a wrong adjective.

    Covenant monitoring

    A covenant is a test written into the loan agreement, such as "total debt ÷ EBITDA must not exceed 3.5x" (EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). The model reads the agreement; a calculator runs the test.

    The hard part is definitions. "Debt" may or may not include leases. "EBITDA" often allows negotiated add-backs. A later waiver can change an earlier term. Take a borrower with AED 420m of debt and AED 150m of EBITDA: leverage is 2.8x, comfortably under 3.5x. If leases of AED 60m count as debt, it becomes 480 ÷ 150 = 3.2x, a much thinner cushion, with no change in the business.

    Part 3

    Uplift for collections

    A collections team can afford to call 3,000 of 10,000 late customers. The usual approach calls those most likely to pay, but many would have paid anyway. Like a flu jab, a call only adds value where it changes the outcome.

    Uplift is the extra chance of paying because a customer was called. An uplift of +0.45 means 45 more payers per 100 calls; 0 means the call changes nothing; negative means it backfires.

    Customers fall into four types:

    • Persuadables pay only if called. They are the target.
    • Sure things pay either way. The call is wasted.
    • Lost causes pay neither way. Also wasted.
    • Sleeping dogs are less likely to pay if contacted: a call prompts a dispute, complaint or exit.

    A likelihood-to-pay model puts sure things at the top, which looks like success and is not. In a simple simulation of 10,000 customers, calling 30% by uplift won about 920 extra payers, against about 60 by likelihood.

    Nobody's uplift is seen directly, so estimate it from a randomised test: call a random half, hold back the rest, and compare.

    Part 4

    Climate and ESG risk

    Climate risk reaches a bank through two channels. Physical risk: heat and flooding damage collateral and interrupt income, and coastal real estate matters in the Gulf. Transition risk: carbon prices and shifting demand squeeze emissions-heavy sectors.

    No history of a 2040 climate exists, so use scenarios laid over existing models, like a fire drill for a fire you have never had.

    1. Define a scenario, such as a carbon price or a coastal flood.
    2. Translate it into shocks to revenue, costs or collateral, by sector and place.
    3. Push those through the PD and LGD models.
    4. Compare expected loss with the base case.

    A toy example: a flood that lifts coastal real estate LGD by 10 points, on AED 8,000m of exposure at 1.8% PD, raises expected loss by 8,000 × 0.018 × 0.10 = AED 14.4m.

    The hard part is data. Borrower emissions are often estimated from sector averages, with large error, and ESG ratings from different vendors disagree widely. A scenario is a disciplined "what if", not a forecast.

    Part 5

    Which to use

    UpliftA random call listRandomised holdout, Qini curveNoise sold as signal

    Strong at:

    • LLM drafting and scenarios: turning stacks of documents into a first draft, with the human checking citations, and stressing models where no history exists.

    Weak at:

    • Uplift: needs a randomised test, and collections has conduct rules on contact and vulnerable customers.
    • Scenarios: climate effects build over decades, while loan books reprice in years.
    Show the formulas

    Leverage = covenant debt ÷ covenant EBITDA, compared with the limit (3.5x above).

    Expected loss = exposure × PD × LGD.

    Uplift = chance of paying if called − chance of paying if not called.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting a memo because it reads well

      Check every claim against its cited passage. A plausible sentence with no support is the main failure.

    • Missing the omission

      An accurate draft can still leave out a related-party loan or pending litigation. Test with seeded risks.

    • Calling a propensity model an uplift model

      If there was no randomised held-back group, no uplift was measured.

    • Treating a scenario as a forecast

      It is a stress on assumptions. Report the data gaps with the numbers.

    1. Why do arithmetic in code rather than in the model?

    A model doing sums can be confidently wrong. Code is exact and repeatable.

    2. A model is right on 99% of claims. Why is a 30-claim memo still risky?

    About one memo in four has at least one wrong claim, because errors compound.

    3. Debt AED 480m including leases, EBITDA AED 150m, limit 3.5x. Breach?

    480 ÷ 150 = 3.2x, under the limit, but with a thin cushion.

    4. Who are the sleeping dogs in collections?

    Customers less likely to pay if contacted, because the call prompts a dispute or exit. Never call them.

    5. Why use scenarios for climate risk?

    No history of such a world exists, so you stress the models you already have.
