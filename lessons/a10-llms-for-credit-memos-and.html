    Credit Memos, Uplift and Climate Risk: Three Frontier Tools for the Credit Desk

    How to let a language model draft a credit memo and watch covenants without letting it invent a number, how to spend collections and retention effort only where it changes the outcome, and what supervisors now expect when they ask about climate.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceEach of these three tools earns its place only when you can measure it against the right question: is every claim in the memo traceable to a source, did the action change the customer's behaviour (not just predict it), and does the scenario move the loss numbers the bank already reports.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three problems from a corporate and retail credit floor, with one thing in common.

    The memo. A relationship manager at a Gulf bank spends two days turning a borrower's financial statements, a site visit note and a term sheet into a credit memo for committee. A language model can write the first draft in a minute. The danger is not that the draft reads badly. It reads well, and one of its forty claims may be a number that appears in no document.

    The phone call. A collections team has 10,000 customers who are a month late. Calling costs AED 30 each. The usual approach calls those most likely to pay. But many of those would have paid anyway, and a few will be irritated into a complaint. The call only creates value for customers whose behaviour it changes.

    The weather. The central bank asks how the loan book would fare under a carbon price, or a coastal flood. There is no history of such a world to train on. The answer has to come from scenarios laid over models the bank already has.

    The common thread: the quantity you can easily compute is not the quantity you care about. Fluent text is not accurate text. Likelihood to pay is not the effect of a call. Last year's default rate is not next decade's. Each tool is a way of getting at the real quantity, and each has a specific, checkable failure.

    Hold onto thisAsk three questions of any of these tools. What is the unit of correctness (a claim, a customer, a scenario)? What is the baseline it must beat (an analyst, a random call list, today's stress test)? Who can reproduce the number? If you cannot answer all three, it is a demo, not a control.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    LLMs for credit memos and covenant monitoring

    A credit memo is a structured argument: who the borrower is, what they want, how they have performed, what could go wrong, what protections exist, and a recommendation. Most of it is summarising and arranging facts that already sit in documents: audited accounts, management accounts, bank statements, the facility agreement, a rating report. That is the kind of task a language model does well and fast.

    The design that works in practice separates what a model is good at from what it is not:

    • Extraction turns PDFs and scans into structured fields (revenue, debt, maturity dates). This is document AI, covered in document AI for trade finance and parsing messy PDFs. Errors here propagate everywhere, so extracted values are shown beside the page they came from.
    • Calculation is done by ordinary code. Ratios such as leverage and interest cover are computed from the extracted fields by a function a validator can read. A model that does arithmetic in its head will sometimes be wrong, and will say so with confidence.
    • Drafting is where the model writes sentences, but only from the structured facts and retrieved passages it is handed, and with a citation for every factual claim.
    • Review stays with a human who signs the memo. The model's job is to make that review quicker and better aimed, not to remove it. See guardrails and approval gates.

    Validating a drafted memo

    The unit of validation is the claim, not the memo. Split the draft into atomic statements and ask of each: is it supported by a cited source passage, does it contradict the source, or is it simply not in the source? The third case, a plausible sentence with no support, is the classic hallucination and is the hardest to catch by reading, because it sounds right. The techniques for automating this check are in hallucination detection and groundedness. Try the manual version first.

    Two facts make this harder than it looks, and both come from simple arithmetic.

    Errors compound. If each claim is right with probability 1 − e and a memo has k independent claims, the chance the whole memo is clean is (1 − e)k. At 1% error per claim and 30 claims, that is 0.9930 ≈ 74%. A model that is "99% accurate" produces a flawed memo roughly one time in four. This is why every claim needs a citation and why the human review looks at the cited passages, not at the prose.

    Proving a low error rate takes many claims. If you check n claims and find none wrong, the 95% upper bound on the true error rate is about 3 ÷ n (the "rule of three"). To be able to say the error rate is below 1% you need about 300 clean claims. Below 0.1%, about 3,000. And claims are not equal: a wrong covenant threshold matters more than a wrong adjective, so stratify the test set by claim type. The statistics are the same as in statistics for evals.

    Covenant monitoring

    A loan agreement contains financial covenants: tests such as "total debt divided by EBITDA must not exceed 3.5x, tested quarterly". A breach lets the lender act. Monitoring means (a) reading the agreement to extract each covenant with its exact definition, (b) collecting the borrower's compliance figures every quarter, (c) computing the test, and (d) escalating when headroom is thin or the definition is in dispute.

    The language model does (a) and the reading in (b). A deterministic calculator does (c). The difficulty is that the numbers are not the hard part: definitions are. "Debt" may or may not include lease liabilities. "EBITDA" is often a negotiated figure that allows certain add-backs up to a cap. Amendments and waivers in later letters change earlier terms. Two readings of one clause can flip a pass into a breach.

    What this means for the build: extract the covenant definition as well as the threshold, keep the clause text attached to every extracted term, have the calculator expose which definition it used, and flag any clause where the model's confidence is low or a later amendment touches it. Evidence on how well models read bespoke legal wording is thin (see Part 4), so measure it on your own agreements, with a lawyer-labelled test set.

    Uplift for collections and retention

    A normal churn or payment model predicts who will pay (or stay). A campaign needs to know who will pay (or stay) because we acted. These are different, and the difference is the whole subject. Formally, uplift (also called the conditional average treatment effect) is

    τ(x)=P(pay|x, called)−P(pay|x, not called)

    For a customer with features x, uplift is the extra chance of paying if called. It is never observed for one person, because each customer is either called or not. It must be estimated from groups, which is why it needs a comparison group and links directly to causal inference.

    Marketing people split customers into four types by how they respond:

    • Persuadables pay if called and not otherwise. Uplift is large and positive. This is who you want.
    • Sure things pay either way. The call is a wasted AED 30.
    • Lost causes do not pay either way. Also wasted.
    • Sleeping dogs are less likely to pay if contacted: a call prompts a dispute, a complaint, or a decision to leave. Uplift is negative.

    A propensity model ranks sure things at the top, because they have the highest chance of paying after a call. That looks like success and is not. In retention, the same logic applies: do not spend a discount on a customer who was staying anyway, and be careful about nudging a customer who had not thought about leaving. See churn and retention.

    Money check at 30% calls (3,000 customers) with a perfect ordering: the top 2,000 are persuadables (+0.45 each = 900 extra payers) and the next 1,000 are sure things (+0.02 each = 20). That is 920 extra payers, worth 920 × 6,000 = AED 5.52m, for a call cost of AED 90,000. The likelihood list calls the 3,000 sure things first: 3,000 × 0.02 = 60 extra payers, AED 360,000. Same cost. About fifteen times less benefit.

    How uplift is estimated

    • You need randomisation, or a lot of care. The cleanest data comes from a randomised pilot: call a random 50% and hold back the rest. Without it, past calls were chosen by agents or rules, and customers who were called differ from those who were not (confounding and selection).
    • Two-model ("T-learner"): fit one model on the called group, one on the uncalled, and subtract. Simple, but the noise in two models adds up.
    • One-model ("S-learner") with treatment as a feature. Can underplay the effect, because trees may ignore a weak feature.
    • Uplift trees and causal forests split directly to separate customers whose treatment effect differs. They are the standard for tabular data.
    • Evaluation uses the Qini curve or AUUC: sort customers by predicted uplift, then plot cumulative extra payers in the treated group against the control. It is the quantity in the chart above, estimated from a randomised holdout. Uplift effects are small and noisy, so always give intervals (bootstrap); many benchmarks find a plain outcome model competitive.

    In collections there is also a constraint no marketing campaign faces: a conduct and fairness one. Contact frequency, hardship and treatment of vulnerable customers are regulated areas. An uplift model that says "do not call this person" is usually safe. One that says "call this person more" for a customer in financial difficulty needs policy sign-off. Check the exact conduct rules your regulator and bank apply; do not assume.

    Climate and ESG risk modelling

    Climate risk reaches a bank through two channels.

    • Physical risk: heat, flooding, storm surge and sea-level rise damage collateral and interrupt borrowers' income. Coastal real estate and logistics matter in the Gulf.
    • Transition risk: carbon prices, new regulation, changing demand and technology squeeze borrowers in emissions-heavy sectors. For hydrocarbon-linked economies, the pace of the global energy transition is directly relevant to the loan book.

    Because there is no history of a 2040 climate to train on, the standard method is scenario analysis laid over existing credit models. Define a scenario (a carbon price path, a flood event). Translate it into shocks to borrowers' revenue, costs or collateral values by sector and location. Push those through the PD and LGD models (PD, LGD, EAD) to get expected loss under each scenario, then compare with base. Where it feeds provisioning, it touches IFRS 9 forward-looking information and stress testing.

    The real work is data: the emissions of borrowers (often unreported, so estimated from sector averages), the location and elevation of collateral, and the sensitivity of each sector. Estimated emissions carry large error, and ESG ratings from different vendors disagree widely. Disclose that.

Summed over the five sectors, the toy book goes from AED 184.1m to 251.2m, about 36% higher.

    Two honest caveats. First, a scenario is not a forecast; it is a disciplined "what if", and its use is to find concentrations and test whether the bank's strategy survives. Second, time horizon matters: climate effects can build over decades, while loan books reprice in years. Supervisors ask banks to explain how they handle that mismatch.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    • Memos and groundedness. The claim-audit idea is the same as in groundedness checking, and the sample-size reasoning is statistics for evals. Retrieval of the right passage is document parsing plus search.
    • Covenants and documents. Reading agreements shares tooling with document AI for trade finance. An alert that triggers a call to the borrower is an action, so it belongs behind approval gates.
    • Credit data inputs. Cash-flow data from open-finance underwriting can feed early warning alongside covenants. Both memo and covenant work end up in front of validators: see what validators can accept today and writing for validators.
    • Uplift and causal thinking. It is applied causal inference, depends on clean experiments (experiments and write-ups), and avoids the bias in confounding. The churn model it improves on is in churn and retention. Evaluation needs a leakage-safe set-up: point-in-time features.
    • Climate and credit risk. Scenario shocks flow through PD, LGD and EAD and into IFRS 9 and stress testing.
    • Gulf context. Collections and retention happen under conduct rules; climate is now a supervisory topic (Part 4); credit memos for large local corporates often involve government-related entities, family groups and Islamic structures, where the "obligor" and the source documents need care.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The three areas are at different stages. Climate risk is a supervisory requirement and moving to routine. Uplift is mature as method but under-used in banks. LLMs for credit memos are in active pilot, with strong vendor claims and little independent evidence.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The plausible unsupported sentence

      "Management has a strong record of refinancing on time." Nothing in the file says so.

      Spot itRequire a citation for each factual claim and verify that the cited passage entails it. Sample for claims whose citation is missing or only loosely related.
    • Arithmetic done by the model

      Ratios computed in the prose, often rounded or using the wrong line (net debt for gross debt).

      Spot itRecompute every figure in the memo from structured data. Any difference is a defect.
    • Omission

      The draft is accurate but leaves out a related-party loan, a pending litigation note, or a breach.

      Spot itSeed test memos with known risks and measure how often the draft surfaces them.
    • Automation bias in review

      Reviewers skim fluent drafts and sign. The edit rate falls and everyone calls it quality.

      Spot itInsert known errors into a sample of drafts without telling reviewers. Track the catch rate.
    • Covenant definition misread or superseded

      The model reads the original agreement and misses a waiver letter that changed the test.

      Spot itLink every extracted covenant to clause and amendment history. Flag documents with later amendments for lawyer review.
    • Calling the propensity model an uplift model

      It ranks customers by chance of paying, and the "uplift" claims come from comparing top decile outcomes.

      Spot itAsk: what was the control group, and was it randomised? Without a held-back group, uplift is not measured.
    • Uplift from non-random history

      Past calls went to the customers agents thought most promising; the model learns who agents like.

      Spot itCheck how treatment was assigned. If by judgement or rule, treat the estimates as biased until a randomised pilot confirms them.
    • Noise sold as signal

      Qini curve looks great on one split; with 5,000 customers per arm the interval spans zero.

      Spot itAsk for bootstrap intervals and a comparison with a plain outcome model.
    • Ignoring conduct limits

      The model recommends repeated contact for a customer showing hardship signals.

      Spot itPut policy constraints (contact caps, vulnerable-customer exclusions) in the decision layer, outside the model, and test them.
    • Climate numbers with false precision

      "Climate adds AED 67.2m of expected loss", reported to a decimal on estimated emissions.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity to the main assumptions and the share of borrowers with estimated rather than reported data.
    • Scenario presented as forecast

      A single carbon-price path treated as "what will happen".

      Spot itLook for multiple scenarios, stated horizons, and a note on what is not modelled (second-round effects, adaptation).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a head of credit · 30 seconds
    “We'd use the model to read documents and write the first draft of the memo. Every figure comes from code, and every statement points to its source passage. An analyst signs it. Before we go live we measure, on 300 or more of our own claims, how often a cited statement is not supported, and we plant known risks to check the draft doesn't leave them out. The gain is analyst time, and we'll show it against a control group.”
    To a collections head or validator
    “Likelihood to pay tells us who will pay. We want who will pay because we called. That needs a randomised holdout, say 10% of the pool, left alone. Then we rank by estimated uplift, evaluate with a Qini curve and intervals, and compare with a simple outcome model. Contact rules and vulnerable-customer exclusions sit outside the model, so the model can't override them.”
    “The vendor says their memo tool is 98% accurate. Is that good?”

    Accurate at what, on whose documents? Ask for the unit (claim or memo), the sample size, claim types, and the error rate on figures separately. At 98% per claim and 30 claims a memo, only 0.9830 ≈ 55% of memos are fully clean. I'd test it on our agreements and gate it by citation checks, not trust the headline.

    “Can the model just do the covenant calculation too?”

    I'd keep it out of the arithmetic. Models make occasional calculation errors with full confidence, and a validator can read a function but not a prompt. The model extracts the definition and the inputs; code computes; the output shows which definition was used. The hard cases are definition disputes, which go to a person.

    “We already have a churn model. Why do we need uplift?”

    Churn tells you who is leaving; it doesn't tell you whom a retention offer will save. Customers about to leave and unreachable cost money, and loyal customers given a discount cost margin. Uplift targets customers whose behaviour the offer changes. We'd need a randomised pilot to measure it; the churn model can still help as a feature.

    “Why not train on last year's collection calls?”

    Those calls were not random. Agents chose whom to call, so the data mixes the effect of the call with the agent's choice. The model would learn the choice. A randomised holdout fixes this; if we can't randomise, we need a defensible causal design and we should say the estimates are weaker.

    “What does the regulator actually expect on climate?”

    For the UAE, the central bank has a climate risk regulation, and I'd read the rulebook text for the exact requirements. As I understand it, banks must consider climate risk in capital and solvency assessments and document gaps. For Saudi Arabia I haven't confirmed a specific SAMA rule, so I'd check before saying. Either way, scenario analysis with stated limits is the practical starting point.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A memo tool is right on 99.5% of claims. A memo has 40 claims. What share of memos have no error?

    0.99540 ≈ 0.818, so about 82%. About one in five has at least one error, which is why citations and review remain.

    2. You check 200 claims and none are wrong. What can you say about the error rate?

    With 95% confidence it is below about 3 ÷ 200 = 1.5%. You cannot claim it is zero, and you cannot claim it is below 0.5%; that needs about 600 clean claims.

    3. Debt AED 420m, EBITDA AED 150m, limit 3.5x. By how much can EBITDA fall before a breach? What if lease liabilities of AED 60m are added to debt?

    Breach EBITDA = 420 ÷ 3.5 = 120, a 20% fall. With leases: 480 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 137.1, a fall of only 8.6%. Same business, different definition, less than half the cushion.

    4. Why is a propensity-to-pay model a poor way to choose whom to call?

    It ranks customers by chance of paying after a call. That puts "sure things" at the top, who would pay anyway, and ignores the sleeping dogs. The target is the difference in payment chance with and without a call.

    5. In the uplift groups, 2,000 persuadables (+0.45 uplift) and 1,000 sleeping dogs (−0.15). What is the net effect of calling only these two groups?

    2,000 × 0.45 = 900, minus 1,000 × 0.15 = 150, so 750 extra payers. Calling only the persuadables gives 900. Contacting the dogs costs both the call and 150 payments.

    6. How do you evaluate an uplift model when you can never see one customer both called and not called?

    Use a randomised holdout. Rank customers by predicted uplift, then compare outcomes in treated against control within each rank band (the Qini curve). Give bootstrap intervals, because the effects are small.

    7. Why are climate scenarios not forecasts, and what do they do for a bank?

    No-one knows the carbon price path or hazard timing, and the models are extrapolated beyond any history. Scenarios stress the book to expose concentrations and test strategy and capital. They need stated assumptions and sensitivity ranges, not a single number.
