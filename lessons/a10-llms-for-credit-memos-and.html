    Credit Memos, Uplift and Climate Risk: Three Frontier Tools for the Credit Desk

    How to let a language model draft a credit memo and watch covenants without letting it invent a number, how to spend collections and retention effort only where it changes the outcome, and what supervisors now expect when they ask about climate.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceEach of these three tools earns its place only when you can measure it against the right question: is every claim in the memo traceable to a source, did the action change the customer's behaviour (not just predict it), and does the scenario move the loss numbers the bank already reports.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The memo. A relationship manager spends two days turning financial statements, a site note and a term sheet into a credit memo. A model drafts it in a minute. The risk is that it reads well, and one of its forty claims is a number that appears in no document.

    The phone call. A collections team has 10,000 customers a month late. A call costs AED 30. The usual approach calls those most likely to pay, but many would have paid anyway, and a few will be irritated into a complaint. Like a flu jab, a call only adds value where it changes the outcome.

    The weather. The central bank asks how the book fares under a carbon price or a coastal flood. No history of such a world exists, so the answer is scenarios laid over models the bank already has.

    The common thread: the quantity you can easily compute is not the one you care about. Fluent text is not accurate text. Likelihood to pay is not the effect of a call. Last year's default rate is not next decade's.

    Hold onto thisAsk three questions of any of these tools. What is the unit of correctness (a claim, a customer, a scenario)? What baseline must it beat (an analyst, a random call list, today's stress test)? Who can reproduce the number? Without all three answers, it is a demo, not a control.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    LLMs for credit memos and covenant monitoring

    A credit memo is a structured argument: who the borrower is, what they want, how they have performed, what could go wrong, what protects the bank, and a recommendation. Most of it is summarising facts already in documents (accounts, bank statements, the facility agreement), which a language model does well. Split the job like a kitchen, with each station doing what it is good at:

    • Extraction turns PDFs and scans into fields (revenue, debt, maturities). See document AI for trade finance and parsing messy PDFs. Errors here spread everywhere, so show each value beside its source page.
    • Calculation is ordinary code. A model doing arithmetic in its head will sometimes be wrong, confidently. A validator can read a function.
    • Drafting is where the model writes, only from the facts and passages it is handed, with a citation for every factual claim.
    • Review stays with a human who signs. The model makes review quicker and better aimed (guardrails and approval gates).

    Validating a drafted memo

    Validate per claim, not per memo. Split the draft into atomic statements and ask of each: supported by a cited passage, contradicted by the source, or simply not in it? The third, a plausible sentence with no support, is the classic hallucination and the hardest to catch because it sounds right (automation in groundedness checking). Try the manual version first.

    Errors compound. Like a chain with 30 links, one weak link breaks it. If each claim is right with probability 1 − e and a memo has k independent claims, the chance the memo is clean is (1 − e)k. At 1% error and 30 claims that is 0.9930 ≈ 74%. A "99% accurate" model ships a flawed memo about one time in four. Hence a citation for every claim, and human review of the cited passages, not the prose.

    Proving a low error rate takes many claims. Check n claims and find none wrong: the 95% upper bound on the true error rate is about 3 ÷ n (the "rule of three"). Saying "below 1%" needs about 300 clean claims; below 0.1%, about 3,000. A wrong covenant threshold matters more than a wrong adjective, so stratify the test set by claim type (statistics for evals).

    Covenant monitoring

    A financial covenant is a test in the loan agreement, such as "total debt ÷ EBITDA must not exceed 3.5x, tested quarterly". A breach lets the lender act. Monitoring means: extract each covenant with its exact definition, collect quarterly figures, compute the test, escalate when headroom is thin or the definition is disputed. The model reads; a deterministic calculator computes.

    The numbers are not the hard part. Definitions are. "Debt" may or may not include leases. "EBITDA" is often negotiated, with add-backs up to a cap. Later amendments and waivers change earlier terms. Two readings of one clause can flip a pass into a breach.

    For the build: extract the covenant definition as well as the threshold, keep the clause text attached to each term, make the calculator show which definition it used, and flag low-confidence clauses and any touched by a later amendment. Evidence on how well models read bespoke legal wording is thin, so measure on your own agreements with a lawyer-labelled test set.

    Uplift for collections and retention

    A normal payment or churn model predicts who will pay (or stay). A campaign needs who will pay (or stay) because we acted. Think of an umbrella seller: the people carrying umbrellas are the ones who would have stayed dry anyway. Formally, uplift (the conditional average treatment effect) is

    τ(x)=P(pay|x, called)−P(pay|x, not called)

    The extra chance of paying if a customer with features x is called. +0.45 means 45 more payers per 100 calls; 0 means the call changes nothing; negative means it backfires. It is never observed for one person, so it is estimated from groups and needs a comparison group (causal inference).

    Customers fall into four types:

    • Persuadables pay only if called. Large positive uplift. The target.
    • Sure things pay either way. The AED 30 call is wasted.
    • Lost causes pay neither way. Also wasted.
    • Sleeping dogs are less likely to pay if contacted: a call prompts a dispute, complaint or exit. Negative uplift.

    A propensity model puts sure things at the top, because they are most likely to pay after a call. That looks like success and is not. Retention works the same way: do not discount for a customer who was staying anyway (churn and retention).

    Money check at 30% calls (3,000 customers). A perfect ordering calls the 2,000 persuadables (+0.45 each = 900 extra payers) then 1,000 sure things (+0.02 each = 20). That is 920 extra payers, worth 920 × 6,000 = AED 5.52m, for AED 90,000 of calls. The likelihood list calls the 3,000 sure things first: 3,000 × 0.02 = 60 extra payers, AED 360,000. Same cost, about fifteen times less benefit.

    How uplift is estimated

    • Randomise. Call a random 50% and hold back the rest. Past calls chosen by agents or rules give confounded data (confounding and selection).
    • T-learner: one model on the called group, one on the uncalled, subtract. Simple, but the two models' noise adds up.
    • S-learner: one model with treatment as a feature. Trees may ignore a weak feature and understate the effect.
    • Uplift trees and causal forests split to separate customers whose effect differs. The standard for tabular data.
    • Evaluation: the Qini curve or AUUC. Sort by predicted uplift, plot cumulative extra payers in treated against control. It is the chart above, estimated from a randomised holdout. Effects are small and noisy, so give intervals (bootstrap); benchmarks often find a plain outcome model competitive.

    Collections also carries conduct rules on contact frequency, hardship and vulnerable customers. "Do not call this person" is usually safe. "Call this person more" for someone in difficulty needs policy sign-off. Check your regulator's and bank's exact rules.

    Climate and ESG risk modelling

    Climate risk reaches a bank through two channels.

    • Physical risk: heat, flooding and sea-level rise damage collateral and interrupt income. Coastal real estate and logistics matter in the Gulf.
    • Transition risk: carbon prices, regulation and shifting demand squeeze emissions-heavy sectors. For hydrocarbon-linked economies, the pace of the energy transition bears directly on the loan book.

    No history of a 2040 climate exists, so the method is scenarios laid over existing credit models, like a fire drill for a fire you have never had. Define a scenario (a carbon price path, a flood). Translate it into shocks to revenue, costs or collateral by sector and location. Push those through the PD and LGD models (PD, LGD, EAD) and compare expected loss with base. Where it feeds provisioning, it touches IFRS 9 forward-looking information and stress testing.

    The hard part is data: borrower emissions (often estimated from sector averages, with large error), collateral location and elevation, and sector sensitivity. ESG ratings from different vendors disagree widely. Disclose that.

    A scenario is a disciplined "what if", not a forecast. Its use is to find concentrations and test whether strategy survives. Climate effects build over decades while loan books reprice in years, and supervisors ask banks to explain that mismatch.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The plausible unsupported sentence

      "Management has a strong record of refinancing on time." Nothing in the file says so.

      Spot itRequire a citation per factual claim and check the passage supports it. Sample claims with missing or loose citations.
    • Arithmetic done by the model

      Ratios in the prose, rounded or using the wrong line (net debt for gross debt).

      Spot itRecompute every figure from structured data. Any difference is a defect.
    • Omission

      The draft is accurate but leaves out a related-party loan, pending litigation or a breach.

      Spot itSeed test memos with known risks and measure how often the draft surfaces them.
    • Automation bias in review

      Reviewers skim fluent drafts and sign. The edit rate falls and everyone calls it quality.

      Spot itPlant known errors in a sample of drafts without telling reviewers. Track the catch rate.
    • Covenant definition misread or superseded

      The model reads the original agreement and misses a waiver letter that changed the test.

      Spot itLink every extracted covenant to its clause and amendment history. Send documents with later amendments to a lawyer.
    • Calling the propensity model an uplift model

      It ranks by chance of paying, and the "uplift" claim comes from top-decile outcomes.

      Spot itAsk what the control group was and whether it was randomised. No held-back group means no measured uplift.
    • Uplift from non-random history

      Past calls went to customers agents liked; the model learns who agents like.

      Spot itCheck how treatment was assigned. If by judgement or rule, treat estimates as biased until a randomised pilot confirms them.
    • Noise sold as signal

      The Qini curve looks great on one split; with 5,000 customers per arm the interval spans zero.

      Spot itAsk for bootstrap intervals and a plain outcome-model baseline.
    • Ignoring conduct limits

      The model recommends repeated contact for a customer showing hardship signals.

      Spot itPut policy limits (contact caps, vulnerable-customer exclusions) in the decision layer outside the model, and test them.
    • Climate numbers with false precision

      "Climate adds AED 67.2m of expected loss", built on estimated emissions.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity to the main assumptions and the share of borrowers with estimated rather than reported data.
    • Scenario presented as forecast

      One carbon-price path treated as "what will happen".

      Spot itLook for several scenarios, stated horizons, and a note on what is not modelled (second-round effects, adaptation).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A memo tool is right on 99.5% of claims. A memo has 40 claims. What share of memos have no error?

    0.99540 ≈ 82%. About one memo in five has an error, so citations and review stay.

    2. You check 200 claims and none are wrong. What can you say about the error rate?

    With 95% confidence it is below about 3 ÷ 200 = 1.5%. It is not proven zero, and "below 0.5%" needs about 600 clean claims.

    3. Debt AED 420m, EBITDA AED 150m, limit 3.5x. How far can EBITDA fall before a breach? What if AED 60m of leases join debt?

    Breach EBITDA = 420 ÷ 3.5 = 120, a 20% fall. With leases: 480 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 137.1, a fall of only 8.6%. Same business, different definition, under half the cushion.

    4. Why is a propensity-to-pay model a poor way to choose whom to call?

    It ranks by chance of paying after a call, so "sure things" sit at the top and sleeping dogs are ignored. The target is the difference in payment chance with and without a call.

    5. 2,000 persuadables (+0.45 uplift) and 1,000 sleeping dogs (−0.15). What is the net effect of calling only these two groups?

    900 − 150 = 750 extra payers. Calling only the persuadables gives 900. The dogs cost the call and 150 payments.

    6. How do you evaluate an uplift model when you never see one customer both called and not called?

    Use a randomised holdout. Rank by predicted uplift, then compare treated against control within each rank band (the Qini curve). Give bootstrap intervals, because effects are small.

    7. Why are climate scenarios not forecasts, and what do they do for a bank?

    No-one knows the carbon price path or hazard timing. Scenarios stress the book to expose concentrations and test strategy and capital. They need stated assumptions and sensitivity ranges, not one number.
