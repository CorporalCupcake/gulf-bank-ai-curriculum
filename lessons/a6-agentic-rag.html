    Agentic RAG, GraphRAG and Permission-Aware Retrieval

    What changes when the model decides what to search and when to stop, when the knowledge is a web of relationships instead of a pile of passages, and when every single retrieved chunk must be one the asker is allowed to see.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePlain RAG retrieves once and hopes; agentic RAG loops until it has enough evidence (better answers, higher cost, new ways to stop too early), GraphRAG adds relationships for questions about connections, and in a bank none of it ships unless the permission check happens inside retrieval, before the model ever sees a chunk.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks the bank's internal assistant: "Which of our corporate clients depend on suppliers owned by the group that just defaulted, and what did the credit committee say about them last year?"

    A basic RAG system (M3) embeds the question, pulls the five nearest chunks and asks the model to answer. It will probably find a news article about the default and a credit memo that mentions the group by name. It will not find the supplier two steps away, because no single passage mentions both the defaulted group and that supplier's client. The model then writes a fluent answer from what it was given. That is the failure: one search, one shot, no way to notice it came back with the wrong material.

    Three upgrades address this, and each one has a price.

    • Agentic RAG (A6.3). Let the model run the search like an analyst: search, read, decide what is still missing, search again with a better query, and stop when satisfied. It handles multi-step questions. It costs more and can stop for the wrong reasons.
    • GraphRAG (A6.4). Build a map of entities and relationships (who owns whom, who supplies whom, who guarantees whom) and retrieve by walking the map. Good for "how is A connected to B" and "what are the themes across this whole corpus". Expensive to build and only as good as the extraction.
    • Permission-aware retrieval (A6.5). Make sure the person asking, and the agent acting for them, only ever get chunks they are entitled to see. This is not an upgrade in quality. It is the entry ticket for a bank, and it is harder than it sounds once the corpus spans thousands of sources.
    Hold onto thisAll three are about the same hidden question: what is in the model's context window, and who decided that? Agentic RAG lets the model decide. GraphRAG lets a data structure decide. Permissions put a rule above both. The model should never be the one enforcing access.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Agentic RAG: the agent decides what to search and when to stop

    Strip away the branding and agentic RAG is the agent loop from M4.1 with retrieval as the main tool. The skeleton:

    evidence = []
for step in range(budget):
    plan = llm("What do I still need to know?", question, evidence)
    if plan.sufficient:            # the stopping decision
        break
    query = plan.next_query        # often rewritten, split or routed (M3.7)
    evidence += search(query, tool=plan.source)   # which index, which tool
answer = llm("Answer only from the evidence", question, evidence)

    Compared with the fixed pipeline, the model now makes three decisions that used to be hard-coded: what to ask (rewriting, decomposing a question into sub-questions, M3.7), where to ask (policy index, core-banking facts, a SQL tool, the web), and whether it is done. The research variants differ mostly in how they make the third decision:

    • Self-RAG trains the model to emit special tokens saying whether retrieval is needed and whether a passage is relevant and supports its answer.
    • Corrective RAG scores the retrieved set with a lightweight evaluator and, if it looks poor, falls back to another source such as web search.
    • Search-R1 and its relatives train the model with reinforcement learning (R3) to interleave reasoning and search, rewarded for the final answer being right.
    • Multi-agent research systems have a lead agent split a task across sub-agents that search in parallel, then merge findings. Anthropic described this design for its own Research feature.

    Why it helps is arithmetic. A two-step question needs a fact from document A to know what to look for in document B. One retrieval cannot do that. A loop can, and a retry after a bad query is a second chance. Below, a simulated two-fact question shows the trade, including the thing that quietly goes wrong: the agent's own judgement that it has "enough".

    Three engineering consequences follow from the loop.

    • Cost and latency are variable. Practitioner estimates put agentic RAG at several times the tokens of single-shot per question, and a loop that runs ten steps feels slow to a user. Set a hard budget, stream progress, and use the cheap path (single retrieval) for questions that a router classes as simple (M2.4).
    • The stopping judgement is the weak point. A model asked "do you have enough?" tends to say yes. That is the red bar. Counter it with a separate checker, with a requirement that every claim cites a retrieved passage, and with measurement of groundedness (A4.3).
    • It widens the attack surface. Every retrieved page is text the agent will read and may obey. More retrieval steps means more chances to ingest a poisoned document (A3), and an agent that picks its own tools needs the approval gates of M4.5.

    GraphRAG: retrieval over customer and company relationships

    Vector search finds passages that sound like the question. It is weak when the answer lives in the links between things: a group owns a company, the company supplies a client, the client is guaranteed by a director who also sits on another board. No passage needs to contain the whole chain. A knowledge graph stores the chain explicitly: nodes are entities (companies, people, accounts, devices) and edges are typed relationships (owns, supplies, guarantees, shares an address with).

    "GraphRAG" covers two related ideas, and it is worth keeping them apart in a meeting.

    1. Graph as the index, built from text. This is the Microsoft Research design (2024). An LLM reads every chunk and extracts entities and relationships; the graph is clustered into communities; each community gets a generated summary. Local questions ("tell me about this entity") walk the graph from the entity. Global questions ("what are the main themes across all these credit files?") use the community summaries, which plain RAG cannot answer because no top-k set summarises a whole corpus. Cost is paid up front: an LLM call over every chunk. Lighter variants such as LightRAG and HippoRAG cut that cost.
    2. A graph you already have. Banks hold ownership registers, KYC beneficial-owner structures, payment networks and guarantor links in structured form. Retrieving over those with a graph query (a traversal, not an LLM guess) and passing the result plus supporting documents to the model is cheaper, auditable, and often what actually ships. Fraud rings and shell-company networks (B2, B3) are the classic use.

    The core operation is a k-hop traversal: start at an entity, collect everything within k relationship steps. The choice of k is the whole trade. Too few hops and you miss the indirect exposure; too many and, because the number of neighbours multiplies at each hop, you return most of the bank. Extraction errors add a second problem: a missing edge silently disconnects part of the network.

    The lesson of the picture holds on real graphs: traversal helps with indirect relationships, but it is only as complete as the graph, and the number of results explodes with each hop. Production systems therefore weight edges (a 51% ownership link counts for more than a shared address), filter by relationship type, and rank what comes back before it reaches the model. GraphRAG is not a replacement for vector search. The common pattern is hybrid: vector search finds the entry points and the supporting text, the graph supplies the connections.

    Be honest about when not to build one. If your questions are "what does policy X say about Y", a good chunker and hybrid search (M3.5) beat a graph on cost and simplicity. A graph pays off for relationship questions, for corpus-wide summaries, and where you need to explain a link ("connected through these three filings").

    Permission-aware retrieval at enterprise scale

    In a bank, the sentence "the assistant can search everything" is a breach waiting for a date. A retail branch employee must not read the corporate M&A file. A compliance investigator can read an AML case that nobody on the sales floor may know exists. Material non-public information sits behind information barriers. Staff move teams and their access changes by Monday morning. The assistant must respect all of it, for every chunk, in every language.

    The central rule: enforce access in the retrieval layer, with the end user's identity, never in the prompt. "Do not reveal documents the user is not allowed to see" is an instruction to a model, and models can be talked out of instructions. Once a forbidden chunk is in the context window, you have already lost: it can be paraphrased, summarised or leaked through a clever question.

    The mechanics have a few parts.

    • Carry the identity through. The agent acts on behalf of a user, with that user's entitlements, not with a broad service account. Tool calls made by the agent (A2.1) must propagate the user's identity, or the agent becomes a way to read what the user cannot.
    • Store access control lists with the chunks. Each chunk carries the groups or roles allowed to read it, copied from the source system (SharePoint, the document management system, the case tool). Where the source models access as a relationship graph (Google's Zanzibar paper is the reference design), a policy service answers "can user U read object O?".
    • Filter inside the search, not after it. This is the engineering heart, and the next simulation shows why.
    • Keep entitlements fresh. The index is a copy. When someone leaves a project, the copy must change. A lag between the source's permissions and the index's is a window of unauthorised access. Many designs also re-check the live source permission for the final handful of results at query time.
    • Mind the side channels. A refusal ("I cannot show you the Project Falcon file") reveals that Project Falcon exists. Cached answers, shared embeddings caches and logs can carry content across users. Graph retrieval needs the same care: a path through a node you cannot see can still disclose that it exists.

    At the scale of thousands of sources and millions of chunks, the difference between post-filtering and pre-filtering becomes a practical question of whether the product works at all.

    Practical designs, from simplest to heaviest:

    ApproachHowStrengthWeakness

    Most banks end up layered: coarse isolation by business line or jurisdiction (data residency rules in the UAE and Saudi Arabia may force this anyway, so check your own policy and regulator guidance), a metadata pre-filter for fine control, and a live check on the final few chunks. Log what was retrieved for whom: that log is your audit trail and your incident-response tool.

    How it connects

    These three topics sit on top of the retrieval stack and the agent stack, and they tie directly to risk and governance.

    An agentic loop is only as good as the single search it makes. Weak chunks and weak ranking get repeated, not fixed, by looping.

    M4.1 · M4.3 · M4.5 · The agent loop, memory, guardrailsAgentic RAG is the loop with retrieval as the tool

    Budgets, stopping rules and approval gates from M4.5 apply directly, and memory raises its own permission questions.

    A6.1 – A6.2 · Document-image retrievalRetrieving from scans and layouts

    Trade finance and KYC files are often images. An agent can route between text and visual indexes.

    A3 · Indirect prompt injectionRetrieved text is untrusted input

    The more an agent reads, the more injected instructions it can meet. Permissions limit the damage if one succeeds.

    A4.3 · A4.5 · Groundedness and agent evaluationMeasuring the red bar

    Premature, confident answers are caught by groundedness checks and by simulated trajectories.

    M5 · Evaluating agentsJudge the path, not only the answer

    Number of steps, tool choice and wasted searches are part of quality for a looping retriever.

    B2 · B3 · AML and due diligenceNetworks are the AML problem

    Beneficial ownership and transaction networks are graphs already. GraphRAG makes them askable in plain language, with the sanctions side in B2.3.

    G1 · P1.5 · Threat models and lineageWho can see what, and where it came from

    Lineage and metadata are what let you carry an ACL from the source system to the chunk and prove it afterwards.

    B7.2 · Internal copilotsWhere all three meet

    Staff copilots are the first place a bank hits the permission problem at scale.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit analyst assistant. Multi-step questions over memos, financials and covenants: agentic. Group structures and guarantors: graph.
    • AML investigator support. "Show me every account linked to this device or address and summarise the prior alerts." Graph traversal, strict need-to-know permissions, a full audit log.
    • Policy and regulatory Q&A. Mostly plain hybrid RAG, in Arabic and English. An agent helps with questions spanning a CBUAE circular, an internal policy and a procedure note. Permissions are mild but versioning is not.
    • Relationship manager briefings. A customer view that combines internal notes, news and ownership. The permission model decides what each banker may see about a client they do not cover.
    • Islamic finance product Q&A. Fatwa and Sharia-board decisions cited per product: an agent that must retrieve the governing ruling, not a similar one.

    Where the field is now

    By late 2026 the vocabulary has settled around "retrieval is a tool an agent uses" more than "RAG is a pipeline". The open arguments are cost, evaluation and trust.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Permission enforced in the prompt

      "Only answer from documents the user may see", with the full index behind it. One clever question and the model reveals the contents.

      Spot itAsk where the access check runs. If the answer mentions the system prompt, it is not a control. Test with a user who has no access and look at what is retrieved, not only what is said.
    • Post-filtering that quietly empties results

      Top-10 fetched, 9 dropped, the answer is built on one weak chunk or "I couldn't find anything".

      Spot itLog how many chunks survive filtering per query, by user group. Low-access users with short results are the signal.
    • Stale entitlements

      A leaver, a transfer or a lifted information barrier does not reach the index for days.

      Spot itRevoke access to a test document and time how long until retrieval stops returning it. That lag is your exposure window.
    • The agent with a super-user

      The tool layer uses a service account with broad rights, so the agent reads what the human could not.

      Spot itCheck which identity each tool call runs under in the logs. It should be the user's, or a delegated, narrowed token.
    • Looping on a bad query

      The agent repeats near-identical searches, burns the budget and answers from noise.

      Spot itPlot searches per question and the fraction that hit the budget. Review trajectories with repeated queries.
    • Declaring "enough" too soon

      A confident, cited-looking answer built on one of the two needed facts.

      Spot itEvaluate on multi-hop questions with known evidence sets, and check whether the final answer's citations cover each needed fact.
    • A graph built from wrong extraction

      The LLM merges two companies with similar names or invents a relationship. The graph then states it as fact, and a false link between a client and a sanctioned entity is serious.

      Spot itSample edges and check them against source. Keep a source citation on every edge. Do not use LLM-extracted edges for decisions without review.
    • Hop explosion

      Three hops from a large corporate returns thousands of entities, most of them noise.

      Spot itCount entities returned per query. Cap by edge type, weight and rank before the model sees them.
    • Side-channel leaks

      Refusals, answer caches, shared logs or graph paths that reveal that a restricted item exists.

      Spot itRed-team with a low-access user asking about known restricted projects; check refusal wording, caches and logs.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The simple assistant searches once. For questions that need several steps, like tracing a client to a defaulted group, we let it search in a controlled loop with a hard limit, which costs more per question but finds the evidence. The part I'd not compromise on is access: the system checks who is asking before it retrieves anything, so a junior banker cannot get a document through the assistant that they could not open directly. We'd test that with accounts of different seniority before launch.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd treat retrieval as a tool with a budget: routed sources, query decomposition, a stop condition checked independently of the generator, and answers that must cite a passage for each claim. A graph only where the question is relational, preferably over structured ownership data we already trust, with LLM-extracted edges kept as cited, reviewable candidates. For permissions: user identity propagated to every tool call, ACLs stored with the chunks and applied as a pre-filter inside the search, a live check on the final results, and an alarm on sync lag. I'd evaluate on multi-hop questions with known evidence and on a red-team set of low-access users.”
    “Is agentic RAG just better RAG?”

    Better on multi-step questions, worse on cost, latency and predictability. For simple lookups a single good retrieval is faster and easier to audit. I'd route: simple questions take the cheap path, hard ones get a bounded loop. And I'd measure the loop's characteristic failure, stopping too early, rather than only average accuracy.

    “Do we need a knowledge graph?”

    Only if relationships are the question: ownership chains, guarantor networks, shared devices, or corpus-wide themes. For policy Q&A, good chunking and hybrid search are cheaper and as good. Where the graph is real data we already hold, such as beneficial ownership, query it directly. Building one from text with an LLM is costlier and needs edge-level quality control.

    “Can't we just tell the model not to show restricted documents?”

    No. Instructions are not access control. If the chunk is in the context, the model can be coaxed into using it. The filter has to run in retrieval, using the user's identity, so forbidden content never enters the context. The prompt can be a second layer, never the first.

    “Why not filter the results after search? It's simpler.”

    Because for restricted users most of the top results are forbidden and the survivors are few or none. A user who can read 1% of the corpus gets an empty top-10 about 90% of the time. Pre-filtering inside the search returns a full entitled set. We still add a live check on the final few results for freshness.

    “How do we know the agent isn't just making things up when it stops?”

    We don't, unless we test for it. I'd require citations per claim, run an independent groundedness check, and keep a benchmark of multi-hop questions where we know which documents are needed. Then report how often the agent answered without having found all of them, separately from overall accuracy.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can one retrieval not answer a two-hop question, and what does a loop add?

    The second query depends on a fact in the first result (for example the name of a supplier). A single query cannot name it. A loop reads the first result, forms a better query, and also gets a retry when a search misses.

    2. Each search finds its fact with probability 0.5 and the agent never stops early. With a budget of 2 searches, what is the chance of a complete, correct answer?

    Both searches must succeed: 0.5 × 0.5 = 25%. With a budget of 3 it is 0.25 + (two ways to have one miss among the first two, then success) 2 × 0.5³ = 0.25 + 0.25 = 50%.

    3. Why is "stops too early" a bigger worry than "uses too many steps"?

    Extra steps cost money and time, visibly. Stopping early produces a fluent, apparently well-grounded answer built on partial evidence, and no one notices unless they check. It is an accuracy and trust problem, not a cost problem.

    4. A group has 3 direct counterparties, each with 3 of their own, each of those with 2. How many entities are within 1, 2 and 3 hops, and what does it tell you about choosing k?

    3, then 3 + 9 = 12, then 12 + 18 = 30. Each hop multiplies the result. Beyond the hops that matter for the decision, you mostly add noise, so cap k, weight edges and rank before sending anything to the model.

    5. When is a vector index better than a graph, and when is a graph better?

    Vector search for "what does this passage or policy say" questions, where the answer sits in one place. Graph for indirect relationships, explaining a connection, and corpus-wide summaries. Hybrid is common: vector for entry points and evidence, graph for connections.

    6. A user may read 2% of chunks. You fetch the top 10 and then filter. What is the chance of an empty result, and the average number returned?

    Empty: 0.9810 ≈ 82%. Average survivors: 10 × 0.02 = 0.2 chunks. Pre-filtering inside the search would return 10.

    7. Name three reasons a prompt instruction is not an access control.

    The forbidden text is already in the context and can leak by paraphrase or inference; models can be manipulated into ignoring instructions (including by injected text in retrieved documents); and nothing in the prompt is auditable or testable as a deterministic control.

    8. Permissions are synced to the index nightly. What risk does that leave, and what would you add?

    Up to a day of access for people who lost it, and a day before new staff see what they should. Add an event-driven update for revocations, a live permission check on the final results, and monitoring of sync lag.
