    Agentic RAG and GraphRAG

    Part 1

    What they are

    In one sentenceAgentic RAG lets the model search again and again until it has enough to answer. GraphRAG searches a map of how things are linked, instead of only a pile of passages.

    Basic RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) does one search, takes the top few passages and answers from them. That works for "what does our policy say about X?". It fails when the answer needs a fact from one document to know what to look for in another.

    Running example: a relationship manager asks, "Which of our clients buy from a supplier owned by the group that just defaulted?" No single passage names both the group and the client. One search cannot find the chain, but a loop or a map can.

    How they differ from basic RAG

    • Basic RAG: one search, fixed. Cheap and predictable.
    • Agentic RAG: several searches, and the model chooses the next query, which source to use and when to stop. It is the agent loop from the next lesson, with search as the main tool.
    • GraphRAG: the search itself changes. You store entities (companies, people, accounts) and the links between them, and walk along the links.
    Part 2

    How they work

    Agentic RAG, step by step

    1. Break the question into what you still need to know: "who does the defaulted group own?"
    2. Search for that, and read what comes back.
    3. Ask: "do I have enough?" If not, write a new query using what you just learned: "who do those companies supply?"
    4. Stop when satisfied, or when a step budget (say 6 searches) runs out.
    5. Answer only from the evidence collected, citing each passage.

    The model may also pick where to search: a policy index, a database, or the web. Research variants (Self-RAG, Corrective RAG) mostly differ in how the model judges whether results are good enough.

    GraphRAG, step by step

    1. Build the graph: nodes for companies and people, labelled links such as "owns", "supplies", "guarantees".
    2. Find the starting node, often by ordinary search ("the defaulted group").
    3. Collect everything within k hops: k link-steps away, like friends of friends.
    4. Pass those facts, plus the relevant passages, to the model to write the answer.

    There are two ways to get the graph. An LLM can extract it from text (the Microsoft Research 2024 approach), which suits corpus-wide questions such as "what themes run through these credit files?" but costs one model call per chunk up front. Or you use a graph you already have, such as ownership registers or payment networks. That is cheaper, easier to audit, and often what ships.

    Part 3

    How to think about the settings

    • Step budget too low: the agent runs out before finding the second fact. It should say "I could not find this", not guess.
    • Step budget too high: cost and delay climb, and each extra step is another chance to read a bad or poisoned document.
    • Hops too few: with 1 hop you find Company A but never Client B. Indirect exposure is missed.
    • Hops too many: each hop multiplies the count, so 4 or 5 hops can return most of the bank. Two or three is usual.
    • Missing links: a link the graph does not hold silently cuts off everything behind it. A graph is only as complete as its data.

    The weak point of agentic RAG is the stopping decision. Asked "do you have enough?", a model tends to say yes. Use a separate checker, and require every claim in the answer to cite a passage.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ApproachBest forCost
    Basic RAG"What does policy X say about Y?"One search. Cheapest.
    Agentic RAGQuestions where one fact leads to the nextSeveral searches, variable delay
    GraphRAGOwnership, supply and guarantee chains; themes across many documentsBuilding and maintaining the graph
    Both togetherSearch finds the entry point, the graph supplies the links, the agent loopsHighest, and hardest to test
    • Agentic RAG is strong at multi-step questions, and weak at predictable cost and stopping at the right time.
    • GraphRAG is strong at linked questions and at explaining why (the path is the explanation), and weak where links are missing or the graph is stale.

    Start with basic RAG, with good chunking and hybrid search. Add a loop or a graph only for questions that measurably fail without it.

    In banking, permission checks must run inside the search, using the asker's identity, because a model told "do not reveal restricted files" can be talked out of it. A loop makes this matter more, since there are more searches to check.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Letting the model judge its own sufficiency

      It will often say "enough" after a partial result, and then answer confidently from half the evidence.

    • No step budget

      A loop with no cap can run up cost or repeat the same search forever. Always cap it and hand off to a human.

    • Building a graph for simple questions

      If the answer sits in one passage, a graph adds cost and nothing else.

    • Trusting a graph with gaps

      A missing link makes an exposed client look safe. Check how complete the graph is before relying on it.

    1. Why can one search not answer "which clients buy from the defaulted group's suppliers?"

    No single passage names both ends, and the second search depends on what the first one finds.

    2. What three things does an agentic RAG model decide?

    What to search for, where to search, and when to stop.

    3. What is a 2-hop traversal?

    Collect everything reachable in at most two link-steps from the starting node.

    4. Why not use 5 hops to be safe?

    The count multiplies with each hop, so you flood the model with distant, irrelevant entities.

    5. Which is cheaper to run: an LLM-built graph or an existing ownership register?

    The existing register. The LLM-built graph costs a model call per chunk up front.
