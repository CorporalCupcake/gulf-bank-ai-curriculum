    Agentic RAG, GraphRAG and Permission-Aware Retrieval

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks: "Which corporate clients depend on suppliers owned by the group that just defaulted, and what did the credit committee say about them last year?"

    Basic RAG (M3) pulls the five nearest chunks and answers. It misses the supplier two steps away, because no single passage names both. The answer still reads fluently: one search, no way to notice the wrong material came back.

    Three upgrades, each with a price:

    • Agentic RAG (A6.3). The model searches like an analyst, looping until satisfied. Costs more and can stop too early.
    • GraphRAG (A6.4). A map of who owns, supplies and guarantees whom, which you walk. Expensive to build.
    • Permission-aware retrieval (A6.5). The asker only gets chunks they may see. The entry ticket for a bank.
    Hold onto thisAll three answer one question: what is in the model's context window, and who decided that? The model should never be the one enforcing access.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Agentic RAG: the agent decides what to search and when to stop

    Think of an analyst, not a vending machine: search, read, spot the gap, search again, stop when satisfied. It is the agent loop from M4.1 with retrieval as the main tool.

    evidence = []
for step in range(budget):
    plan = llm("What do I still need to know?", question, evidence)
    if plan.sufficient:            # the stopping decision
        break
    evidence += search(plan.next_query, tool=plan.source)
answer = llm("Answer only from the evidence", question, evidence)

    The model now decides three things: what to ask (rewriting, splitting into sub-questions, M3.7), where to ask (policy index, core-banking facts, SQL, web) and whether it is done. Research variants mostly differ on the third: Self-RAG has the model judge its own passages, Corrective RAG falls back to another source when results look poor, and multi-agent systems split the work across parallel sub-agents.

    Why it helps: a two-step question needs a fact from document A to know what to look for in document B. One retrieval cannot do that; a loop can.

    Three practical consequences.

    • Cost and latency vary. Set a hard budget and route simple questions to single retrieval (M2.4).
    • The stopping judgement is the weak point. A model asked "do you have enough?" tends to say yes. Use a separate checker and require every claim to cite a passage (A4.3).
    • The attack surface widens. More steps mean more chances to ingest a poisoned document (A3); see the gates in M4.5.

    GraphRAG: retrieval over customer and company relationships

    Vector search finds passages that sound like the question. It fails when the answer lives in the links: a group owns a company, the company supplies a client, the client's guarantor sits on another board. Think of a subway map: the lines between stations matter more than the stations. A knowledge graph stores nodes (companies, people, accounts) and typed edges (owns, supplies, guarantees).

    "GraphRAG" means two different things.

    1. Graph built from text (Microsoft Research, 2024). An LLM extracts entities and relationships from every chunk. Good for corpus-wide questions ("main themes across these credit files?"). Costly: an LLM call per chunk, up front.
    2. A graph you already have. Ownership registers, KYC beneficial-owner structures and payment networks are already structured. A traversal over them (not an LLM guess) is cheaper, auditable, and often what ships. Fraud rings and shell companies (B2, B3) are the classic use.

    The core operation is a k-hop traversal: collect everything within k relationship steps. Like friends of friends, the count multiplies each hop: too few hops miss indirect exposure, too many return most of the bank.

    Traversal is only as complete as the graph, and a missing edge silently disconnects part of it. Production systems weight edges (a 51% ownership link beats a shared address) and rank before the model sees anything. The common pattern is hybrid: vectors find entry points, the graph supplies connections.

    Skip the graph for "what does policy X say about Y": good chunking and hybrid search (M3.5) are cheaper.

    Permission-aware retrieval at enterprise scale

    Think of a library with locked rooms. A guard who lets everyone in and asks them not to read the restricted shelves is useless; the door must stay locked.

    The rule: enforce access in the retrieval layer, with the end user's identity, never in the prompt. "Do not reveal documents the user may not see" is an instruction, and models can be talked out of instructions. Once a forbidden chunk is in the context window, it can be paraphrased or leaked.

    • Carry the identity through. The agent acts on behalf of a user, not through a broad service account (A2.1).
    • Store access lists with the chunks, and filter inside the search, not after it. The next simulation shows why.
    • Keep entitlements fresh. The index is a copy; sync lag is a window of unauthorised access.
    • Mind the side channels. A refusal ("I cannot show you the Project Falcon file") reveals that Falcon exists. Caches and logs can leak across users.

    Four designs, simplest to heaviest:

    ApproachHowStrengthWeakness
    Metadata filter in the vector storeChunks store allowed groups; the query passes the user's groups.Simple, fast, widely supported.Huge filters for users in many groups; permissions drift.
    Separate index per audienceOne index per business line.Hard isolation.Duplicates data.
    Query-time check against the sourceRetrieve candidates, then ask the source if the user can read each.Always current. Good last gate.Latency. Alone, it is post-filtering.
    Relationship-based access (Zanzibar-style)Permissions as a graph of users, groups and objects.Handles sharing at scale.Heavy infrastructure.

    Most banks layer these: coarse isolation by business line or jurisdiction (check your own residency policy), a metadata pre-filter, and a live check on the final few chunks. Log what was retrieved for whom: that is your audit trail.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Permission enforced in the prompt

      "Only answer from documents the user may see", with the full index behind it. One clever question and the model reveals the contents.

      Spot itAsk where the access check runs. If it is the system prompt, it is not a control. Test with a no-access user and inspect what is retrieved.
    • Post-filtering that quietly empties results

      Top-10 fetched, 9 dropped, so the answer rests on one weak chunk or "I couldn't find anything".

      Spot itLog chunks surviving the filter per query, by user group. Short results for low-access users are the signal.
    • The agent with a super-user

      The tool layer uses a broad service account, so the agent reads what the human could not.

      Spot itCheck which identity each tool call runs under. It should be the user's.
    • Declaring "enough" too soon

      A confident answer built on one of the two needed facts.

      Spot itTest on multi-hop questions with known evidence; check the citations cover each needed fact.
    • A graph built from wrong extraction

      The LLM merges two similarly named companies or invents a relationship, and the graph states it as fact. A false link to a sanctioned entity is serious.

      Spot itSample edges against source and keep a citation on every edge. Review before using LLM-extracted edges for decisions.
    • Side-channel and stale-permission leaks

      Refusals, caches or graph paths reveal that a restricted item exists; a leaver keeps access until the index catches up.

      Spot itRed-team with a low-access user; revoke access to a test document and time how long it stays visible.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can one retrieval not answer a two-hop question, and what does a loop add?

    The second query needs a fact from the first result, which one query cannot name. A loop reads, re-queries, and gets a retry.

    2. Giving an agent a bigger step budget helps, but only up to a point. Why does it not fix everything?

    More searches fix missed facts, not a wrong "I have enough" decision.

    3. Why is "stops too early" a bigger worry than "uses too many steps"?

    Extra steps cost visible money. Stopping early gives a fluent answer on partial evidence that nobody notices: a trust problem.

    4. Why does adding hops to a graph traversal stop helping, and what do you do about it?

    Each hop multiplies the entities returned, so deeper hops add mostly noise. Cap hops, weight edges and rank.

    5. When is a vector index better than a graph, and when is a graph better?

    Vector for single-place answers; graph for indirect links and corpus-wide summaries. Hybrid is common.

    6. A user may read only 2% of the corpus. Why does fetching the top 10 and then filtering usually leave them with nothing, and what is the fix?

    Almost all ten are forbidden and dropped. Filter inside the search so the top 10 are all readable.

    7. Name three reasons a prompt instruction is not an access control.

    The forbidden text is already in context and can leak by paraphrase; models can be talked out of instructions; a prompt is not an auditable, testable control.

    8. Permissions are synced to the index nightly. What risk does that leave, and what would you add?

    Up to a day of access for people who lost it. Add event-driven revocation and a live check on final results.
