    F3.6 · F3.7 Day one About 45 minutes

    Experiments, and Analyses That Survive Review

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    An experiment compares two groups that differ only by a coin flip. The cards team texts new cardholders to lift activation (a first purchase within 30 days) from about 12%. A coin flip per customer, unlike a month-on-month comparison, leaves the nudge as the only systematic difference.

    Hold onto thisUnit, primary metric, sample size, run length and checks are all decided before you see the outcome. Anything chosen afterwards looks like a free choice made to get the answer you wanted.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What an experiment is

    You randomly assign a treatment to units, then compare an outcome. Choose three things: the unit (customer, branch), the control (the current experience, not "nothing") and one primary metric. Randomisation makes the result unbiased, not precise, and not automatically true next quarter.

    Choosing the randomisation unit

    Count what you randomised, not what you measured. Ten pupils from ten schools tell you more than ten from one class, because classmates resemble each other. Sessions look like extra data but are not.

    This clustering inflates noise (the design effect): 100,000 customers across branches can carry the information of about 11,000. Analyse them as independent and false wins appear far more than 5% of the time. Analyse at the level you randomised.

    Sample size: MDE, power and variance reduction

    Power is the chance a test detects an effect that is really there. The minimum detectable effect (MDE) is the smallest true lift you have an 80% chance of seeing. Halving it costs four times the customers.

    CUPED cuts noise without bias by removing what customers' pre-test behaviour already predicts. Two warnings. Underpowered tests that reach significance overestimate the effect (the winner's curse). Checking daily until p dips below 0.05 inflates false positives, so look once or use a sequential method.

    Is the experiment trustworthy? SRM, novelty, guardrails

    Run these checks before reading any result. Sample ratio mismatch (SRM) means the arm counts differ from the plan by more than chance would allow (teams use a strict p < 0.001). Something other than your coin flip decided who landed where, such as a crash or logging bug, so the effect cannot be trusted.

    If SRM fires, don't adjust for it. Find the cause and rerun. Also plot the effect by week since first exposure: a steep fall is novelty. Set a tolerated harm for each guardrail (complaints, opt-outs, delinquency). By Twyman's law, a figure that looks too interesting is usually a bug.

    Holdouts for always-on models

    A model that scores everyone daily leaves no "before". Keep a standing holdout: a random 2 to 5% whose model action is withheld or defaulted. It gives a continuous, unbiased estimate of the model's value. Where you can't withhold, hold out only at low risk, with governance approval.

    Interference, networks and referrals

    Interference means customers affect each other. A treated customer refers a friend who sits in control, so the gap understates the truth. Randomise where spillover stays inside the unit (household, payroll, branch catchment) and accept the design effect.

    When you cannot randomise

    Sometimes you must not: credit decisions for protected groups, differential pricing, disclosed profit rates on regulated products. Use a quasi-experiment: difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity or synthetic control. Each rests on an assumption you must state. The honest label is "estimate under assumption X", never "proof".

    Writing an analysis that survives review

    Reviewers ask the same dozen questions of every analysis, so answer them first: the decision up front, the baseline in absolute terms ("up 9%" could be 12% to 13.1% or 0.2% to 0.22%), the effect with a 95% interval rather than a p-value alone, the checks passed, what was not tested and any deviations.

    Judge the decision against the interval, not the point estimate, and state the break-even lift.

    Pre-registering metrics

    Choose metric, segment or window after seeing results and you drift towards an interesting answer. With 12 metrics and no true effect, one "wins" about 46% of the time. Pre-registration means writing down, dated and before launch, the primary metric and MDE, guardrails, sample size, stopping rule and decision rule. Off-plan findings are hypotheses.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Wrong unit, or spillover ignored

      "Two million sessions, p < 0.0001" from 40,000 randomised customers.

      Spot itAsk what was randomised. That count is the sample size.
    • Ignoring SRM

      Arms are 51/49 against a plan of 50/50, and the report shows lift anyway.

      Spot itLook for arm counts and a mismatch test.
    • Forking paths

      The primary metric was flat, so the headline became a metric or segment that worked.

      Spot itCompare the headline with the plan. Treat off-plan results as hypotheses.
    • Weak tests read too strongly

      "No significant difference, so it doesn't work", with an interval from −1 to +3 points.

      Spot itAsk for the interval and MDE. A wide interval means inconclusive, not negative.
    • Novelty or harm missed

      A 10-day test shows a big lift, and nobody looked at complaints or opt-outs.

      Spot itAsk for the effect by week and a guardrail table with margins.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. You randomised 30 branches, 800 customers each. Why is your effective sample far smaller than the customer count?

    Customers in a branch resemble each other, so the honest sample is nearer the number of branches. Analyse at branch level and expect low power.

    2. Planned 50/50, observed 50,600 versus 49,400. Worry?

    Yes at 100,000: far too large to be chance, so something other than the coin is assigning customers. In 5,000 customers the same split is ordinary noise.

    3. You tracked 12 metrics with no plan and one shows p = 0.03. Why is that not a finding?

    With 12 metrics you would see one almost half the time by chance. Report it as exploratory, and rerun with it as the single pre-registered metric.

    4. A 10-day test of a new app dashboard shows +6% logins. What do you ask for before scaling?

    The effect by day since first exposure, the absolute rate with an interval, and a long-term holdback.

    5. The test shows +1.1 points, 95% interval (+0.07, +2.13). Why is "significant, so it works" a poor summary?

    The data fit anything from negligible to about two points. Say so, and base the rollout case on low cost and a later check.
