    A/B Tests and Experiments

    Part 1

    What an A/B test is

    In one sentenceAn A/B test splits people at random into two groups, gives each a different experience, and compares the results.

    Running example: an online shop emails a reminder to new sign-ups, hoping more of them make a first purchase within 30 days. Today about 12% do. Did the email work, or would those people have bought anyway?

    The coin flip is the key. Because chance decides who gets the email, the two groups are alike in everything else, so any gap in purchases can be put down to the email.

    How it differs from other comparisons

    • Before and after: compares this month with last month. Seasons, holidays and other changes get mixed in.
    • Comparing people who chose: people who open emails are keener buyers anyway. This is the confounding trap from the previous lesson.
    • A/B test: only the email differs, on average, between the groups.
    Part 2

    How to run one

    1. Pick the unit. What you randomise: customer, store, or session.
    2. Pick the control. The current experience, not "nothing".
    3. Pick one main metric. Here, first purchase within 30 days. Add guardrails such as unsubscribes.
    4. Work out the sample size. Before launch, using the smallest lift that matters to you.
    5. Randomise and run. For the planned time, without peeking and stopping early.
    6. Check, then read the result. Confirm the experiment was healthy first (Part 4).

    Decide all of this before you see any results. Choices made afterwards look like free choices made to get the answer you wanted.

    Part 3

    How many people, and how to read the result

    Small lifts are hard to see through noise, so they need big groups. To spot a lift from 12% to 13.5% reliably you need about 7,800 customers per group.

    • Power: the chance your test spots a lift that is really there. 80% is the usual aim.
    • Minimum detectable effect: the smallest lift you have that chance of seeing. Halving it costs four times the customers.
    Control 12.0%, treatment 13.5%, interval for the lift 0.6 to 2.4 points
    The interval excludes 0, so this is unlikely to be luck. The true lift is probably between 0.6 and 2.4 points.
    Lift 0.4 points, interval -0.5 to +1.3
    It includes 0, so you can't rule out no effect. That is "no clear answer", not "the email does nothing".
    A p-value of 0.03
    If the email did nothing, a gap this big would turn up by luck about 3 times in 100. Prefer the interval, which also shows the size.
    Show the sample-size rule of thumb

    Per group, customers needed is roughly 16 × p(1 − p) ÷ lift², with p as the baseline rate. With p = 0.12 and a lift of 0.015 that gives about 7,500, close to the 7,800 from a full calculation. Halve the lift and the need quadruples.

    Part 4

    Is the test trustworthy?

    • Sample ratio mismatch (SRM): you planned a 50/50 split but the counts are off by more than chance allows. Something other than the coin decided who landed where, such as a logging bug, so don't trust the result. Find the cause and rerun.
    • Novelty effect: a new email gets extra attention at first, which fades. A three-week test can oversell a lift. Run longer, and check the effect by week.
    • Peeking: checking daily and stopping when the result looks good raises false wins well above 5%. Look once at the planned end, or use a method built for repeated looks.
    • Wrong unit: randomise by store, then analyse by store. Customers in one store resemble each other, so 100,000 customers across a few stores carry far less information than they seem to.
    • Too good to be true: a result that looks amazing is more often a bug than a discovery.
    Part 5

    When to use it

    MethodShows cause?Use it when
    A/B testYesYou can randomise and wait
    Before and afterWeaklyNothing else changed, and you have no choice
    Adjusted comparisonPartlyYou cannot randomise, but measured the confounders
    • Strong at: answering "did this change cause that?" fairly, including for factors you never measured.
    • Weak at: speed, small effects, and results that may not hold next quarter.

    In banking, campaigns, collections messages and app changes can be tested this way, with guardrails such as complaints and fairness checks agreed before launch.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Stopping when it looks good

      Peeking and stopping early produces false winners. Fix the run length first.

    • Analysing at the wrong level

      Randomising stores but analysing customers makes the noise look far smaller than it is.

    • Skipping the health checks

      An uneven split can create a lift out of nothing.

    • Reading "not significant" as "no effect"

      An interval that includes 0 and is wide only means you don't know yet.

    1. Why does randomising make the comparison fair?

    Chance decides who gets what, so the groups are alike in everything except the change you test.

    2. You want to detect a lift half the size. What happens to the sample you need?

    You need about four times as many customers.

    3. A 50/50 test shows 51,500 versus 48,500 customers. What do you do?

    That is a sample ratio mismatch. Investigate the cause and rerun rather than reading the result.

    4. Why can a three-week test overstate a lift?

    Novelty gives an early boost that fades, so the short-run average is higher than the long-run effect.

    5. Why use the interval rather than just a p-value?

    The interval shows how big the effect might be, not just whether it is probably non-zero.
