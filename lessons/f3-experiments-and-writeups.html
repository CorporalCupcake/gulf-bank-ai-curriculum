    Experiments, and Analyses That Survive Review

    How to run a test a sceptical risk committee will believe, which traps quietly break it, what to do when you can't randomise, and how to write the result so a senior can act on it and a reviewer can't pull it apart.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRandomise the right unit, size the test before you start, check it ran as designed before you read the result, and then write down what you did, what you found with its uncertainty, and what you did not test.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The cards team wants to know whether a push notification and SMS nudge, sent three days after a new credit card is issued, raises activation: the share of new cardholders who make a first purchase within 30 days. Baseline activation is about 12%. A product manager would be delighted with a lift of 1.5 points.

    There are two ways to find out. One is to send the nudge to everyone and compare this month with last month. That tells you almost nothing: salary week, Ramadan, a new merchant offer and the nudge all moved at once. The other is to flip a coin for each new cardholder, nudge half, and compare the halves over the same weeks. Everything else that moves, moves for both groups. The only systematic difference is the nudge.

    That is the entire logic of an experiment, and F2.6 showed why it is so powerful: a coin flip cannot know who was going to activate anyway, so the selection-bias term is zero by construction. This lesson is about everything that goes wrong after you decide to flip the coin. You flip it on the wrong thing. You run it too short, or too long. Half your customers silently never arrive in one arm. The first week is exciting and the fifth is not. And then someone writes "activation up 9% (p<0.05), recommend rollout" and the credit risk committee asks four questions the author cannot answer.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Sample size is decided by the smallest effect you would act on, not by the data you happen to have. Halve the effect and you need four times the customers, because precision improves only with the square root of the sample.
    • A test measures the effect during the window you watched. If that window is dominated by novelty, you have measured curiosity. A nudge that works because it is new may not work in month three.
    • The plan is part of the result. A test with 40% power that "found nothing" has not shown there is no effect. It was never able to see one.
    Hold onto thisEverything that makes an experiment trustworthy is decided before you look at the outcome: the unit, the primary metric, the sample size, the run length, the checks. Anything chosen afterwards is a researcher degree of freedom, and a reviewer will treat it as one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What an experiment is, and the question it answers

    An experiment is a procedure that assigns a treatment to units by a random mechanism you control, then compares an outcome across the groups. Four choices define it, and you should be able to state all four in one sentence each:

    • Unit. What gets randomised: a customer, a session, a transaction, a branch, a call-centre agent.
    • Treatment and control. Control is not "nothing". It is the current experience, and defining it precisely is half the design.
    • Primary metric. One number that decides the question, with a direction and a minimum size worth acting on. Everything else is secondary or a guardrail.
    • Population and window. Who is eligible, and for how long they are followed. For a new-card nudge, "issued between 1 and 21 March, followed for 30 days from issue".

    The estimate it produces is the average treatment effect for that population: the difference in mean outcome between the groups. Randomisation guarantees it is unbiased. It does not guarantee it is precise, and it does not guarantee that the effect in this population carries over to next quarter or to another country. Precision is what the next sections buy. Generalisation is a judgement you have to state, not a statistic.

    Two properties of the setup are assumed so quietly that they are easy to break. First, no interference: one customer's treatment does not change another customer's outcome. Second, one version of each arm: "treatment" means the same thing for everyone. The unit section and the interference section below are about the first. Both are cases of the assumption statisticians call SUTVA, which is worth knowing by name for interviews and nothing else.

    Choosing the randomisation unit

    The unit decides two things at once: what you can say and how many independent observations you really have. Randomise customers and analyse customers, and each row is independent. Everything below is a way of losing that.

    • Customer is the default for anything the customer experiences repeatedly: messages, pricing display, app features. It also avoids the same person seeing both versions.
    • Session or page view sounds like more data, but it is not. One customer opens the app eight times; those eight sessions share the same person, so they resemble each other. Randomising sessions also means the same customer sees different versions on different days, which is a different treatment altogether ("sometimes the new screen").
    • Branch or agent is forced when the treatment cannot be delivered per customer: a new queue-ticketing flow, a relationship manager's script, a collections playbook. Customers within a branch share staff, footfall and local economy.
    • Transaction only for treatments with no memory, such as which fraud-rule threshold to apply to an individual card payment, and even then a customer can notice and call.

    The cost of clustering has a precise form. If each cluster has m observations and observations within a cluster have intra-cluster correlation ρ (the share of variance that is "which cluster", not "which observation"), the variance of a cluster-randomised mean is inflated by the design effect:

    DEFF=1+(m−1)ρ

    With m = 400 customers per branch and a modest ρ of 0.02, DEFF is about 9. Your 100,000 customers carry the information of roughly 11,000 independent ones. And if you wrongly analyse them as independent, your standard errors are too small by a factor of √9 = 3, so "significant" results appear far more often than 5% of the time under no effect. This is pseudo-replication, and it is among the most common errors in analyses written by people who have not been caught yet.

    The fix is the same in every case: analyse at the level you randomised. Aggregate to one number per cluster and compare clusters, or use cluster-robust standard errors (F2.5). Both give honest intervals. They also make plain what the design gave you: 30 branches per arm is a small experiment, whatever the customer count says.

    Because clusters are few, randomise with care. With 24 branches, plain coin flips can easily put the busiest into one arm. Use stratified or matched-pair randomisation: sort branches on pre-period outcomes (past activation, size, emirate or region), pair them up, and flip a coin within each pair. This is the same variance-reduction idea as the next section.

    Sample size: MDE, power and variance reduction

    You met power in F2.7 and the logic of tests in F2.3. This section is the practical use. Fix the false-positive rate α (usually 5%, two-sided) and the power 1 − β (usually 80%). For two arms of size n and a binary outcome, the sample needed to detect a true difference δ is:

    n=(zα/22p¯(1−p¯)+zβp1(1−p1)+p2(1−p2))2δ2

    p1 is the baseline rate, p2 = p1 + δ, and p̄ is their average. The z values are 1.96 for 5% two-sided and 0.84 for 80% power. In words: noise in the outcome, times a constant that rises with how sure you want to be, divided by the square of the effect. The squared δ in the denominator is the part to remember. Aiming for a lift half as big costs four times the customers.

    Turn it round and it becomes the number worth quoting: the minimum detectable effect (MDE), the smallest true lift your test has 80% chance of detecting. Sample size is then a negotiation between three quantities, and whoever asks "how long will it take?" is asking you for the third.

    • The MDE is a business number. Set it from what the lift is worth. If 1 point more activation is worth AED 2 million a year and the nudge costs AED 400,000, a lift below about 0.2 points is not worth detecting. Do not set the MDE from what is convenient to test.
    • Traffic sets the clock. Rare events are the problem. A lift in default rate, fraud loss or attrition may need months, and monthly customers are not interchangeable: run in whole weeks, and for anything tied to salary or card statements, across a full pay cycle.
    • Noise can be reduced. Cheaper than more customers is a less noisy metric. CUPED (controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data) subtracts the part of each customer's outcome predicted by their own pre-test behaviour. If last month's spend explains half the variance in this month's spend, the variance of the comparison halves, and so does the sample you need. Stratified assignment does the same job at the design stage. Neither adds bias, because the pre-test data cannot be affected by the treatment.

    Two warnings. Winner's curse: among underpowered tests, the ones that reach significance have, by selection, overestimated the effect. A lift of 4 points "found" by a test that could only detect 3 is more likely a lucky 1 than a true 4. Gelman and Carlin call these exaggeration (type M) and sign (type S) errors. Peeking: checking daily and stopping when p dips below 0.05 raises the true false-positive rate well above 5% (F2.4 covered why). Either fix the sample size in advance and look once, or use a sequential method built for repeated looks (see Part 4).

    Is the experiment trustworthy? SRM, novelty, guardrails

    An experiment can be correctly designed and still run badly. The checks below come before any reading of the result, and the most important is the first.

    Sample ratio mismatch (SRM). You planned a 50/50 split. You got 50,200 versus 49,100. Is that chance? The test is a chi-squared (or equivalently a z) test of the observed counts against the planned ratio. When it rejects, something other than your coin flip decided who ended up in which group: an app crash that only affects one arm, a redirect that drops users, bot filtering that applies after assignment, an eligibility rule that depends on the treatment, a logging bug. Whatever decided it is almost certainly related to the outcome, so the arms are no longer comparable and the effect estimate cannot be trusted at all. Fabijan and co-authors found it in a few percent of experiments at large firms (Part 4). Because the checks run on every test, with huge sample sizes, teams use a strict threshold, commonly p < 0.001, so that routine noise does not cry wolf.

    If SRM fires, do not "adjust for it" and do not report a result. Find the cause, fix it and rerun. The usual trick for localising it is to slice the counts: by platform, app version, day, language, emirate. The mismatch usually lives in one slice.

    Three more checks, in the order you would run them.

    • Balance on pre-treatment variables. Compare arms on things fixed before assignment: tenure, income band, prior activation, nationality group, channel. Randomisation guarantees balance on average, not in a given draw, and persistent imbalance is a second sign the assignment was broken. Do not use this to "fix" a good randomisation by adding controls after seeing a difference. Pre-specify any adjustment.
    • Novelty and primacy. Plot the effect by week since each customer was first exposed. A rapidly decaying curve means a novelty effect: customers respond to the new thing, then stop. A rising curve is a primacy or learning effect: customers resist change, then adapt. Both bias a short test. Cohorting by first exposure week, rather than by calendar week, keeps the two apart. A long-term holdback (keep 1–5% of customers on the old experience for months) is the reliable way to measure the settled effect (Hohnhold et al., 2015).
    • Guardrail metrics. A guardrail is something you must not harm while improving the primary metric: complaints, opt-outs of marketing, calls to the contact centre, fee income, delinquency, app crash rate, the share of messages delivered in the customer's preferred language (Arabic or English). Pre-specify the guardrails and the harm you will tolerate, as a non-inferiority margin, not as "no significant increase". A test that cannot see a small harm has not shown there is none. Banks add a second kind of guardrail: regulatory and conduct limits. A nudge that raises card usage by pushing customers into revolving credit is a win on the primary metric and a problem for treating customers fairly.

    Finally, Twyman's law, Kohavi's rule of thumb: any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong. A 40% lift in a bank's retail activation means a bug, not a breakthrough. Treat large surprising effects as a reason to look for a logging error before you celebrate.

    Holdouts for always-on models

    Much of a bank's AI is not a one-off test. It is a model that scores every customer, every day, and triggers an action: a credit-line offer, an attrition outreach, a fraud block, an AML alert. Once it is live, there is no "before". Everyone is treated, so comparing the new year to the old year is a before-and-after, with every confounder F2.6 listed.

    The answer is a standing holdout: a small random share of eligible customers, typically 2–5%, for whom the model's action is withheld or replaced by the default strategy. Because it is random and always running, it gives you a continuous, unbiased estimate of what the model is worth, and a defence at model-review time against "how do you know it works?". It also gives you unbiased labels: customers whose outcome was not shaped by the model (the F2.6 feedback-loop problem).

    Design choices that matter:

    • Hold out the action, keep the score. Log the model's score for the holdout so you can see what it would have done. Then you can analyse by score band: is the effect concentrated in the top decile, as the model claims?
    • Pick the holdout size from the MDE. A 2% holdout of 500,000 customers is 10,000, plenty for an activation lift, hopeless for a fraud-loss difference. Size it as in the planner.
    • Where you can't withhold the action. You will not leave a high-risk card payment unblocked to measure fraud savings. Options: hold out only at low risk, where the cost is small and the model's value is least certain; randomise the threshold a little, so some customers get a slightly stricter or looser cut-off; or review a random sample of unflagged transactions. These need governance approval, but they are standard in mature fraud teams.
    • Rotate or fix? A fixed holdout drifts: those customers age, and some become non-representative. A rotating holdout stays representative but cannot measure long-run effects. Many teams do both: a small permanent group and a larger rotating one.

    Interference, networks and referrals

    The no-interference assumption fails whenever customers affect each other, and banks have more of this than they expect.

    • Referrals. You test an AED 200 refer-a-friend bonus. A treated customer refers a friend who is in the control group, and the friend opens an account. The control arm now contains an effect of the treatment. The contrast between arms understates the true effect, and by an amount you can't see.
    • Peer-to-peer and remittance products. A new instant-transfer feature is only useful if the person you send to has it too. Randomised customers dilute each other.
    • Shared resources. A collections playbook that works better on the treated half still uses the same call-centre agents. If treated cases take longer, control cases wait longer. Likewise a pricing test in a marketplace where treated and control compete for the same inventory.
    • Households and companies. Spouses, family members and employees of one company (salary-transfer accounts) influence each other's decisions. Randomise individuals inside one household and you test the message on both.

    The remedy is to randomise at a level where spillover mostly stays inside the unit: the household, the company payroll, the referral cluster, the branch catchment. This is the same trade as in the unit section. You buy validity with a design effect. Beyond that, two methods are worth knowing by name: switchback experiments, which alternate the entire system between treatment and control in time blocks (useful for shared-resource settings like pricing or queue management), and two-sided or graph-cluster randomisation for networks, which Saveski et al. showed can be used to detect spillover itself. When spillover is the thing you want to measure (does a referral programme work through the friend?), design a two-level experiment: randomise referrers, then randomise the incentive offered to their friends.

    When you cannot randomise

    Sometimes the answer to "why not just test it?" is a firm no. Credit decisions for protected groups, differential pricing, anything that denies a service or changes disclosed profit rates on a regulated product (including Islamic finance contracts whose terms need Sharia board approval) may not be randomised at will. Randomising approval decisions raises fairness and consumer-protection questions that belong to compliance and risk, not to the data science team. Other reasons are practical: the change was already made, the rollout is nationwide, or the outcome is too slow to wait for.

    You then fall back on quasi-experiments, which find randomness that the world supplied. Each rests on an assumption you must state and defend. The honest label is "estimate under assumption X", never "proof".

    Writing an analysis that survives review

    A reviewer in a bank is not hostile. They are doing a job: model risk, internal audit, the credit committee, a regulator's thematic review. They have read many analyses that were wrong, and they will ask the same dozen questions of yours. A write-up that survives review answers them before they are asked. It also lets a busy senior act without reading it all.

    A strong write-up has a fixed skeleton. None of the parts is long.

    1. The decision and the answer, first. One paragraph a senior can act on. Include the recommendation, the size of the effect in business terms, the main uncertainty, and the condition under which it would change. Write it last, put it first.
    2. Population and period. Who was eligible, who was excluded and why, the dates, and how many ended up in each arm after each exclusion.
    3. Baseline. The control group's outcome, in absolute terms. A lift is meaningless without it: "up 9%" could be 12% to 13.1% or 0.2% to 0.22%.
    4. Effect size with an interval. The difference in absolute points, the relative change if you wish, and a 95% interval (F2.1). Then translate to dirhams with the assumptions written down. Never report a p-value alone: it says nothing about size, and a tiny effect in a huge sample is "significant".
    5. Checks that passed. SRM, pre-treatment balance, whether the effect holds by week, and the guardrails with their margins.
    6. What was not tested. The most neglected section and the one that earns trust. Other segments, other countries, longer horizons, other treatments, interference, and any pre-registered comparison you skipped. Say what you do not know.
    7. Deviations. Anything that differed from the plan, and when it was decided. Not hiding the messy parts is what separates analysis from advocacy.
    8. Reproducibility. Where the query and code live, the data snapshot date, the random seed or assignment log.

    Report uncertainty as a range with a plain reading: "The best estimate is +1.1 points; the data are consistent with anything from a negligible +0.1 to a substantial +2.1." Avoid "not significant, so no effect" (it confuses absence of evidence with evidence of absence), and avoid "significant" used as a synonym for "important".

    Also decide how the decision depends on the interval rather than on the point estimate. If the nudge costs AED 400,000 and a point of activation is worth AED 2 million, the central estimate pays back about five times over, but a lower bound near zero means payback is not guaranteed. A good write-up states the break-even lift so the reader can see where the interval sits against it.

    Pre-registering metrics

    Every analysis has many forks: which metric, which segment, which window, which exclusions, which adjustment. If you choose after seeing the results, you will tend, honestly and unconsciously, to choose the path that gives the interesting answer. That is the forking-paths problem from F2.4. With 12 independent outcome metrics and no true effect, the chance at least one "wins" at 5% is 1 − 0.9512 ≈ 46%.

    Pre-registration means writing down, before launch and with a date, exactly what you will do. In a bank, this fits in a page in the ticket or model-risk record. It states:

    • the hypothesis and the single primary metric with its direction and MDE;
    • the secondary metrics and the guardrails, each with its margin;
    • the population, randomisation unit and mechanism, and the planned split;
    • the sample size, run length and stopping rule (one look, or a sequential rule);
    • the analysis: estimator, adjustments (for example CUPED covariates), how multiple comparisons are handled, and the planned segment cuts;
    • the decision rule: what result leads to rollout, to a rerun, to stopping.

    This does not forbid exploration. It labels it. Anything not in the plan goes in a section called exploratory, reported with its interval and no claim, flagged as a hypothesis for the next test. Reviewers read that as strength. Nosek and colleagues make the same case for science generally. A bank has an extra reason: model validation teams and auditors already want a dated record of intent, and a test registered in advance is one of the few pieces of evidence that is hard to challenge later.

    How it connects

    Experiments are the end of the road for most of the earlier statistics, and the beginning of anything you will say to a senior. These are the connections.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and bootstrapEvery effect arrives with a range

    The interval in the write-up is a standard error you already know how to compute. For skewed money outcomes, bootstrap at the unit you randomised.

    F2.3 · F2.7 · Tests and powerThe machinery under the planner

    The sample-size formula is the power calculation turned inside out. Type S and M errors are what low power does to the estimates that survive.

    F2.4 · Forking pathsWhy pre-registration works

    Peeking, many metrics and segment-hunting are the same error as multiple comparisons. A plan fixes the number of comparisons before you see them.

    F2.6 · Confounding and selectionWhat randomisation buys

    It zeroes the selection-bias term. SRM is the check that it really did. A standing holdout fixes the feedback-loop labels described there.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsCluster-robust errors, CUPED

    CUPED is regression adjustment on a pre-treatment covariate. Cluster-robust standard errors are how you analyse a branch-level test honestly.

    F7 · Precision, recall, calibrationOffline metrics are not outcomes

    A model with better recall on a test set has not shown that anything improves. An experiment measures the change in the business outcome, such as losses avoided, after the action is taken.

    F3.1 · LeakagePost-treatment data

    An analysis that conditions on a post-assignment variable is leaking the treatment into its own control, the same root cause as target leakage.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceQuasi-experiments in full

    Difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, synthetic control and instruments, with the assumptions and diagnostics for each.

    A10.4, B6.2 · UpliftTreatment-effect models need experiments

    Uplift models are trained on randomised data. A small, permanent random split is what makes them possible.

    R8.4 · Off-policy evaluationLearning from logs

    Evaluating a new policy from old decisions requires known randomness in those decisions. Holdouts and randomised thresholds provide it.

    G2.1, G2.2 · Fairness and governanceWho is allowed to be randomised?

    Experiments on credit, pricing and eligibility are governance decisions. Record who was included, and test effects by group before rollout.

    Model risk and auditThe write-up is the record

    Validators ask for exactly the skeleton in this lesson. A dated pre-registration and a "not tested" section shorten review rather than extend it.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Randomised experiments are old. Fisher worked out the logic in agriculture in the 1920s. What is recent is running thousands of them a day, which forced the field to solve the practical problems this lesson covers: variance reduction, valid repeated looks, automated quality checks, and interference.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes in experiment write-ups, including ones written by AI. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Analysing sessions or transactions as if independent

      "Two million sessions, p < 0.0001" from a test that randomised 40,000 customers.

      Spot itAsk what was randomised and how many of those there were. The number of independent units is the sample size.
    • Ignoring SRM

      The arms are 51/49 against a plan of 50/50, and the report shows lift anyway.

      Spot itLook for the arm counts and a mismatch test in the report. If neither is there, assume nobody looked.
    • Stopping when it turns significant

      "We checked every morning and it crossed 5% on day nine."

      Spot itAsk how many times the result was inspected and what the stopping rule was. No rule means an inflated false-positive rate.
    • An underpowered test read as "no effect"

      "No significant difference, so the nudge doesn't work." The interval runs from −1 to +3 points.

      Spot itAsk for the interval and the MDE. If the interval contains effects the business would care about, the test was inconclusive, not negative.
    • Novelty read as a lasting effect

      A 10-day test of a new app home screen shows a big lift in engagement.

      Spot itAsk for the effect by week since first exposure. A steep fall means a novelty effect. Ask for a holdback.
    • Metric shopping

      The primary metric was activation. It was flat, so the report headlines "card spend per active customer", which was up.

      Spot itCompare the headline metric against the plan. If there is no plan, ask how many metrics were examined.
    • Segment fishing

      "The nudge works strongly for Arabic-language customers in Sharjah aged 30–35."

      Spot itAsk whether the segment was pre-specified and how many cuts were tried. Treat it as a hypothesis for the next test, never as a finding.
    • Missing or unjustified guardrails

      Activation rose, and nobody looked at complaints or opt-out rates until the regulator asked.

      Spot itLook for a guardrail table with a margin for each. "No significant harm" with no margin is not a guardrail.
    • Contamination

      Referral friends, shared agents or households in both arms; the effect is diluted without anyone noticing.

      Spot itAsk how the treatment could reach control customers, and whether the unit contains the spillover.
    • Winner's curse on rollout

      The pilot said +3 points, and the national rollout delivers +1.

      Spot itAsk about the power of the pilot. Shrink effects from small, significant tests, and quote the interval rather than the point.
    • Relative lift without a baseline

      "Up 40%", from 0.5% to 0.7%.

      Spot itAsk for the absolute rates and counts. The effect in dirhams comes from the absolute difference.
    • AI-drafted analysis

      Fluent, with a confident causal verb, a p-value and no sample sizes. LLMs are fast at producing the shape of a report and unreliable on numbers they did not compute.

      Spot itCheck every figure against the notebook output. Ask where the interval comes from, and what it did not test. A draft with no limitations section is unfinished.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The card-activation nudge works, but probably by less than the pilot headline. We randomised 16,000 new cardholders; activation went from 12.0% to 13.1%, and the range that fits the data is from almost nothing to about two points. The nudge is cheap to send, so the downside is small and I recommend rolling out, with a permanent 3% holdout so we can confirm the effect holds after the novelty fades. We haven't tested corporate cardholders or customers outside the UAE.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Customer-level randomisation, one pre-registered primary metric with an MDE set from the value of a point of activation, sample size from the power formula with CUPED to cut the requirement, and a fixed look at the end. Before reading any outcome, SRM at p < 0.001, pre-treatment balance, and the effect by week since exposure. Guardrails with non-inferiority margins for complaints and opt-outs. For branch changes I'd pair branches and analyse at branch level, since the design effect makes the effective sample far smaller than the customer count. And where I can't randomise, I'd name the quasi-experimental assumption and say how I'd probe it.”
    “Can't we just roll it out and compare to last month?”

    We can, but we couldn't tell the nudge from everything else that changed: salary timing, seasonality, other campaigns. A random holdout of a few percent costs almost nothing and removes all of that. If a holdout is truly impossible, a staggered rollout with a comparison group is the next best, and I'd state its assumption.

    “How long does the test need to run?”

    Until it has the sample to see the smallest lift worth acting on, rounded up to whole weeks and at least one full pay cycle for anything tied to salary. At 3,000 eligible customers per arm per week and a 12% baseline, detecting 1.5 points takes about three weeks. Detecting half a point takes roughly nine times as long, five to six months, so it's worth asking whether half a point matters.

    “It's not significant after two weeks. Can we run it a week longer?”

    Only if that was the plan. Adding time after seeing the result is peeking and inflates false positives. If we want the flexibility, the right way is a sequential design built for repeated looks, set up before launch. Otherwise we report what we have, with its interval, and plan a longer follow-up test.

    “The arms ended up at 50.4% and 49.6%. Is that a problem?”

    It depends on the sample, and that's the point of testing it. With 2,000 customers that's well within chance. With 200,000 it's a serious mismatch. I'd run the chi-squared check against the planned split with a strict threshold, and if it fires, find the cause before reading any result.

    “Why not just put the model's best customers in the treatment group? They're the most likely to respond.”

    Because then the comparison measures who we chose, not what the treatment did. Targeting is fine, but randomise within the targeted group, or randomise across all and analyse by score band. That also tells us whether the model's ranking actually predicts who benefits.

    “Legal won't let us randomise credit approvals. What now?”

    That's a reasonable constraint. We can randomise things around the decision, such as reminders, communication and collections scripts, and use quasi-experimental methods for the decision itself: regression discontinuity at the cut-off, or difference-in-differences across a staged rollout. They rest on assumptions, which I'd state and test. For the approval decision itself I'd work with compliance on what a governed, small random sample could look like.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You randomised 30 branches (15 per arm), 800 customers each. ICC for the outcome is 0.03. What is the design effect, and the effective sample per arm?

    DEFF = 1 + (800 − 1) × 0.03 = 24.97, about 25. Each arm has 12,000 customers, so the effective sample is 12,000 / 25 ≈ 480 independent customers. The test has far less power than its customer count suggests, and a naive analysis would use standard errors about 5 times too small.

    2. A product manager wants to detect a 0.75-point lift instead of 1.5. By what factor does the required sample change?

    Roughly four times larger. The required sample varies with 1/δ². Precision improves only with the square root of the sample, so halving the detectable effect needs four times the customers. In practice the factor is slightly different because the variance term shifts a little with the rates, but four is the right answer to give.

    3. Planned 50/50, observed 50,600 versus 49,400 (100,000 in total). Is it an SRM at p < 0.001?

    The standard error of the count in one arm is √(100,000 × 0.25) ≈ 158. The gap from expected is 600, so z ≈ 3.8 and p ≈ 0.00015. Yes, it's an SRM at that threshold. The same 1.2-point imbalance in a test of 5,000 customers would give z ≈ 0.85, entirely ordinary.

    4. A 10-day test of a new mobile-app dashboard shows +6% logins. A colleague wants to scale. What do you ask for?

    The effect by day since each customer's first exposure, to see if it fades (novelty). The pre-specified primary metric and run length. The absolute rate and the interval, not only the relative figure. And a long-term holdback in the rollout to measure the settled effect.

    5. A referral bonus test randomised customers. Control customers had a referral rate of 4.1%; treated 5.0%. Why might the true effect be larger, and what design would fix it?

    Treated customers refer friends, some of whom are in the control arm. Those friends join through the treatment, so control outcomes are partly treated, which pulls the two arms together. The measured gap understates the effect. Randomise at a level that contains the referrals, such as household or social cluster, or run a two-level design that randomises both the referrer's incentive and the friend's.

    6. A fraud model is live for everyone. How can you measure what it saves, without leaving high-risk payments unblocked?

    Use a small random holdout at low risk scores, where being wrong is cheap, to compare outcomes with and without the action. Randomise the threshold slightly for some cards. And review a random sample of unflagged transactions to estimate the fraud the model misses. All need governance approval, and all give unbiased labels as a by-product.

    7. You tracked 12 outcome metrics, with no plan. One shows p = 0.03. What is the chance of at least one such result with no true effect, and what do you do with it?

    1 − 0.9512 ≈ 46%. Report it as exploratory, with its interval and without a claim, and design a follow-up test with that metric as the single pre-registered primary. Do not headline it.

    8. The test shows +1.1 points, 95% interval (+0.07, +2.13). A colleague writes "significant, so it works". Rewrite it in one sentence.

    “Activation rose by about 1.1 points (from 12.0% to 13.1%); the data are consistent with a gain anywhere from negligible to about two points, so the case for rollout rests on the cost being low and on confirming the effect holds after the first weeks.”
