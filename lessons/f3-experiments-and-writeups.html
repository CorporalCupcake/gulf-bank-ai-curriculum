    Experiments, and Analyses That Survive Review

    How to run a test a sceptical risk committee will believe, which traps quietly break it, what to do when you can't randomise, and how to write the result so a senior can act on it and a reviewer can't pull it apart.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRandomise the right unit, size the test before you start, check it ran as designed before you read the result, and then write down what you did, what you found with its uncertainty, and what you did not test.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An experiment compares two groups that differ only by a coin flip. Say the cards team texts new cardholders three days after issue, hoping to raise activation (a first purchase within 30 days) from about 12%.

    Comparing this month with last month tells you almost nothing: salary week, Ramadan and the nudge all moved together. Flip a coin per cardholder instead and everything else moves for both groups, so the only systematic difference is the nudge. Like two cups poured from the same jug, selection bias is zero by construction (F2.6).

    This lesson covers what goes wrong after you decide to flip the coin: the wrong unit, a test too short, customers silently missing from one arm, a first-week boost that fades, and a write-up a reviewer can pull apart.

    • The smallest effect you would act on sets the sample size. Halve that effect and you need four times the customers.
    • A test measures the window you watched. A short window dominated by novelty measures curiosity, like a restaurant's opening week.
    • The plan is part of the result. A test with 40% power that "found nothing" has not shown there is no effect. It could never have seen one.
    Hold onto thisEverything that makes an experiment trustworthy is decided before you see the outcome: unit, primary metric, sample size, run length, checks. Anything chosen afterwards, a reviewer will treat as a free choice made to get the answer you wanted.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What an experiment is

    An experiment assigns a treatment to units by a random mechanism you control, then compares an outcome across groups. Three choices:

    • Unit. What gets randomised: a customer, session, branch or agent.
    • Treatment and control. Control is the current experience, not "nothing".
    • Primary metric. One number that decides the question, with a minimum size worth acting on.

    The result is the average treatment effect: the difference in mean outcome between groups. Randomisation makes it unbiased, not precise, and not automatically true next quarter or in another country. Sample size buys precision. Generalisation is a judgement you state.

    One assumption breaks quietly: one customer's treatment must not change another's outcome (no interference).

    Choosing the randomisation unit

    The unit fixes what you can claim and how many independent observations you really have. Ten pupils from one class tell you less than ten pupils from ten schools, because classmates resemble each other. Customers are the same.

    • Customer is the default for anything experienced repeatedly: messages, pricing display, app features.
    • Session or page view looks like more data but is not. One customer's eight sessions resemble each other.
    • Branch or agent is forced when you can't deliver treatment per customer, such as a collections playbook.

    Clustering has a cost, called the design effect: how much the noise is inflated because observations in a cluster resemble each other. With 400 customers per branch and only a modest resemblance (0.02), it is about 9. Your 100,000 customers then carry the information of roughly 11,000 independent ones. Analyse them as independent and "significant" results appear far more than 5% of the time when nothing is going on. This is pseudo-replication, a very common error. A small resemblance is not harmless, because cluster size multiplies it.

    The fix is to analyse at the level you randomised: one number per cluster, or cluster-robust standard errors (F2.5). 30 branches per arm is a small experiment, whatever the customer count says. With few clusters, pair branches on pre-period outcomes (past activation, size, region) and flip a coin within each pair, so chance can't put the busiest branches in one arm.

    Sample size: MDE, power and variance reduction

    Power (F2.7) is the chance your test detects an effect that is really there, like a smoke alarm's sensitivity. Sample size depends on three things: how noisy the metric is, how sure you want to be (usually 5% false positives, 80% power), and the size of effect you want to see. The last matters most, because halving the effect costs four times the customers.

    Turn it round and you get the minimum detectable effect (MDE): the smallest true lift your test has an 80% chance of detecting. A test with a 1.5-point MDE will usually miss a true 0.8-point lift.

    • The MDE is a business number. If 1 point of activation is worth AED 2 million a year and the nudge costs AED 400,000, a lift below about 0.2 points isn't worth detecting.
    • Noise can be cut. CUPED removes the part of each customer's outcome that their own pre-test behaviour already predicts. If last month's spend explains half the variance in this month's, the sample you need halves. It adds no bias, because pre-test data can't be affected by treatment.

    Two warnings. Winner's curse: among underpowered tests, those that reach significance have overestimated the effect. A "found" 4 points from a test that could only detect 3 is more likely a lucky 1. Peeking: checking daily and stopping when p dips below 0.05 pushes the true false-positive rate well above 5% (F2.4). Fix the sample and look once, or use a sequential method built for repeated looks.

    -- Pre-test planning from the warehouse: baseline and noise for the metric
SELECT AVG(activated_30d::int)            AS baseline_rate,
       COUNT(*)                           AS eligible_per_week
FROM   cards_issued
WHERE  issue_date BETWEEN '2026-08-01' AND '2026-08-28'
  AND  segment = 'new_to_bank_retail';
-- Feed baseline_rate and the MDE into the power formula; divide the required n
-- by eligible_per_week / 2 to get run length in weeks.

    Is the experiment trustworthy? SRM, novelty, guardrails

    A well-designed experiment can still run badly. Run these checks before reading any result.

    Sample ratio mismatch (SRM). You planned 50/50 and got 50,200 versus 49,100. A simple test of the counts against the planned ratio tells you whether that is chance. If it rejects, something other than your coin flip decided who landed where: an app crash in one arm, a redirect dropping users, a logging bug. That cause is almost certainly related to the outcome, so the effect estimate cannot be trusted. It is like a lottery where one drawer jams. Teams use a strict threshold, commonly p < 0.001, so routine noise doesn't cry wolf.

    If SRM fires, don't "adjust for it" and don't report a result. Find the cause (slice counts by platform, app version, day, language), fix it and rerun.

    Two more checks.

    • Novelty and primacy. Plot the effect by week since each customer's first exposure. A steeply decaying curve is novelty. A rising one is customers adapting to change. A long-term holdback (1 to 5% of customers kept on the old experience for months) measures the settled effect.
    • Guardrail metrics. Things you must not harm while lifting the primary metric: complaints, opt-outs, contact-centre calls, delinquency. Pre-specify a tolerated harm for each. A test that can't see a small harm hasn't shown there is none. A nudge that lifts card use by pushing customers into revolving credit wins on the metric and fails on fair treatment.

    Twyman's law: any figure that looks too interesting is usually wrong. A 40% lift in retail activation means a bug, not a breakthrough.

    Holdouts for always-on models

    A live model that scores every customer daily (credit-line offers, attrition outreach, fraud blocks) leaves no "before". Everyone is treated, so any comparison is a before-and-after with every confounder from F2.6.

    The answer is a standing holdout: a random 2 to 5% of eligible customers whose model action is withheld or replaced by the default strategy. Like a permanent control group in a drug trial, it gives a continuous, unbiased estimate of the model's value, and an answer to "how do you know it works?" at model review. It also yields unbiased labels (the F2.6 feedback loop).

    • Size it from the MDE. 2% of 500,000 customers is 10,000: plenty for an activation lift, hopeless for a fraud-loss difference.
    • Where you can't withhold the action. You won't leave a high-risk payment unblocked. Hold out only at low risk, randomise the threshold slightly, or review a random sample of unflagged transactions. All need governance approval.

    Interference, networks and referrals

    Interference means customers affect each other. Think of vaccinating half a town: the unvaccinated half gets protection too.

    • Referrals. You test an AED 200 refer-a-friend bonus. A treated customer refers a friend in the control arm. Control now contains a treatment effect, so the gap understates the truth.

    The remedy is to randomise where spillover mostly stays inside the unit: household, company payroll, referral cluster, branch catchment. You pay for that validity with a design effect. For systems like pricing or queues, switchback experiments alternate the whole system between treatment and control in time blocks.

    When you cannot randomise

    Sometimes the answer to "why not just test it?" is a firm no. Credit decisions for protected groups, differential pricing, and changes to disclosed profit rates on regulated products (including Islamic finance contracts needing Sharia board approval) raise fairness and consumer-protection questions that belong to compliance and risk.

    Then use quasi-experiments, which find randomness the world supplied. Each rests on an assumption you must state and defend. The honest label is "estimate under assumption X", never "proof".

    Writing an analysis that survives review

    A reviewer (model risk, audit, credit committee, regulator) is doing a job, not being hostile. They ask the same dozen questions of every analysis. Answer them first and a busy senior can act without reading everything.

    1. Decision and answer, first. Recommendation, effect in business terms, main uncertainty, what would change it. Write it last, put it first.
    2. Baseline. The control outcome in absolute terms. "Up 9%" could be 12% to 13.1% or 0.2% to 0.22%.
    3. Effect with an interval. Absolute difference in points, a 95% interval (F2.1), then dirhams with assumptions written down. Never a p-value alone: a tiny effect in a huge sample is "significant".
    4. Checks that passed. SRM, effect by week, guardrails.
    5. What was not tested. The most neglected section and the one that earns trust: other segments, longer horizons, interference.
    6. Deviations. Anything that differed from the plan.

    Report uncertainty as a range with a plain reading: "Best estimate +1.1 points; the data fit anything from a negligible +0.1 to a substantial +2.1." Avoid "not significant, so no effect" and "significant" as a synonym for "important".

    Judge the decision against the interval, not the point estimate. If the nudge costs AED 400,000 and a point of activation is worth AED 2 million, the central estimate pays back about five times, but a lower bound near zero means payback isn't guaranteed. State the break-even lift.

    Pre-registering metrics

    Every analysis has forks: metric, segment, window, exclusions. Choose after seeing results and you will, honestly and unconsciously, take the path that gives an interesting answer (F2.4). With 12 independent metrics and no true effect, the chance at least one "wins" at 5% is about 46%, like rolling a die twelve times and being surprised by a six.

    Pre-registration means writing down, dated and before launch, what you will do. In a bank it fits on one page in the ticket or model-risk record:

    • hypothesis and the single primary metric, with direction and MDE;
    • secondary metrics and guardrails, each with a margin;
    • population, unit, sample size, run length and stopping rule;
    • analysis plan and the decision rule: what leads to rollout, rerun or stop.

    This labels exploration rather than forbidding it. Anything off-plan goes in an exploratory section, with its interval and no claim, flagged as a hypothesis for the next test. Reviewers read that as strength.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Wrong unit, or spillover ignored

      "Two million sessions, p < 0.0001" from a test that randomised 40,000 customers. Or referral friends and shared agents sit in both arms.

      Spot itAsk what was randomised and how many of those there were. That count is the sample size. Ask how treatment could reach control customers.
    • Ignoring SRM

      Arms are 51/49 against a plan of 50/50, and the report shows lift anyway.

      Spot itLook for arm counts and a mismatch test. If neither is there, assume nobody looked.
    • Forking paths

      "We checked every morning and it crossed 5% on day nine." Or the primary metric was flat, so the headline became a metric that rose, or a segment that worked.

      Spot itCompare the headline with the plan. Ask how many looks, metrics and cuts were tried. Treat off-plan results as hypotheses.
    • Weak tests read too strongly

      "No significant difference, so the nudge doesn't work", with an interval from −1 to +3 points. Or the pilot said +3 and rollout delivers +1.

      Spot itAsk for the interval and MDE. If the interval holds effects the business cares about, the test was inconclusive, not negative. Shrink effects from small significant tests.
    • Novelty read as a lasting effect

      A 10-day test of a new app home screen shows a big engagement lift.

      Spot itAsk for the effect by week since first exposure. A steep fall means novelty. Ask for a holdback.
    • Missing guardrails

      Activation rose, and nobody looked at complaints or opt-outs until the regulator asked.

      Spot itLook for a guardrail table with a margin each. "No significant harm" without a margin is not a guardrail.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. You randomised 30 branches (15 per arm), 800 customers each. Why is your effective sample far smaller than 12,000 per arm, and what do you do about it?

    Customers in a branch carry overlapping information, so even a small resemblance multiplies into a large design effect. The honest sample is nearer the number of branches. Analyse at branch level and expect low power.

    2. A product manager wants to detect a 0.75-point lift instead of 1.5. What happens to the sample you need?

    It grows about four times. Precision improves only with the square root of the sample, so halving the effect costs four times the customers.

    3. Planned 50/50, observed 50,600 versus 49,400 (100,000 in total). Should you worry, and would the same split in 5,000 customers worry you?

    Yes at 100,000: the imbalance is far too large to be chance, so something other than the coin flip is assigning customers. In 5,000 customers the same split is ordinary noise.

    4. A 10-day test of a new mobile-app dashboard shows +6% logins. A colleague wants to scale. What do you ask for?

    The effect by day since first exposure, to see if it fades. The pre-specified primary metric. The absolute rate and interval. And a long-term holdback in the rollout.

    5. A referral bonus test randomised customers. Control referral rate was 4.1%; treated 5.0%. Why might the true effect be larger, and what design would fix it?

    Treated customers refer friends who sit in the control arm, so control outcomes are partly treated and the arms are pulled together. Randomise at a level that contains the referrals, such as household or social cluster.

    6. A fraud model is live for everyone. How can you measure what it saves, without leaving high-risk payments unblocked?

    Use a small random holdout at low risk scores, where being wrong is cheap, randomise the threshold slightly, or review a random sample of unflagged transactions. All need governance approval.

    7. You tracked 12 outcome metrics, with no plan. One shows p = 0.03. Why is that not a finding, and what do you do with it?

    With 12 metrics and no real effect, you would see at least one such result almost half the time. Report it as exploratory, with its interval and no claim, and run a follow-up with that metric as the single pre-registered primary. Don't headline it.

    8. The test shows +1.1 points, 95% interval (+0.07, +2.13). A colleague writes "significant, so it works". Rewrite it in one sentence.

    “Activation rose by about 1.1 points (12.0% to 13.1%); the data fit a gain anywhere from negligible to about two points, so the case for rollout rests on low cost and on confirming the effect holds after the first weeks.”
