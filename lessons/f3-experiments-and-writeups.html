    Experiments, and Analyses That Survive Review

    How to run a test a sceptical risk committee will believe, which traps quietly break it, what to do when you can't randomise, and how to write the result so a senior can act on it and a reviewer can't pull it apart.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRandomise the right unit, size the test before you start, check it ran as designed before you read the result, and then write down what you did, what you found with its uncertainty, and what you did not test.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An experiment compares two groups that differ only by a coin flip. Say the cards team texts new cardholders three days after issue, hoping to raise activation (a first purchase within 30 days) from a baseline of about 12%. A lift of 1.5 points would delight the product manager.

    The lazy way is to nudge everyone and compare this month with last month. That tells you almost nothing: salary week, Ramadan, a merchant offer and the nudge all moved together. The better way is to flip a coin per new cardholder and compare the halves over the same weeks. Everything else moves for both groups, so the only systematic difference is the nudge. Like a taste test with two cups poured from the same jug, the coin flip cannot know who would have activated anyway, so selection bias is zero by construction (F2.6).

    This lesson covers what goes wrong after you decide to flip the coin: the wrong unit, a test too short or too long, customers silently missing from one arm, and a first-week boost that fades. Then someone writes "activation up 9% (p<0.05), recommend rollout" and the credit risk committee asks four questions the author cannot answer.

    Three takeaways.

    • The smallest effect you would act on sets the sample size. Halve that effect and you need four times the customers, because precision grows only with the square root of the sample.
    • A test measures the window you watched. A short window dominated by novelty measures curiosity, like a new restaurant's opening week. It may not hold in month three.
    • The plan is part of the result. A test with 40% power that "found nothing" has not shown there is no effect. It could never have seen one.
    Hold onto thisEverything that makes an experiment trustworthy is decided before you see the outcome: unit, primary metric, sample size, run length, checks. Anything chosen afterwards is a researcher degree of freedom, and a reviewer will treat it as one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What an experiment is

    An experiment assigns a treatment to units by a random mechanism you control, then compares an outcome across groups. State four choices in one sentence each:

    • Unit. What gets randomised: a customer, session, transaction, branch or agent.
    • Treatment and control. Control is not "nothing". It is the current experience, and defining it precisely is half the design.
    • Primary metric. One number that decides the question, with a direction and a minimum size worth acting on. Everything else is secondary or a guardrail.
    • Population and window. Who is eligible and how long they are followed, e.g. "cards issued 1 to 21 March, followed 30 days".

    The result is the average treatment effect: the difference in mean outcome between groups. Randomisation makes it unbiased, not precise, and not automatically true next quarter or in another country. Precision is what sample size buys. Generalisation is a judgement you state, not a statistic.

    Two assumptions break quietly. No interference: one customer's treatment does not change another's outcome. One version of each arm: "treatment" means the same thing for everyone. Together they are called SUTVA, a name worth knowing for interviews.

    Choosing the randomisation unit

    The unit fixes what you can claim and how many independent observations you really have. Ten pupils from one class are less informative than ten pupils from ten schools, because classmates resemble each other. Customers are the same.

    • Customer is the default for anything experienced repeatedly: messages, pricing display, app features.
    • Session or page view looks like more data but is not. One customer's eight sessions resemble each other, and they would see different versions on different days, which is a different treatment ("sometimes the new screen").
    • Branch or agent is forced when you can't deliver treatment per customer: a queue flow, a relationship manager's script, a collections playbook. Customers in a branch share staff, footfall and local economy.
    • Transaction only for memoryless treatments, such as a fraud-rule threshold on a single card payment.

    Clustering has a precise cost. With m observations per cluster and intra-cluster correlation ρ (the share of variance that is "which cluster" rather than "which observation"; 0 means clusters don't matter, 1 means everyone in a cluster is identical), variance inflates by the design effect:

    DEFF=1+(m−1)ρ

    With m = 400 customers per branch and a modest ρ of 0.02, DEFF is about 9. Your 100,000 customers carry the information of roughly 11,000 independent ones. Analyse them as independent and your standard errors are too small by √9 = 3, so "significant" results appear far more than 5% of the time under no effect. This is pseudo-replication, a very common error. Misreading to avoid: a small ρ is not harmless, because m multiplies it.

    The fix is the same every time: analyse at the level you randomised. Aggregate to one number per cluster and compare clusters, or use cluster-robust standard errors (F2.5). Both show what you really have: 30 branches per arm is a small experiment, whatever the customer count says.

    With few clusters, coin flips can put the busiest branches in one arm. Use stratified or matched-pair randomisation: sort branches on pre-period outcomes (past activation, size, region), pair them up, and flip a coin within each pair.

    Sample size: MDE, power and variance reduction

    Power (F2.7) is the chance your test detects an effect that is really there, like a smoke alarm's sensitivity. Fix the false-positive rate α (usually 5%, two-sided) and power 1 − β (usually 80%). For two arms of size n and a binary outcome, detecting a true difference δ needs:

    n=(zα/22p¯(1−p¯)+zβp1(1−p1)+p2(1−p2))2δ2

    p1 is the baseline rate, p2 = p1 + δ, p̄ is their average, and z is 1.96 for 5% two-sided and 0.84 for 80% power. In words: noise, times how sure you want to be, divided by the effect squared. Aiming for a lift half as big costs four times the customers.

    Turn it round to get the number worth quoting: the minimum detectable effect (MDE), the smallest true lift your test has an 80% chance of detecting. A test with a 1.5-point MDE will usually miss a true 0.8-point lift. Three levers set it:

    • The MDE is a business number. If 1 point of activation is worth AED 2 million a year and the nudge costs AED 400,000, a lift below about 0.2 points isn't worth detecting. Don't set it from what is convenient to test.
    • Traffic sets the clock. Rare events (default, fraud loss, attrition) may need months. Run whole weeks, and for anything tied to salary or card statements, a full pay cycle.
    • Noise can be cut. CUPED (controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data) subtracts the part of each customer's outcome predicted by their own pre-test behaviour. If last month's spend explains half the variance in this month's, the comparison's variance halves, and so does the sample you need. Stratified assignment does the same at design time. Neither adds bias, because pre-test data can't be affected by treatment.

    Two warnings. Winner's curse: among underpowered tests, those that reach significance have overestimated the effect by selection. A "found" 4 points from a test that could only detect 3 is more likely a lucky 1 than a true 4 (Gelman and Carlin's type M and type S errors: wrong magnitude, wrong sign). Peeking: checking daily and stopping when p dips below 0.05 pushes the true false-positive rate well above 5% (F2.4). Fix the sample and look once, or use a sequential method built for repeated looks.

    -- Pre-test planning from the warehouse: baseline and noise for the metric
SELECT AVG(activated_30d::int)            AS baseline_rate,
       COUNT(*)                           AS eligible_per_week
FROM   cards_issued
WHERE  issue_date BETWEEN '2026-08-01' AND '2026-08-28'
  AND  segment = 'new_to_bank_retail';
-- Feed baseline_rate and the MDE into the power formula; divide the required n

    Is the experiment trustworthy? SRM, novelty, guardrails

    A well-designed experiment can still run badly. Run these checks before reading any result, starting with the most important.

    Sample ratio mismatch (SRM). You planned 50/50 and got 50,200 versus 49,100. Is that chance? A chi-squared (or z) test of observed counts against the planned ratio answers it. If it rejects, something other than your coin flip decided who landed where: an app crash in one arm, a redirect dropping users, bot filtering after assignment, a logging bug. That cause is almost certainly related to the outcome, so the effect estimate cannot be trusted at all. It is like a lottery where one drawer jams: the winners no longer represent everyone. It appears in a few percent of experiments at large firms. Teams use a strict threshold, commonly p < 0.001, so routine noise doesn't cry wolf.

    If SRM fires, don't "adjust for it" and don't report a result. Find the cause, fix it and rerun. Localise it by slicing counts by platform, app version, day, language or emirate. The mismatch usually lives in one slice.

    Three more checks, in order.

    • Balance on pre-treatment variables. Compare arms on things fixed before assignment: tenure, income band, prior activation, channel. Randomisation guarantees balance on average, not in one draw. Persistent imbalance is a second sign of broken assignment. Don't add controls after seeing a difference; pre-specify any adjustment.
    • Novelty and primacy. Plot the effect by week since each customer's first exposure. A steeply decaying curve is novelty: customers respond to the new thing, then stop. A rising curve is primacy: customers resist change, then adapt. Both bias a short test. A long-term holdback (1–5% of customers kept on the old experience for months) measures the settled effect.
    • Guardrail metrics. Things you must not harm while lifting the primary metric: complaints, marketing opt-outs, contact-centre calls, fee income, delinquency, crash rate, messages delivered in the customer's preferred language. Pre-specify each with a tolerated harm as a non-inferiority margin, not "no significant increase". A test that can't see a small harm hasn't shown there is none. Banks add conduct limits: a nudge that lifts card use by pushing customers into revolving credit wins on the metric and fails on fair treatment.

    Twyman's law (Kohavi): any figure that looks interesting is usually wrong. A 40% lift in retail activation means a bug, not a breakthrough. Look for a logging error before you celebrate.

    Holdouts for always-on models

    A live model that scores every customer daily (credit-line offers, attrition outreach, fraud blocks, AML alerts) leaves no "before". Everyone is treated, so any comparison is a before-and-after with every confounder from F2.6.

    The answer is a standing holdout: a random 2–5% of eligible customers whose model action is withheld or replaced by the default strategy. Like a permanent control group in a drug trial, it gives a continuous, unbiased estimate of the model's value and an answer to "how do you know it works?" at model review. It also yields unbiased labels, since those outcomes weren't shaped by the model (the F2.6 feedback loop).

    • Hold out the action, keep the score. Log the model's score for holdout customers so you can check whether the effect concentrates in the top decile, as claimed.
    • Size it from the MDE. 2% of 500,000 customers is 10,000: plenty for an activation lift, hopeless for a fraud-loss difference.
    • Where you can't withhold the action. You won't leave a high-risk payment unblocked. Options: hold out only at low risk, where cost is small; randomise the threshold slightly; or review a random sample of unflagged transactions. All need governance approval and are standard in mature fraud teams.
    • Rotate or fix? A fixed holdout ages and drifts from the population. A rotating one stays representative but can't measure long-run effects. Many teams keep a small permanent group plus a larger rotating one.

    Interference, networks and referrals

    Interference means customers affect each other, and banks have plenty of it. Think of vaccinating half a town: the unvaccinated half gets protection too.

    • Referrals. You test an AED 200 refer-a-friend bonus. A treated customer refers a friend in the control arm, who opens an account. Control now contains a treatment effect, so the gap between arms understates the truth by an amount you can't see.
    • Peer-to-peer and remittance products. An instant-transfer feature is only useful if the recipient has it too.
    • Shared resources. A better collections playbook still uses the same agents. If treated cases take longer, control cases wait longer.
    • Households and companies. Spouses and colleagues on one payroll influence each other. Randomise individuals within a household and you test the message on both.

    The remedy is to randomise where spillover mostly stays inside the unit: household, company payroll, referral cluster, branch catchment. You buy validity with a design effect. Two methods to know by name: switchback experiments alternate the whole system between treatment and control in time blocks (pricing, queues), and graph-cluster randomisation for networks (Saveski et al. used it to detect spillover itself). To measure spillover directly, use a two-level design: randomise referrers, then randomise the incentive offered to their friends.

    When you cannot randomise

    Sometimes the answer to "why not just test it?" is a firm no. Credit decisions for protected groups, differential pricing, and anything that denies a service or changes disclosed profit rates on a regulated product (including Islamic finance contracts needing Sharia board approval) raise fairness and consumer-protection questions that belong to compliance and risk. Other reasons are practical: the change is already made, the rollout is nationwide, or the outcome is too slow.

    Then use quasi-experiments, which find randomness the world supplied. Each rests on an assumption you must state and defend. The honest label is "estimate under assumption X", never "proof".

    Writing an analysis that survives review

    A reviewer (model risk, audit, credit committee, regulator) is doing a job, not being hostile. They have read many wrong analyses and ask the same dozen questions. Answer them before they are asked, and a busy senior can act without reading everything. Use this skeleton; no part is long.

    1. Decision and answer, first. One paragraph: recommendation, effect in business terms, main uncertainty, and what would change it. Write it last, put it first.
    2. Population and period. Who was eligible, who was excluded and why, dates, and counts per arm after each exclusion.
    3. Baseline. The control outcome in absolute terms. "Up 9%" could be 12% to 13.1% or 0.2% to 0.22%.
    4. Effect with an interval. Absolute difference in points, a 95% interval (F2.1), then dirhams with assumptions written down. Never a p-value alone: it says nothing about size, and a tiny effect in a huge sample is "significant".
    5. Checks that passed. SRM, balance, effect by week, guardrails with margins.
    6. What was not tested. The most neglected section and the one that earns trust: other segments, countries, longer horizons, interference, skipped comparisons.
    7. Deviations. Anything that differed from the plan, and when it was decided. Showing the mess separates analysis from advocacy.
    8. Reproducibility. Where query and code live, data snapshot date, seed or assignment log.

    Report uncertainty as a range with a plain reading: "Best estimate +1.1 points; the data fit anything from a negligible +0.1 to a substantial +2.1." Avoid "not significant, so no effect" (absence of evidence is not evidence of absence) and "significant" as a synonym for "important".

    Judge the decision against the interval, not the point estimate. If the nudge costs AED 400,000 and a point of activation is worth AED 2 million, the central estimate pays back about five times over, but a lower bound near zero means payback isn't guaranteed. State the break-even lift so the reader sees where the interval sits against it.

    Pre-registering metrics

    Every analysis has forks: metric, segment, window, exclusions, adjustment. Choose after seeing results and you will, honestly and unconsciously, take the path that gives an interesting answer (F2.4). With 12 independent metrics and no true effect, the chance at least one "wins" at 5% is 1 − 0.9512 ≈ 46%, like rolling a die twelve times and being surprised by a six.

    Pre-registration means writing down, dated and before launch, what you will do. In a bank it fits on one page in the ticket or model-risk record:

    • hypothesis and the single primary metric, with direction and MDE;
    • secondary metrics and guardrails, each with a margin;
    • population, randomisation unit and mechanism, planned split;
    • sample size, run length, stopping rule (one look, or sequential);
    • analysis: estimator, adjustments (e.g. CUPED covariates), multiple-comparison handling, planned segment cuts;
    • decision rule: what leads to rollout, rerun or stop.

    This labels exploration rather than forbidding it. Anything off-plan goes in an exploratory section, with its interval and no claim, flagged as a hypothesis for the next test. Reviewers read that as strength (Nosek et al.). Model validators and auditors already want a dated record of intent, and a pre-registered test is hard to challenge later.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Analysing sessions or transactions as if independent

      "Two million sessions, p < 0.0001" from a test that randomised 40,000 customers.

      Spot itAsk what was randomised and how many of those there were. That count is the sample size.
    • Ignoring SRM

      Arms are 51/49 against a plan of 50/50, and the report shows lift anyway.

      Spot itLook for arm counts and a mismatch test. If neither is there, assume nobody looked.
    • Stopping when it turns significant

      "We checked every morning and it crossed 5% on day nine."

      Spot itAsk how many looks and what the stopping rule was. No rule means an inflated false-positive rate.
    • An underpowered test read as "no effect"

      "No significant difference, so the nudge doesn't work." The interval runs from −1 to +3 points.

      Spot itAsk for the interval and MDE. If the interval holds effects the business cares about, the test was inconclusive, not negative.
    • Novelty read as a lasting effect

      A 10-day test of a new app home screen shows a big engagement lift.

      Spot itAsk for the effect by week since first exposure. A steep fall means novelty. Ask for a holdback.
    • Metric shopping

      Activation was the primary metric and flat, so the report headlines "card spend per active customer", which rose.

      Spot itCompare the headline metric with the plan. No plan: ask how many metrics were examined.
    • Segment fishing

      "The nudge works strongly for Arabic-language customers in Sharjah aged 30–35."

      Spot itAsk whether the segment was pre-specified and how many cuts were tried. Treat it as a hypothesis, never a finding.
    • Missing or unjustified guardrails

      Activation rose, and nobody looked at complaints or opt-outs until the regulator asked.

      Spot itLook for a guardrail table with a margin each. "No significant harm" without a margin is not a guardrail.
    • Contamination

      Referral friends, shared agents or households in both arms dilute the effect unnoticed.

      Spot itAsk how treatment could reach control customers, and whether the unit contains the spillover.
    • Winner's curse on rollout

      The pilot said +3 points; national rollout delivers +1.

      Spot itAsk about the pilot's power. Shrink effects from small significant tests and quote the interval, not the point.
    • Relative lift without a baseline

      "Up 40%", from 0.5% to 0.7%.

      Spot itAsk for absolute rates and counts. Dirhams come from the absolute difference.
    • AI-drafted analysis

      Fluent, with a confident causal verb, a p-value and no sample sizes. LLMs are fast at the shape of a report and unreliable on numbers they didn't compute.

      Spot itCheck every figure against notebook output. Ask where the interval comes from and what wasn't tested. A draft with no limitations section is unfinished.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. You randomised 30 branches (15 per arm), 800 customers each. ICC for the outcome is 0.03. What is the design effect, and the effective sample per arm?

    DEFF = 1 + (800 − 1) × 0.03 ≈ 25. Each arm has 12,000 customers, so the effective sample is 12,000 / 25 ≈ 480. Power is far lower than the customer count suggests, and a naive analysis would have standard errors about 5 times too small.

    2. A product manager wants to detect a 0.75-point lift instead of 1.5. By what factor does the required sample change?

    About four times larger. Sample scales with 1/δ², and precision grows only with the square root of the sample. The exact factor shifts slightly with the rates, but four is the answer to give.

    3. Planned 50/50, observed 50,600 versus 49,400 (100,000 in total). Is it an SRM at p < 0.001?

    The standard error of one arm's count is √(100,000 × 0.25) ≈ 158. The gap is 600, so z ≈ 3.8 and p ≈ 0.00015. Yes. The same 1.2-point imbalance in 5,000 customers gives z ≈ 0.85, entirely ordinary.

    4. A 10-day test of a new mobile-app dashboard shows +6% logins. A colleague wants to scale. What do you ask for?

    The effect by day since first exposure, to see if it fades. The pre-specified primary metric and run length. The absolute rate and interval, not only the relative figure. And a long-term holdback in the rollout.

    5. A referral bonus test randomised customers. Control referral rate was 4.1%; treated 5.0%. Why might the true effect be larger, and what design would fix it?

    Treated customers refer friends who sit in the control arm. Those friends join through the treatment, so control outcomes are partly treated and the arms are pulled together. Randomise at a level that contains the referrals (household or social cluster), or use a two-level design that randomises the referrer's and the friend's incentives.

    6. A fraud model is live for everyone. How can you measure what it saves, without leaving high-risk payments unblocked?

    Use a small random holdout at low risk scores, where being wrong is cheap. Randomise the threshold slightly for some cards. Review a random sample of unflagged transactions to estimate the fraud the model misses. All need governance approval, and all give unbiased labels as a by-product.

    7. You tracked 12 outcome metrics, with no plan. One shows p = 0.03. What is the chance of at least one such result with no true effect, and what do you do with it?

    1 − 0.9512 ≈ 46%. Report it as exploratory, with its interval and no claim, and run a follow-up with that metric as the single pre-registered primary. Don't headline it.

    8. The test shows +1.1 points, 95% interval (+0.07, +2.13). A colleague writes "significant, so it works". Rewrite it in one sentence.

    “Activation rose by about 1.1 points (12.0% to 13.1%); the data fit a gain anywhere from negligible to about two points, so the case for rollout rests on low cost and on confirming the effect holds after the first weeks.”
