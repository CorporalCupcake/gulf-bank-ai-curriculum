    Synthetic Data, Cheaper Training and Model Merging

    Why a model trained on its own output forgets the rare cases first, and what keeps that from happening; how 8-bit and 4-bit arithmetic, a different optimiser and transferable hyperparameters cut the cost of training; and how two fine-tuned models can be combined by arithmetic on their weights.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceSynthetic data is safe and powerful when it is checked, diverse and added to real data, and ruinous when it replaces real data in a closed loop; the same trade between cheapness and fidelity runs through low-precision training and model merging, where a little noise is fine and compounding noise is not.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three ideas share this lesson because they answer one question: how do you get a capable model for less than the obvious price? The obvious price is a huge pile of human-written text, a huge cluster of high-precision chips, and a fresh training run for every variant you want.

    Synthetic data replaces some of the human text with text written by another model. Training efficiency (lower-precision numbers, a better optimiser, hyperparameters you tune on a small model and reuse on a big one) makes each training step cheaper. Model merging avoids the extra run altogether by adding together the weights of models that were already trained.

    Start with the one that has the best story. Imagine photocopying a photocopy. The first copy is fine. By the tenth, thin lines have vanished and the dark areas have bled together. Nothing dramatic happened at any single step. Each copy lost a little of the detail that was rare in the page, and the loss compounds. A language model trained on its own output does the same thing, and the details it loses first are the rare ones: the unusual phrasing, the odd customer, the minority class, the tail of the distribution.

    Now the counterweight. A student who works through problems, checks the answers against the back of the book, and keeps only the worked solutions that are right will get better, even though the solutions are the student's own writing. The difference is not "synthetic versus real". It is whether new information enters the loop: real data, or a verifier that knows when an answer is correct. Part 2 makes this precise with a toy you can run.

    Hold onto thisTraining on a model's output is not inherently bad. Training on it instead of real data, with no outside check, for many generations, is. Every efficiency trick in this lesson has the same shape: it works until errors compound, and the engineering is in stopping them compounding.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Synthetic data and model collapse (R2.3)

    Why do models use synthetic data at all? Three reasons. Real high-quality text is finite, and frontier labs have already used most of the open web. Some data barely exists in the wild (long worked chains of reasoning, tool-use transcripts, rare edge cases). And a strong model can be a cheap, patient writer: given a topic, it can produce thousands of clean explanations, questions, or rewrites of messy pages.

    The risk was named in the 2024 Nature paper by Shumailov and colleagues: model collapse. Train generation 1 on real data. Sample from it to build the data for generation 2, and so on. They found two stages. In early collapse the model loses the tails of the distribution, the low-probability events, while the average looks fine, so headline metrics hide the damage. In late collapse the model converges on a narrow, repetitive output that has little to do with the original data.

    The mechanism is statistical and very simple. Any finite sample under-represents rare events: draw 20 points and you will rarely see anything beyond two standard deviations. A model fitted to that sample learns a distribution slightly narrower than the truth. Sample from that and you get a still narrower one. Write σ for the model's spread and n for the number of samples per generation. For a Gaussian fitted by maximum likelihood (F1.6):

    E[σg2]=(n−1n)g·σ02

    The expected variance after g generations of training only on the previous generation's samples. Each generation shaves a factor (n − 1)/n off the variance. With n = 25 and 50 generations that is 0.9650 ≈ 0.13, so the spread falls to about 36% of the original. The typical (median) run does worse than this average, because the spread also does a random walk and can only drift to zero, never recover. Real language models are not Gaussians, but the pressure is the same: sampling then refitting sharpens the model onto its own mode, and rare things go first.

    Two other effects stack on the sampling one. Approximation error: no model fits its data exactly, and each generation inherits the previous generation's mistakes as if they were facts. Decoding sharpening: people sample with temperature below 1 or top-p, which cuts the tail further before the data is even written down.

    What fixes it? The question was settled more precisely by a follow-up. Gerstgrasser and colleagues (2024) separated two regimes. In replace, each generation trains only on the previous generation's output: error grows without bound. In accumulate, each generation trains on all the data so far, the original real data plus every synthetic batch: test error stays bounded. Real-world data does not vanish when new web text is polluted; it piles up. Dohmatob and colleagues (2025, "strong model collapse") added a warning: if the synthetic fraction stays a non-trivial share, even mixed data can degrade the scaling you hoped to get from more data. So accumulation is a safeguard, not a licence.

    The simulation below runs the toy exactly. It uses Gaussians so the maths is clean, and three recipes: replace, accumulate, and "mix" (each generation trains on a fixed fraction of fresh real data plus synthetic data from the last model).

    The toy shows why the practical recipes work.

    • Keep real data in the mix. A small, steady stream of real data pins the tails in place. The exact share needed depends on the task and is an empirical question.
    • Add a verifier. Maths answers can be checked, code can be run against tests, a translation can be back-translated. Keep only what passes. The verifier is the new information the loop needs. This is the engine of reasoning-model training (R3.2) and why code and maths improve fastest.
    • Use a stronger teacher, or a different one. Distilling a larger model into a smaller one injects the larger model's knowledge. Training a model on its own raw samples injects nothing.
    • Insist on diversity. Sample with a wide range of prompts, personas and seeds, deduplicate, and measure the spread of what you generated. A synthetic set of 1 million near-duplicates is worth far fewer than 1 million rows.
    • Rewrite rather than invent. Rephrasing real documents into cleaner, more explicit forms (as in the "rephrasing the web" line of work) keeps facts tied to real sources. Inventing new "facts" from nothing is where hallucinations get baked in.

    Successful examples are worth knowing: Microsoft's Phi series built much of its pretraining mix from generated "textbook" text; NVIDIA reported that over 98% of the alignment data for Nemotron-4 340B was synthetic, produced with a reward model and a strong instruction model; and distilled reasoning traces from large models are used throughout open-weight releases.

    Training efficiency: FP8 and FP4, Muon, μP (R2.4)

    Training cost is roughly the number of arithmetic operations times the cost of each operation, plus the time spent moving numbers between memory and chip. Three levers act on this without changing the model's design. (This topic is "in brief": you need to recognise the ideas and say what each buys, not implement them.)

    1. Lower-precision numbers. A weight stored in 16 bits (BF16) can be stored in 8 (FP8) or 4 (FP4). Halving the width roughly halves the memory and bandwidth, and modern accelerators run 8-bit and 4-bit matrix multiplication much faster than 16-bit. The catch is range and resolution. A floating-point number has an exponent (how big) and a mantissa (how finely): FP8 comes in E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits; max value 448; finer) and E5M2 (5 and 2; max 57,344; wider). FP4 in the E2M1 format has only eight non-negative values: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 6.

    A format that coarse only works with scaling. Split the tensor into blocks, find the largest absolute value in each block, and divide the block by a scale so that it fits the format's range. The stored number is the scale plus the tiny values. Writing s for the block scale and Q for rounding to the nearest representable value:

    xq=s·Q(x/s),s=max|x|qmax

    qmax is the format's largest value (6 for FP4 E2M1, 448 for FP8 E4M3). The smaller the block, the closer the scale tracks local magnitudes, so one outlier ruins fewer neighbours, at the price of storing more scales. The OCP "microscaling" formats (MXFP4) use blocks of 32 with a power-of-two scale; NVIDIA's NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with an FP8 scale plus one per-tensor scale. Worked example: a block's largest value is 12, so s = 2. The value 5.2 becomes 2.6, which rounds to 3, and is stored as 6. Error 0.8.

    Training adds difficulties that inference does not. Gradients are small and noisy, tiny updates must accumulate over millions of steps, and a rounding bias adds up where random error cancels. So production recipes keep a high-precision master copy of the weights and accumulate sums in 32 bits, use stochastic rounding (round up or down at random, in proportion to the remainder, so the error averages out), and keep a few sensitive layers in 16 bits. DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) showed FP8 mixed-precision training working at very large scale with fine-grained tile and block scaling. FP4 pretraining is newer and still being proven; Part 4 lists what is reported.

    2. A different optimiser: Muon. AdamW, the default for years, treats every weight as an independent number and rescales its update by a running estimate of its gradient size (F1.3 and F1.8 cover gradient methods). Muon, introduced by Keller Jordan and collaborators in 2024, works on whole weight matrices. It takes the momentum-averaged gradient matrix and replaces it with the nearest matrix whose singular values are all equal, an "orthogonalised" update, computed cheaply with a few Newton-Schulz iterations rather than a full singular value decomposition. Intuition: raw gradient matrices are dominated by a few directions, so most of the update goes into the same few directions. Equalising the singular values spreads learning across the many rare, weaker directions. Moonshot AI reported that, with some adjustments (weight decay, and matching update scale to AdamW's), Muon reached the same loss with roughly half the compute at scale ("Muon is Scalable for LLM Training", 2025), and later trained Kimi K2, a one-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (R1.1), with a variant called MuonClip that adds a safeguard against exploding attention logits. Muon is applied to the hidden weight matrices only; embeddings, the output layer and small vectors stay on AdamW.

    3. Hyperparameter transfer: μP. The best learning rate for a 100-million-parameter model is not the best for a 10-billion-parameter one under the standard setup, and tuning at full size is ruinously expensive (F4.5 covers tuning). Greg Yang and colleagues' "maximal update parametrisation" (μP, 2022) rescales initialisation and learning rates layer by layer with the width, so that how fast features change stays constant as the model grows. The optimal learning rate then barely moves with width, so you tune on a small proxy and reuse the setting. They showed this by transferring hyperparameters from a 40-million-parameter model to a 6.7-billion-parameter one, matching a published GPT-3 baseline at a fraction of the tuning cost. It is the practical companion to scaling laws (R2.1): scaling laws tell you how big and how long; μP lets you set the knobs without paying for the experiments at full size. It handles width well; depth and non-standard optimisers need extensions, and not every lab uses it.

    Together: FP8 or FP4 makes a step cheaper, Muon makes each step count for more, μP stops you wasting runs on bad settings. None changes what the model can learn. All change who can afford to train one.

    Model merging (R2.5)

    Fine-tune one base model on Arabic banking correspondence, another on Python and SQL, a third on safety. You now have three models and want one that does all three. Options: train again on the union of the data (expensive, needs all the data in one place), serve three models (costly), or merge the weights: no gradient steps, no data, minutes of work on a CPU.

    Why would that work at all? Neural network weights live in a landscape with many equivalent solutions (R6.2 on loss landscapes). Two models trained from different random starts sit in different valleys, and averaging them gives garbage. But models fine-tuned from the same pretrained model start in the same basin and move only a short distance, so the straight line between them tends to stay in a low-loss region. This is called linear mode connectivity. Average several fine-tunes of one base, trained with different seeds or settings, and you often get a model better than any single one: the "model soup" result (Wortsman et al., 2022).

    The next idea is task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022). A task vector is what fine-tuning changed: τ = θfine-tuned − θbase. Add task vectors to the base and you add skills; subtract one and you remove a behaviour.

    θmerged=θ0+λ·∑iτi

    θ0 is the base model, τi the task vectors, λ a strength you choose. λ = 1 adds every change in full; λ = 1/k for k models is plain averaging. If the tasks use different parts of the network the vectors barely interfere and λ = 1 keeps everything. If they push the same weights in different directions, they interfere: adding them cancels some skills and distorts others, and a smaller λ is the compromise.

    The follow-up methods attack interference. TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023) trims each task vector to its largest changes, elects a sign for each weight where tasks disagree, and merges only the agreeing entries. DARE (Yu et al., 2023) randomly drops a large share of each task vector (often 90% or more) and rescales the rest, on the observation that fine-tuning changes are highly redundant. SLERP interpolates along a curved path on the sphere, used for two models. Evolutionary merging (Sakana AI, 2024) searches automatically over which layers to take from which model and with what weights. The open-source toolkit mergekit made all of these a configuration file, which is why community leaderboards fill with merges.

    The honest caveat from recent evaluations: gains are real but uneven, simple task arithmetic or averaging is a strong baseline that the fancier methods do not reliably beat, and most published merging work uses models of 7 billion parameters or fewer. A study of merging at scale ("What Matters for Model Merging at Scale?", 2024) found merging gets easier when the base model is stronger and when fewer models are combined, with the benefit of cleverness shrinking as the base improves.

    Three practical rules follow. Merge only models fine-tuned from the same base checkpoint. Keep the task vectors small (low learning rates, short fine-tunes, or LoRA adapters, which are already low-rank task vectors). And always re-evaluate the merged model on every skill you wanted, because merging is a search, not a guarantee. Labs use the idea internally too: the Llama 3 report describes averaging models from different fine-tuning runs as a final step. For a bank, a merged model is a new model for governance purposes (G3.2), and its safety behaviour is not the average of its parents' (Part 5).

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These three ideas sit between how models are trained (R1, R2, M1) and what a bank can safely do with the result.

    R2.1 – R2.2 · Scaling lawsWhy data runs out

    Scaling laws say loss falls with more data and compute. When the supply of good human text flattens, synthetic data and efficiency tricks are the two ways to keep going. μP is how those laws are used without paying for every run.

    M1.2 · Pretraining then post-trainingWhere synthetic data is used

    Most synthetic data goes into post-training: instruction tuning, preference data and reasoning traces. Merging also lives here, combining fine-tunes of one pretrained base.

    R3 · Reasoning and verifiersThe verifier is the new information

    Keeping only verified samples is why synthetic reasoning data works and plain self-training does not. See also preference optimisation and AI feedback, which are synthetic-data pipelines by another name.

    F6.3 · AugmentationThe older cousin

    Flipping an image or paraphrasing a sentence is classical augmentation. Synthetic data from a generator is the same move with a far more powerful transformer.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataSynthetic fraud rows

    SMOTE and generated fraud examples are synthetic data for a rare class. The same cautions apply: no new information, leakage if done before the split, and shifted probabilities.

    F1.6 · LikelihoodWhy fitting narrows

    Maximum likelihood on a finite sample under-estimates spread, which is the engine of the collapse formula above.

    P3 · QuantizationSame trick, different stage

    FP8 and FP4 training use the same block-scaling idea as post-training quantisation for serving. See very low-bit quantisation and why inference got cheap.

    R1.1 · Mixture of expertsWhat these recipes train

    DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2 are mixture-of-experts models trained with FP8 and Muon respectively. Efficiency and architecture are chosen together.

    R6.2 · Loss landscapesWhy averaging weights can work

    Merging is only possible because fine-tunes of one base stay in one basin. Different initialisations do not.

    G1.2 · G2.2 · R11 · Poisoning, privacy, memorisationRisks that travel with these methods

    A backdoor in one merged parent is a backdoor in the child. Synthetic data can leak memorised real records (R11.2, G2.2).

    A11 · A14 · Sovereign and Arabic modelsWho actually trains models in the Gulf

    Cheaper training matters most to those who pre-train: national programmes and labs. See also Arabic LLMs, where scarce high-quality Arabic text makes synthetic and translated data both attractive and risky.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Synthetic tabular data for development. Generating look-alike customer or transaction tables so teams can build and test without real records. Useful, but "synthetic" does not mean "anonymous": a generator can reproduce real rows, so privacy still needs testing and a legal view.
    • Training sets for rare cases. Generating examples of rare fraud typologies or complaint types. Check that the generated cases carry information the real ones do not, not merely copies, and evaluate only on real held-out cases.
    • Arabic and bilingual instruction data. Bank-specific question-and-answer sets for an internal copilot (B7.2), written by a stronger model from real policy documents, then reviewed. Translation-based data can sound unnatural, so check dialect and register with native speakers.
    • Evaluation data. Synthetic test questions are good for coverage and bad as the only measure; they share the generator's blind spots (see A4).
    • Vendor conversations. A vendor claiming a "new model for a tenth of the cost" is usually citing some of these three levers. You will rarely run them yourself, but you should know what a claim of "trained in FP8" or "a merge of two open models" implies for quality, licensing and validation.
    • Open-weight merges. Community checkpoints are often merges. Their provenance, licences and safety behaviour are harder to establish than those of a base model.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Collapse is understood as a real but avoidable failure: the live question is how much synthetic data is too much, and for which tasks. Low-precision training has moved from 16 to 8 bits in production and is moving to 4. Muon has gone from blog post to trillion-parameter training run. Merging is a standard tool in open-weight practice with an unsettled theory.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Closed-loop training

      A model is trained on the previous model's output with no fresh real data or filter. Tails vanish first, so headline scores look fine for several rounds.

      Spot itAsk what share of the training mix is real, and what check decided which generated samples were kept. Measure rare-case performance, not just the average.
    • Low diversity

      A million generated samples that are near-duplicates of a few templates. Fine-tuning on them narrows the model's style and coverage.

      Spot itDeduplicate and cluster the set; look at the number of distinct topics, lengths and phrasings. Compare held-out performance against a smaller, more varied sample.
    • Inherited errors and bias

      A teacher's mistakes, its Arabic dialect habits and its stereotypes are copied faithfully into the student, and now look like ground truth.

      Spot itSample and read the generated data. Have a bilingual reviewer check a random slice. Compare accuracy on real, human-labelled test data.
    • Evaluating on synthetic data

      A generator-written test set shares the generator's blind spots, and a student trained on the teacher's data looks good on the teacher's test.

      Spot itAsk whether the test set is real, independent and from the target period. Treat synthetic evaluation as a supplement only.
    • "Synthetic" taken to mean "private"

      A generator trained on customer records can emit near-copies of them.

      Spot itAsk for a nearest-neighbour distance test against the real data and a membership-inference result (R11.2). Don't treat the output as anonymous without a privacy review.
    • Low precision that quietly hurts

      FP8 or FP4 training diverges, or finishes with a small but real quality loss not noticed in the loss curve.

      Spot itCompare against a higher-precision run at small scale. Check downstream benchmarks, not training loss alone. Watch for spikes in loss or in activation maxima, which signal outliers breaking the scaling.
    • Optimiser and hyperparameter claims that don't transfer

      "Muon is 2x faster" or "μP removed tuning" is quoted from a paper whose model size, data or baseline tuning differs from yours.

      Spot itAsk whether the baseline was tuned as hard as the new method, at what scale, and how many seeds.
    • Merging models that shouldn't be merged

      Different base models, different tokenisers, or aggressive fine-tunes drifting far from the base. The merge scores well on one skill and quietly loses another.

      Spot itConfirm the same base checkpoint. Evaluate the merge on every parent's task, including safety and Arabic behaviour, against the parents.
    • Safety and backdoors travel through merges

      A refusal behaviour can be diluted by averaging, and a backdoor in one parent can survive into the child.

      Spot itRe-run safety and red-team evaluations on the merged model. Check the provenance and licence of every parent (G1.2).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “AI-generated training data is useful, and the leading labs use a lot of it, but there is one rule. It has to be added to real data and checked, not fed back in a loop. Models trained on their own output lose the rare cases first, and rare cases are our business: unusual fraud, unusual customers. For us the practical use is generating test and development material and covering gaps, with a human check and a real-data test before anything goes live. The cheaper-training advances mainly matter to the labs; for us they show up as falling prices.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Collapse comes from finite sampling plus refitting, so tails shrink each generation; it's bounded if real data accumulates, and avoided in practice with a real-data share, a verifier or stronger teacher, and diversity checks. For efficiency I'd separate three levers: FP8 or FP4 with block scaling, stochastic rounding and higher-precision accumulation; Muon, which orthogonalises matrix updates; and μP, which lets learning rates tuned on a small model transfer. And merging is task arithmetic on weights from one base: it needs a held-out check for each skill because interference is a measured quantity, not a promise.”
    “Will AI-generated content on the internet ruin future models?”

    It makes careful data curation more valuable, but it is not an inevitable collapse. The risk is training on a pool where synthetic content has replaced real content and nobody filters. Evidence suggests that when real data keeps accumulating, error stays bounded, and labs filter and weight sources. The sound position: some degradation risk, managed by data provenance, deduplication and keeping verified human data in the mix.

    “Can we use synthetic data to fix our fraud class imbalance?”

    It can help if the generated cases add something the real ones don't, and we evaluate only on real held-out data split by time. It adds no new information by itself, shifts predicted probabilities, and leaks if done before the split. I'd test against a plain baseline with a tuned threshold first (F3.5) and treat generated cases as an experiment, not a default.

    “Is synthetic customer data automatically safe to share with a vendor?”

    No. A generator trained on real records can reproduce them. I'd ask what privacy testing was done, such as nearest-neighbour checks and membership-inference attacks, and get the privacy and legal teams to decide. If the generator was trained with differential privacy, the guarantee is stronger, and that is something to ask for specifically.

    “What does 'trained in FP8' or '4-bit' mean for quality?”

    It means arithmetic during training used 8 or 4-bit numbers with scaling, usually keeping a high-precision master copy. For FP8, large labs report quality close to 16-bit at lower cost. For 4-bit, results are promising but newer, and typically some layers stay in higher precision. It is a cost claim about training, separate from how the model is quantised for serving, though the two use related ideas.

    “Why is everyone talking about Muon? Should we care?”

    It's an optimiser that updates whole weight matrices in a balanced way. One lab reported roughly half the compute for the same loss and used it for a trillion-parameter model. It matters to organisations pre-training models. For us it matters indirectly: cheaper training pushes prices down and affects which open models appear. We wouldn't switch our own fine-tuning to it without evidence, partly because mixing optimisers between pretraining and fine-tuning can be awkward.

    “Could we merge our Arabic model and our finance model instead of retraining?”

    Possibly, if both were fine-tuned from the same base and the changes are modest. It's quick and cheap to try, but the result must be evaluated on both skills and on safety, and it is a new model for governance, needing independent validation (G3.2). If it works, good. If not, the fallback is multi-task fine-tuning on the combined data.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A Gaussian is refitted each generation to 25 samples from the previous generation's fit, with no real data. Roughly what is the expected variance after 50 generations?

    (24/25)50 ≈ 0.13 of the original, so the spread is about 36%. The typical single run is lower, because the variance performs a random walk with a downward drift and cannot recover.

    2. Why do early signs of collapse hide in average metrics?

    Sampling under-represents the tails first, and the centre of the distribution barely changes. Averages and common-case accuracy therefore look fine while rare events lose probability. You catch it by measuring performance on rare cases and the diversity of the output.

    3. What is the difference between "replace" and "accumulate", and why does it matter?

    Replace trains each generation only on the previous model's output; accumulate keeps the original real data plus all synthetic batches. Theory and experiments show error grows unboundedly under replace and stays bounded under accumulate. Real-world data is closer to accumulate, but an ever-larger synthetic share can still hurt, so it isn't a free pass.

    4. Why does a verifier make self-generated data useful when raw self-training is not?

    The verifier (unit tests, an answer key, a reward model) injects information the model did not have: it separates good samples from bad. Training only on the good ones moves the model towards correct behaviour rather than towards its own habits. Without it, the loop only repeats what the model already believes.

    5. An FP4 E2M1 block has maximum absolute value 12 (so scale s = 12 ÷ 6 = 2). What is stored for the value 5.2, and what is the error?

    5.2 ÷ 2 = 2.6. The nearest representable values are 2 and 3, so it rounds to 3. Stored value = 3 × 2 = 6. Error 0.8, about 15%. Smaller blocks would give a smaller scale when the block holds no big outlier, and so finer steps.

    6. One tensor has a few values 40 times larger than the rest. Why does one scale per tensor hurt FP4 so much, and what helps?

    The scale is set by the largest value, so ordinary values land at a tiny fraction of the format's range and many round to zero. Smaller blocks (16 or 32) confine the outlier's damage to its own block. Keeping a few sensitive layers in 8 or 16 bits also helps.

    7. In the merge toy with overlap κ = 0.5, what strength λ is best and how much of each skill is kept? What does plain addition (λ = 1) keep?

    Best λ = 1/(1+κ) = 0.667; kept = 1/(1+κ) = 67%. Plain addition keeps 1 − κ = 50%, and averaging (λ = 0.5) keeps 0.75 − 0.25κ = 62.5%.

    8. Why can't you merge two models trained from different random initialisations?

    They end up in different basins of the loss landscape, with neurons arranged in different orders, so the weights don't line up and the midpoint sits on a high-loss ridge. Fine-tunes of one pretrained base stay near each other and in one basin, which is what makes weight arithmetic work. (Methods that permute neurons to align models exist but work less reliably at LLM scale.)
