    Synthetic Data, Cheaper Training and Model Merging

    Why a model trained on its own output forgets the rare cases first, and what keeps that from happening; how 8-bit and 4-bit arithmetic, a different optimiser and transferable hyperparameters cut the cost of training; and how two fine-tuned models can be combined by arithmetic on their weights.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceSynthetic data is safe and powerful when it is checked, diverse and added to real data, and ruinous when it replaces real data in a closed loop; the same trade between cheapness and fidelity runs through low-precision training and model merging, where a little noise is fine and compounding noise is not.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Three ideas answer one question: how do you get a capable model for less than the obvious price (huge human text, huge high-precision clusters, a fresh run per variant)?

    • Synthetic data replaces some human text with text written by another model.
    • Training efficiency makes each step cheaper: lower-precision numbers, a better optimiser, hyperparameters tuned small and reused big.
    • Model merging skips the extra run by adding the weights of models already trained.

    Think of photocopying a photocopy. By the tenth copy the thin lines have vanished and the dark areas have bled together. No single step did much. Each copy lost a little of what was rare on the page, and the loss compounds. A model trained on its own output loses the rare things first: the unusual phrasing, the odd customer, the minority class.

    The counterweight is a student who checks answers against the back of the book and keeps only the correct worked solutions. They improve, though the solutions are their own writing. The difference is not "synthetic versus real". It is whether new information enters the loop: real data, or a verifier that knows when an answer is right.

    Hold onto thisTraining on a model's output is fine. Training on it instead of real data, unchecked, for many generations is not. Every trick here works until errors compound, and the engineering is stopping that.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Synthetic data and model collapse (R2.3)

    Models use synthetic data for three reasons. Good real text is finite and the open web is largely used up. Some data barely exists (long reasoning chains, tool-use transcripts, rare edge cases). And a strong model is a cheap, patient writer of clean explanations and rewrites.

    The risk is model collapse (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024): train generation 1 on real data, sample from it to train generation 2, and repeat. Early collapse loses the tails (low-probability events) while the average looks fine, so headline metrics hide it. Late collapse converges on narrow, repetitive output unrelated to the original data.

    The mechanism is plain sampling. Draw 20 points and you will rarely see anything beyond two standard deviations, so a model fitted to them is slightly narrower than the truth. Sample from that and the next is narrower still. Like a game of telephone, each retelling drops the odd details. For a Gaussian fitted by maximum likelihood (F1.6), with σ the spread and n the samples per generation:

    E[σg2]=(n−1n)g·σ02

    Expected variance after g generations of training only on the previous one's samples. Each generation keeps a fraction (n − 1)/n. With n = 25 and 50 generations, 0.9650 ≈ 0.13, so the spread falls to about 36%. A typical run does worse than this average, because the spread random-walks and can only drift to zero. Misreading to avoid: this is a Gaussian toy. Real models are not Gaussian, but the pressure is the same: sample, refit, and rare things go first.

    Two effects stack on top. Approximation error: no fit is exact, and each generation treats the last one's mistakes as facts. Decoding sharpening: temperature below 1 or top-p cuts the tail before the data is even written.

    Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) separated two regimes. Replace: each generation trains only on the previous output, and error grows without bound. Accumulate: each generation trains on the original real data plus every synthetic batch, and error stays bounded. Real web data piles up like this. Dohmatob et al. (2025, "strong model collapse") warn that a non-trivial synthetic share can still degrade scaling, so accumulation is a safeguard, not a licence.

    The simulation runs the Gaussian toy with replace, accumulate and "mix" (a fixed fraction of fresh real data each generation).

    The practical recipes follow from the toy.

    • Keep real data in the mix. A small steady stream pins the tails. The share needed is an empirical question per task.
    • Add a verifier. Maths can be checked, code run against tests, translation back-translated. Keep only what passes. The verifier is the new information the loop needs, and the engine of reasoning-model training (R3.2).
    • Use a stronger or different teacher. Distilling a larger model injects its knowledge. A model's own raw samples inject nothing.
    • Insist on diversity. Vary prompts, personas and seeds, deduplicate, measure spread. A million near-duplicates are worth far fewer rows.
    • Rewrite rather than invent. Rephrasing real documents keeps facts tied to sources. Inventing "facts" from nothing bakes in hallucinations.

    Successes: Microsoft's Phi series used generated "textbook" text; NVIDIA reported over 98% of Nemotron-4 340B's alignment data was synthetic, made with a reward model and a strong instruction model; open-weight releases widely use distilled reasoning traces.

    Training efficiency: FP8 and FP4, Muon, μP (R2.4)

    Training cost is roughly operations times cost per operation, plus time moving numbers between memory and chip. Three levers cut it without changing the model's design. You need to recognise each and say what it buys, not implement it.

    1. Lower-precision numbers. A 16-bit weight (BF16) can be stored in 8 bits (FP8) or 4 (FP4). Half the width means roughly half the memory and bandwidth, and faster matrix multiplication. The catch is range and resolution, like measuring with a ruler marked only in whole centimetres. A float has an exponent (how big) and a mantissa (how finely). FP8 E4M3 has 4 and 3 bits (max 448, finer); E5M2 has 5 and 2 (max 57,344, wider). FP4 E2M1 has only eight non-negative values: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6.

    So FP4 needs scaling: split the tensor into blocks, divide each by a scale s so its largest value fits the format, round with Q to the nearest representable value, and store the scale plus the tiny numbers.

    xq=s·Q(x/s),s=max|x|qmax

    qmax is the format's largest value (6 for FP4, 448 for E4M3). Smaller blocks track local size, so one outlier ruins fewer neighbours, at the cost of storing more scales. MXFP4 uses blocks of 32 with a power-of-two scale; NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with an FP8 scale plus a per-tensor scale. Worked example: block maximum 12, so s = 2. The value 5.2 becomes 2.6, rounds to 3, and is stored as 6. Error 0.8.

    Training is harder than inference: gradients are small and noisy, and a rounding bias accumulates where random error cancels. Recipes therefore keep a high-precision master copy of the weights, accumulate sums in 32 bits, use stochastic rounding (round up or down at random in proportion to the remainder, so error averages out), and keep a few sensitive layers in 16 bits. DeepSeek-V3 (Dec 2024) showed FP8 training at very large scale. FP4 pretraining is newer and still being proven.

    2. A different optimiser: Muon. AdamW treats each weight as an independent number. Muon (Keller Jordan et al., 2024) treats a whole weight matrix: it replaces the momentum-averaged gradient with the nearest matrix whose singular values are all equal (an "orthogonalised" update), computed cheaply with a few Newton-Schulz iterations. Raw gradient matrices are dominated by a few directions, like a choir where three voices drown the rest. Equalising them spreads learning across the weaker directions. Moonshot AI reported about half the compute for the same loss at scale ("Muon is Scalable for LLM Training", 2025), then trained Kimi K2, a one-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (R1.1), with a variant, MuonClip, that guards against exploding attention logits. Muon is used on hidden weight matrices only; embeddings, the output layer and small vectors stay on AdamW. (F1.3 and F1.8 cover gradient methods.)

    3. Hyperparameter transfer: μP. Under standard setups the best learning rate for a 100-million-parameter model is wrong for a 10-billion one, and tuning at full size is ruinous (F4.5). Like tasting a recipe in a small pot before cooking for a banquet, μP (Greg Yang et al., 2022) rescales initialisation and learning rates layer by layer with width, so the optimal learning rate barely moves as the model grows. Tune a small proxy, reuse the setting. They transferred from 40 million to 6.7 billion parameters, matching a published GPT-3 baseline at a fraction of the tuning cost. Scaling laws (R2.1) tell you how big and how long; μP sets the knobs. It handles width well; depth and non-standard optimisers need extensions, and not every lab uses it.

    Together: low precision makes a step cheaper, Muon makes each step count for more, μP avoids wasted runs. None changes what the model can learn. All change who can afford to train one.

    Model merging (R2.5)

    Say you fine-tune one base on Arabic banking correspondence, one on Python and SQL, one on safety, and want one model that does all three. Retraining on the union needs all data in one place; serving three is costly. Merging weights needs no gradient steps and no data, only minutes on a CPU.

    Why it works: two models trained from different random starts sit in different valleys of the loss landscape (R6.2), and averaging them gives garbage. Fine-tunes of the same pretrained model start in one basin and move only a short way, so the straight line between them stays in a low-loss region (linear mode connectivity). Averaging several fine-tunes of one base often beats any single one: the "model soup" result (Wortsman et al., 2022).

    Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022): a task vector is what fine-tuning changed, τ = θfine-tuned − θbase. Add vectors to add skills; subtract one to remove a behaviour.

    θmerged=θ0+λ·∑iτi

    θ0 is the base, τi the task vectors, λ the strength you choose. λ = 1 adds every change in full; λ = 1/k for k models is plain averaging. If tasks use different parts of the network, the vectors barely interfere and λ = 1 keeps everything. If they push the same weights in different directions, they cancel or distort each other, like two people pulling one rope; a smaller λ is the compromise.

    Follow-up methods attack interference.

    • TIES (Yadav et al., 2023): trim each vector to its largest changes, elect a sign where tasks disagree, merge only agreeing entries.
    • DARE (Yu et al., 2023): randomly drop 90% or more of each vector and rescale, since fine-tuning changes are highly redundant.
    • SLERP: interpolate along a curved path on the sphere, for two models.
    • Evolutionary merging (Sakana AI, 2024): search automatically over which layers to take from which model.

    mergekit turned all of these into config files, which is why community leaderboards fill with merges. The caveat: gains are real but uneven, and simple task arithmetic or averaging is a baseline the fancier methods do not reliably beat. Most published work uses models of 7 billion parameters or fewer. "What Matters for Model Merging at Scale?" (2024) found merging gets easier with a stronger base and fewer models.

    Three rules follow. Merge only models fine-tuned from the same base checkpoint. Keep task vectors small (low learning rates, short fine-tunes, or LoRA adapters, which are already low-rank task vectors). And re-evaluate the merged model on every skill, because merging is a search, not a guarantee. The Llama 3 report describes averaging models from different fine-tuning runs as a final step. For a bank, a merged model is a new model for governance (G3.2), and its safety behaviour is not the average of its parents'.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Closed-loop training

      Training on the previous model's output with no fresh real data or filter. Tails vanish first, so headline scores look fine for several rounds.

      Spot itAsk what share of the mix is real and what check kept each generated sample. Measure rare-case performance, not just the average.
    • Low diversity

      A million samples that are near-duplicates of a few templates narrow the model's style and coverage.

      Spot itDeduplicate and cluster; count distinct topics, lengths and phrasings. Compare held-out performance against a smaller, more varied sample.
    • Inherited errors and bias

      A teacher's mistakes, dialect habits and stereotypes are copied into the student and now look like ground truth.

      Spot itRead a sample. Have a bilingual reviewer check a random slice. Compare accuracy on real human-labelled data.
    • Evaluating on synthetic data

      A generator-written test set shares the generator's blind spots; a student of the teacher's data looks good on the teacher's test.

      Spot itAsk whether the test set is real, independent and from the target period. Use synthetic evaluation only as a supplement.
    • "Synthetic" taken to mean "private"

      A generator trained on customer records can emit near-copies.

      Spot itAsk for a nearest-neighbour distance test and a membership-inference result (R11.2). Do not treat output as anonymous without privacy review.
    • Low precision that quietly hurts

      FP8 or FP4 training diverges, or finishes with a small real quality loss the loss curve hides.

      Spot itCompare against a higher-precision run at small scale. Check downstream benchmarks. Watch for spikes in loss or activation maxima, which signal outliers breaking the scaling.
    • Optimiser and hyperparameter claims that don't transfer

      "Muon is 2x faster" or "μP removed tuning" quoted from a paper whose model size, data or baseline tuning differs from yours.

      Spot itAsk whether the baseline was tuned as hard, at what scale, and with how many seeds.
    • Merging models that shouldn't be merged

      Different bases, different tokenisers, or aggressive fine-tunes far from the base. The merge scores well on one skill and quietly loses another.

      Spot itConfirm the same base checkpoint. Evaluate against the parents on every parent's task, including safety and Arabic behaviour.
    • Safety and backdoors travel through merges

      Averaging can dilute a refusal behaviour, and a parent's backdoor can survive into the child.

      Spot itRe-run safety and red-team evaluations on the merge. Check provenance and licence of every parent (G1.2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A Gaussian is refitted each generation to 25 samples from the previous fit, with no real data. Expected variance after 50 generations?

    (24/25)50 ≈ 0.13 of the original, so spread is about 36%. A typical run is lower: the variance random-walks downward and cannot recover.

    2. Why do early signs of collapse hide in average metrics?

    Sampling drops the tails first and barely moves the centre, so averages look fine while rare events lose probability. Catch it by measuring rare-case performance and output diversity.

    3. What is the difference between "replace" and "accumulate", and why does it matter?

    Replace trains only on the previous model's output; accumulate keeps the real data plus all synthetic batches. Error grows unboundedly under replace and stays bounded under accumulate. Real data is closer to accumulate, but a growing synthetic share can still hurt.

    4. Why does a verifier make self-generated data useful when raw self-training is not?

    The verifier (tests, answer key, reward model) injects information the model lacked by separating good samples from bad. Training on the good ones moves the model towards correct behaviour. Without it, the loop only repeats what the model already believes.

    5. An FP4 E2M1 block has maximum absolute value 12 (so s = 12 ÷ 6 = 2). What is stored for 5.2, and what is the error?

    5.2 ÷ 2 = 2.6, which rounds to 3. Stored 3 × 2 = 6. Error 0.8, about 15%. Smaller blocks without an outlier give a smaller scale and finer steps.

    6. A tensor has a few values 40 times larger than the rest. Why does one scale per tensor hurt FP4, and what helps?

    The scale is set by the largest value, so ordinary values land in a tiny part of the range and many round to zero. Smaller blocks (16 or 32) confine the damage to the outlier's block. Keeping sensitive layers in 8 or 16 bits also helps.

    7. In the merge toy with overlap κ = 0.5, what is the best λ and how much of each skill is kept? What does plain addition (λ = 1) keep?

    Best λ = 1/(1+κ) = 0.667, keeping 1/(1+κ) = 67%. Plain addition keeps 1 − κ = 50%. Averaging (λ = 0.5) keeps 0.75 − 0.25κ = 62.5%.

    8. Why can't you merge two models trained from different random initialisations?

    They sit in different basins with neurons in different orders, so weights do not line up and the midpoint lies on a high-loss ridge. Fine-tunes of one pretrained base stay in one basin. (Neuron-permutation alignment exists but is less reliable at LLM scale.)
