    Scams on Instant Payments and Money Mules

    Part 1

    What the scam is, and who the mule is

    In one sentenceAn authorised push payment (APP) scam is one where the customer is tricked into sending the money themselves, and the money lands in a mule account: an ordinary bank account used to receive and move stolen funds.

    A customer gets a call from someone who says they are from the bank's fraud team. "Move your money to a safe account, now." She opens her own app, passes her own biometric check and presses send. The payment settles in seconds.

    Nothing looks wrong: her device, her login, her behaviour are all genuine. The fraud lives in the story she was told. It is like a burglar who talks the homeowner into opening the door, so the alarm never goes off.

    A money mule may be someone who sold their account for a few hundred dirhams, someone who answered a "receive and forward payments" job advert, or a romance-scam victim themselves. Within minutes the money is split, forwarded and cashed out.

    How it differs from card fraud

    • The session is genuine. Device, location and login signals, the backbone of account-takeover models, all say "normal".
    • The label is contested. Whether the bank owes the victim money depends on rules that differ by country and change over time.
    • Speed removes the safety net. An instant transfer is final in seconds.
    • The criminal is also a customer. The mule has a real account with real identity checks (KYC), and may be a victim too.
    Part 2

    How banks fight it

    There are two fronts, and each is a different problem.

    1. Sending side: score each payment with signals that suggest coaching. Is the customer on a live call? Is remote-access software running? Is it a large first payment to a new payee?
    2. Add friction in proportion to risk: a quiet score, then a tailored warning, a short delay, a staff call, and only then a block.
    3. Receiving side: score accounts for mule behaviour before any complaint arrives, using the shape of their money flows.
    4. Act in minutes: freeze or restrict while money is still in the account.
    5. Feed outcomes back: every investigation and victim report becomes a label for the next model.

    What a mule account looks like

    A salary account is a reservoir: one regular credit, spent slowly across many shops. A mule account is a pipe: credits from many unrelated senders, most of it gone within hours, and very little ordinary spending.

    Three useful single-account features follow from this:

    • Fan-in and fan-out: how many different senders and recipients in a time window.
    • Retention: the share of money still in the account after 24 hours. Near zero means a pipe.
    • Connections: an account is suspicious partly because of who it is linked to. This is where graph methods (accounts as dots, payments as arrows) help.

    Graph methods range from adding graph features to an ordinary model, to spreading suspicion outward from confirmed mules, to graph neural networks.

    Part 3

    How to read the numbers

    Rule of thumbMoney leaves a mule account quickly, and scam victims report late. Value sits at the start of the clock, not the end.
    Money halves every 20 minutes
    After one hour about an eighth is left; after three hours almost nothing. This is a teaching model: real mules range from instant cash-out to holding money for days.
    Victim reports after 3 hours
    By then most of the money has gone. A recall request is clean-up, not control. A receiving bank that scores accounts and acts in minutes recovers far more.
    Model with AUC 0.90, scams are 0.2% of payments
    AUC is a score of how well the model ranks scams above genuine payments; 1 is perfect, 0.5 is a coin flip. At this scam rate most held payments are still genuine, so always ask for precision and alert volume.
    Hold 1,000 of 100,000 payments, catching 120 scams
    Precision is 120 ÷ 1,000 = 12%. Eighty-eight of every hundred holds are genuine customers: fine for a quick warning, too many for a 24-hour block.
    Who pays when a scam succeeds
    This sets the economics. If the bank bears none of the loss, the cheapest policy is to intervene on nothing. If it bears all of it, it should hold far more payments. In the UK, mandatory reimbursement began in October 2024 for Faster Payments and CHAPS, capped at £85,000 and split equally between sending and receiving firms.
    Part 4

    Which approach to use

    ApproachLooks atStrong atWeak at
    Card-fraud modelDevice, login, amountStolen cards, account takeoverScams: the session is genuine
    Sending-side behaviour modelCalls, remote access, new payeeCoached victimsNeeds device data and privacy review
    Mule model on one accountFan-in, fan-out, retentionA strong, simple baselineFlows split across many small accounts
    Graph methodsLinks between accountsNetworks and guilt by associationHubs; scarce, biased labels
    • Strong at (graphs): finding accounts that look ordinary alone but sit next to confirmed mules.
    • Weak at (graphs): the hub problem. Landlords, big merchants and payroll accounts connect to thousands of customers, so suspicion flowing through them flags the innocent. Fix it by splitting a node's suspicion across its neighbours, so a hub with 9 neighbours gives each a ninth.
    • Strong at (friction): stopping a scam before it settles.
    • Weak at (friction): it slows everyone, and over-use trains customers to click through warnings.

    In banking, use graded actions (monitor, hold large credits, ask for extra verification, restrict outflows briefly, escalate), give customers a way to be heard, and test hold rates across nationality and age groups, which can act as proxies.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Training on card-fraud labels and hoping

      The model finds unauthorised fraud and passes scams. Ask how many labelled scams are in training and what recall is on those alone.

    • Letting a hub flood the graph

      Check the highest-degree accounts in the top-scoring list. If a big merchant or landlord is there, suspicion is leaking.

    • Leaky graph evaluation

      A random split puts a mule's neighbours in both training and test, so the model "finds" the mule through its labelled neighbour. Split by time, with each account kept on one side.

    • Trusting the AUC alone

      AUC 0.90 sounds strong, yet at a 0.2% scam rate about 96 in 100 held payments can still be genuine. Report precision and daily alert volume.

    1. Why do device and login features fail against APP scams?

    The real customer uses their own device and credentials, so everything looks normal. Better signals are session context (live call, remote access), payee reputation and the receiving account.

    2. Why is waiting for the victim's report a weak control?

    Victims usually report hours later, after the money has left in several half-lives. Detecting the mule on the receiving side in minutes recovers much more.

    3. What is the difference between a reservoir and a pipe?

    A reservoir (salary account) has one regular credit and slow, varied spending. A pipe (mule) has many unrelated credits and most of it leaves within hours.

    4. If the bank's liability share is zero, what is the best friction threshold?

    Hold nothing: every intervention is pure cost to the bank. Liability rules shape the economics more than any model does.

    5. What is the hub problem, and how is it handled?

    Large merchants or landlords touch thousands of customers, so suspicion spreading through them flags the innocent. Split each node's score across its neighbours and inspect high-degree nodes.
