    Scams on Instant Rails, and the Mules Who Cash Them Out

    Why a scam payment is the one fraud where the customer really did press send, why speed makes it final, what UK reimbursement rules changed for banks, and how graph methods find the mule accounts.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA scam needs the victim's own hand on the button and a mule account to catch the money within minutes, so the bank gets two chances: well-aimed friction on the riskiest payments at the sending end, and spotting the mule at the receiving end.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer gets a call. The caller knows her name, says he is from the bank's fraud team, and tells her to move her money to a "safe account" now. She opens her own app, passes her own biometric check and presses send. The payment settles in seconds.

    Every classic fraud signal is clean: her device, her login, her behaviour. The fraud is the story in her head. This is authorised push payment (APP) fraud: the victim authorises the transfer but is deceived about who gets it or why. Like a burglar who talks the homeowner into opening the door, the alarm never goes off.

    The "safe account" belongs to a money mule: someone who sold their account for a few hundred dirhams, answered a "receive and forward payments" job advert, or is a romance-scam victim themselves. Within minutes the money is split, forwarded and cashed out.

    Hold onto thisSending side: "is this customer being manipulated right now?" Hard, and friction costs innocent people. Receiving side: "is this account behaving like a mule?" A question about flows and structure, where graph methods earn their place.

    Unlike the card fraud in B1:

    • The label is contested. Whether the bank owes money depends on rules that differ by country and change over time.
    • Speed removes the safety net. An instant transfer is final in seconds; recovery depends on how fast the receiving bank acts.
    • The criminal is also a customer. The mule has a real account with real KYC, and may be a victim too.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Anatomy of an APP scam, and why the usual model struggles

    TypeThe story the victim is toldWhat the bank may see
    Impersonation of bank, police or regulator"Your account is compromised, move your money to safety."A large first payment to a new payee, often while on a live call.
    Investment and cryptoA fake platform shows rising "profits" and asks for more.Growing payments to a new exchange-like payee, over weeks.
    Invoice redirection"Our bank details have changed."A normal-looking business payment to a changed beneficiary.
    Purchase and job scamsA fake listing, or a job that asks the "employee" to receive and forward money.Small payments to individuals, or a new customer who starts receiving and forwarding (a mule being recruited).

    A model trained on card fraud does badly here, for three reasons.

    • The session is genuine. Device, location and login features, the backbone of account-takeover models, say "normal".
    • The payment looks like others. Customers do send AED 40,000 to a new individual to buy a car.
    • The best signals are behavioural, and labels are slow. Is she on a call? Is remote-access software running? Victims report hours or days later, or never, which is the late-label problem of B1.

    What speed does: the recovery window

    Instant systems such as the UAE's Aani settle in seconds, around the clock, and money does not stay where it lands. Think of a leaky bucket: the money left in the first account halves every so often (its half-life). If that is 20 minutes, half is left after 20 minutes, about an eighth after an hour, almost nothing after three hours. This is a teaching model: real mules range from instant cash-out to holding money for days.

    The common misreading is to treat a recall request as a control. The victim usually reports hours later, once the "bank" stops calling, so the report arrives when almost nothing is left. The receiving bank can act in minutes if a mule model scores accounts before any complaint arrives.

    Recall requests are clean-up, not control: the value is at the start of the clock. And the receiving bank often holds the better evidence, because it sees the mule's whole pattern while the sender sees one payment.

    Friction, liability and the UK experience

    The sending bank's main tool is targeted friction: a specific warning, a delay, a staff call, a payee confirmation, or a block. Friction is like airport security: it catches threats only by slowing everyone, and if overdone it trains customers to click through. So the design question is a threshold set from costs (F7).

    What changes the costs is who pays when a scam succeeds. In the UK, mandatory reimbursement began in October 2024 for Faster Payments and CHAPS, with a £85,000 cap and the sending and receiving firms splitting the cost equally. When the bank pays, prevention is worth money, and the receiving bank, long without a stake, now has one in finding mules. If the bank pays nothing, the best policy is to intervene on nothing, so liability rules matter more to the economics than any model does.

    This simulation contains no better model. At the same separation, the sensible threshold swings across the whole range as liability and effectiveness change. A modeller who only asks "what is the AUC?" is answering the smaller question: AUC 0.90 sounds strong, yet at a 0.2% scam rate most held payments are still genuine. The effectiveness slider is the least known number in practice; measure it by experiment (warn a random subset, hold another, compare).

    The 50:50 split brings in the receiving bank, who must share mule data and answer for onboarding. I did not find a published CBUAE or SAMA rule mirroring UK reimbursement: ask compliance what binds the bank today.

    Intervention is a ladder, not a switch: quiet score, tailored warning, short delay, call, block. Tie each rung to a risk range so friction stays proportional.

    Money mules: who they are and what they look like

    A mule is an account used to receive and move criminal proceeds. The person behind it may be unwitting (a romance victim or "job" applicant), complicit (takes a fee, often young or short of money), or a seller of their account.

    The behaviour has a shape. A salary account is a reservoir: one regular credit, spent across many merchants. A mule account is a pipe: credits from many unrelated senders, most of it leaving within hours in a few large transfers or cash withdrawals, thin ordinary spending. Useful single-account features:

    • Fan-in and fan-out: distinct senders and recipients in a window.
    • Retention: share of inflow still there after 24 hours. Near 0 means a pipe.

    A boosted-tree model on these is a strong baseline (F4). It misses networks that split flows across many small accounts (smurfing, B2), and that an account is dangerous because of who it is connected to. A graph captures that.

    Graph methods: guilt by association, done carefully

    Accounts are nodes; payments are directed edges with amounts and times. Three methods, in rising order of machinery:

    • Graph features. Known-mule neighbours, size of the connected cluster, devices or phones shared with flagged accounts. Feed them to an ordinary model.
    • Propagation. Like a rumour fading with each retelling: start at confirmed mules and let suspicion flow along edges, weaker at each hop. Personalised PageRank does this.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Each node learns from a summary of its neighbours, so after a few layers it reflects its wider neighbourhood. They can learn shapes hand features miss, but need good labels and care about leakage.

    The shared danger is the hub problem. Landlords, large merchants and payroll accounts connect to thousands of customers; suspicion flooding through them flags the innocent. The fix is to share a node's suspicion across its neighbours, so a hub with 9 neighbours gives each a ninth. Simulation 3 lets you switch this on and off.

    What the toy hides:

    • Labels are scarce and biased. You know only mules someone investigated or a victim named (F2.6). Feed every investigation outcome back.
    • Evaluation. Precision at the number of accounts analysts can review per day, tested forward in time with entities grouped (F3.5). Random edge splits leak badly.

    What happens after a mule score: action and fairness

    Freezing a genuine customer who is receiving a house deposit is a serious harm; leaving a mule alone is the harm of Simulation 1. Use graded actions: monitor, hold credits above a limit, require extra verification, restrict outflows briefly, then escalate to investigators. Give the customer a route to be heard and a fast way to undo a wrong decision. Nationality and age can act as proxies, so test hold rates across groups (G2).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training on card-fraud labels and hoping

      The model finds unauthorised fraud and passes scams, because the session is genuine.

      Spot itAsk how many labelled scams are in training, and recall on those alone.
    • A hub floods the graph

      Suspicion flows through a large merchant or landlord and lights up hundreds of innocent customers.

      Spot itLook at the highest-degree nodes in the top-scoring list.
    • Leakage in graph evaluation

      Random splits put a mule's neighbours in train and test, so the model "finds" the mule through its labelled neighbour.

      Spot itAsk for a time-ordered split with entities grouped.
    • Believing the AUC

      AUC 0.90 sounds strong, but at a 0.2% scam rate about 96 in 100 held payments are still genuine (Simulation 2).

      Spot itAlways ask for precision and alert volume at the operating point.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why do device and login features, strong for account takeover, fail for APP scams?

    The real account holder uses their own device and credentials, so those features look normal. The deception is in the customer's mind. Useful signals are session context (call, remote access), payee reputation and the receiving account.

    2. Why is waiting for the victim's report a weak control against mule cash-out, and what does a shorter half-life do to that?

    Each half-life halves what is still in the first account, and victims usually report hours later, after several halvings. The faster the mule moves money, the less a late report is worth. Receiving-side detection in minutes recovers far more.

    3. 100,000 payments, 0.2% scams. A threshold holds 1,000 payments and catches 120 scams. What is the precision, and what does it mean for the queue?

    120 ÷ 1,000 = 12%. Eighty-eight of every hundred holds are genuine customers. Acceptable for a quick warning, not for a 24-hour block.

    4. What happens to the best friction threshold if the bank's liability share is zero? If it rises to 100%?

    At zero the bank bears no loss, so any intervention is pure cost and the best policy holds nothing. At 100% every missed scam costs the bank in full, so the best threshold falls and more payments are held.

    5. What is the hub problem in mule graphs, and how is it handled?

    Large merchants or landlords connect to thousands of customers, so suspicion spreading through them flags the innocent. Share each node's score across its neighbours, cap known hubs, and inspect high-degree nodes in the results.

    6. Why is a random split wrong for evaluating a mule model on a graph?

    A mule's neighbours and earlier behaviour land in both train and test, so the model relies on information it will not have for a new account. Use time-ordered splits with entities grouped.

    7. A report says "88% of APP fraud was reimbursed" and another says "97%". Can both be right?

    Yes, with different denominators (eligible against in-scope claims) or date bases. Always ask what the percentage is a percentage of.
