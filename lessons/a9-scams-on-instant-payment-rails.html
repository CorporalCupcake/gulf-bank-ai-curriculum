    Scams on Instant Rails, and the Mules Who Cash Them Out

    Why a scam payment is the one fraud where the customer really did press send, why speed turns a bad decision into a lost one, what UK reimbursement rules changed for banks, and how graph methods find the accounts that receive and move the money.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOn instant rails the scammer needs the victim's own hand on the button and a mule account to catch the money within minutes, so the bank gets two chances: add well-aimed friction to the few riskiest payments at the sending end, and spot and freeze the mule at the receiving end. Both are decided by thresholds, costs and graph structure, not by "better AI".

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer gets a call. The caller knows her name, says he is from the bank's fraud team, and says her account is under attack. To keep her money safe she must move it to a "safe account" now. She opens her own app, passes her own biometric check and presses send. The payment settles in seconds.

    Every classic fraud signal is clean: her device, her login, her behaviour. The fraud is the story in her head. This is authorised push payment (APP) fraud: the victim authorises the transfer but is deceived about who gets it or why. It is like a burglar who talks the homeowner into opening the door: the alarm never goes off.

    The "safe account" belongs to a money mule: a student who sold their account for a few hundred dirhams, someone who answered a "receive and forward payments" job advert, or a romance-scam victim. Within minutes the money is split, forwarded and cashed out. Two or three hops later, nobody gets it back.

    Hold onto thisScams give the bank two decision points. Sending side: "is this customer being manipulated right now?" Hard, and friction costs innocent people. Receiving side: "is this account behaving like a mule?" A question about flows and structure, where graph methods earn their place.

    Three things make this unlike the card fraud in B1.

    • The label is contested. Card fraud is "unauthorised"; a scam is "authorised but induced". Whether the bank owes money depends on rules that differ by country and change over time.
    • Speed removes the safety net. Cards can be disputed for weeks; an instant transfer is final in seconds. Recovery depends on how fast the receiving bank acts.
    • The criminal is also a customer. The mule has a real account with real KYC, and may be a victim too.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Anatomy of an APP scam, and why the usual model struggles

    TypeThe story the victim is toldWhat the bank may see
    Impersonation of the bank, police or a regulator"Your account is compromised, move your money to safety."A customer on a live call, often making a large first payment to a new payee.
    Investment and cryptoA fake platform shows rising "profits" and asks for more deposits.Growing payments to a new exchange-like payee, over weeks.
    Romance and friendshipA long online relationship, then an emergency needing money.Repeated, often modest payments. The relationship leaves no trace in the data.
    Invoice and payee redirection"Our bank details have changed."A normal-looking business payment to a changed beneficiary.
    Purchase and job scamsA fake listing, or a job that asks for fees or for the "employee" to receive and forward money.Small payments to individuals, or a new customer who starts receiving and forwarding (a mule being recruited).

    A model trained on card fraud does badly here, for four reasons.

    • The session is genuine. Device, location and login features, the backbone of account-takeover models, say "normal".
    • The payment looks like others. Customers do send AED 40,000 to a new individual to buy a car. Separation is only about 1.8 standard deviations in Simulation 2, far weaker than for stolen-card fraud.
    • Labels are slow and uneven. Victims take hours or days to realise, or never report from embarrassment. This is the late-label problem of B1 and F3.5, sharper.
    • The best signals are behavioural. Is she on a call? Is a remote-access app running? Is she copying digits from elsewhere or editing the amount repeatedly? These come from device intelligence (A9.5) and the destination's reputation, which is the mule problem below.

    Synthetic voices and fluent generated scripts make the story cheaper to scale (A3), but the payment-side picture is unchanged.

    What speed does: the recovery window

    Instant systems such as the UAE's Aani (run by Al Etihad Payments, a Central Bank subsidiary) settle in seconds, around the clock. Money does not stay where it lands. Think of a leaky bucket: the share left in the first account decays with a half-life h, and after t minutes the share left is

    R(t)=2−t/h

    R is the share still recoverable from the first account; t is minutes since the payment; h is the half-life of funds there. With h = 20 minutes: half left after 20 minutes, 12.5% after an hour, 0.2% after three hours. A teaching model, not a measured law: real mules range from instant cash-out to holding money for days.

    Read it as a clock: each half-life halves what you can still freeze, so every extra half-life of delay costs the same fraction, not the same amount. The common misreading is to treat a recall request as a control. The victim usually reports hours later, once the "bank" stops calling, so the report arrives when almost nothing is left. The receiving bank can act in minutes if a mule model scores accounts before any complaint arrives.

    Two conclusions. Recall requests are clean-up, not control: the value is at the start of the clock. And the receiving bank often holds the better evidence, because it sees the mule's whole pattern while the sender sees one payment.

    Friction, liability and the UK experience

    The sending bank's main tool is targeted friction: a specific warning ("the bank will never ask you to move money"), a delay, a staff call, a payee confirmation, or a block. Friction is like airport security: it catches threats only by also slowing everyone, and if overdone it trains customers to click through. So the design question is the F7 one (F7): set a threshold from costs.

    What changes the costs is who pays when a scam succeeds. In the UK, mandatory reimbursement began in October 2024 for Faster Payments and CHAPS. The Payment Systems Regulator (PSR) set a maximum claim of £85,000 and made the sending and receiving firms split the cost equally. Reimbursement is the default, with exceptions such as first-party fraud and gross customer negligence. Before that, voluntary-code reimbursement was patchy; the PSR's first-year figures show about 88% of eligible claims repaid, with fraud losses falling. When the bank pays, prevention is worth money, and the receiving bank, long without a stake, now has one in finding mules.

    Let the bank's share of each scam loss be s, the loss per scam L, the cost per intervened payment c, and e the share of caught scams that the intervention actually stops (a warned victim may still pay). The expected daily cost is:

    cost=s·L·(nscams−e·caught)+c·interventions

    First term: loss the bank still bears. Second: cost of friction. Lowering the threshold raises "caught" and "interventions" together. If s is zero, the best policy is to intervene on nothing, so liability rules matter more to the economics than any model does.

    This simulation contains no better model. At the same separation, the sensible threshold swings across the whole range as liability and effectiveness change. A modeller who only asks "what is the AUC?" is answering the smaller question. AUC 0.90 sounds strong, yet at a 0.2% scam rate most held payments are still genuine. The effectiveness slider is the least known number in practice; measure it by experiment (warn a random subset, hold another, compare), as in F3.6.

    Other lessons from the UK:

    • Incentives work through investment. Reports point to more prevention spend and falling APP losses after the rules.
    • The 50:50 split brings in the receiving bank. Banks must share mule data and answer for onboarding and monitoring.
    • Side effects are real. Delayed legitimate payments and de-risking are debated; check regulator evidence, not assertions.
    • Definitions matter. Merchant disputes are carved out from scams; careless labels mix them in training data.

    I did not find a published CBUAE or SAMA rule mirroring UK reimbursement. Do not assume one exists or not: ask compliance what binds the bank today.

    Intervention is a ladder, not a switch: quiet score, tailored warning, short delay, call, block. Tie each rung to a probability range so friction stays proportional and each rung's effect can be measured. A customer who sees a specific warning and proceeds anyway is also telling you something.

    Money mules: who they are and what they look like

    A mule is an account used to receive and move criminal proceeds. The person behind it is one of:

    • Unwitting: a romance victim or "job" applicant who thinks they process payments for a company.
    • Complicit: knows, or chooses not to ask, and takes a fee. Often young or short of money.
    • Sold or rented: a real-identity account handed over, sometimes with app and phone.
    • Synthetic or stolen identity: built to be a mule from day one (A9.3).

    The behaviour has a shape. A salary account is a reservoir: one regular credit, spent across many merchants. A mule account is a pipe: pass-through, with a sudden rise in credits from many unrelated senders, most of it leaving within hours in a few large transfers or cash withdrawals, and thin ordinary spending. Useful single-account features:

    • Fan-in and fan-out: distinct senders and recipients in a window, and their ratio.
    • Retention ratio: share of inflow still there after 24 hours. Near 0 means a pipe; a salary account sits high.
    • Velocity and burstiness: credits per hour against the account's own history.
    • Age and change events: new account, new device, changed phone number, first credit after months of dormancy.
    • Counterparty quality: how many senders were reported as scam victims, how many recipients are flagged.

    A boosted-tree model on these is a strong baseline (F4). It misses two things. Networks stay under single-account thresholds by splitting flows across many small accounts (smurfing, B2). And it misses that an account is dangerous because of who it is connected to. A graph captures that.

    Graph methods: guilt by association, done carefully

    Accounts are nodes; payments are directed edges with amounts and times. Three methods, in rising order of machinery:

    • Graph features. Per account: known-mule neighbours, connected-component size, devices or phones shared with flagged accounts, position on a short path from a known victim to a known cash-out. Feed them to an ordinary model. Often the best return for effort.
    • Propagation. Like a rumour spreading and fading with each retelling: start at confirmed mules and let suspicion flow along edges, weaker at each hop. Personalised PageRank (random walk with restart) does this; a node's score is the long-run chance a walker who keeps restarting at confirmed mules is standing there. Simulation 3 uses a few iterations.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Each node collects its neighbours' representations, combines them (mean, sum or attention) and updates its own. After two or three layers a node reflects its two- or three-hop neighbourhood, and a classifier predicts mule or not. GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) is the usual template since it samples neighbours and scales to millions of nodes. GNNs can learn shapes hand features miss, but need good labels and care about leakage.

    The shared danger is the hub problem. Landlords, large merchants, payroll and exchange accounts connect to thousands of customers; suspicion flooding through them flags the innocent. Divide a node's outgoing suspicion by its degree, so a hub with 9 neighbours gives each a ninth. Simulation 3 lets you switch this on and off.

    What the toy hides:

    • Direction and time. A mule is inflow then outflow within hours. Real graphs are directed and temporal (time-respecting paths, temporal GNNs).
    • Labels are scarce and biased. You know only mules someone investigated or a victim named, shaped by old rules (F2.6). Semi-supervised and positive-unlabelled methods help, as does feeding every investigation outcome back.
    • One bank sees one slice. You see your customers and the first hop; later hops are at other banks. Cross-bank sharing is the frontier (A9.4).
    • Evaluation. Precision at the number of accounts analysts can review per day, tested forward in time with entities grouped (F3.5). Random edge splits leak badly.
    • Benchmarks are thin. Real bank graphs are private. Researchers use the Elliptic bitcoin dataset and IBM's synthetic AML generator (Altman et al., 2023). Results transfer only partly.

    What happens after a mule score: action and fairness

    Freezing a genuine customer who is receiving a house deposit is a serious harm; leaving a mule alone is the harm of Simulation 1. Use graded actions: monitor, hold credits above a limit, require extra verification, restrict outflows for a short review, then escalate to investigators and, if warranted, a suspicious activity report. Keep audit logs, a route for the customer to be heard, and a fast way to undo a wrong decision. Nationality, age and employer can act as proxies, so test hold rates across groups (G2). Ask compliance what the bank may lawfully say to a customer or counterparty bank about a suspicion.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Training on card-fraud labels and hoping

      The model finds unauthorised fraud and passes scams, because the session is genuine.

      Spot itAsk how many labelled scams are in training, and recall on those alone.
    • Counting reimbursed customers as "no fraud"

      A refused or never-raised claim is treated as a good payment.

      Spot itCheck how the label was built (reported, confirmed, reimbursed, blocked?). Plot labelled rate by week for the late-label effect.
    • Friction that trains customers to click through

      The same generic warning on every payment fades and stops carrying information.

      Spot itTrack proceed-after-warning rate by warning type and over time. Near 100% means wallpaper.
    • A hub floods the graph

      Suspicion flows through a large merchant or landlord and lights up hundreds of innocent customers.

      Spot itLook at the highest-degree nodes in the top-scoring list. Check degree normalisation; exclude or cap known hubs.
    • Leakage in graph evaluation

      Random splits put a mule's neighbours in train and test, so the model "finds" the mule through its labelled neighbour.

      Spot itAsk for a time-ordered split with entities grouped, and whether test-node labels fed neighbour features.
    • Freezing victims

      A romance victim used as a mule is treated as a criminal; a genuine house-deposit recipient is frozen.

      Spot itReview freeze precision, time to release, complaints and hold rates by customer group.
    • Displacement

      Tightening one rail pushes scammers to another channel or to a less protected bank, while dashboards show progress.

      Spot itWatch total losses across rails and banks where data allows.
    • Quoting reimbursement figures without a basis

      "88% reimbursed" and "97% reimbursed" can both be true for different populations and dates.

      Spot itAsk for the denominator (all, eligible, in-scope claims) and the date basis (transaction or claim closure).
    • Believing the AUC

      AUC 0.90 sounds strong, but at a 0.2% scam rate about 96 in 100 held payments are still genuine (Simulation 2).

      Spot itAlways ask for precision and alert volume at the operating point.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why do device and login features, strong for account takeover, fail for APP scams?

    The real account holder uses their own device and credentials, so those features look normal. The deception is in the customer's mind. Useful signals are session context (call, remote access), payee reputation and the receiving account.

    2. Mule funds have a half-life of 30 minutes. What share is left after 2 hours, and what does that say about waiting for a victim report?

    2 hours is 4 half-lives: 2−4 = 1/16 = 6.25%. A report at 2 hours or later finds about a sixteenth in the first account. Receiving-side detection in minutes recovers far more.

    3. 100,000 payments, 0.2% scams. A threshold holds 1,000 payments and catches 120 scams. What is the precision, and what does it mean for the queue?

    120 ÷ 1,000 = 12%. Eighty-eight of every hundred holds are genuine customers. Acceptable for a quick warning, not for a 24-hour block. It depends on cost per intervention against avoided loss.

    4. In the cost formula, what happens to the best threshold if the bank's liability share is zero? If it rises to 100%?

    At zero the bank bears no loss, so any intervention is pure cost and the best policy holds nothing. At 100% every missed scam costs the bank in full, so the best threshold falls and more payments are held. Liability rules shape behaviour as much as model quality.

    5. What is the hub problem in mule graphs, and how is it handled?

    Large merchants or landlords connect to thousands of customers, so suspicion spreading through them flags the innocent. Divide outgoing score by degree, cap or exclude known hubs, and inspect high-degree nodes in the results.

    6. Why is a random split wrong for evaluating a mule model on a graph?

    A mule's neighbours and earlier behaviour land in both train and test, so the model relies on information it will not have for a new account. Use time-ordered splits with entities grouped, and compute neighbour features only from information available at the prediction date.

    7. A report says "88% of APP fraud was reimbursed" and another says "97%". Can both be right?

    Yes, with different denominators (eligible against in-scope claims) or date bases (scam date against claim-closure date). Always ask what the percentage is a percentage of.
