    Scams on Instant Rails, and the Mules Who Cash Them Out

    Why a scam payment is the one fraud where the customer really did press send, why speed turns a bad decision into a lost one, what UK reimbursement rules changed for banks, and how graph methods find the accounts that receive and move the money.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOn instant rails the scammer needs the victim's own hand on the button and a mule account to catch the money within minutes, so the bank has two chances: slow down the right few payments at the sending end with well-aimed friction, and spot and freeze the receiving account at the other, and both are decided by thresholds, costs and graph structure rather than by "better AI".

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer gets a call. The caller knows her name, says he is from the bank's fraud team, and says her account is under attack. To keep her money safe she must move it to a "safe account" now. She opens her own banking app, on her own phone, passes her own biometric check, types in the details and presses send. The payment settles in seconds.

    Every classic fraud signal is clean. The device is hers. The login is hers. The behaviour is hers. Nobody stole a credential. What is fraudulent is the story in her head, and a model that reads only the payment can see almost none of it. This is authorised push payment (APP) fraud: the victim authorises the transfer, but has been deceived about who gets it or why.

    The second half of the story happens at the other end. The "safe account" belongs to someone who is not the scammer: a student who sold their account for a few hundred dirhams, a person who answered a "work from home, receive and forward payments" advert, or a victim of a romance scam who is now being used to move money. These are money mules. Within minutes the money is split, forwarded and cashed out or converted. Once it is two or three hops away, no one can get it back.

    Hold onto thisScams give the bank two separate decision points. At the sending side the question is "is this customer being manipulated right now?", which is hard and costs friction on innocent people. At the receiving side the question is "is this account behaving like a mule?", which is a question about structure and flows and is where graph methods earn their place.

    Before the mechanics, three things that make this unlike the card-fraud problem in B1.

    • The label is contested. A card fraud is "unauthorised". A scam is "authorised but induced". Whether the bank owes the customer money depends on rules, not on the data, and the rules differ by country and change over time.
    • Speed removes the safety net. Card payments can be disputed for weeks. An instant transfer is final in seconds, so recovery depends on how quickly the receiving bank acts, not on a dispute process.
    • The criminal is also a customer. The mule has a real account with real KYC. Treating them as an enemy and as a possible victim at the same time is part of the job.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Anatomy of an APP scam, and why the usual model struggles

    The main families, as the banking industry groups them:

    TypeThe story the victim is toldWhat the bank may see
    Impersonation of the bank, police or a regulator"Your account is compromised, move your money to safety."A customer on a live phone call, often making a large first payment to a new payee.
    Investment and crypto scamsA fake platform or advisor shows rising "profits" and asks for more deposits.A series of growing payments to a new exchange-like payee, over weeks.
    Romance and friendshipA long online relationship, then an emergency needing money.Repeated payments, often modest at first. The relationship leaves no trace in the data.
    Invoice and payee redirection"Our bank details have changed" on a supplier or property invoice.A correct-looking business payment, but to a changed beneficiary.
    Purchase and job scamsA listing that is not real, or a job that asks for fees or for the "employee" to receive and forward money.Small payments to individuals, or a new customer who starts receiving and forwarding (a mule being recruited).

    Why does a model trained on card fraud do badly here?

    • The session is genuine. Device, location and login features, the backbone of account-takeover models, say "normal".
    • The payment looks like others. Customers do sometimes send AED 40,000 to a new individual: buying a car, paying a deposit. Separation between scam and genuine payments is modest. In Simulation 2 it is a gap of about 1.8 standard deviations, much weaker than for stolen-card fraud.
    • Labels are slow and uneven. A victim may take hours or days to realise, or never report from embarrassment. This is the delayed and selective labelling problem of B1 and F3.5 in a sharper form.
    • The best signals are behavioural and contextual. Is the customer on a call while making the payment? Is a remote-access app running? Is she hesitating, copying digits from another source, or changing the amount several times? Those come from device and behaviour intelligence, see A9.5, and from the destination's reputation, which is the mule problem below.

    Newer scam tactics use synthetic voices and video to impersonate relatives or officials, and the content that persuades the victim can be written by language models in fluent Arabic or English. See A3. That makes the "story" cheaper to scale but does not change the payment-side picture.

    What speed does: the recovery window

    Instant payment systems such as the UAE's Aani, run by Al Etihad Payments, a subsidiary of the Central Bank, settle in seconds, around the clock. That is the point of them. The consequence for fraud is that the money does not stay where it landed. If the mule moves funds on at some typical pace, the share still sitting in the first account decays roughly exponentially with time. A simple and useful model: the funds have a half-life h, and after t minutes the share left is

    R(t)=2−t/h

    R is the share still recoverable from the first receiving account; t is minutes since the payment; h is the half-life of funds there. With h = 20 minutes, after 20 minutes half is left, after an hour 12.5%, after three hours 0.2%. The rest has moved to second-hop accounts, where each further hop costs another round of requests between banks. This is a teaching model, not a measured law; real mules vary from instant cash-out to holding money for days.

    The victim's report time is usually the weak link. People realise they were scammed when the "bank" stops calling or a relative asks questions: hours later. So what can the bank do within minutes? The receiving bank can notice that an unusual inflow hit a high-risk account and freeze or hold it before it moves. That needs a mule-detection model that scores accounts before any complaint arrives. The next simulation shows how large the difference is.

    Two practical conclusions. First, recall requests are a clean-up step, not a control; the value is at the beginning of the clock. Second, the receiving bank often holds the better evidence. It sees the mule account's whole pattern, and the sending bank sees only one payment.

    Friction, liability and the UK experience

    At the sending bank, the main tool is targeted friction: a confirmation screen with a specific warning ("the bank will never ask you to move money"), a delay of an hour or a day for the riskiest payments, a call from staff, a requirement to confirm the payee, or a blocked payment. All of them cost something. Friction hurts genuine customers (the car dealer who needs the deposit now), burns call-centre time and, if overdone, trains customers to click through. So the design question is the same as in F7: set a threshold from costs.

    What changes the costs is who pays when a scam succeeds. In the UK, mandatory reimbursement began in October 2024 for Faster Payments and CHAPS transfers. The Payment Systems Regulator (PSR) set a maximum claim of £85,000 and required the sending and receiving firms to share the cost equally. Reimbursement is the default, with exceptions such as first-party fraud and gross negligence by the customer. Before the rules, reimbursement under a voluntary code was patchy; the PSR's figures show the share of claims repaid rising to around the high 80s per cent in the first year, with fraud losses falling (Part 4). The intent was to shift the incentive: when the bank pays, preventing scams becomes worth money, and the receiving bank, long without a stake, now has one in finding mule accounts.

    Let the bank's share of each scam loss be s. With a scam loss of L, a cost c per intervened payment, a share e of caught scams that the intervention actually stops (a warned victim may still pay), the expected daily cost at a threshold is:

    cost=s·L·(nscams−e·caught)+c·interventions

    The first term is the loss the bank still bears; the second is the cost of friction. Lowering the threshold increases "caught" and "interventions" together. If s is zero, the optimum is to intervene on nothing, because the bank bears no loss: any friction is pure cost. That is not an argument; it is why liability rules matter more to the economics than any model does.

    Notice what this simulation does not contain: a better model. At the same separation, the sensible threshold swings across the whole range as liability and effectiveness change. That is why a modeller who only asks "what is the AUC?" is answering the smaller question. The effectiveness slider is also the least known number in practice. It can only be measured by experiment: warn a random subset, hold another, compare outcomes, with the experiment design of F3.6.

    The UK experience also taught several things beyond money.

    • Reimbursement did not simply raise costs. Industry reporting points to more investment in prevention and a fall in APP losses after the rules (Part 4). The incentive is the mechanism.
    • The 50:50 split was designed to bring the receiving bank in. Banks now have to share data on mule accounts and be accountable for their onboarding and monitoring.
    • Over-friction and de-risking are real side effects. Reports of delayed legitimate payments and of banks becoming wary of some customers are part of the debate; check current regulator evidence rather than assertions on either side.
    • Definitions matter. Claims about "customer-initiated fraud" versus "genuine disputes with a merchant" are carved out; careless label design can mix them up in training data.

    As of this writing I did not find a published CBUAE or SAMA rule that mirrors the UK reimbursement model. Do not assume one exists, or that one does not: ask compliance what binds the bank today, and what industry conduct standards say about scam losses.

    The intervention is a menu, not a switch: a quiet score, then a tailored on-screen warning, then a short delay, then a call, then a block. Pairing each rung with a probability range keeps friction proportional and lets you measure each rung's effect. A warning is also a model input to later fraud decisions, since a customer who sees a specific warning and goes ahead anyway is telling you something.

    Money mules: who they are and what they look like

    A mule is an account used to receive and move criminal proceeds. The person behind it falls somewhere on a spectrum:

    • Unwitting. A romance-scam victim or a "job" applicant who thinks they are processing payments for a company.
    • Complicit. Someone who knows, or chooses not to ask, and takes a fee. Often young or short of money.
    • Sold or rented accounts. Accounts opened with real identities and handed over, sometimes with the app and phone.
    • Synthetic or stolen identities. Accounts built to be mules from day one, see A9.3.

    Whatever the origin, the behaviour has a shape. An ordinary salary account receives one regular credit and spends it across many merchants. A mule account tends to show pass-through: a sudden rise in credits from many unrelated senders, most of the money leaving within hours, often in a small number of large outward transfers or cash withdrawals, with a thin history of ordinary spending before or after. Useful single-account features:

    • Fan-in and fan-out: number of distinct senders and recipients in a window, and their ratio.
    • Retention ratio: share of inflow still in the account after 24 hours. Low means pass-through.
    • Velocity and burstiness: credits per hour compared with the account's own history.
    • Account age and change events: new account, new device, a changed phone number, the first credit after months of dormancy.
    • Counterparty quality: how many senders have been reported as scam victims, how many recipients are themselves flagged.

    Those catch a good share of mules, and a boosted-tree model on them is a strong baseline (F4). They miss two things. Mule networks stay under single-account thresholds by splitting flows across many small accounts (smurfing, the typologies of B2). And they miss that an account is dangerous because of who it is connected to, not what it does. That is what a graph captures.

    Graph methods: guilt by association, done carefully

    Treat accounts as nodes and payments as edges (directed, with amounts and times). Three kinds of method, in rising order of machinery:

    • Graph features. For each account, compute neighbourhood statistics: how many neighbours are known mules, size of its connected component, shared devices or phone numbers with flagged accounts, whether it lies on a short path between a known scam victim and a known cash-out. Feed these to an ordinary model. Often the best return for effort.
    • Propagation. Start from confirmed mules and let suspicion flow along edges, weakening at each hop. Personalised PageRank, or "random walk with restart", does exactly this. The score of a node is the long-run chance that a walker who keeps restarting at the confirmed mules is standing there. Simulation 3 uses a few iterations of it.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Each node starts with its features. In each layer, it collects its neighbours' representations, combines them (mean, sum or attention) and updates its own. After two or three layers a node's representation reflects its two- or three-hop neighbourhood, and a classifier on top predicts mule or not. GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) is the usual template because it samples neighbours and scales to millions of nodes. They can learn patterns that hand features miss, such as particular fan-in, fan-out shapes, but need good labels and care about leakage.

    The danger shared by all of them is the hub problem. Landlords, large merchants, payroll accounts and exchange accounts connect to thousands of customers. If suspicion floods through a hub, it flags innocent people. The standard remedy is to divide a node's outgoing suspicion by its degree, so a hub with 9 neighbours gives each only a ninth of it. Simulation 3 lets you switch this on and off.

    Things this toy hides, which matter in a real build.

    • Direction and time. Money flows one way, and a mule is characterised by inflow then outflow within hours. Real graphs are directed and temporal, and temporal GNNs and time-respecting paths exist for this reason.
    • Labels are scarce and biased. You know the mules that someone investigated or a victim named. That set is tiny against millions of accounts and is shaped by old rules (F2.6). Semi-supervised approaches, positive-unlabelled learning and anomaly scores help. So does feeding every investigation outcome back.
    • The graph is within one bank. The sender sees their own customers and the first hop; the next hops are at other banks. Cross-bank sharing, via consortia or privacy-preserving methods, is the frontier (A9.4).
    • Evaluation. Precision at the number of accounts analysts can review per day, tested forward in time with entities grouped, as in F3.5. Random edge splits leak badly in graphs.
    • Public benchmarks are thin. Real bank graphs are private. Researchers use the Elliptic bitcoin dataset (labelled illicit and licit transactions) and synthetic generators such as IBM's AML data (Altman et al., 2023). Results on them transfer only partly.

    What happens after a mule score: action and fairness

    A high mule score creates a hard decision. A freeze on a genuine customer who is receiving a house deposit is a serious harm. A mule account left alone is the harm of Simulation 1. Good designs use graded actions: monitor, hold incoming credits above a limit, require extra verification, restrict outflows for a short review window, then escalate to investigators and, if warranted, a suspicious activity report through the compliance route. Keep audit logs, a route for the customer to be heard, and a rule for undoing a wrong decision quickly. Features such as nationality, age and employer can act as proxies; test hold rates across groups before deploying (G2). Be cautious about what the bank can lawfully say to a customer or a counterparty bank about a suspicion; ask compliance rather than assume.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Scams sit where fraud, financial crime, customer protection and graph analytics meet.

    B1 · Card and payment fraudThe baseline this extends

    Everything about scoring, thresholds and alert capacity applies. The new problem is that the session is genuine and the label is contested.

    B1 · Delayed labelsSlow, selective labels

    Victims report late and some never do. Embargo recent weeks and keep a random review sample so you do not only learn from what you already flagged.

    F3.5 · F7 · Imbalance and thresholdsRare events, cost-based cuts

    Simulation 2 is the F3.5 argument with liability added: the threshold is a business decision, and precision is low whatever you do.

    B2 · AML monitoringTypologies and rules

    Mule detection is AML monitoring aimed at a specific typology. Rules for pass-through and fan-in are still the first layer.

    F9.2 · Graph analyticsGraphs and graph ML

    Centrality, communities, PageRank and message passing in the general case. This lesson applies them to one financial-crime problem.

    A9.3 · A9.4 · Synthetic identity and sharingWhere mules come from, and how to see further

    Some mule accounts are fabricated at onboarding; cross-bank signals need privacy-preserving collaboration.

    A9.5 · Device intelligenceThe session signals

    Call detection, remote access and behavioural cues are the strongest sending-side evidence for coached victims.

    A3 · DeepfakesSynthetic voices in the story

    Cloned voices and fluent generated scripts make the persuasion cheaper to run. The control points at the payment and the mule stay the same.

    G2 · Fairness and contestabilityHarm from false positives

    Freezing accounts and delaying payments land on real people. Group-level hold rates and an appeal route belong in the design.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Retail digital banking. Instant-transfer journeys with warnings, delays and call-back flows; the fraud-ops team wants a precision target they can staff.
    • Financial crime operations. A mule-account queue, investigator workflow and suspicious activity reporting. A graph view of linked accounts is often what an investigator asks for first.
    • Onboarding. New-account risk features that anticipate mule use: device overlap with known mules, applicant behaviour, age and employer mismatch.
    • Customer protection and complaints. Decisions about whether a payment was a scam, how the bank responds, and what it tells customers. Arabic and English scripts for calls and warnings need testing for clarity.
    • Regulatory and industry engagement. Central bank and industry-body expectations about scam controls and data sharing. The analyst who can model the economics (Simulation 2) is useful in that conversation.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Training on card-fraud labels and hoping

      The model finds unauthorised fraud and passes scams because the session is genuine.

      Spot itAsk how many labelled scams are in the training data, and what recall the model has on those alone, not on all fraud.
    • Counting reimbursed customers as "no fraud"

      A claim that was refused, or never raised, is treated as a good payment.

      Spot itCheck how the label was built: reported, confirmed, reimbursed, or only blocked? Look at labelled rate by week for the late-label effect.
    • Friction that trains customers to click through

      The same generic warning on every payment. Effectiveness fades and the warning stops carrying information.

      Spot itTrack the proceed-after-warning rate by warning type and over time. A near-100% rate means the warning is wallpaper.
    • A hub floods the graph

      Suspicion flows through a large merchant or landlord and lights up hundreds of innocent customers.

      Spot itLook at the highest-degree nodes in the top-scoring list. Check degree normalisation, and exclude known hubs or cap their contribution.
    • Leakage in graph evaluation

      Random edge or node splits put a mule's neighbours in train and test. The model "finds" the mule through its labelled neighbour.

      Spot itAsk for a time-ordered split with entities grouped, and whether labels of test nodes were used in neighbour features.
    • Freezing victims

      A romance-scam victim being used as a mule is treated as a criminal; a genuine house-deposit recipient is frozen.

      Spot itReview precision of the freeze action, time to release, complaints, and hold rates by customer group.
    • Displacement

      Tightening one rail pushes scammers to another channel, or to mules at a bank that is less protected. Dashboards show progress while the problem moved.

      Spot itWatch total losses across rails and across banks where data allows, not only the rail you changed.
    • Quoting reimbursement or loss figures without a basis

      "88% reimbursed" and "97% reimbursed" can both be true for different populations and dates.

      Spot itAsk for the denominator (all claims, eligible claims, in-scope claims) and whether it is by transaction date or claim-closure date.
    • Believing the AUC

      An AUC of 0.90 sounds strong. At 0.2% scam rate it still leaves about 96 in 100 held payments genuine at a typical threshold (Simulation 2).

      Spot itAlways ask for precision and alert volume at the operating point.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “In a scam, the customer really does press send, so most fraud signals look normal. On instant rails the money is gone within hours, usually within minutes of being moved on. We have two levers: slow down the few riskiest payments with the right warning, and catch the receiving account early enough to hold the money. Both cost real customers some friction, so the question is where the line goes, and the answer depends on who bears the loss. I'd bring you the numbers at two or three thresholds, with how many genuine customers each one touches.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd treat it as two linked models. On the sending side, a calibrated risk score including session and payee features, with interventions in tiers and each tier tested by randomised experiment, since effectiveness is the unknown. On the receiving side, a mule model on account behaviour plus neighbour features from the payment graph, with degree normalisation so hubs don't spread suspicion. Labels are late and selective, so I'd embargo recent weeks and keep a random review sample, evaluate forward in time with entities grouped, and measure precision at analyst capacity, not AUC.”
    “Why can't our fraud model just catch these? It caught the card fraud.”

    Card fraud was mostly unauthorised: stolen details, unusual device, unusual behaviour. In a scam, the real customer uses her own device and credentials, so the strongest features are silent. The signal is in context: a live call, a remote-access app, a new payee, and the receiving account. I'd add those and judge the model on scam labels specifically.

    “Should we just hold every large first-time payment for 24 hours?”

    It would stop many scams but touch a lot of genuine payments, and customers will route around it or complain. Whether it pays depends on loss per scam, the share of held payments that are scams, and how many scammed customers the delay actually saves. I can estimate that from a pilot. A tiered approach, with the hold only for the top risk band, is usually cheaper.

    “What did the UK's reimbursement rules do, and would they work here?”

    They made banks pay for scams, split between sender and receiver, up to £85,000 a claim, and reported scam losses fell while more claims were repaid. The mechanism is incentive. Whether anything similar applies in the UAE or Saudi Arabia is a regulatory matter that I haven't verified; I'd ask compliance what binds us and plan the analytics so they work under either regime.

    “Do we need a graph neural network?”

    Not first. Account-level features plus a few neighbour features (shared devices, number of flagged neighbours, inflow from reported victims) in a boosted-tree model get much of the gain and are easier to explain to investigators. A GNN is worth trying once that baseline exists and we can measure improvement on a forward-in-time test, with the cost of explaining and monitoring it.

    “We flagged a customer as a mule and they're furious. They say they were scammed too.”

    That may be true; many mules are victims. The action should have been proportionate: hold and review, not a closure, with a quick route to explain and release. I'd check the case: precision of that action, time to resolution, and whether similar customers are over-represented. Then adjust the policy and the threshold.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why do device and login features, strong for account takeover, fail for APP scams?

    The customer is the real account holder using their own device and credentials, so those features look normal. The deception is in the customer's mind. Useful signals are session context (call, remote access), payee reputation, and the receiving account.

    2. Mule funds have a half-life of 30 minutes. What share is left after 2 hours, and what does that say about waiting for a victim report?

    2 hours is 4 half-lives: 2−4 = 1/16 = 6.25%. If the victim reports in 2 hours or more, only about a sixteenth is still in the first account. Detection on the receiving side, in minutes, recovers far more.

    3. 100,000 payments, 0.2% scams. A threshold holds 1,000 payments and catches 120 scams. What is the precision and what does that mean for the queue?

    120 ÷ 1,000 = 12%. Eighty-eight of every hundred holds are genuine customers. That may be acceptable if the friction is a quick warning, and unacceptable if it is a 24-hour block. The right answer depends on the cost per intervention against the avoided loss.

    4. In the cost formula, what happens to the best threshold if the bank's liability share is zero? If it rises to 100%?

    At zero, the bank bears no scam loss, so any intervention is cost only and the best policy is to hold nothing. At 100% every missed scam costs the bank in full, so the best threshold falls and more payments are held. This is why liability rules shape behaviour as much as model quality.

    5. What is the hub problem in mule graphs, and how is it handled?

    Accounts like large merchants or landlords connect to thousands of customers. If suspicion spreads through them, innocent customers are flagged. Divide a node's outgoing score by its degree, cap or exclude known hubs, and inspect the high-degree nodes in the results.

    6. Why is a random split wrong for evaluating a mule model on a graph?

    A mule's neighbours, and the mule's own earlier behaviour, end up in both train and test, so the model can rely on information it will not have for a new account. Use time-ordered splits with entities grouped, and compute neighbour features using only information available at the prediction date.

    7. A report says "88% of APP fraud was reimbursed" and another says "97%". Can both be right?

    Yes, if they use different denominators, for example eligible claims against in-scope claims, or different periods or date bases (when the scam occurred or when the claim closed). Always ask what the percentage is a percentage of.
