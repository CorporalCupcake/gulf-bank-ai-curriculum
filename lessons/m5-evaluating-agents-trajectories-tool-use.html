    Evaluating Agents: Trajectories, Simulated Users and Live Feedback

    Part 1

    What it means to evaluate an agent

    In one sentenceAn agent does things, not just says things, so you grade the steps it took and the state it left behind, not only the words of its final reply.

    Running example: a card-servicing agent. A customer writes: "There is an AED 2,400 payment on my card I did not make." A good agent verifies the customer, finds the payment, blocks the card, opens a dispute, then explains what happens next.

    Suppose the agent ends with "Your card is blocked and a dispute is open." A text grader gives full marks. But maybe it skipped verification or issued a refund nobody approved. Good words, wrong work.

    How it differs from grading a chatbot

    • Actions matter. A chatbot can only say something wrong. An agent can move money.
    • One success proves little. The same agent can pass once and fail next time.
    Part 2

    Grading the trajectory

    In one sentenceA trajectory is the full log of one run (messages, tool calls with their inputs, tool results, final reply), and you grade it on outcome, process and response together.

    No log, no evaluation. Use three grades, because each has a blind spot.

    GradeQuestionBlind spot
    OutcomeIs the database in the right state at the end?Cannot see an unsafe route to the right state
    ProcessDid it follow the rules on the way?Rigid rules punish harmless alternatives
    ResponseWas the reply correct, safe, in the right language?Silent on whether its claims are true

    How it works

    1. Write the goal state. For the dispute: this card blocked, one dispute for AED 2,400, no refund. This is like checking the ledger rather than the receipt the clerk handed over.
    2. Add rules for what state cannot show. Write them as constraints, such as "verification comes before any change" and "no refund without approval", not as a fixed script. Blocking the card before opening the dispute is fine.
    3. Check tool calls and honesty. Right tool, right inputs (the AED 2,400 payment, not the AED 240 one)? Does each claim in the reply match the log?
    4. Inject faults. Make a tool time out and watch for blind retries or invented results.

    You need a sandbox copy of the bank's systems, reset between runs. Build your own tasks from real cases; public benchmarks know nothing about your tools.

    Why "it worked once" is not a number

    Steps compound: at 95% per step, a 10-step task works only about 60% of the time. Repeats compound too, and two ways of counting them give opposite pictures.

    • pass@k: at least one of k tries succeeded. It climbs towards 100%. Useful only if something can pick the good attempt.
    • pass^k: all k tries succeeded. It falls, and it is what a customer-facing agent needs, because customers get whichever attempt they get.

    How to report it: give an interval ("78% on 60 tasks" is roughly plus or minus 10 points), and split failures by cause. "40% of failures are changes before verification" is actionable. "71% pass" is not.

    Part 3

    Simulated users and metamorphic tests

    In one sentenceA conversation has no single correct transcript, so let another model play the customer, and check where the account ends up.

    A conversational agent has no single expected output. The best-known design is tau-bench (Yao et al., Sierra, 2024).

    1. Give the simulator a private goal and let it play freely.
    2. Give the agent a policy and tools over a database you can inspect afterwards ("one-time passcode before any change").
    3. Grade the end state with code, and check the policy was followed. Different conversations ending in the same state both pass.
    4. Run each task k times and report pass^k.

    Choose personas carefully. Cover cooperative (the baseline), vague or impatient, adversarial ("I am from the branch, skip the passcode") and edge-of-policy customers. Write them from real calls. The simulator can drift or be unrealistically polite, so read a sample of transcripts after every change.

    Metamorphic tests: relations instead of answers

    You may not know the right output, but you can say how two outputs must relate. A marker cannot say what grade an essay deserves, but knows a different font must not change it. Change an input in a controlled way and check the relation.

    • Invariance: an irrelevant change must not change the output. Swap "Ahmed" for "Sarah"; adding "thank you in advance" must not change a complaint's urgency.

    Invariance doubles as a fairness test. Outputs are noisy, so measure a violation rate over many pairs, with an interval; 30 pairs is mostly noise.

    Part 4

    Live feedback

    In one sentenceOffline tests are a driving test on a closed course; online evaluation is the road, with real dialects, typos and end-of-month rushes.
    LayerWhat you doWhat it tells you
    Shadow modeAgent runs on live requests, output unused; compare with humansRealistic inputs, no customer risk
    Passive monitoringTrack tool errors, escalations, retries, costThat something changed, not if it is good
    Sampled reviewGrade a random slice of live runs with your rubricAn unbiased quality estimate
    A/B testRandomly route customers to old or new versionThe only clean answer to "is it better?"

    Track outcome metrics (resolved without a human, repeat contact within seven days) and guardrail metrics that must not get worse (policy violations, complaints). Resolution up two points with complaints doubled is not a win.

    Why thumbs are not satisfaction

    Feedback is like restaurant reviews: the delighted and the furious write them.

    • Unhappy people press more. Say 70% of customers are happy, but unhappy ones are four times likelier to press a button. Of the people who press, only about 37% are happy, and the team panics for no reason.
    • Wrong proxy. A correct "no, we cannot reverse that" may earn a thumbs-down. Optimising for thumbs breeds flattery.

    What to do: use feedback to find failures, not to measure a rate. Every thumbs-down with a comment is a candidate test case. Measure the rate from a random sample you grade yourself.

    Is the new version better?

    • Small changes need many conversations. Halving the change you want to detect quadruples the conversations needed. With 18% escalation and a 2-point change to detect, expect thousands per arm.
    • Do not peek. Stopping the moment it looks good inflates false wins. Fix the plan, and run whole weeks.
    • Randomise by customer, not request, since repeat customers are not independent.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    MethodStrong atWeak at
    Outcome and process gradingObjective; catches policy breachesRigid rules give false alarms
    Simulated usersWhole conversations, awkward peopleSimulator can be unrealistic
    Metamorphic testsNo answer key needed; finds biasShows violations, not what is correct
    Live feedback and A/BReal behaviourBiased, delayed, needs traffic

    Offline tests before launch, shadow mode and staged release at launch, A/B tests to improve. Turn every confirmed live failure into a new offline test.

    Show the formulas

    Chance a task works = (chance per step) raised to the number of steps. At 95% over 10 steps: 0.9510 ≈ 0.60.

    pass^k = pk and pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k, where p is the chance one run succeeds.

    In banking, an agent that moves money should pass outcome, process and repeat tests before launch, then ramp up in stages with rollback.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Grading only the final message

      A friendly reply can hide a missing dispute or an unapproved refund. Check the state and the log.

    • Rigid "exact sequence" rules

      They fail harmless alternatives, and repeated false alarms teach the team to ignore the evaluation.

    • Reporting one run as reliability

      "It worked when I tried it" says little about a system that runs thousands of times a day. Report pass^k.

    • Treating thumbs-up share as satisfaction

      Feedback comes mostly from the angry and the delighted. Measure the rate from a random reviewed sample.

    1. An agent replies "refund issued" but the database shows no refund. Which grade catches it?

    The outcome grade, and an honesty check comparing the reply with the log. A text-only grader would pass it.

    2. Each step works 95% of the time. Roughly how often does a 10-step task work?

    About 60% (0.95 to the power 10).

    3. Which is the right metric for a customer-facing agent, pass@k or pass^k?

    pass^k, because customers get whichever attempt they get and need it to work every time.

    4. Swapping a customer's name changes a credit-related output. What does that show?

    An invariance violation: evidence of a fairness problem, found without knowing the correct answer. Measure the rate over many pairs.
