    ISO 20022 and Open Finance: Richer Data, and What It Is Worth

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Old SWIFT MT messages were like a handwritten note: a name and address squeezed into four 35-character lines, plus free-text "details of payment". Screening software had to guess whether "AL FARSI TRDG LLC, DUBAI" matched a listed entity.

    ISO 20022 is the replacement, more like a form with labelled boxes: debtor name, town and country as separate fields, a legal entity identifier (LEI), a purpose code, structured remittance information and a unique end-to-end reference. Same payment, many more labelled facts.

    Open finance is the same shift on the customer side. With consent, a bank can see accounts and products held elsewhere, not only what customers do with its products.

    Both invite "more fields, so more signal". A field helps only if it is present often enough, filled honestly, and adds something your existing features lacked.

    Hold onto thisRicher data does not create signal. It makes existing signal cheaper to get (clean names, purposes, entities) and occasionally adds some. Ask three things: what is the fill rate, who fills it in, and what does it add beyond what we have?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A13.1 ISO 20022: what is in the message and what you can do with it

    ISO 20022 is a family of message types. The ones you will meet most:

    MessageWhat it isWhy a data scientist cares
    pain.001The customer tells their bank to pay.Corporate files with rich remittance data.
    pacs.008 / pacs.009Bank-to-bank transfer and cover transfer (ISO versions of MT 103 and MT 202).The core payment record; cover payments now expose the real payer and payee.
    pacs.002 / pacs.004Payment status report and payment return.Rejection and return reasons are labels and features.

    The elements that pay off most:

    • Structured parties and addresses. Separate town and country give exact country-risk features and better sanctions matching. SWIFT removes the unstructured address option for cross-border traffic in November 2026.
    • Identifiers (LEI, BIC, UETR). They link the same entity, and follow the same payment across banks, without fuzzy name matching. This is the basis of graph features (A8, A9).
    • Ultimate debtor and creditor, and purpose codes. The real parties behind an intermediary and a coded reason replacing free text. For AML, often the most useful additions.
    The catch: fill rateA field empty on 80% of payments is nearly useless. A field filled with defaults ("N/A", "OTHR", the bank's own address) is worse: the model treats it as information. Measure fill rate, distinct values and who populates each field before building.

    A worked benefit: fewer false sanctions hits

    A fuzzy match on "Mohammed Al Farsi" fires on dozens of listed people, and each alert costs an analyst minutes. Extra fields (a different country, a different birth date, an LEI resolving to another company) let the engine clear some automatically. But only payments that carry the field benefit, so the saving is roughly fill rate times how much the field clears.

    Clearing a true match is far worse than keeping a false hit, so tune with recall on known true cases as the constraint, and quote management a number measured on your own history.

    Using the data for models

    • Replace parsed text with fields. Purpose, invoice number or counterparty can be read directly: cleaner features, and one error-prone parsing step removed.
    • Link entities. LEIs, BICs and UETRs give a graph of who pays whom through which banks, less noisy than fuzzy name matching.

    Two risks: semantics change between versions, so features shift silently, and other banks' messages are only as good as their systems, so track quality per sender.

    A13.2 UAE open finance: what shared customer data unlocks

    Open finance extends open banking from payment accounts to cards, loans, insurance and investments, shared with the customer's explicit consent. The UAE Central Bank runs this under its Open Finance Regulation and the Al Tareq trust platform, phased in over time.

    • Consent-based and purpose-bound. The customer approves a specific use for a period, like a hotel key card that stops working at checkout. You cannot keep the data and train on it forever, so lineage (which data, which consent, which model) becomes a core requirement.
    • Pull, not a feed. Data arrives when a customer grants access: rich for consenters, nothing for the rest.

    Main uses (see A10 and A9): credit from cash flow, including thin-file customers; affordability checks without paper; and mule detection, where an account just funded from three other banks tells you something your own data cannot.

    How much does a new data source add?

    Ask this of every new source. Two forecasters reading the same radar add little to each other; one with a different instrument adds a lot. If two signals are strong but nearly identical, the second is almost worthless.

    Two things drive the result: how much own and other-bank data overlap (an established customer mostly looks the same elsewhere), and customer type (for a new-to-bank customer your own data says little, so nothing overlaps).

    A new source's value is its incremental value, in the segment where you are weakest, times the share of customers you can reach. Expect small gains on mature salaried customers and large ones on new-to-credit or self-employed ones.

    Consent adds selection bias (F2.6). People who share data are not random: they may be more engaged or expect approval. Check calibration on consenters and non-consenters separately, and do not extrapolate.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Assuming a field is populated, or that defaults are information

      Most fields are optional. Senders leave them empty or fill "OTHR" or their own address.

      Spot itProfile fill rate and top values per field, per sending bank. Treat defaults as missing.
    • Training on the old format and scoring on the new

      Features built from parsed MT text behave differently once the fact arrives as a clean field.

      Spot itCompare feature distributions before and after cutover (PSI); validate across the migration date.
    • Counting redundant data as new

      Other-bank data that mostly repeats what you already see.

      Spot itTest incremental lift over the existing model, on the segment that matters, not the new source's standalone AUC.
    • Consent bias

      A model trained on consenters, then applied to everyone.

      Spot itCompare consenters and others on shared features; check calibration separately.
    • Using data beyond the consent

      Data shared for an affordability check is reused for marketing or another model.

      Spot itEvery dataset needs lineage to a consent and permitted purpose. If you cannot show it, do not use it; ask compliance.
    • Trusting vendor figures, or overfitting many new columns to few labels

      "Cuts false positives by 40%" from a deck; hundreds of fields against a few hundred confirmed cases.

      Spot itMeasure on your own history. Count positives per feature and test forward in time.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name three ISO 20022 elements useful for AML models and one thing that limits each one's value.

    Structured address (senders vary), LEI (individuals have none), ultimate debtor or creditor (often empty), purpose code (often defaulted to "other"). The common limit is fill rate and honest population.

    2. Structured address and an LEI each clear a share of false sanctions hits, but only 60% of payments carry them. Why is the real saving far smaller than the headline?

    Clearing only helps payments that carry the fields. The other 40% get no benefit, so realised saving is roughly fill rate times the per-field clearing rate. Here that is about a third of the queue, not over half.

    3. Two signals each separate good from bad customers equally well, but are highly correlated. Is the second one worth buying?

    Usually not. If the second signal mostly repeats the first, it adds almost nothing beyond it, however strong it looks on its own. Value comes from information the model does not already have.

    4. Why can the same open-finance data add 0.3 points of AUC for one customer and 5 points for another?

    The value is incremental. For an established customer, your own data already holds most of the same information. For a new-to-bank customer it does not, so the other-bank data is genuinely new.

    5. Why is training only on customers who consented a problem?

    Consenters are self-selected (more engaged, more confident of approval). What the model learns may not hold for the rest, and calibration can differ. Evaluate and calibrate by group; do not extrapolate unchecked.

    6. A feature built from regex-parsed MT narratives shifts after the ISO 20022 cutover. Why, and what do you do?

    The same fact now arrives as a structured field with different coverage and errors, so the distribution moves though customers did not. Rebuild the feature from the new fields, check drift, and retrain on data spanning the cutover.

    7. An auto-clearing rule removes 60% of false hits. What must you also check?

    That it removes no true matches. Test on known true cases and sample the cleared hits; one missed sanctions match costs far more than a few hundred wasted reviews.
