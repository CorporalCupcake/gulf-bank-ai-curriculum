    ISO 20022 and Open Finance: Richer Data, and What It Is Worth

    How structured payment messages and consented customer data become model features, how much each field and each new data source is actually worth, and the traps in both: sparse fields, redundant signals, and consent that is not random.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTwo forces are replacing thin, free-text financial data with structured, richer data (ISO 20022 messages between banks, open finance data from customers), and the data scientist's job is to measure what each new field adds beyond what you already had, because richness only pays where the fields are filled in, trustworthy and not redundant.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    For decades, a cross-border payment between banks travelled as a short message in the SWIFT MT format. Its key fields were lines of free text: a name and address squeezed into four lines of 35 characters, a "details of payment" line for whatever the sender typed. Screening software had to guess whether "AL FARSI TRDG LLC, DUBAI" was the same as a listed entity. Analytics got a few numbers and a blob of text.

    ISO 20022 is the replacement. It is an international standard for financial messages written in XML. Instead of a text blob, there are named, typed fields: debtor name, postal address split into town and country, a legal entity identifier (LEI), a purpose code, structured remittance information, and a unique end-to-end reference. The message carries the same payment with far more labelled facts attached.

    Open finance is the same shift on the customer side. Today a bank knows what its customers do with its products. With the customer's consent, regulated data sharing lets a bank (or a licensed provider) see accounts, transactions and other products held elsewhere. The customer's financial life stops being cut into pieces by institution.

    Both bring the same temptation: "more fields, so more signal." That is sometimes true and often not. A field only helps if it is present for enough payments, is filled in honestly, and tells you something your existing features did not. Most of this lesson is about measuring those three things.

    Hold onto thisRicher data does not create signal by itself. It lowers the cost of getting signal that was already there (cleaner names, purposes, entities) and occasionally adds something new. Always ask: what is the fill rate, who fills it in, and what does it add beyond what we already had?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A13.1 ISO 20022: what is in the message and what you can do with it

    ISO 20022 is a family of message definitions, built on a shared data dictionary. The names carry meaning. The ones you will meet most:

    MessageWhat it isWhy a data scientist cares
    pain.001Customer credit transfer initiation: the customer or corporate tells their bank to pay.Corporate payment files with rich remittance data: purpose, invoices, ultimate parties.
    pacs.008Financial institution credit transfer: bank to bank, the ISO equivalent of MT 103.The core cross-border and domestic payment record, with structured parties and addresses.
    pacs.009Bank-to-bank (cover) transfers between institutions.Exposes the real payer and payee in cover payments, which MT 202 hid.
    pacs.002 / pacs.004Payment status report and payment return.Rejections and returns, and their reason codes, are labels and features for data quality and fraud.
    camt.053 / camt.052Account statement and intraday report.Structured statements: counterparty, reference and purpose per entry, replacing parsing of free-text narratives.

    Which elements help models? In rough order of payoff:

    • Structured parties and addresses. Town and country as separate fields mean exact country-risk features and far better name-and-address matching in sanctions screening. SWIFT's rules move cross-border traffic to structured or hybrid addresses, with the unstructured option being removed in November 2026 (Part 4).
    • Identifiers. LEI for legal entities, BIC for banks, and organisation or private IDs where supplied. These let you link the same entity across payments without fuzzy name matching, which is the basis of network and graph features (A8, A9).
    • Purpose and category purpose codes. A coded reason (salary, trade, family support) instead of free text. Usable as a feature directly, and as a consistency check: a "salary" code on a payment from a shell company to a stranger is informative.
    • Structured remittance information. Invoice numbers and references that can be matched against receivables, giving reconciliation and invoice-fraud checks.
    • Unique transaction reference (UETR) and end-to-end ID. A key that follows the payment across banks, so you can reconstruct the chain and its timing, and join status messages back to the instruction.
    • Ultimate debtor and ultimate creditor. The real parties behind an intermediary. For AML, this is often the single most useful addition.
    • Status and return reason codes. Structured reasons for rejection let you separate "bad data" from "suspected fraud".

    ISO 20022 is also a data model, not just a format. Its messages have mandatory, optional and conditional elements, and what is mandatory depends on the usage guideline for the scheme (for cross-border payments, the CBPR+ guideline; for domestic schemes, each market's own). Two payments in "the same format" can differ widely in how much they contain.

    The catch: fill rateA field that exists but is empty on 80% of payments is nearly useless as a feature, and a field filled with defaults ("N/A", "OTHR", the bank's own address) is worse than empty because the model treats it as information. Measure fill rate, distinct values and who populates it (originating bank, customer or the sending system's default) before building anything.

    A worked benefit: fewer false sanctions hits

    Name screening produces many false hits: a fuzzy match on "Mohammed Al Farsi" fires on dozens of listed people. Each alert takes an analyst minutes. Extra structured fields let the screening engine clear a share of false hits automatically (a different country, a different date of birth, an LEI that resolves to another company). If each available field clears a fraction c of the remaining false hits, independently, the false hits left are

    remaining=hits·∏i(1−ci)

    Each field multiplies what is left by (1 − c). Two fields that each clear 35% and 30% leave 0.65 × 0.70 = 45.5% of the hits. The gains compound, but only for payments that actually carry the field, so the realised clearing rate is fill rate × c. Independence is optimistic: fields often clear the same easy cases.

    Two honest caveats about what you just saw. First, an engine that auto-clears hits must be validated for the opposite error: clearing a true match is far worse than keeping a false one, so the tuning is done with recall on known true cases as the constraint, not the false-hit count. Second, the number you promise management should be a measured one: run the engine on a historical sample with the new fields, count the hits, and review a random sample of the auto-cleared ones.

    Using the data for models

    Three patterns carry most of the value for modelling.

    • Replace parsed text with fields. Anything you extracted from narratives with regexes or an NLP model (purpose, invoice number, counterparty) can be read directly. This gives cleaner features and removes a model that was itself a source of errors.
    • Link entities. With LEIs, BICs and UETRs you can build a graph of who pays whom through which banks and measure path length, intermediary count and new corridors. This was possible before, but fuzzy matching on names made it noisy.
    • Check consistency. Compare fields against each other and against the customer's profile: purpose code against the declared business, ultimate debtor against the customer on file, currency against the corridor. Inconsistency is a feature, and a rule-friendly one.

    Two practical risks. Message semantics change between versions and schemes, so a feature built on one usage guideline can silently shift when a rule changes (the November 2026 address change is an example). And ISO 20022 messages from other banks are only as good as those banks' systems, so fill rates and quality differ by corridor and counterparty. Track data quality per sender, not just overall.

    A13.2 UAE open finance: what shared customer data unlocks

    Open finance extends open banking from payment accounts to a wide range of financial data: bank accounts, cards, loans, insurance, investments and more, shared through standard interfaces with the customer's explicit consent. In the UAE, the Central Bank has issued an Open Finance Regulation and operates a trust framework and platform, Al Tareq, through which participants connect. The regulation is being phased in, starting with banks and insurers; check the Central Bank's published phase schedule for what applies to your institution (Part 4).

    The mechanics matter to a data scientist for three reasons.

    • It is consent-based and purpose-bound. The customer approves a specific use, for a period. You cannot simply keep the data and train on it forever; what you may use it for is limited by the consent and by data-protection law. Lineage (what data, which consent, which model) becomes a core engineering requirement.
    • It is pull, not a feed. Data arrives when a customer grants access, so coverage grows application by application. You get a patchwork: rich data for customers who consented, nothing for the rest.
    • Standard schemas. Account, transaction and balance data arrive in agreed formats, with categories and identifiers that are comparable across institutions. That is a big saving over scraping and bespoke contracts.

    What does it unlock? Four uses stand out, covered further in A10 and A9:

    • Credit assessment from cash flow. Income, obligations and spending seen across all a customer's accounts, including customers with a thin bureau file. A10 develops this.
    • Affordability and income verification without paper documents, and faster onboarding.
    • Financial wellbeing and personalisation: a full picture of the customer for recommendations, with the care that implies.
    • Fraud and mule detection: seeing that an account has just been funded from three other banks tells you something your own data cannot.

    How much does a new data source add?

    This is the question to ask of any new source. A clean answer exists in the simplest model: suppose each signal is a normal score whose mean is δ standard deviations higher for bad customers than good ones, and the signals can be correlated. A model combining them optimally achieves a separation d′ given by

    d′2=δTΣ−1δ,AUC=Φ(d′/2)

    δ lists each signal's separation, Σ is the correlation matrix, and Φ is the normal curve's cumulative probability. If signals are independent, the squares just add: d′² = δ₁² + δ₂² + … . If two signals are correlated, the second adds less. For two signals with correlation ρ: d′² = (δ₁² + δ₂² − 2ρδ₁δ₂) ÷ (1 − ρ²). When ρ is high and the signals have the same δ, the second is nearly worthless.

    The simulation uses this. It starts with a bureau score (δ = 1.2) and the bank's own transaction data, then adds other-bank transaction data via open finance. The key variable is the correlation between the bank's own data and the other-bank data, because a customer who banks with you mostly looks the same elsewhere. The other key variable is customer type: for a customer new to your bank, your own data says little, so the other-bank data has no redundancy to fight.

    The lesson of the simulation is general. The value of a new source is its incremental value given what you already have, for the segment where you are weakest, multiplied by the share of customers you can actually reach. A bank with mature data on its salaried customers will see small gains there and large ones on young, new-to-credit or self-employed customers, who are usually where the commercial opportunity is as well.

    Consent adds a statistical wrinkle. People who share their data are not a random sample: they may be more digitally engaged, more confident of a good result, or applying precisely because they expect to be approved. A model trained on consenters can be miscalibrated for non-consenters, and the act of declining can itself be informative (and, depending on the rules, may not be used against the customer). This is selection bias in the sense of F2.6, and it gets handled the same way: measure the two groups separately, check calibration on each, and do not extrapolate.

    A13.3 · A13.4 · mBridge and CBDCsThe next generation of payment rails

    New rails reuse the idea of rich, structured messages. Data quality questions here carry over.

    F3 · Imbalanced dataFew positives, still

    Richer features do not create labels. AML and fraud positives stay rare and late-labelled, so extra columns raise overfitting risk with the same few cases.

    F4 · Features and temporal validationValidate through the migration date

    When a format changes, features shift. Time-ordered validation across the cutover shows whether a model survived the change.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringFill rate is a drift metric

    Track field fill rate, value distribution and sender mix with PSI-style monitors, since a new sender or rule can change a feature overnight.

    F2.6 · Selection biasConsenters are not everyone

    Open finance models are trained on the customers who agreed. Treat consent as a selection mechanism.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Sanctions and AML screening. Fewer false hits, better entity resolution, a clearer view of ultimate parties. Part of the business case for payment-data modernisation.
    • Cross-border fraud and trade finance. Purpose codes and invoice references against the customer's profile and history.
    • Retail and SME credit. Cash-flow signals for thin-file and self-employed customers, with Islamic financing products using the same affordability data.
    • Reconciliation and corporate cash management. Structured remittance data lets corporate clients match payments to invoices automatically; the data you analyse is also a product you sell.
    • Regulatory reporting and data quality. Better lineage, and a push to clean customer master data so that identifiers in messages match the internal record.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The infrastructure change is largely done; the argument has moved to who captures the benefit. Is the richer data converting into better models, or only being stored? Open finance is at an earlier stage: live at the first institutions, with the question being adoption and value.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Assuming a field is populated because the schema has it

      The element exists and is optional. Most senders leave it empty or default it.

      Spot itProfile fill rate and top five values per field, per sending bank and corridor, before any modelling.
    • Default values read as information

      "OTHR" purpose codes, a bank's own address in the debtor field, "NOTPROVIDED".

      Spot itOne value covering more than half of rows is a red flag. Treat defaults as missing.
    • Training on the old format and scoring on the new

      Features built from parsed MT text behave differently when the same fact arrives as a clean field.

      Spot itCompare feature distributions before and after cutover (PSI) and validate across the migration date.
    • Translation losing information

      Many banks convert between ISO and legacy internally, truncating fields when going back to MT.

      Spot itCheck whether a field is lost in your own pipeline before blaming the counterparty.
    • Counting redundant data as new

      Other-bank transactions that mostly repeat what you see.

      Spot itTest incremental lift over the existing model, on the segment that matters, not the standalone AUC of the new source.
    • Consent bias

      A model trained on customers who consented, then applied to everyone.

      Spot itCompare consenters and others on the features you have for both; check calibration separately.
    • Using data beyond the consent

      Data shared for an affordability check is reused for marketing or another model.

      Spot itEvery dataset needs lineage to a consent and a permitted purpose. If you cannot show it, do not use it, and ask compliance.
    • Quoting vendor benefit figures as your own

      "ISO 20022 cuts false positives by 40%" from a deck.

      Spot itAsk for the sample, the baseline and whether true matches were checked. Measure on your own history.
    • Overfitting a few labels with many new columns

      Hundreds of structured fields and a few hundred confirmed AML cases.

      Spot itCount positives per feature; use regularisation and a forward-in-time test.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “ISO 20022 gives us structured payment data in place of free text, and open finance gives us a consented view of customers' other accounts. Both are real opportunities, but the benefit depends on how complete the data is. I'd measure it on our own history: how many payments carry the new fields, and how many fewer false sanctions alerts or how much better credit ranking we get. Then we size the business case on measured numbers rather than vendor claims.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start by profiling fill rate, defaults and distinct values per field and per sender. Then I'd test incremental lift over the current model on a time-ordered holdout, by segment, because correlated sources add little for established customers and a lot for thin files. For open finance I'd track consent and purpose as lineage, and check calibration separately for consenters, since consent is a selection mechanism.”
    “Doesn't ISO 20022 automatically make our AML models better?”

    It makes better models possible. The data is only useful if the fields are filled in and not defaulted, which varies by sender and corridor. I'd measure fill rate and test lift against the current features before promising anything, and keep an eye on true-match recall when auto-clearing.

    “What can we do with open finance data that we couldn't before?”

    See a customer's finances across institutions, with consent. The biggest gain is for customers where our own data is thin, such as new-to-bank, young or self-employed applicants, where a cash-flow view can improve credit decisions. For established customers the data overlaps with ours, so the lift is smaller.

    “Can we use that data to train a general model?”

    Only as far as the consent and the regulation allow. Consent is for a stated purpose. I'd ask compliance and legal what uses are permitted, keep data lineage tied to the consent, and not assume that training a different model is covered. I would not guess at the rules.

    “Why not just wait for all the data to be complete?”

    Because adoption is gradual and sender quality varies. We can start with the fields that are well populated, build monitoring on fill rate, and add features as coverage improves. We also need history to train on, and the structured data accumulates only from the migration date.

    “The model got worse after the November change.”

    First check what changed in the inputs: address structure, field fill rate, sender mix. Compare feature distributions before and after the date. If a feature was built from parsed text, rebuild it from the new fields and validate across the cutover.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name three ISO 20022 elements useful for AML models and one thing that limits each one's value.

    Examples: structured address (only useful if populated, and cross-border senders vary); LEI (only exists for entities that have one, so individuals are not covered); ultimate debtor or creditor (optional in many flows, often empty); purpose code (often defaulted to "other"). The common limit is fill rate and honesty of population.

    2. 200 false hits a day. Structured address clears 35% and an LEI clears 30%, independently, but only 60% of payments carry them. What is left?

    Clearing rates apply only to payments carrying the fields. For those: 0.65 × 0.70 = 0.455 remain. So for 60% of hits, remaining is 0.455; for 40%, it stays at 1. Average: 0.6 × 0.455 + 0.4 = 0.673, so about 135 hits remain, a 33% cut instead of the 54.5% a full fill rate would give.

    3. Two signals each have δ = 1.0 and correlation 0.8. What is d′², and how does it compare with independence?

    (1 + 1 − 2 × 0.8) ÷ (1 − 0.64) = 0.4 ÷ 0.36 ≈ 1.11. If independent, d′² would be 2. High correlation means the second signal adds about 0.11, around 5% of what independence would give.

    4. Why can the same open-finance data add 0.3 points of AUC for one customer and 5 points for another?

    The value is incremental. For an established customer the bank's own data already captures most of the same information, so redundancy is high. For a new-to-bank customer there is no such history, so the other-bank data supplies information not otherwise available.

    5. Why is training only on customers who consented a problem?

    Consenters are a self-selected group, possibly more engaged or more confident of approval. Relationships learned on them may not hold for the rest, and calibration can differ. Evaluate and calibrate by group and do not extrapolate without checking.

    6. A feature built from regex-parsed MT narratives shifts after the ISO 20022 cutover. Why, and what do you do?

    The same fact now comes from a structured field, with different coverage, errors and values. The distribution moves even if the customers have not changed. Rebuild the feature from the new fields, compare before and after with a drift metric and retrain or recalibrate with data spanning the cutover.

    7. An auto-clearing rule removes 60% of false hits. What must you also check?

    That it removes no true matches, or as few as the policy tolerates. Test on known true cases and review a random sample of the cleared hits. The cost of one missed sanctions match far exceeds a few hundred wasted reviews.
