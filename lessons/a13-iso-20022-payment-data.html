    ISO 20022 and Open Finance: Richer Data, and What It Is Worth

    How structured payment messages and consented customer data become model features, how much each field and each new data source is actually worth, and the traps in both: sparse fields, redundant signals, and consent that is not random.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePayment and customer data are moving from thin free text to structured, richer data (ISO 20022 messages, open finance), and your job is to measure what each new field adds beyond what you already had, because richness pays only where fields are filled in, trustworthy and not redundant.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Old SWIFT MT messages were like a handwritten note: a name and address squeezed into four 35-character lines, plus a free-text "details of payment". Screening software had to guess whether "AL FARSI TRDG LLC, DUBAI" matched a listed entity.

    ISO 20022 is the replacement, an XML standard for financial messages, more like a form with labelled boxes. It has named, typed fields: debtor name, address split into town and country, a legal entity identifier (LEI), a purpose code, structured remittance information and a unique end-to-end reference. Same payment, many more labelled facts.

    Open finance is the same shift on the customer side. A bank sees only what customers do with its products. With consent, regulated sharing lets it see accounts, transactions and products held elsewhere.

    Both invite "more fields, so more signal". That is sometimes true. A field helps only if it is present often enough, filled honestly, and adds something your existing features lacked.

    Hold onto thisRicher data does not create signal. It makes existing signal cheaper to get (clean names, purposes, entities) and occasionally adds some. Ask three things: what is the fill rate, who fills it in, and what does it add beyond what we have?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A13.1 ISO 20022: what is in the message and what you can do with it

    ISO 20022 is a family of message definitions on a shared data dictionary. The ones you will meet most:

    MessageWhat it isWhy a data scientist cares
    pain.001Customer credit transfer initiation: the customer or corporate tells their bank to pay.Corporate payment files with rich remittance data: purpose, invoices, ultimate parties.
    pacs.008Financial institution credit transfer: bank to bank, the ISO equivalent of MT 103.The core cross-border and domestic payment record, with structured parties and addresses.
    pacs.009Bank-to-bank (cover) transfers between institutions.Exposes the real payer and payee in cover payments, which MT 202 hid.
    pacs.002 / pacs.004Payment status report and payment return.Rejections and returns, and their reason codes, are labels and features for data quality and fraud.
    camt.053 / camt.052Account statement and intraday report.Structured statements: counterparty, reference and purpose per entry, replacing parsing of free-text narratives.

    Elements, in rough order of payoff:

    • Structured parties and addresses. Town and country as separate fields give exact country-risk features and far better sanctions name matching. SWIFT is removing the unstructured address option for cross-border traffic in November 2026.
    • Identifiers. LEI (entities), BIC (banks), organisation or private IDs. They link the same entity across payments without fuzzy name matching, the basis of graph features (A8, A9).
    • Purpose codes. A coded reason (salary, trade, family support) replaces free text. A "salary" code on a payment from a shell company to a stranger is informative.
    • Structured remittance information. Invoice numbers matched against receivables give reconciliation and invoice-fraud checks.
    • UETR and end-to-end ID. A key that follows the payment across banks, to rebuild the chain and its timing and join status messages to the instruction.
    • Ultimate debtor and creditor. The real parties behind an intermediary. For AML, often the most useful addition.
    • Status and return reason codes. Separate "bad data" from "suspected fraud".

    ISO 20022 is a data model, not just a format. Elements are mandatory, optional or conditional depending on the scheme's usage guideline (CBPR+ for cross-border; each market's own for domestic). Two payments in "the same format" can hold very different amounts.

    The catch: fill rateA field empty on 80% of payments is nearly useless. A field filled with defaults ("N/A", "OTHR", the bank's own address) is worse: the model treats it as information. Before building, measure fill rate, distinct values and who populates it (originating bank, customer or system default).

    A worked benefit: fewer false sanctions hits

    A fuzzy match on "Mohammed Al Farsi" fires on dozens of listed people, and each alert costs an analyst minutes. Extra fields (different country, different birth date, an LEI resolving to another company) let the engine clear some automatically. If each field clears a fraction c of remaining false hits, independently, what is left is

    remaining=hits·∏i(1−ci)

    Each field multiplies what is left by (1 − c). Fields clearing 35% and 30% leave 0.65 × 0.70 = 45.5%. Only payments carrying the field benefit, so realised clearing is fill rate × c. Independence is optimistic: fields often clear the same easy cases.

    Two caveats. First, validate auto-clearing for the opposite error: clearing a true match is far worse than keeping a false hit, so tune with recall on known true cases as the constraint. Second, promise management a measured number: run the engine on a historical sample with the new fields, count hits, and review a random sample of the auto-cleared ones.

    Using the data for models

    • Replace parsed text with fields. Purpose, invoice number or counterparty once pulled out with regexes or NLP can be read directly: cleaner features, and one error-prone model removed.
    • Link entities. LEIs, BICs and UETRs give a graph of who pays whom through which banks: path length, intermediary count, new corridors. Possible before, but noisy under fuzzy name matching.
    • Check consistency. Compare purpose code with declared business, ultimate debtor with the customer on file, currency with corridor. Inconsistency is a feature, and rule-friendly.

    Two risks. Semantics change between versions and schemes, so a feature can shift silently when a rule changes (the November 2026 address change is one). And messages from other banks are only as good as their systems, so track data quality per sender, not just overall.

    A13.2 UAE open finance: what shared customer data unlocks

    Open finance extends open banking from payment accounts to bank accounts, cards, loans, insurance and investments, shared through standard interfaces with the customer's explicit consent. The UAE Central Bank has issued an Open Finance Regulation and runs the Al Tareq trust platform. It is being phased in, starting with banks and insurers; check the Central Bank's phase schedule for your institution.

    Three things matter to a data scientist:

    • Consent-based and purpose-bound. The customer approves a specific use for a period, like a hotel key card that stops working at checkout. You cannot keep the data and train on it forever. Lineage (which data, which consent, which model) becomes a core requirement.
    • Pull, not a feed. Data arrives when a customer grants access, so you get a patchwork: rich data for consenters, nothing for the rest.
    • Standard schemas. Accounts, transactions and balances arrive in agreed formats with comparable categories and identifiers, saving scraping and bespoke contracts.

    Four uses (see A10 and A9):

    • Credit from cash flow. Income, obligations and spending across all accounts, including customers with a thin bureau file (A10).
    • Affordability and income verification without paper, with faster onboarding.
    • Personalisation: a fuller picture for recommendations, handled with care.
    • Fraud and mule detection: an account just funded from three other banks tells you something your own data cannot.

    How much does a new data source add?

    Ask this of every new source. Two forecasters who both read the same radar add little to each other; one with a different instrument adds a lot. In the simplest model, each signal is a normal score whose mean is δ standard deviations higher for bad customers than good, and signals may be correlated. A model combining them optimally separates the groups by d′:

    d′2=δTΣ−1δ,AUC=Φ(d′/2)

    δ lists each signal's separation, Σ is the correlation matrix, Φ is the normal cumulative probability. Independent signals just add squares: d′² = δ₁² + δ₂² + … . For two signals with correlation ρ: d′² = (δ₁² + δ₂² − 2ρδ₁δ₂) ÷ (1 − ρ²). With high ρ and equal δ, the second signal is nearly worthless.

    The simulation starts with a bureau score (δ = 1.2) and the bank's own transaction data, then adds other-bank data. Two things drive the result. One is the correlation between own and other-bank data: a customer who banks with you mostly looks the same elsewhere. The other is customer type: for a new-to-bank customer your own data says little, so there is no redundancy to fight.

    A new source's value is its incremental value given what you have, in the segment where you are weakest, times the share of customers you can reach. Like a second weather app that copies the first, it adds little where you are already well covered. Expect small gains on mature salaried customers and large ones on young, new-to-credit or self-employed customers, which is also where the commercial opportunity is.

    Consent adds selection bias (F2.6). People who share data are not random: they may be more engaged, or apply because they expect approval. A model trained on consenters can be miscalibrated for everyone else, and declining can itself be informative (though rules may bar using it against the customer). Measure the two groups separately, check calibration on each, and do not extrapolate.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Assuming a field is populated because the schema has it

      It is optional. Most senders leave it empty or default it.

      Spot itProfile fill rate and top five values per field, per sending bank and corridor, before modelling.
    • Default values read as information

      "OTHR" purpose codes, a bank's own address as debtor, "NOTPROVIDED".

      Spot itOne value on over half the rows is a red flag. Treat defaults as missing.
    • Training on the old format and scoring on the new

      Features built from parsed MT text behave differently once the fact arrives as a clean field.

      Spot itCompare feature distributions before and after cutover (PSI); validate across the migration date.
    • Translation losing information

      Banks convert between ISO and legacy internally, truncating fields on the way back to MT.

      Spot itCheck your own pipeline for lost fields before blaming the counterparty.
    • Counting redundant data as new

      Other-bank transactions that mostly repeat what you already see.

      Spot itTest incremental lift over the existing model, on the segment that matters, not the new source's standalone AUC.
    • Consent bias

      A model trained on consenters, then applied to everyone.

      Spot itCompare consenters and others on shared features; check calibration separately.
    • Using data beyond the consent

      Data shared for an affordability check is reused for marketing or another model.

      Spot itEvery dataset needs lineage to a consent and permitted purpose. If you cannot show it, do not use it; ask compliance.
    • Quoting vendor benefit figures as your own

      "ISO 20022 cuts false positives by 40%" from a deck.

      Spot itAsk for sample, baseline and whether true matches were checked. Measure on your own history.
    • Overfitting a few labels with many new columns

      Hundreds of structured fields, a few hundred confirmed AML cases.

      Spot itCount positives per feature; regularise and test forward in time.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name three ISO 20022 elements useful for AML models and one thing that limits each one's value.

    Structured address (only if populated; senders vary). LEI (only entities have one, so individuals are missing). Ultimate debtor or creditor (optional, often empty). Purpose code (often defaulted to "other"). The common limit is fill rate and honest population.

    2. 200 false hits a day. Structured address clears 35% and an LEI clears 30%, independently, but only 60% of payments carry them. What is left?

    Clearing applies only to payments carrying the fields. For those, 0.65 × 0.70 = 0.455 remain. The other 40% stay at 1. Average: 0.6 × 0.455 + 0.4 = 0.673, so about 135 hits remain: a 33% cut, not the 54.5% of full fill.

    3. Two signals each have δ = 1.0 and correlation 0.8. What is d′², and how does it compare with independence?

    (1 + 1 − 2 × 0.8) ÷ (1 − 0.64) = 0.4 ÷ 0.36 ≈ 1.11. Independence would give 2. The second signal adds about 0.11, around 5% of what independence would give.

    4. Why can the same open-finance data add 0.3 points of AUC for one customer and 5 points for another?

    The value is incremental. For an established customer, your own data already holds most of the same information. For a new-to-bank customer it does not, so the other-bank data is genuinely new.

    5. Why is training only on customers who consented a problem?

    Consenters are self-selected (more engaged, more confident of approval). What the model learns may not hold for the rest, and calibration can differ. Evaluate and calibrate by group; do not extrapolate unchecked.

    6. A feature built from regex-parsed MT narratives shifts after the ISO 20022 cutover. Why, and what do you do?

    The same fact now arrives as a structured field with different coverage, errors and values, so the distribution moves though customers did not change. Rebuild the feature from the new fields, compare before and after with a drift metric, and retrain or recalibrate on data spanning the cutover.

    7. An auto-clearing rule removes 60% of false hits. What must you also check?

    That it removes no true matches, or as few as policy tolerates. Test on known true cases and sample the cleared hits. One missed sanctions match costs far more than a few hundred wasted reviews.
