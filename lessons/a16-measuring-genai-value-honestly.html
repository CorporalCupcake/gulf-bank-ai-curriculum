    Measuring GenAI Value Honestly

    Part 1

    What honest value means

    In one sentenceHonest GenAI value is the benefit that actually arrives in the budget, minus the full cost, not the hours people say they saved.

    Take one running example. A bank gives 800 staff a GenAI assistant for a quarter. A survey says people save about two hours a week. Multiply by 46 weeks and AED 150 an hour and you get AED 11 million a year. The executive committee approves, and six months later the programme quietly shrinks.

    That 11 million was a gross number, like quoting a salary before tax and rent. Counted properly, the benefit was about AED 1.8 million against AED 2.9 million of cost. The pilot loses money.

    How it differs from the pilot slide

    • Pilot slide: survey answers, opinions, and no costs.
    • Honest number: system data, a comparison group, and every cost counted.

    Before any pilot, write one line: what number moves, who owns it, what it is today (the baseline) and what it costs to run for a year. If any part is missing, it is a demonstration, not a business case.

    Part 2

    How to measure it

    1. Start from the claim. 800 users, 2 hours a week, 46 weeks, AED 150 an hour.
    2. Apply adoption. Only the share still using the tool after the novelty fades counts.
    3. Subtract checking. Some saved time goes on verifying the output.
    4. Apply realisation. Only freed hours that finance would recognise count, such as a vacancy not filled. The rest is relief for people, not a business case.
    5. Subtract the full cost. Seats (paid for all 800, logged in or not), integration, evaluation and monitoring, model risk validation, security review, support, and the people who maintain prompts and retrieval.

    Three kinds of benefit

    • Cashable: cost avoided or revenue earned, visible in a budget line.
    • Capacity: time freed. Valuable only if someone redeploys it.
    • Risk: fewer errors, valued as expected loss avoided.

    Selling capacity as if it were cash is what disappoints boards.

    Part 3

    How to read a business case

    "Users say they save 2 hours a week"
    Opinion, and usually generous, like asking drivers how fast they go. Ask for handling time or tickets closed, compared with a matched team without the tool.
    Net per year below zero
    Honest benefit does not cover the cost. Either lift adoption and realisation, or do not scale it.
    Benefit is all "capacity"
    Ask who will use the freed hours and for what. If nobody, the cash value is close to zero.
    Six gates, each passed 70% of the time
    Only about 12% of pilots get through all six (0.7 six times over). Choosing better pilots lifts every gate at once, which beats fixing one.
    • Value defined: a named business owner and baseline first, not "people like it".
    • Real data: test a real sample in week one. Real documents are scanned and bilingual.
    • Evaluated: a fixed test set with intervals, not 20 examples judged by eye.
    • Risk: involve compliance and validation at scoping, not at the end.
    • Adopted: training, a champion per team, and a 90-day review that can turn it off.
    Part 4

    Choosing use cases and who owns them

    Choosing the first five

    Build a portfolio, like a football squad, not five players in one position. Score each candidate 1 to 5 on four axes:

    • Value: money or capacity at stake, counted honestly.
    • Feasibility: reachable data, a task models suit, errors a human can catch.
    • Risk (5 is most dangerous): customer harm and regulatory exposure.
    • Visibility: a visible win buys cover, a visible failure costs it.

    The score is a conversation tool, not an oracle. Then apply three rules: mix the risk (at least two internal, human-reviewed cases, one or two with real money attached, at most one customer-facing and launched narrowly), share the plumbing (retrieval, evaluation, a guardrail gateway), and make sure each case has an owner with a budget line and data reachable within weeks.

    Operating models

    ModelHow it worksStrong atWeak at
    Centre of excellenceOne central team holds talent and standardsScarce talent, quick startBottleneck; benefits stay with the centre
    Hub and spokeHub owns platform; embedded teams build on itBusiness ownership with central qualityNeeds a real platform and clear contract
    DistributedEach business line runs its own teamClose to the problem, fastDuplication, uneven controls

    The common path is centre first, hub and spoke later. The trigger is a queue: when the backlog exceeds a quarter and business leaders start hiring their own people, formalise the spokes.

    Build, buy or partner is decided capability by capability. Buy foundation models and generic productivity tools. Build or buy a platform for retrieval and evaluation, but own the evaluation set. Build core use cases on bank data, such as fraud, credit and anti-money-laundering, because your data is the edge. Partner for specialities such as Arabic. Ask three questions: is this where we differentiate, can we run and validate it ourselves, and what is the exit?

    In banking, check central bank expectations with compliance, watch data residency rules, and for Islamic finance retrieve approved Sharia-board wording rather than generating freely.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting gross benefit

      The headline ignores adoption, checking and realisation. Always show the honest number next to it.

    • Forgetting the bill

      Seats, integration, validation, security and running costs are missing from most pilot slides.

    • Counting capacity as cash

      Freed hours are only worth money if someone redeploys them.

    • Leaving risk until the end

      Compliance arrives late and says no. Bring them in at scoping.

    1. Name the three steps where a pilot's benefit shrinks.

    Adoption, checking and realisation.

    2. Why use system data instead of a survey?

    Self-reported savings are generous. System data such as handling time shows what really changed.

    3. What is the difference between cashable and capacity benefit?

    Cashable shows up in a budget line. Capacity is freed time, valuable only if redeployed.

    4. Six gates each pass 70% of pilots. How many of 20 reach production?

    About 12% survive, so roughly 2 or 3 of 20.

    5. When should a bank move from a centre of excellence to hub and spoke?

    When the backlog exceeds a quarter and business leaders start hiring their own AI people.
