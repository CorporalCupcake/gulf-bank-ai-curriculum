    Making GenAI Pay: Honest Value, the First Five Use Cases and the Operating Model

    Why most pilots never reach production, how to count the benefit and the cost without flattering yourself, how to choose the first five use cases at a Gulf bank, and whether the AI team should sit in the centre, in the business lines, or partly with a vendor.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA GenAI pilot succeeds when it answers a question the bank cares about, so the winners are the use cases with a real baseline, a measured cashable benefit, a risk the bank can carry and an owner in the business, and the operating model is just the structure that keeps those four things true after the demo.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank gives 800 staff a GenAI assistant for a quarter. The survey says people save about two hours a week. Multiply by 46 weeks and AED 150 an hour and you get AED 11 million a year. The executive committee approves. Six months later the programme quietly shrinks.

    The number was gross, like quoting a salary before tax and rent. Only some people kept using the tool, and they spent part of the "saved" time checking its output. Saved time is not saved money unless someone acts on it. And the bill was missing: seats, integration, validation, running costs. Counted properly, the benefit is about AED 1.8 million against AED 2.9 million of cost. It loses money.

    That gap explains most stalled pilots; the rest is poor selection. Three questions a board will ask:

    • A16.1, measuring. Is it worth it in money, after adoption, checking and cost?
    • A16.2, choosing. Which five first? Build a portfolio, not a ranking.
    • A16.3, structuring. Who builds, owns and pays? Centre, hub-and-spoke or distributed, and build, buy or partner.
    Hold onto thisBefore any pilot, write one line: what number moves, who owns it, what it is today (the baseline, C1.3), and what it costs to run for a year. If any is missing, it is a demonstration, not a business case.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring GenAI value honestly (A16.1)

    Net value is the benefit actually realised minus the full cost. The benefit shrinks at three steps, each a place where pilots overstate:

    • Adoption. Share still using it after the novelty fades.
    • Checking. Part of the saved time goes on verifying the output.
    • Realisation. Share of freed hours finance would recognise, such as a vacancy not filled. The rest is relief for people, not a business case.

    Three habits keep the number honest.

    • Measure behaviour, not opinion. Self-reported savings are generous, like asking drivers how fast they go. Use system data such as handling time or tickets closed, and compare matched teams with and without the tool (F3.6).
    • Count the full cost. Licences or inference, integration, evaluation and monitoring (A4), model risk validation, security review, support, and the people who maintain prompts and retrieval.
    • Separate three kinds of benefit. Cashable: cost avoided or revenue earned, visible in a budget line. Capacity: time freed, valuable only if redeployed. Risk: fewer errors, valued as expected loss avoided. Selling capacity as cash is what disappoints.

    Gates are a chain, like traffic lights in a row. If each of six passes 70% of the time, only about 12% get through. Lifting all of them by choosing better pilots helps far more than fixing one.

    Choosing the first five use cases (A16.2)

    The first five do more than deliver their own value. They shape the bank's view of AI and build parts later cases reuse. Choose a portfolio, like a football squad, not five players in one position.

    Score each candidate 1 to 5 on four axes:

    • Value. Money or capacity at stake, counted honestly.
    • Feasibility. Accessible data, a task models suit, errors a human can catch.
    • Risk (5 is most dangerous). Customer harm, regulatory exposure. Human-reviewed uses score low; autonomous customer decisions score high.
    • Visibility. Will people feel it? A visible win buys cover; a visible failure costs it.

    A weighted score is a conversation tool, not an oracle.

    After scoring, apply three portfolio rules.

    • Mix the risk. At least two internal, human-reviewed cases that ship in a quarter; one or two with real money attached (fraud triage, KYC); at most one customer-facing, launched narrowly with a fallback. No autonomous customer decisions.
    • Share the plumbing. Prefer cases that build reusable parts: retrieval, evaluation, a gateway with guardrails (A15).
    • Check owner and data. Each case needs a business owner with a budget line and a data source reachable within weeks.

    Regional points: Arabic quality affects customer-facing cases (A14); data residency may rule options out (A11); confirm central bank expectations with compliance (A12). For Islamic finance, retrieve approved Sharia-board wording rather than generate freely.

    AI operating models (A16.3)

    The operating model decides where AI people sit and who owns what is built.

    ModelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
    Centre of excellence (CoE)One central team holds talent, standards and backlog.Scarce talent used well; quick to start.Bottleneck; benefits don't land in the business.
    Hub and spokeHub owns platform and standards; embedded teams build on it.Business ownership with central quality; scales.Needs a real platform and a clear hub-spoke contract.
    Distributed / federatedEach business line runs its own AI team.Close to the problem; fast.Duplicated effort; inconsistent controls; no single story for the regulator.

    The common path is CoE first, hub-and-spoke later. The trigger is a queue: when the backlog exceeds a quarter and business leaders start hiring their own people, formalise the spokes.

    Build, buy or partner is decided capability by capability. Foundation models and generic productivity tools: buy (M2). Retrieval, evaluation and gateway plumbing: build or buy a platform, but own the evaluation set. Core use cases on bank data (fraud, credit, AML): build, because your data is the edge. Arabic specialities: partner or use regional models.

    Three questions settle most debates. Is this where we differentiate? If not, buy. Can we run and validate it ourselves? If not, it becomes shelfware. What is the exit? The model market changes quarterly.

    Whatever the structure, four roles must exist: a business product owner accountable for the number, a platform owner, risk and compliance partners from scoping, and a single intake forum that stops zombie projects at the 90-day review.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each has a quick check.

    • Hours saved presented as money saved

      "2 hours a week × 800 staff = AED 11 million." Nobody will cash it.

      Spot itAsk which budget line falls or which output rises. If "capacity", label it so.
    • Self-reported or pilot-only benefit

      The survey says people feel faster, or a hand-picked team with free licences and a dedicated engineer is extrapolated to 10,000 users at full price.

      Spot itAsk which system data backs it and whether there is a comparison group.
    • Ignoring cost beyond the licence

      The case has seats but not validation, monitoring or integration.

      Spot itAsk for year-two cost with no project team. Who pays then?
    • Choosing by visibility, with risk brought in late

      The customer chatbot goes first because the CEO wants to see it, and compliance sees it at go-live.

      Spot itCheck the first five include internal, reviewable cases, and ask when risk first saw the scope.
    • No stop rule

      Pilots drift for a year because stopping feels like failure.

      Spot itAsk for the date and metric at which this pilot is stopped, scaled or redesigned.
    • An org chart, not a funding model

      Business owners have no budget or number, so they ignore AI as someone else's service.

      Spot itAsk who is accountable for each case's benefit, and whose budget it sits in.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A pilot claims 500 users each save 3 hours a week, worth AED 8 million a year. What questions turn that into an honest benefit?

    How many are still using it after the novelty fades? How much of the saved time goes on checking the output? How much of what is left becomes cash (a vacancy not filled, volume absorbed) rather than idle capacity? Each answer shrinks the headline.

    2. Five gates are each passed 80% of the time. Why do so few pilots reach production, and what helps most?

    Gates multiply, like traffic lights in a row, so five 80% gates leave only about a third of pilots. Fixing one gate helps a little; lifting all of them by choosing better pilots (an owner, real data, risk involved early) helps far more.

    3. What is the difference between cashable, capacity and risk benefit? Give one banking example of each.

    Cashable: a contractor contract not renewed, or case volume absorbed without hiring. Capacity: analysts summarise less and investigate more, valuable only if work is redeployed. Risk: fewer missed sanctions hits, valued as expected loss avoided. Show them separately so capacity isn't treated as cash.

    4. Why not simply rank use cases by value and take the top five?

    Top-value cases are often the riskiest (an autonomous credit agent). The first five must also build shared plumbing, earn risk trust and ship quickly.

    5. Your CoE has a nine-month backlog and two business lines have started hiring their own data scientists. What do you do?

    Move towards hub and spoke before shadow teams entrench. Keep platform, standards and risk tooling central; let business lines own delivery.

    6. A vendor offers a customer-service assistant that does well in a demo. What do you ask before treating it as a pilot?

    How it does on a sample of our real Arabic and English conversations. Where data is stored and processed. Full cost at our volume. The exit: data return, model switching, ownership of our evaluation set. And who in the business owns the metric.

    7. A pilot shows a 12% fall in handling time, but the comparison team was the most senior. What's wrong and how do you fix it?

    Selection bias (F2.6): senior staff differ from average, so the gain may reflect their baseline speed and may not transfer. Compare against a matched group over the same period, or stagger the rollout at random, and report an interval.
