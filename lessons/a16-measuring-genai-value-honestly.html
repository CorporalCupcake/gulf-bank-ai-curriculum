    Making GenAI Pay: Honest Value, the First Five Use Cases and the Operating Model

    Why most pilots never reach production, how to count the benefit and the cost without flattering yourself, how to choose the first five use cases at a Gulf bank, and whether the AI team should sit in the centre, in the business lines, or partly with a vendor.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA GenAI pilot succeeds when it answers a question the bank cares about, so the winners are the use cases with a real baseline, a measured cashable benefit, a risk the bank can carry and an owner in the business, and the operating model is just the structure that keeps those four things true after the demo.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank gives 800 staff a GenAI assistant for a quarter. The survey says people save about two hours a week. Multiply: 800 people, 2 hours, 46 weeks, AED 150 an hour loaded cost. That is AED 11 million a year. The executive committee approves. Six months later the programme quietly shrinks.

    The number was gross, like quoting a salary before tax, rent and bills. Only some people kept using the tool. Those who did spent part of the "saved" time checking its output. Saved time is not saved money unless someone acts on it: fewer contractors, more cases closed. And the bill was missing: seats for everyone, integration, validation, running costs. Counted properly, the benefit is about AED 1.8 million against AED 2.9 million of cost. It loses money.

    That gap between demo number and audited number explains most stalled pilots. The rest is poor selection: no data, risk brought in late, a volunteer instead of a budget-holding owner.

    Three questions a board will ask, one per topic:

    • A16.1, measuring. Is it worth it in money, after adoption, checking and cost? Why do pilots die between "it works" and "it runs"?
    • A16.2, choosing. Of a hundred ideas, which five first? Score value, feasibility, risk and visibility together, and build a portfolio, not a ranking.
    • A16.3, structuring. Who builds, owns and pays? Centre of excellence, hub-and-spoke or distributed, and build, buy or partner per capability.
    Hold onto thisBefore any pilot, write one line: what number moves, who owns it, what it is today (the baseline, C1.3), and what it costs to run for a year. If any of the four is missing, it is a demonstration. That is fine, but don't promise a return.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring GenAI value honestly (A16.1)

    Annual net value is the benefit actually realised, minus the full cost of keeping it running:

    net=users·adoption·hours saved·(1−checking share)·realisation·AED per hour−total cost of ownership

    Every factor except users and AED per hour is a place where pilots overstate. Adoption: share still using it after the novelty fades (0.55 means 45% of seats sit idle). Checking share: fraction of saved time spent verifying. Realisation: share of freed hours finance would recognise, such as a vacancy not filled or volume absorbed without hiring. The rest is real relief for people, not a business case. Misreading to avoid: 2 hours saved is not AED 300 earned.

    Three habits keep the number honest.

    • Measure behaviour, not opinion. Self-reported savings are generous, like asking drivers how fast they go. Use system data: handling time, tickets closed per day, workflow timestamps. Where possible, compare matched teams with and without the tool over the same period (F3.6; variance-reduced tests help with small samples).
    • Count the full cost. Licences or inference, integration, evaluation and monitoring (A4), model risk validation, security review, support, and the people who maintain prompts and retrieval indexes. Inference is often the smaller part. Spread the one-off build over its useful life.
    • Separate three kinds of benefit. Cashable: cost avoided or revenue earned, visible in a budget line. Capacity: time freed, valuable only if redeployed. Risk: fewer errors or better controls, valued as expected loss avoided, hard to prove. Show separate columns. Selling capacity as cash is what disappoints.

    Why pilots die. Failure statistics in circulation measure different things, so treat them as a mood, not a benchmark. The reported causes are consistent and mostly not about the model.

    Six gates is a chain, like six traffic lights in a row. If each passes 70% of the time, only 12% get through all six. That is what any funnel does, not a special GenAI problem. Lifting one gate from 70% to 90% helps; lifting all of them by choosing better pilots helps far more.

    Choosing the first five use cases (A16.2)

    The first five do more than deliver their own value. They shape the bank's view of AI, build the platform pieces later cases reuse, and earn or burn goodwill with risk and compliance. Choose a portfolio, like a football squad, not the five highest scorers in one position.

    Score each candidate 1 to 5 on four axes:

    • Value. Money or capacity at stake, counted honestly. Large and measurable beats large and vague.
    • Feasibility. Is the data accessible and clean? Do current models suit the task, with errors a human can catch? Is there a system to integrate with? Can a team ship in two or three quarters?
    • Risk (5 is most dangerous). Customer harm, regulatory exposure, data sensitivity, reputational damage in Arabic or English. Reversible, human-reviewed uses score low; autonomous customer decisions score high.
    • Visibility. Will executives, staff or customers feel it? A visible early win buys cover for harder work; a visible failure does the opposite.

    A weighted score is a conversation tool, not an oracle. It makes the room argue about a number rather than a mood, and shows which weights change the answer.

    After scoring, apply three portfolio rules.

    • Mix the risk. At least two internal, human-reviewed cases (staff copilot, call-centre assist) that ship in a quarter; one or two with real money attached (fraud or scam triage, KYC document processing); at most one customer-facing, launched narrowly with a fallback. No autonomous customer decisions in the first five.
    • Share the plumbing. Prefer cases that build reusable parts: retrieval over bank documents, an evaluation harness, a gateway with logging and guardrails (A15), Arabic handling. The third case should cost less than the first.
    • Check owner and data. Each case needs a business owner with a budget line and a data source reachable within weeks. If either is "we'll find out", it is not a first-five case.

    Regional specifics: bilingual Arabic and English quality and dialects affect customer-facing and call-centre cases (A14). Data residency and where the model runs may rule options in or out (A11, P4). Central bank expectations for AI are still developing, so confirm the current text with compliance (A12). For Islamic finance products, generated explanations must match approved Sharia-board wording, so retrieve from approved text rather than generate freely.

    AI operating models (A16.3)

    The operating model decides where AI people sit, who sets priorities and who owns what is built. Three standard shapes:

    ModelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
    Centre of excellence (CoE)One central team holds talent, standards, platform and backlog. Business lines send requests.Scarce talent used well; common standards; quick to start.Bottleneck and ivory tower; business lines feel unowned; benefits don't land in the business.
    Hub and spokeCentral hub owns platform, standards, risk tooling. Embedded teams in business lines build use cases on it.Business ownership with central quality; reuse; scales.Needs a real platform and a clear hub-spoke contract; reporting lines cause friction.
    Distributed / federatedEach business line runs its own AI team with light central guidance.Close to the problem; fast where talent exists.Duplicated effort; inconsistent controls; talent spread thin; no single story for the regulator.

    The common path is CoE first, hub-and-spoke later. The centre builds the first cases, platform and rules; delivery then moves outwards while platform, standards and risk tooling stay central. The trigger is a queue: when the CoE backlog exceeds a quarter and business leaders start hiring their own people, formalise the spokes before shadow teams appear.

    Build, buy or partner is decided capability by capability:

    Generic productivity (office, code)BuyCommodity; governance is the work (A15).

    Three questions settle most debates. Is this where we differentiate? If not, buy. Can we run and validate it ourselves? If not, a build becomes shelfware. What is the exit? Contracts, data return and the ability to swap models matter because the model market changes quarterly.

    Whatever the structure, some roles must exist: a business product owner accountable for the number; a platform owner for shared tooling; model risk and compliance partners from scoping; and a single intake forum that applies the A16.2 scoring and stops zombie projects at the 90-day review. Fund the platform and first cases centrally, then have business lines fund scaling once benefit is measured, so owners have something at stake.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These recur in business cases and steering decks, from people and AI tools alike. Each has a quick check.

    • Hours saved presented as money saved

      "2 hours a week × 800 staff = AED 11 million." Nobody will cash it.

      Spot itAsk which budget line falls or which output rises. If "capacity", label it so.
    • Self-reported benefit

      The survey says people feel faster. Studies of coding assistants found self-perception and measured time can diverge.

      Spot itAsk which system timestamp or count backs it, and whether there is a comparison group.
    • Pilot economics extrapolated to scale

      A hand-picked team, free licences, a dedicated engineer. Then 10,000 users, full price, no engineer.

      Spot itCompare pilot and scale conditions line by line: who was in it, who paid, who supported.
    • Ignoring cost beyond the licence

      The case has seats but not validation, monitoring, integration or the people who fix retrieval.

      Spot itAsk for year-two cost with no project team. Who pays then?
    • Choosing by visibility alone

      The customer chatbot goes first because the CEO wants to see it. Risk is highest, data messiest, and an early public failure sets the programme back.

      Spot itCheck whether the first five include internal, reviewable cases that ship in a quarter.
    • Risk brought in at the end

      Compliance sees the project at go-live and asks for six months of evidence.

      Spot itAsk when risk, security and data protection first saw the scope. After build means delay.
    • No stop rule

      Pilots drift for a year because stopping feels like failure.

      Spot itAsk for the date and metric at which this pilot is stopped, scaled or redesigned.
    • An org chart, not a funding model

      Boxes are drawn, but business owners have no budget or number, so they ignore AI as someone else's service.

      Spot itAsk who is accountable for each case's benefit, and whose budget it sits in.
    • Building what you should buy, buying what you should own

      A team trains a custom model for a commodity task; elsewhere the bank signs a lock-in contract covering its evaluation data and prompts.

      Spot itAsk: do we differentiate here, can we run and validate it, what is the exit?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. 500 users claim 3 hours a week saved, at AED 120 an hour, 46 weeks. Adoption is 60%, 20% of the time is spent checking, and 50% of freed hours are cashable. What is the honest benefit?

    Claimed: 500 × 3 × 46 × 120 = AED 8.28 million. Honest: 500 × 0.6 = 300 users; × 3 = 900 h; × 0.8 = 720; × 0.5 = 360 h a week; × 46 × 120 = AED 1.987 million, about 24% of the headline.

    2. Five gates are each passed 80% of the time. What share of pilots reach production, and how many from 30?

    0.8⁵ = 0.328, so about 33%, roughly 10 of 30. Lifting one gate to 100% gives 41%. Lifting all five to 90% gives 59%. Broad improvement beats fixing one gate.

    3. What is the difference between cashable, capacity and risk benefit? Give one banking example of each.

    Cashable: a contractor contract not renewed, or case volume absorbed without hiring. Capacity: analysts summarise less and investigate more, valuable only if work is redeployed. Risk: fewer missed sanctions hits, valued as expected loss avoided. Show them separately so capacity isn't treated as cash.

    4. Why not simply rank use cases by value and take the top five?

    Top-value cases are often the riskiest and least feasible (an autonomous credit agent). The first five must also build shared plumbing, earn risk and compliance trust, and ship quickly. Score all four axes, then check the mix: two or more quick internal wins, one or two with money attached, at most one customer-facing, each with an owner and accessible data.

    5. Your CoE has a nine-month backlog and two business lines have started hiring their own data scientists. What do you do?

    Move towards hub and spoke before shadow teams entrench. Keep platform, standards, evaluation and risk tooling central; let business lines own delivery with embedded product owners and central templates. Have lines fund scaling their own cases, and keep one intake forum so risk and the regulator see the whole portfolio.

    6. A vendor offers a customer-service assistant that does well in a demo. What do you ask before treating it as a pilot?

    Performance on a sample of our real Arabic and English conversations. Where data is stored and processed. The evidence a validator can get. Full cost at our volume, not demo pricing. The exit: data return, model switching, ownership of our evaluation set. And who in the business owns the metric.

    7. A pilot shows a 12% fall in handling time, but the comparison team was the most senior. What's wrong and how do you fix it?

    Selection bias (F2.6): senior staff differ from average, so the effect may not transfer and part of the gain may be their baseline speed. Compare against a matched group over the same period, or randomise which teams get the tool first with a staggered rollout, and report an interval.
