    Making GenAI Pay: Honest Value, the First Five Use Cases and the Operating Model

    Why most pilots never reach production, how to count the benefit and the cost without flattering yourself, how to choose the first five use cases at a Gulf bank, and whether the AI team should sit in the centre, in the business lines, or partly with a vendor.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA GenAI pilot succeeds when it answers a question the bank cares about, so the winners are the use cases with a real baseline, a measured cashable benefit, a risk the bank can carry and an owner in the business, and the operating model is just the structure that keeps those four things true after the demo.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank gives 800 staff a GenAI assistant for a quarter. The pilot survey comes back warm: people say it saves them about two hours a week. Multiply it out: 800 people, two hours, 46 working weeks, AED 150 an hour loaded cost. That is AED 11 million a year. A sponsor takes it to the executive committee. Six months later the programme is quietly shrunk, and nobody can say why.

    The number was not a lie. It was gross. Only some people kept using the tool. Those who did spent part of the "saved" time checking its output. Time saved is not money saved unless someone does something with it: fewer contractors, more cases closed, a faster turnaround that customers notice. And the bill was never in the slide: seats for everyone, integration, a validation team, someone to run it. Counted properly, the same pilot is worth about AED 1.8 million of benefit against AED 2.9 million of cost. It loses money.

    That gap between a demo number and an audited number is most of the story of why GenAI pilots stall. The rest is that nobody chose the pilot with production in mind: the data was not there, risk was brought in at the end, the business owner was a volunteer rather than a budget holder.

    This lesson has three parts that belong together, and each answers a question a board will actually ask.

    • A16.1, measuring honestly. Is it worth it, in money, after adoption, checking and cost? Why do pilots die between "it works" and "it is running"?
    • A16.2, choosing. With a hundred ideas, which five do we do first? A good list scores value, feasibility, risk and visibility together, and is a portfolio, not a ranking.
    • A16.3, structuring. Who builds, who owns, who pays? Centre of excellence, hub-and-spoke or distributed teams, and for each capability, build, buy or partner.
    Hold onto thisBefore any pilot, write down in one line: what number moves, who owns that number, what it is today (the baseline, C1.3), and what it costs to run for a year. If any of the four is missing, the pilot is a demonstration, which is fine, but then call it that and don't promise a return.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring GenAI value honestly (A16.1)

    Value has a simple skeleton. Whatever the use case, annual net value is the benefit that is actually realised, minus everything it costs to keep running:

    net=users·adoption·hours saved·(1−checking share)·realisation·AED per hour−total cost of ownership

    Every factor except "users" and "AED per hour" is a place where pilots overstate. Adoption is the share still using the tool after the novelty fades. Checking share is the fraction of the saved time spent reading, correcting and verifying. Realisation is the share of freed hours that turn into something the finance team would recognise: a vacancy not filled, volume absorbed without hiring, a cheaper vendor contract, extra revenue. The rest is real relief for people but not a business case.

    Three habits make the number honest.

    • Measure behaviour, not opinion. Self-reported time savings are generous. Compare against a baseline measured the same way: handling time from the case system, tickets closed per day, memo turnaround from workflow timestamps. Where you can, run a controlled comparison (F3.6; variance-reduced tests help when samples are small): some teams get the tool, matched teams do not, same period.
    • Count the full cost. Licences or inference, integration, the evaluation and monitoring work (A4), model risk validation, security review, support, and the people who maintain prompts and retrieval indexes. Inference is often the smaller part. Include the one-off build spread over its useful life.
    • Separate three kinds of benefit. Cashable (cost avoided or revenue earned, visible in a budget line), capacity (time freed, valuable only if redeployed), and risk (fewer errors or better controls, valued as expected loss avoided, hard to prove). Show them in separate columns. A programme that sells capacity as cash is the one that disappoints.

    Why pilots die. The failure statistics in circulation measure different things (see Part 4), but the causes people report are consistent, and they are mostly not about the model.

    Notice the arithmetic of a chain. If each gate is passed 70% of the time, six gates pass 12% of the time. That is not a story about GenAI being unusually hard. It is what any six-step funnel does. It also tells you where to work: lifting one gate from 70% to 90% matters, but lifting all of them by choosing better pilots matters far more. The next simulation lets you see this.

    Choosing the first five use cases (A16.2)

    The first five use cases carry more than their own value. They set the bank's view of what AI is, they build the first platform pieces everyone else will reuse, and they either earn or burn goodwill with risk and compliance. So choose a portfolio, not the five highest scores.

    Score every candidate on four axes, each from 1 to 5:

    • Value. Money or capacity at stake, using the honest method above. Large and measurable beats large and vague.
    • Feasibility. Is the data accessible and clean? Does the task suit current models, with errors that a human can catch? Is there a system to integrate with? Can a team ship it within two or three quarters?
    • Risk (scored so that 5 is the most dangerous). Customer harm, regulatory exposure, data sensitivity, reputational damage if it says something wrong, in Arabic or English. Reversible, human-reviewed uses sit low; autonomous decisions affecting customers sit high.
    • Visibility. Will executives, staff or customers see and feel it? Visibility is not vanity. A visible early win buys political cover for harder work. A visible failure does the opposite.

    A simple weighted score is a conversation tool, not an oracle. Its use is that it forces the room to disagree about a number rather than about a mood, and shows which weights change the answer.

    After scoring, apply three portfolio rules before you commit.

    • Mix the risk. At least two internal, human-reviewed use cases (staff copilot, call-centre assist) that can ship in a quarter; one or two with real money attached (fraud or scam triage, KYC document processing); at most one that is customer-facing, launched narrowly and with a fallback. Avoid autonomous decisions about customers in the first five.
    • Share the plumbing. Prefer cases that build reusable parts: a retrieval layer over bank documents, an evaluation harness, a gateway with logging and guardrails (A15), Arabic handling. The third use case should be cheaper than the first.
    • Check the owner and the data. Each case needs a business owner with a budget line and a data source you can reach within weeks. If either is "we'll find out", it is not a first-five case.

    The region adds specifics. Bilingual Arabic and English performance and dialect handling affect customer-facing and call-centre cases (A14). Data residency and the choice of where the model runs may rule options in or out (A11, P4). The central bank expectations for AI use in licensed institutions are developing, so confirm the current text with compliance rather than relying on memory (A12). Islamic finance products add a layer: any generated explanation of a product must match the approved Sharia-board wording, which makes retrieval from approved text, and not free generation, the sensible design.

    AI operating models (A16.3)

    The operating model answers where AI people sit, who decides priorities, and who owns what is built. There are three standard shapes.

    ModelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
    Centre of excellence (CoE)One central team holds the talent, standards, platform and the backlog. Business lines send requests.Scarce talent used well; common standards; easy to govern; quick to start.Becomes a bottleneck and an ivory tower; business lines feel unowned; benefits do not land in the business.
    Hub and spokeA central hub owns platform, standards, risk tooling and shared components. Embedded teams in business lines build use cases using them.Business ownership with central quality; reuse; scales.Needs real platform and a clear contract between hub and spokes; unclear reporting lines cause friction.
    Distributed / federatedEach business line hires and runs its own AI team with light central guidance.Close to the problem; fast where talent exists.Duplicated effort; inconsistent controls and tooling; talent spread thin; hard to give the regulator one story.

    A common path is CoE first, hub-and-spoke later. The early centre builds the first use cases, the platform and the rules; as the business lines gain capability and demand, ownership of delivery moves outwards while platform, standards and risk tooling stay central. The trigger for the shift is a queue: when the CoE backlog is longer than a quarter and business leaders start hiring on their own, formalise the spokes before shadow teams appear.

    Cutting across the structure is the build, buy or partner decision, made capability by capability:

    LayerUsuallyWhy
    Foundation modelBuy access (API or hosted), occasionally self-host open weightsTraining your own is not a bank's business; choice is about cost, latency, residency and exit options (M2).
    Generic productivity (office, code)BuyCommodity; governance is the work (A15).
    Retrieval, evaluation, gateway, guardrailsBuild on open components, or buy a platformThis is the reusable plumbing and where the bank's standards live. Own the evaluation set whatever you buy.
    Core use cases on bank data (fraud, credit, AML)Build, or partner with a vendor that integrates deeplyThe edge is in your data and your processes; a vendor product trained elsewhere may not fit local patterns.
    Arabic or regional specialitiesPartner, or use regional modelsScarce skills and models (A14, A11).

    Three questions settle most build-versus-buy debates. Is this where we differentiate? If not, buy. Can we run and validate it ourselves? If not, a build will become shelfware. What is the exit? Contracts, data return and the ability to swap models matter more as the model market changes quarterly.

    Whatever structure you pick, a few roles must exist somewhere: a product owner in the business accountable for the number; a platform owner for shared tooling; model risk and compliance partners involved from scoping; and a single intake and prioritisation forum that applies the scoring from A16.2 and stops zombie projects at the 90-day review. Funding matters as much as boxes: central funding for the platform and first cases, business-line funding for scaling once the benefit is measured, so owners have something at stake.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Annual planning and business cases. Finance asks for a return; you bring a net number with adoption, checking and realisation shown separately.
    • Steering committees. Which pilots continue, which stop. A stop rule agreed at the start makes this painless.
    • Vendor selection. Vendor demos are always pilots in best conditions. The build, buy or partner questions and the exit terms decide the real cost.
    • Regulator and audit conversations. A portfolio with owners, evidence and a clear inventory is easier to defend than a scatter of enthusiastic teams.
    • Talent and organisation. Deciding whether new hires sit in the centre or the lines is the operating-model decision made concrete.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The evidence on enterprise GenAI value is real but messy. Headline failure rates are quoted everywhere, measure different things, and often come from vendors or surveys with self-selected respondents. Use them as a mood, not as a benchmark. The better evidence is the repeated pattern of causes.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026. Searches were light; several items are secondary reports of a primary study and are marked as such. Verify before quoting a figure externally.

    1. 2024
      RAND: most AI projects fail, for ordinary reasons

      RAND interviewed experienced practitioners and reported that more than 80% of AI projects fail, about twice the rate of non-AI IT projects, citing misunderstood problems, missing data, chasing technology over problems, and weak infrastructure. The causes are management and data causes, not model causes.

      RAND Research Report RR-A2680-1, 2024 (reported via secondary summaries; check the original)
    2. 2025
      MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide"

      Widely quoted as finding that about 95% of organisations see no measurable P&L return from GenAI pilots. The study has been criticised for its sample and definitions, and many later articles simply repeat it. Treat it as a hypothesis the field keeps testing, not a measured base rate.

      MIT NANDA, 2025 (reported via secondary sources; I did not check the primary document)
    3. 2025
      Gartner on agentic projects

      Gartner predicted that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing cost, unclear value and weak risk controls. A forecast, from memory of the announcement; confirm the wording.

      Gartner press release, June 2025 (cited from memory)
    4. 2026
      Rollouts being cancelled

      A vendor survey (AvePoint) reported that 40.7% of organisations cancelled a GenAI assistant rollout in 2026, up from 31.7% in 2025. Vendor survey; useful as direction, not as a number to defend.

      AvePoint, State of AI 2026 (reported via a secondary summary)
    5. 2026
      Gulf banks move from pilots to execution

      Finastra's February 2026 research on UAE institutions reported a shift from experimentation to execution, with about half using AI to improve accuracy and cut errors and under half citing operating cost. Large UAE banks describe dedicated analytics and AI units: Emirates NBD publishes an approach to adopting AI that includes an Advanced Analytics centre of excellence. Press reports of bank-wide copilot rollouts are secondary.

      Finastra press release, 2026; Emirates NBD, Our Approach to Adopting AI (secondary reports of details)
    6. 2026
      Regulatory guidance on AI in licensed institutions

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These recur in business cases and steering decks, written by people and by AI tools. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Hours saved presented as money saved

      "2 hours a week × 800 staff = AED 11 million." Nobody will cash it.

      Spot itAsk which budget line falls, or which output rises. If the answer is "capacity", label it so.
    • Self-reported benefit

      The survey says people feel faster. Studies of coding assistants and other tools have found self-perception and measured time can diverge.

      Spot itAsk what system timestamp or count backs the claim, and whether there is a comparison group.
    • Pilot economics extrapolated to scale

      A hand-picked team, free licences and a dedicated engineer. Then 10,000 users, full price and no engineer.

      Spot itCompare pilot conditions with scale conditions line by line: who was in it, who paid, who supported.
    • Ignoring cost beyond the licence

      The case includes seats and not validation, monitoring, integration or the people who fix retrieval.

      Spot itAsk for the cost of year two with no project team. Who pays then?
    • Choosing by visibility alone

      The customer chatbot goes first because the CEO wants to see it. Risk is highest, data is messiest, and an early public failure sets the programme back.

      Spot itCheck whether the first five contain any internal, reviewable cases that can ship in a quarter.
    • Risk brought in at the end

      Compliance sees the project at go-live and asks for evidence that takes six months.

      Spot itAsk when risk, security and data protection first saw the scope. If after build, expect delay.
    • No stop rule

      Pilots drift for a year because stopping feels like failure.

      Spot itAsk for the date and the metric at which this pilot is stopped, scaled or redesigned.
    • An operating model that is a org chart, not a funding model

      Boxes drawn, but business owners have no budget and no number, so they treat AI as a service they can ignore.

      Spot itAsk who is accountable for the benefit of each case, and whose budget it sits in.
    • Building what you should buy, buying what you should own

      A team trains a custom model for a commodity task; elsewhere the bank signs a lock-in contract for its evaluation data and prompts.

      Spot itAsk: is this where we differentiate, can we run and validate it, and what is the exit?
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The pilot numbers say 11 million dirhams of time saved. Counted the way finance would count it, allowing for who keeps using it, the time spent checking, how much of the freed time turns into real savings, and the full running cost, we're near break-even or below on this case. That doesn't mean stop. It means we pick the three or four cases where a named owner can show a number in a budget line, start with the internal ones that can go live this quarter, and review at 90 days with a rule for stopping.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd define value before building: a baseline from system data, a named owner, and a controlled comparison where we can. I'd separate cashable, capacity and risk benefits, and count adoption, verification time and total cost of ownership including evaluation, monitoring and validation. For selection I'd score value, feasibility, risk and visibility, then build a portfolio that mixes internal low-risk cases with one or two cases with money attached. Risk and validation join at scoping so the evidence package exists when we need it.”
    “Why do most GenAI pilots fail to reach production?”

    Mostly not because the model is bad. The usual reasons are an undefined or unowned value, a demo that did not meet real data, risk and validation brought in late, a tool that doesn't fit the workflow, and poor adoption. Each gate passes maybe 60 to 80% of the time, so six in a row leave a small fraction. Fix it by choosing pilots with an owner, a baseline and risk involvement from day one. I'd also say that quoted failure rates like 80% or 95% are of uncertain quality, and I wouldn't lean on them.

    “Our users say it saves them two hours a week. Isn't that a strong case?”

    It's a good sign, not a case. I'd check three things: how many keep using it after the first months, how much of the saved time goes on checking, and whether the freed time turns into something the business can count. Then I'd compare against full cost. If we can measure handling time or throughput against a matched group, that's far stronger than a survey.

    “Which five use cases would you start with?”

    I'd want a mix. Two internal human-reviewed ones that can ship quickly, such as a policy and procedures assistant for staff and call-centre summarisation. One or two where money is measurable, such as fraud or scam alert triage or KYC document processing. And at most one customer-facing case, launched narrowly with a fallback. Final choice depends on owners and data access. I'd keep autonomous customer decisions out of the first wave.

    “Should we set up a central AI team or let each business line build its own?”

    Start central to get scarce talent, a shared platform and common controls, then move delivery out to embedded teams as demand and capability grow, keeping platform, standards and risk tooling at the centre. Pure central teams become bottlenecks; pure distributed teams duplicate work and give the regulator an inconsistent story. The best trigger to shift is a growing backlog and business lines hiring on their own.

    “Build, buy or partner?”

    By layer. Buy foundation-model access and commodity productivity tools. Own the evaluation set, the retrieval and gateway plumbing, and the use cases on our own data where we differentiate. Partner for scarce skills, such as Arabic specialities, or fast starts. For any vendor I want an exit plan: data return, ability to swap models, and no lock-in on our prompts and evaluations.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. 500 users claim 3 hours a week saved, at AED 120 an hour, 46 weeks. Adoption is 60%, 20% of the time is spent checking, and 50% of freed hours are cashable. What is the honest benefit?

    Claimed: 500 × 3 × 46 × 120 = AED 8.28 million. Honest: 500 × 0.6 × 3 × 0.8 × 0.5 × 46 × 120 = 500 × 0.6 = 300; × 3 = 900; × 0.8 = 720; × 0.5 = 360 hours a week; × 46 = 16,560; × 120 = AED 1.987 million, about 24% of the headline.

    2. Five gates are each passed 80% of the time. What share of pilots reach production, and how many from 30?

    0.8⁵ = 0.328, so about 33%, or roughly 10 of 30. Raising only one gate from 80% to 100% gives 41%. Raising all five from 80% to 90% gives 59%. Broad improvement beats fixing one gate.

    3. What is the difference between cashable, capacity and risk benefit? Give one banking example of each.

    Cashable: a contractor contract not renewed, or case volume absorbed without hiring. Capacity: analysts spend less time summarising and more on investigating, valuable only if work is actually redeployed. Risk: fewer missed sanctions hits, valued as expected loss avoided. Show them separately so the sponsor doesn't treat capacity as cash.

    4. Why not simply rank use cases by value and take the top five?

    Highest-value cases are often the riskiest and least feasible (an autonomous credit decision agent, say). The first five also have to build shared plumbing, earn trust with risk and compliance, and ship quickly. So score value with feasibility, risk and visibility, then check the mix: at least two quick internal wins, one or two with money attached, at most one customer-facing, each with an owner and accessible data.

    5. Your CoE has a nine-month backlog and two business lines have started hiring their own data scientists. What do you do?

    Move towards hub and spoke before shadow teams entrench. Keep platform, standards, evaluation and risk tooling central; let business lines own delivery with embedded product owners and the central team's templates. Agree funding so lines pay for scaling their own cases, and keep one intake forum so the portfolio stays visible to risk and the regulator.

    6. A vendor offers a customer-service assistant that does well in a demo. What do you ask before treating it as a pilot?

    Performance on a sample of our real Arabic and English conversations. Where data is stored and processed. The evidence they can give a validator. The full cost at our volume, not demo pricing. The exit: data return, ability to switch models, ownership of our evaluation set. And who in the business owns the metric.

    7. A pilot shows a 12% fall in handling time, but the comparison team was the most senior. What's wrong and how do you fix it?

    Selection bias (F2.6): senior staff differ from the average, so the effect may not transfer, and any gain could be partly their baseline speed. Compare against a matched group over the same period, or randomise which teams get the tool first with a staggered rollout, and report an interval.
