    Where the Model Runs: Hybrid and On-Premises Deployment, and Training Across Many GPUs

    Why data residency and regulator approval often decide where a model may run, what hybrid really means in practice, when owning GPUs beats renting them, and how training is split across machines when one GPU is not enough.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceIn a Gulf bank, classification, regulation and latency decide where a model may run before cost does, so the usual answer is hybrid (sensitive and latency-critical work inside the bank or an approved in-country environment, bursty or non-sensitive work on elastic compute); and when you need many GPUs, data parallelism with sharded memory is the workhorse, limited by network bandwidth rather than arithmetic.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist wants to fine-tune an open-weight model on call-centre transcripts. Before any modelling, three questions arrive from people she has not met yet. Information security: do the transcripts contain names, account numbers or voices, and where would the GPUs sit? Compliance: is the provider approved, may the data leave the country, who holds the keys? Infrastructure: four on-premises GPUs are shared with fraud, so which week do you want them?

    None is about model quality, and all decide whether the project happens. Where a workload runs is an early design decision, constrained from outside the data science team.

    The mirror image: when one GPU is not enough, you split training across several. Data parallelism gives every GPU a full copy of the model and a different slice of the data, then averages what they learn. The limit is mostly moving numbers between machines, not computing.

    Shared idea: moving data, and getting permission to move it, costs more than computing. Like a heavy piece of furniture, it is easier to build the room around it than to carry it. Put compute next to the data.

    Hold onto thisThree questions settle most placement: how sensitive is the data, how fast must the answer return, how steady is the load? Sensitive, fast, steady: on-premises. Public or pseudonymised, slow, bursty: rented elastic compute in an approved place. Most banks use both, with a rule for which workload goes where.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What forces a workload to stay put (P4.3)

    Five forces decide placement, roughly in the order they bite.

    • Data classification. Banks label data (public, internal, confidential, restricted). Card numbers, balances, ID documents and customer voice sit at the top, and policy ties each label to allowed environments. It is the first filter.
    • Residency and outsourcing law. The UAE and Saudi Arabia have personal-data laws with cross-border conditions, and financial regulators add outsourcing, cloud and cyber rules. Using a cloud provider is usually a material outsourcing: approval, exit plan, audit rights. This is the shape from memory; get the current text from compliance or legal.
    • Latency and coupling. A fraud score inside a card authorisation has tens of milliseconds, and the path runs through the bank's own data centres. A trip to a distant region eats the budget and adds a failure mode.
    • Economics and supply. GPUs are scarce, power and cooling are real, and the bank may already own capacity.

    Classification, law and outsourcing, and latency are constraints (pass or fail, like a visa check). Only economics is a trade-off. That is why a cost model alone gives wrong answers in a bank.

    The placement patterns, and a way to choose

    PatternStrengthCost or catch

    The simulation below is a decision aid: constraints first, then comfort, then cost.

    What "on-prem ML" actually requires

    Running models inside the bank means assembling what the cloud gives free.

    • A GPU scheduler (Kubernetes or Slurm) with quotas, so fraud inference is never starved by a student's experiment. Treat GPUs as a budget.
    • Internal mirrors. Production networks often have no internet, so packages, images and model weights come from scanned internal mirrors. This stalls new teams first.
    • Registry, serving and governed data. Tracked and registered models with rollback (P2), serving with batching and a KV cache for language models (M1), and data through paths with lineage (P1). Keys sit in the bank's hardware security modules; who holds them is often what regulators care about most.

    Open-weight models (including Arabic-focused ones, A14) make on-premises language-model work possible. Model size drives the hardware bill, so compression (P3) is a direct lever: 4-bit can fit on one GPU what full precision needed four for.

    What makes hybrid work, and what makes it hurt

    Hybrid gives a clean rule per workload. The hard part is everything crossing the boundary.

    • Data gravity. Moving terabytes is slow, costs egress fees, and every copy is another thing to secure and delete. Move the code to the data. What must cross is usually reduced: aggregated, pseudonymised with keys kept on-premises, or synthetic. Pseudonymised data is still personal data under most regimes (G2).
    • Environments drifting apart. Train in the cloud and serve on-premises, and mismatched libraries or feature code cause training-serving skew (P1). Fix: one container image, built once, promoted through stages.
    • Failure domains. If real-time scoring needs a cloud link, a link outage is a fraud-control outage. Critical paths need a local fallback, even a simpler model.

    The common pattern is a split by phase. Training is bursty and tolerates delay, so it goes where compute is elastic and the data allows. Serving is steady and latency-bound, so it sits inside the bank beside the calling systems.

    Own or rent? Utilisation decides

    A car you own costs the same parked or driven; a taxi costs only per ride. A GPU server is the same: its cost is fixed (purchase spread over its life, plus power, cooling, space and staff), so the cost per useful hour depends on how much of the time it works. Doubling utilisation from 30% to 60% roughly halves it. Break-even is the utilisation where owning matches the rented price: below it, rent; above it, own.

  1. Utilisation is the lie in every on-prem business case. Clusters idle at night, between projects and while waiting for data. 60 to 70% sustained is good. Ask for measured utilisation before buying more.
  2. Mixed workloads shift it. Real-time inference is sized for peak and idles off-peak. Adding queued, interruptible training on the same GPUs raises utilisation and favours owning.
  3. Many bank models need no GPU. Tabular fraud and credit models train on CPUs in minutes. Check the need first.

    4. Training on many GPUs: data parallelism (P4.4)

    Think of four chefs each tasting a different spoonful of the same soup, then agreeing one average verdict before adjusting the recipe. Every GPU holds an identical copy of the model and gets a different slice of each batch. Each works out its gradient (the direction to nudge the weights), then an all-reduce averages them so every copy takes the same step and stays identical.

    This is exact, not approximate: data parallelism is big-batch training, done faster. The cost is the averaging. Adding GPUs barely slows the all-reduce itself, but once workers span machines, the slowest link is the network between them, often 10 to 30 times slower than links inside one machine. In PyTorch it is a few lines on ordinary training code, launched with one process per GPU:

    # launched with: torchrun --nproc_per_node=8 train.py
import torch, torch.distributed as dist
from torch.nn.parallel import DistributedDataParallel as DDP
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torch.utils.data.distributed import DistributedSampler

dist.init_process_group("nccl")            # workers find each other
rank = dist.get_rank()
torch.cuda.set_device(rank % torch.cuda.device_count())
model = DDP(MyModel().cuda())               # wraps the model: same weights everywhere

sampler = DistributedSampler(dataset)       # each rank sees a different slice
for epoch in range(epochs):
    sampler.set_epoch(epoch)                # different shuffle each epoch
    for x, y in DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=32, sampler=sampler):
        loss = loss_fn(model(x.cuda()), y.cuda())
        loss.backward()                     # gradients are all-reduced during this call
        opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

    The library starts reducing the last layers' gradients while earlier layers are still computing. This overlap hides communication behind computation, which is why data parallelism can scale almost linearly on a fast network.

    The global batch has a side effect: 64 GPUs at the same learning rate means a 64 times larger batch. The usual fix is to raise the learning rate with the batch size and warm it up over the first epochs. Beyond some size returns diminish: you pay for 64 GPUs and need nearly as many epochs.

    When the model does not fit: sharding the memory (ZeRO and FSDP)

    Plain data parallelism wastes memory: every GPU holds a full copy of everything. Training state (weights, gradients and the optimiser's running averages) takes about 16 bytes per parameter, so a 7B model needs about 112 GB before activations. One 80 GB GPU cannot hold it, yet 8 GPUs have 640 GB and all store the same 112 GB.

    ZeRO and PyTorch's native version, FSDP, remove that duplication by giving each GPU only its share. Stage 1 shards the optimiser state (cheap, big saving), stage 2 adds gradients, and stage 3 also shards the weights, gathering each layer just in time. On 8 GPUs stage 3 cuts the 7B model to about 14 GB each, for roughly 1.5 times the traffic. It trades memory for communication; the training result is unchanged.

    Other ways to split, and what a bank actually needs

    For the very largest models, other splits are layered on: tensor parallelism (splits single matrix multiplications, so stays in one machine), pipeline parallelism (different layers on different GPUs) and expert parallelism (mixture-of-experts experts on different devices, R1). A bank usually needs far less, and saying so sounds senior:

    • Tabular models (fraud, credit, churn): gradient-boosted trees on CPUs or one GPU. No distributed training.
    • Fine-tuning an open-weight model: usually LoRA-style adapters on 1 to 8 GPUs, often sharded. One node is the common case (M2.2).
    • Pretraining from scratch: rarely a bank's job; a bank consumes the result.

    The advice that saves most money: measure single-GPU throughput, fix the data loader and batch size, and only then add GPUs. A starved job wastes ten GPUs as surely as one.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing by cost before checking constraints

      The spreadsheet says cloud is 30% cheaper, for a workload that may not legally run there.

      Spot itAsk who classified the data and who approved the provider. If "not yet", the comparison is premature.
    • Treating pseudonymised data as safe to export

      Hashed customer IDs ship abroad. Hashes can be reversed by enumeration, and linkage re-identifies people.

      Spot itAsk who holds a mapping key, and whether Legal classed the data as personal.
    • Real-time scoring over a distant link

      The model sits in a cloud region and the authorisation path calls it. A network blip becomes declined cards or missed fraud.

      Spot itLook for the timeout and local fallback. Without one, the critical path has a new single point of failure.
    • Training and serving in different environments

      Different library versions train and serve the model. Predictions differ slightly and no one knows why.

      Spot itReplay sample rows through both and compare scores. Check one container image is promoted.
    • The utilisation fantasy

      The business case assumes 90% use. The fleet runs at 25%.

      Spot itAsk for weekly measured utilisation and the queue times that justify new GPUs.
    • Adding GPUs to a job bound elsewhere

      Throughput barely moves because data loading, a small batch or the network is the bottleneck. Related slips: forgetting to rescale the learning rate, ignoring activation memory, and having no checkpoints.

      Spot itIf GPU utilisation keeps dropping to zero between steps, suspect the loader or the all-reduce. Compare throughput per GPU at 1, 8 and 16, and confirm checkpoints can be restored.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening.

    1. Name the five forces on placement; which are constraints and which a trade-off?

    Classification, residency and law, outsourcing, latency, economics. The first are pass/fail constraints; only economics is a trade-off.

    2. Why does an owned GPU server only beat renting above a certain utilisation, and what should you ask before buying more?

    An owned server costs the same idle or busy; renting costs only when used. Ask for measured utilisation, not the forecast.

    3. Why is data parallelism the same as training with a bigger batch?

    Each GPU works out the average lesson from its own slice, and averaging those gives the average lesson from the whole batch. Only who does the computing changes, not the result.

    4. Why does adding GPUs stop paying off once the job spans machines, and what helps instead?

    Gradients are averaged every step and the slowest link sets the time. Between machines links are far slower than inside one. A faster network, overlap, a bigger per-GPU batch or a smaller model help; more GPUs do not.

    5. Why does scaling often bend sharply from 8 to 16 GPUs?

    Eight GPUs share one machine's very fast links. At 16 the ring crosses the network between machines, and the slowest link sets the all-reduce time.

    6. A 7B model does not fit on one 80 GB GPU for training. What does sharding (ZeRO-3 or FSDP) change, and what does it cost?

    Plain data parallelism stores the full training state (about 112 GB) on every GPU. Sharding gives each GPU only its share, so on 8 GPUs about 14 GB each, before activations. The price is roughly 1.5 times the communication.

    7. Give two reasons to train in an in-country cloud region but serve on-premises.

    Training is bursty and delay-tolerant, so elastic capacity suits it. Serving is steady, latency-sensitive and should not depend on an external link. The cost is keeping environments consistent: one container image.

    8. Fine-tuning goes from 1 to 8 GPUs and is only 2.5 times faster. Name three checks before buying more GPUs.

    GPU idle gaps between steps (data loader), per-GPU batch size (too small and communication dominates), and whether communication overlaps compute.
