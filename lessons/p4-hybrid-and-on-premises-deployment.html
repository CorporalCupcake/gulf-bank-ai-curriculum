    Where the Model Runs: Hybrid and On-Premises Deployment, and Training Across Many GPUs

    Why data residency and regulator approval often decide where a model may run, what hybrid really means in practice, when owning GPUs beats renting them, and how training is split across machines when one GPU is not enough.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceIn a Gulf bank the question "where may this data and this model live?" is answered by classification, regulation and latency before cost, so the normal answer is hybrid (sensitive and latency-critical work inside the bank or in an approved in-country environment, bursty or non-sensitive work where compute is elastic), and when you do need many GPUs, data parallelism with sharded memory is the workhorse, limited by network bandwidth rather than by arithmetic.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist joins a bank and finds an excellent open-weight language model, a free afternoon and a cloud account. She wants to fine-tune the model on call-centre transcripts. Then three questions arrive, usually in this order, usually from people she has not met yet.

    • From information security: do the transcripts contain customer names, account numbers or voices? (Yes.) Where would the GPUs be?
    • From compliance and legal: is that provider approved as an outsourcing arrangement? May this data leave the country? Who holds the encryption keys?
    • From infrastructure: there are four GPUs on-premises, shared with fraud. Which week do you want them?

    None of these is about model quality. All of them decide whether the project happens. That is the point of this lesson. In a regulated bank, where a workload runs is a design decision made early, with constraints that come from outside the data science team. It is not an afterthought you hand to DevOps once the model works.

    The second half of the lesson is the mirror image. Sometimes the constraint is not that you cannot use big compute but that one machine is not enough. A model, or the data it trains on, does not fit on one GPU, or training would take a month. Then you split the work across many GPUs. The simplest split, data parallelism, gives every GPU a full copy of the model and a different slice of the data, and averages the gradients. It is easy to describe and easy to get slightly wrong, and the thing that limits it is mostly moving numbers between machines, not computing.

    Both halves share one idea: compute is cheap compared with moving data and getting permission to move it. Data has gravity (it is slow, risky and sometimes illegal to move), and gradients have a bandwidth bill. Good design puts the compute next to the data and pays for movement only where it must.

    Hold onto thisThree questions settle most placement decisions: how sensitive is the data, how fast must the answer come back, and how steady is the load? Sensitive and fast and steady says on-premises. Public or pseudonymised, slow and bursty says rent elastic compute in an approved place. Most banks end up with both and a clear rule for which workload goes where.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What forces a workload to stay put (P4.3)

    Five forces decide placement. They are listed roughly in the order they bite.

    • Data classification. Banks label data (public, internal, confidential, restricted). Card numbers, account balances, identity documents, AML case files and customer voice sit at the top. Policy ties each label to allowed environments. This is the first filter and it is internal, so you can read it.
    • Residency and transfer law. Both the UAE and Saudi Arabia have personal-data protection laws with conditions on cross-border transfer, and financial regulators add their own expectations for banks. The UAE has a central-bank outsourcing regime and cloud guidance for licensed institutions. Saudi Arabia has SAMA rules on outsourcing and cyber security, a national cybersecurity authority (NCA) set of controls including ones for cloud, and a communications regulator framework for cloud services. Free zones such as DIFC and ADGM have their own data-protection regimes. I am describing the shape from memory: the exact clauses, thresholds and approval steps change and differ between entities, so always get the current text from your compliance or legal team (Part 4 has primary links).
    • Outsourcing and exit. Even where data may sit in a cloud, using a provider is usually a material outsourcing that needs notification or approval, an exit plan, audit rights and concentration-risk thinking. A model that depends on one external API is a dependency the regulator can ask about.
    • Latency and coupling. A fraud score inside a card authorisation has tens of milliseconds, and the path runs through payment switches and core systems that live in the bank's data centres. A round trip to a distant region eats the budget and adds a failure mode in the most critical path the bank has.
    • Economics and supply. GPUs are scarce, power and cooling are real, and the bank may already own capacity. Whether owning beats renting depends on utilisation (below).

    Notice that the first four are constraints (a workload either satisfies them or does not) and only the last is a trade-off. This is why a cost model alone gives wrong answers in a bank.

    What "on-prem ML" actually requires

    Running models inside the bank is not just buying servers. The platform pieces are the same ones cloud gives you for free, and you assemble them yourself:

    • A scheduler for scarce GPUs. Usually Kubernetes with GPU support, or Slurm, with quotas and queues so fraud inference is never starved by a student's experiment. GPUs are the expensive, contended resource; treat them like a budget.
    • Internal mirrors. Production networks often have no internet. Python packages, container images and model weights must come from internal mirrors that security has scanned. This sounds dull and is the first thing that stalls a new team. Pinning versions and weights hashes (see P2) is also a supply-chain control.
    • A model registry and serving layer. The same lifecycle as in the cloud: tracked experiments, registered versions, staged promotion, rollback, and serving for batch or real time (P2.2). For language models, a serving engine with batching and a KV cache (M1).
    • Data access that follows the rules. Features and training sets pulled through governed paths with lineage (P1), not copied to a laptop.
    • Observability and key management. Monitoring for the model and for the GPU fleet, and keys held in the bank's hardware security modules. Who holds the keys is often the question regulators actually care about.

    Two practical consequences. First, open-weight models (several strong ones now exist, including Arabic-focused ones, see A14) are what makes on-premises language-model work possible at all. Second, model size drives the hardware bill, so compression (P3) is a direct lever: a model quantised to 4-bit can fit on one GPU where the full-precision version needed four.

    What makes hybrid work, and what makes it hurt

    Hybrid has a good side and a hard side. The good side is a clean rule per workload. The hard side is everything that crosses the boundary:

    • Data gravity. Moving terabytes between on-premises and a cloud region is slow, costs egress fees in one direction, and each copy is another thing to secure and delete. Prefer moving the code to the data.
    • Crossing safely. What may cross is usually data that has been reduced: aggregated, pseudonymised with keys kept on-premises, or synthetic. Be precise: pseudonymised data is still personal data under most regimes (G2). Techniques such as clean rooms and differential privacy (A11) are for the cases where it must be shared more widely.
    • Two environments drifting apart. If the model is trained in the cloud and served on-premises, the libraries, hardware and feature code must match, or you get training-serving skew (P1). The usual fix is one container image, built once, promoted through stages.
    • Network and identity. Private links, one identity provider, consistent logging. A hybrid system that cannot be audited end to end is the worst of both worlds.
    • Failure domains. If real-time scoring depends on a cloud link, a link outage becomes a fraud-control outage. Critical paths usually need a local fallback, even if it is a simpler model.

    The pattern that appears most often, for good reasons, is a split by phase: train or fine-tune where compute is elastic and the data allows (including in-country cloud on pseudonymised or approved data), and serve inside the bank next to the systems that call the model. Training is bursty and tolerant of delay; serving is steady and latency-bound. They have opposite needs, so they often live in different places.

    Own or rent? Utilisation decides

    A GPU you own costs the same whether it works or sleeps. A GPU you rent costs only while you hold it. So the question is not "which is cheaper per hour?" but "how much of the time will the owned GPU do useful work?". Cost per useful hour for an owned server is its fixed yearly cost divided by the hours it actually works:

    cost per useful hour=capex/life+yearly running costGPUs×8760×u

    u is utilisation, the fraction of the year's hours the GPUs do useful work; 8760 is the hours in a year. Capex is spread over the server's useful life. Running cost covers power, cooling, space, support and the share of staff who run it. Break-even is the u at which this equals the rented price per hour. Below it, renting is cheaper; above it, owning is.

    What the picture hides, and what to say about it:

    • Utilisation is the lie in every on-prem business case. Real clusters idle at nights, between projects and while waiting for data. 60 to 70% sustained is good. Ask for measured utilisation of the existing fleet before agreeing to buy more.
    • Mixed workloads change it. Real-time inference must be provisioned for peak and idles off-peak. Pairing steady serving with queued, interruptible training on the same GPUs raises utilisation and moves the break-even in owning's favour.
    • The rented side has extra costs too: egress, storage, managed-service premiums, and the effort of getting a provider approved. The owned side has a delay: ordering GPUs can take months.
    • Many bank models need no GPU. Tabular fraud and credit models (gradient boosting) train on CPUs in minutes. GPUs matter for deep learning and language models. Check the need before buying the hardware.

    Training on many GPUs: data parallelism (P4.4)

    Now the second topic. You have a model and a lot of data, and one GPU is too slow. The idea behind data parallelism fits in four lines.

    1. Put an identical copy of the model on each of N GPUs (workers).
    2. Give each worker a different slice of the batch. With 32 examples per worker, the global batch is 32 × N.
    3. Each worker computes the gradient on its slice (see F1.3).
    4. All-reduce: average the gradients across workers, so every worker holds the same average. Each then takes the same optimiser step, and the copies stay identical.

    Why this is exactly correct: the gradient of an average loss is the average of the gradients. Averaging the workers' gradients gives precisely the gradient of one big batch of 32 × N examples, computed in parallel. So data parallelism is not an approximation to training with a big batch; it is training with a big batch, done faster.

    The averaging step is where the cost lives. The standard method, ring all-reduce, arranges the workers in a ring and passes chunks of the gradient round it twice. Each worker sends and receives about twice the gradient's size, nearly regardless of N (Patarasuk and Yuan, 2009):

    tcomm≈2(N−1)N·Bbw

    B is the gradient size in bytes (2 bytes per parameter in 16-bit), bw the bandwidth of the slowest link in the ring. The factor 2(N−1)/N is below 2 and barely changes as N grows. Adding GPUs does not make the all-reduce much slower in itself, but when workers span several machines, the slowest link in the ring becomes the network between machines, and that is typically 10 to 30 times slower than the links inside one machine. This ignores latency, which matters for small messages.

    Libraries hide the mechanics. In PyTorch, a data-parallel job is a few lines on top of ordinary training code, launched by a tool that starts one process per GPU:

    # launched with: torchrun --nproc_per_node=8 train.py
import torch, torch.distributed as dist
from torch.nn.parallel import DistributedDataParallel as DDP
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torch.utils.data.distributed import DistributedSampler

dist.init_process_group("nccl")            # workers find each other
rank = dist.get_rank()
torch.cuda.set_device(rank % torch.cuda.device_count())
model = DDP(MyModel().cuda())               # wraps the model: same weights everywhere

sampler = DistributedSampler(dataset)       # each rank sees a different slice
for epoch in range(epochs):
    sampler.set_epoch(epoch)                # different shuffle each epoch
    for x, y in DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=32, sampler=sampler):
        loss = loss_fn(model(x.cuda()), y.cuda())
        loss.backward()                     # gradients are all-reduced during this call
        opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

    Note the comment on backward(). The library starts reducing the gradients of the last layers while earlier layers are still computing theirs. This overlap hides part of the communication behind computation, and it is why well-built data parallelism can scale almost linearly on a fast network.

    One more consequence of the "global batch" idea. If you go from 1 GPU to 64 and keep the learning rate, you are now training with a batch 64 times larger, and the optimiser behaves differently: fewer steps per epoch, less gradient noise. A standard rule is to scale the learning rate with the batch size and warm it up over the first few epochs (Goyal et al., 2017). Beyond some batch size there are diminishing returns: you pay for 64 GPUs and need nearly as many epochs. So data parallelism buys speed up to a point, not unlimited speed.

    When the model does not fit: sharding the memory (ZeRO and FSDP)

    Plain data parallelism has a hidden waste: every GPU holds a full copy of everything. For a model trained in 16-bit with the Adam optimiser, the usual accounting is about 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the weights, 2 for the gradients, and 12 for the optimiser's full-precision copy of the weights plus its two running averages (Rajbhandari et al., 2020). A 7-billion-parameter model needs about 112 GB before counting activations. No single 80 GB GPU can hold that, yet 8 GPUs together hold 640 GB, and they are all storing the same 112 GB.

    ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) removes the redundancy in stages, and PyTorch's FSDP (fully sharded data parallel) is the native version of the last stage:

    • Stage 1 shards the optimiser state across the N GPUs. Cheap, big saving.
    • Stage 2 also shards the gradients.
    • Stage 3 (what FSDP does) also shards the parameters. Each GPU owns 1/N of the weights, and gathers a layer's full weights just in time for its forward or backward pass, then drops them. Memory falls to roughly 16/N bytes per parameter, at the price of extra communication (about 1.5 times the traffic of plain data parallelism).

    The trade is memory for communication. The maths of training is unchanged; it is still the same big-batch gradient step.

    Other ways to split, and what a bank actually needs

    Data parallelism needs the model to fit on a GPU, or to be sharded as above. For the very largest models, other splits are layered on top: tensor parallelism splits individual matrix multiplications across GPUs (needs very fast links, so it stays inside one machine), pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different GPUs and streams micro-batches through, and expert parallelism places the experts of a mixture-of-experts model on different devices (R1). Frontier labs combine all of them. Megatron-LM is the reference for combining them (Narayanan et al., 2021).

    What a bank usually needs is much more modest, and saying so with confidence is part of sounding senior:

    • Tabular models (fraud, credit, churn): gradient-boosted trees on CPUs, or one GPU. No distributed training.
    • Fine-tuning an open-weight model on bank text: usually parameter-efficient methods (LoRA and relatives) on 1 to 8 GPUs, often with sharding so the base model fits. A single node is the common case. Keep the base model in 16-bit or quantised and train small adapters (M2.2 covers when fine-tuning is the right tool).
    • Pretraining a large model from scratch: rarely a bank's job. Sovereign national efforts do it (A11, A14); a bank consumes the result.

    The advice that saves the most money: measure single-GPU throughput first, fix the data loader and the batch size, and only then add GPUs. A job starved by slow data loading or small batches wastes ten GPUs as surely as it wastes one.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the recurring ways placement and distributed-training plans go wrong, with the quickest check for each.

    • Choosing the platform by cost before checking the constraints

      A neat spreadsheet shows the cloud is 30% cheaper, for a workload that may not legally or contractually run there.

      Spot itAsk who classified the data and who approved the provider. If the answer is "not yet", the cost comparison is premature.
    • Treating pseudonymised data as safe to export

      Customer IDs are hashed, so the dataset is shipped abroad for training. Hashes of account numbers can be reversed by enumeration, and linkage with other data re-identifies people.

      Spot itAsk whether anyone holds a key or table that maps back, and whether Legal has classified the data as personal data.
    • Real-time scoring that depends on a distant link

      The model sits in a cloud region and the authorisation path calls it. A network blip becomes declined cards or missed fraud.

      Spot itLook for the timeout and the fallback in the design. If there is no local fallback, the critical path has a new single point of failure.
    • Training and serving in different environments

      The model is trained in one place with one library version and served in another. Predictions differ slightly and no one knows why.

      Spot itReplay a sample of rows through both and compare scores. Check that one container image is promoted through stages.
    • The utilisation fantasy

      A business case for buying GPUs assumes 90% use. The fleet runs at 25%.

      Spot itAsk for measured utilisation of the existing GPUs by week, and for the queue time that justifies new ones.
    • Adding GPUs to a job that is bound elsewhere

      Throughput barely moves with more GPUs because data loading, a small batch or the network is the bottleneck.

      Spot itCheck GPU utilisation during training. If it keeps dropping to zero between steps, the data loader or the all-reduce is the problem. Compare 1, 8 and 16 GPUs on throughput per GPU.
    • Changing the batch size and forgetting the learning rate

      Scaling from 1 to 32 GPUs, the loss behaves differently or training diverges, and people blame the framework.

      Spot itThe global batch is per-GPU batch × GPUs. Check that the learning rate was rescaled and warmed up, and that results at small scale are reproduced.
    • Running out of memory late

      The model fits for the first steps, then fails with out-of-memory on a long sequence or large batch because activations were not counted.

      Spot itBudget for activations and test with the longest realistic input. Use activation checkpointing or smaller micro-batches.
    • Silent divergence between workers

      A bug (different random seeds, different data order that is not intended, a layer excluded from the reduction) leaves worker copies unequal and training quietly degrades.

      Spot itPeriodically checksum parameters across ranks. Log the loss from every rank for a short test run and check they agree.
    • Unreproducible and unsaved distributed runs

      A week-long job dies at day six and there is no checkpoint.

      Spot itConfirm that checkpoints are sharded, saved regularly, restorable on a different number of GPUs, and recorded in the registry.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Where a model runs is decided by three things before cost: how sensitive the data is, what our regulators and policies allow, and how fast the answer must come back. Card scoring stays next to our payment systems. Heavy training and experiments can use approved elastic capacity where the data allows. Owning GPUs only pays if we keep them busy more than about two thirds of the time, so I'd bring you measured utilisation before we buy.”
    To a technical interviewer or platform architect
    “I'd split by phase: train or fine-tune where compute is elastic and the data classification and approvals allow, serve inside the bank behind one promoted container image so training and serving can't drift, with a local fallback on any critical path. For multi-GPU work I'd start with one node and sharded data parallelism, FSDP or ZeRO-3, with communication overlapped, and I'd measure per-GPU throughput as I scale, because past one machine the inter-node network is usually the limit. For fine-tuning, LoRA on a quantised base model usually removes the need for multi-node.”
    “Why not put everything in the cloud? It's cheaper and faster to start.”

    For some workloads it is. But for restricted data, transfer rules and outsourcing approval may rule it out regardless of price, and for real-time scoring the latency and the failure mode matter. I'd classify each workload and put it in the cheapest place that satisfies its constraints, which for a bank is usually a mix. Cost comparison comes after constraints, not before.

    “Why not keep everything on-premises and avoid the compliance work?”

    You can, and for the most sensitive workloads you should. The costs are capital, procurement delay for GPUs, and fixed capacity: you pay for peak whether or not you use it. Bursty work, like experiments and stress tests, is where elastic capacity pays off, if it is permitted. And on-premises still brings its own compliance work: patching, access control, audit.

    “Can we send pseudonymised data to a foreign cloud for training?”

    I would not assume so. Pseudonymised data is generally still personal data, since someone holds the key, and cross-border and outsourcing rules likely apply. That is a question for Legal and Compliance with the current UAE or Saudi text in front of them. What I can do is design so the question rarely arises: train in-country, or use aggregated and synthetic data where it is enough.

    “We have 64 GPUs now, so training should be 64 times faster, right?”

    Only if communication and data loading keep up. Gradients must be averaged every step; inside a machine that is fast, but across machines the network is much slower, so efficiency drops unless the communication is overlapped with compute and the network is fast. Also the effective batch is 64 times larger, so the learning rate needs rescaling and there are diminishing returns. I'd measure throughput at 8, 16 and 32 and decide from the curve.

    “The model doesn't fit on one GPU. Do we need a cluster?”

    Not necessarily. First reduce what must fit: quantise, use 16-bit, fine-tune with adapters. Then shard the state across the GPUs in one machine with FSDP or ZeRO. A cluster is only needed when one machine's memory or time budget is exceeded even after that, which for fine-tuning at a bank is unusual.

    “What happens if the cloud link goes down?”

    For anything on a critical path, nothing should depend on it. Real-time scoring is served locally, possibly with a simpler fallback model, and the link is used for training, batch jobs and non-critical services. I'd test the failure on purpose and write down the degraded mode.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones use only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. Name the five forces that decide where a workload may run, and say which are constraints and which a trade-off.

    Data classification, residency and transfer law, outsourcing and exit requirements, latency and coupling, and economics and supply. The first four are constraints (pass or fail). Only economics is a trade-off, so cost is compared among the options that remain.

    2. An 8-GPU server costs AED 1.5 million with a 4-year life and 20% yearly running costs. Renting costs AED 14.70 per GPU-hour. What is the break-even utilisation?

    Yearly cost = 1,500,000 ÷ 4 + 300,000 = AED 675,000. Hours available = 8 × 8,760 = 70,080. Cost per useful hour at full use = 9.63. Break-even u = 9.63 ÷ 14.70 ≈ 65.5%. Below that, renting is cheaper; above, owning is.

    3. Why is data parallelism exactly equivalent to training with a bigger batch?

    Because the loss is an average over examples, its gradient is the average of per-example gradients. Averaging the per-worker averages (equal slice sizes) gives the gradient of the whole 32 × N batch. The parallelism changes who computes it, not what is computed.

    4. A 1-billion-parameter model has 16-bit gradients. About how long does a ring all-reduce take over a 12.5 GB/s link with 64 workers?

    Gradient size B = 2 GB. Factor 2 × 63 ÷ 64 = 1.97. Time ≈ 1.97 × 2 ÷ 12.5 ≈ 0.32 s. If compute per step is 0.5 s, then without overlap the step is 0.82 s and efficiency about 61%. Overlap can hide much of the 0.32 s.

    5. Why does scaling often bend sharply from 8 to 16 GPUs?

    Eight GPUs fit in one machine, linked by very fast connections. At 16 the ring crosses the network between machines, which is often an order of magnitude slower, so the slowest link in the ring sets the all-reduce time.

    6. How much memory per GPU for the model state of a 7B-parameter model with Adam, mixed precision, on 8 GPUs, under plain data parallelism and under ZeRO-3/FSDP?

    Plain: 16 bytes × 7B = 112 GB per GPU, which does not fit in 80 GB. ZeRO-3/FSDP: 16 ÷ 8 = 2 bytes per parameter, so 14 GB per GPU, before activations. The saving is paid for with about 1.5 times the communication.

    7. Give two reasons a team might train in an in-country cloud region but serve on-premises.

    Training is bursty and tolerates delay, so elastic capacity suits it, while serving is steady and latency-sensitive and sits next to core systems. Also, real-time scoring should not depend on an external link. The cost is keeping the two environments consistent, usually through one container image and the same feature code.

    8. Your fine-tuning job goes from 1 to 8 GPUs and is only 2.5 times faster. List three things to check before buying more GPUs.

    GPU utilisation between steps (a data-loader bottleneck shows as idle gaps), per-GPU batch size (too small and communication dominates), whether communication overlaps with compute and which link the reduction is using. Also check that sharding is not adding avoidable traffic and that the learning rate was rescaled.
