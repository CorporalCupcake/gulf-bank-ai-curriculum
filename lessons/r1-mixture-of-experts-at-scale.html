    Mixture-of-Experts at Scale, and the Recurrent Challengers

    Why a 671-billion-parameter model can cost about as much per token as a 37-billion one, why serving it is still hard, and how state-space hybrids tackle long inputs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA mixture-of-experts model stores many specialists but sends each token through only a few, so you pay compute for the active slice and memory for the whole; state-space hybrids do something similar for context length.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture an operations floor with 256 specialists: sanctions, trade finance, card disputes. A triage clerk hands each case to the eight best-placed people. The floor knows a lot, but any one case uses a small part of it.

    That is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model. A standard transformer (F5.5) sends every token through every parameter. An MoE has many small "experts" and a router that picks a few per token. DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion parameters in total but only 37 billion active per token.

    The catch: all 671 billion must sit in fast memory, since the next token might need any expert. MoE trades compute for memory. State-space hybrids make a similar trade for long inputs.

    Hold onto thisCompute follows the active parameters; memory follows the total. "37B active" does not mean it fits in 37B of memory.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.1 · The sparse layer, the router and fine-grained experts

    In a transformer block, the feed-forward network (FFN) holds most parameters. An MoE layer swaps it for many smaller FFNs (the experts) plus a router, a small layer that scores every expert for the current token and picks the top few. The triage clerk scoring each desk.

    Three design choices matter. Many small experts beat few big ones: Mixtral used 8 large experts, top-2, while DeepSeekMoE splits them finer, so a token gets a precise mix instead of two blunt tools. Shared experts are always on and soak up general knowledge, so routed experts can specialise. Routing style is usually token choice, where each token picks its experts.

    The router is just one linear layer: it turns the token's hidden vector into one score per expert, keeps the top k, and uses their (renormalised) scores as mixing weights. The other experts never run for that token. A cousin, "expert choice", lets each expert pick its tokens instead. Load is balanced by construction, but it is awkward when generating one token at a time, so token choice dominates.

    Why do many small experts help? With 8 experts and top-2 there are 28 possible combinations per layer. With 256 and top-8 there are about 4 × 1014 (try Simulation 1). More combinations mean a token's needs can be met by a finer mix of specialists, at the same compute.

    Reading names: "Qwen-30B-A3B" means 30B total, 3B active ("A" is active). Cost is visible at a glance.

    Load balancing: the part that makes or breaks training

    Left alone, routers collapse. A few experts get slightly better early on, so they receive more tokens and improve further while the rest starve, like one popular restaurant with a queue while the others empty. Hardware makes it worse: a step ends when the busiest expert finishes.

    The classic fix is an extra balancing loss, which competes with quality. DeepSeek-V3 instead adds a bias to each expert's score when picking the top-k, nudged down for overloaded experts and up for underloaded ones. It is almost loss-free, with no interfering gradient. The mixing weights still use the unbiased scores, and V3 keeps a tiny sequence-level balance loss as a safeguard.

    The classic loss (Switch Transformer) penalises uneven token shares. Its knob is the coefficient: too large hurts language quality, too small lets routing collapse. Two related details. Capacity and dropping: older systems gave each expert a fixed buffer and silently dropped overflow tokens, which hurts quality. Router stability: routers can produce huge scores and unstable training, so some models add a small penalty on them (a z-loss).

    Why the compute saving does not come free

    Decoding is mostly reading weights from memory, not multiplying. When memory reads dominate, a step is memory-bound and extra compute is free. Each token picks its own experts, so a batch of tokens touches the union of their choices, and at modest batches nearly all the weights get read.

    A rough rule: 256 experts with top-8 means one token touches 3% of experts, 16 tokens about 40%, and 64 tokens almost 90%. So the MoE's low arithmetic does not show up as speed until batches reach the thousands, across many users. A dense model gets there far sooner, at a batch near 150.

    In practice, training is where MoE shines, because huge batches make the saving real. Serving needs scale: a bank hosting one model for a few hundred staff will often pay the memory cost without the throughput benefit. Large deployments spread experts over many GPUs (expert parallelism) and batch many users. DeepSeek reported pre-training V3 on 14.8 trillion tokens in about 2.79 million H800 GPU-hours, far less than a comparable dense model would be expected to need.

    A small-active MoE can still suit a single machine: a 120B-total, 5B-active model, quantized, can fit one large GPU and decodes fast at batch 1. Fine-tuning has quirks too: adapters on attention layers are easy, but training the experts risks disturbing routing. See R2.1 and R12.1.

    R1.2 · State-space models and hybrids

    A second cost grows with input length. Attention keeps keys and values for every earlier token (the KV cache), so a long contract or call transcript eats memory per user.

    State-space models (SSMs) work like the recurrent networks of F5.4. They read one token at a time and update a fixed-size state, a running summary. Memory stays flat, and decoding costs the same at token 100 or 1,000,000. Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023) made the update selective: what to write or forget depends on the current token, so the model can ignore filler and keep a sanctioned name. Mamba-2 linked SSMs to a form of attention, which allowed faster implementations.

    The weakness is the state's size. Exact retrieval ("what was the second clause's penalty rate?") is easy for attention and harder for a compressed state. So the practical answer is hybrids: mostly SSM layers with a few attention layers for lookup. Jamba (AI21) uses roughly one attention layer per seven or eight Mamba layers, plus MoE. Nemotron-H (NVIDIA) is mostly Mamba-2. Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi) runs attention and SSM side by side in each block, spans 0.5B to 34B parameters, handles up to 256K context and is openly licensed. It is the one to know in the UAE.

    Hybrids are also faster at long lengths, since attention's per-token cost grows with length and an SSM's does not. Attention still wins at exact recall, so designers tune the ratio, and the best ratio is still moving.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at:

    • Quality per unit of compute. Each token uses only the active slice, so for a fixed training or inference arithmetic budget you can store far more knowledge than a dense model.
    • Training efficiency. Huge training batches keep every expert busy, so the saving is real where it matters most.
    • Throughput at scale. With thousands of concurrent requests, a big MoE becomes compute-bound and its cost per token is low.
    • Fast decoding at small batch when memory fits. Only the chosen experts are read, so a small-active model on one big GPU responds quickly.
    • Long inputs (hybrids). A fixed SSM state keeps memory per user flat, so many more long documents fit per GPU.

    Weak at, or do not use when:

    • Low-traffic private serving. You pay memory for all experts but rarely batch enough to use the arithmetic saving.
    • Tight memory. Footprint follows total parameters, so an edge device or a single small GPU favours a dense model.
    • Fine-tuning on small data. Routing can shift and experts overfit, so tuning is touchier than for dense models.
    • Exact recall over long text (pure SSMs). A compressed state loses detail, so clause lookups and copying fail more than with attention.
    • Mature tooling needs. Serving stacks, quantization recipes and debugging are better trodden for dense models.

    Alternatives and how to choose:

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when

    In practice: try a dense model of the size you can host first, and a hosted MoE API second. Judge both on your own task. Self-host an MoE only when expected concurrency is high enough to fill big batches, and size memory from total parameters at your chosen precision. The knobs that matter are the precision (8-bit or 4-bit), the number of GPUs for expert parallelism, and the batch size or concurrency limit. For hybrids, test retrieval on your own long documents before trusting any context-length claim.

    In banking, most staff-assistant and document-summarisation workloads run on hosted MoE models without anyone noticing. Self-hosting arises for data-residency reasons, where a regulator or policy keeps customer text inside the country. Hybrids suit long credit files, contracts and call transcripts, but pair them with retrieval checks.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "671B parameters, but only 37B, so it fits on a small machine"

      Compute is small, memory is not. All experts must be loaded.

      Spot itAsk for the memory footprint in GB at the planned precision, and the GPU count.
    • Quoting the active size as the "real" size

      MoE quality sits between active and total. Neither number is the right comparison.

      Spot itCompare on your own task against a dense model of similar cost.
    • Assuming cheap API prices mean cheap self-hosting

      Prices reflect huge shared batches. A lightly used private deployment leaves GPUs idle.

      Spot itCompare your expected concurrent requests with the batch size where throughput peaks.
    • Reading "experts" as human-style specialists

      Trained experts tend to specialise by token type or syntax, not clean topics like "finance". There is no "sanctions expert".

      Spot itDistrust claims of interpretable domain experts without evidence.
    • "Infinite context" claims for SSMs

      A fixed state cannot store everything. Exact retrieval from the middle of a long document exposes this.

      Spot itRun needle-in-a-haystack lookups on your own documents.
    • Routing collapse and dropped tokens

      Poor balancing leaves experts idle, or drops tokens under tight capacity limits, silently degrading outputs under load.

      Spot itAsk for expert load histograms, whether tokens are dropped, and whether quality was tested at production batch sizes.
    • Quantization and fine-tuning that disturb routing

      Aggressive compression or full fine-tuning can change router decisions and cause losses dense-model experience would not predict.

      Spot itRe-run your own evaluation after any compression or fine-tuning, on your languages and document types.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model is called "Model-80B-A6B". What do the numbers mean, and which sets compute and which sets memory?

    80B total, 6B active per token. Active sets the arithmetic; total sets the memory you must hold.

    2. Why does a shared expert help?

    Every token needs common knowledge. One shared expert holds it once, so routed experts can specialise instead of relearning the basics.

    3. Why does load balancing matter?

    Without it a few experts hog the tokens and the rest starve. The busiest expert sets the speed of the whole step. A per-expert bias nudged after each step fixes this without a competing loss term.

    4. Why are SSMs weaker than attention at exact recall, and how do hybrids cope?

    An SSM squeezes everything into a fixed-size state, so details can be lost. Hybrids keep a few attention layers for lookup and use SSM layers for the cheap bulk.

    5. A vendor quotes very low per-token prices for a large MoE. Why might your private deployment cost more?

    Their price assumes large shared batches that keep every expert busy. A private deployment with few users batches little and leaves expensive GPUs idle.

    6. A team of 30 analysts needs a private assistant. Would you host a large MoE or a mid-sized dense model, and why?

    The dense model. With 30 users the batches are small, so the MoE's arithmetic saving goes unused while its memory bill covers all experts. Start dense; revisit if concurrency grows a lot.

    7. You must answer questions over 500,000-token case files. Would you choose a pure SSM, a hybrid or full attention, and why?

    A hybrid, with retrieval checks. Full attention's cache per user is very large at that length, and a pure SSM may lose exact details. A few attention layers give lookup, and the SSM layers keep memory manageable.
