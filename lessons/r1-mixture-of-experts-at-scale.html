    Mixture-of-Experts at Scale, and the Recurrent Challengers

    Why a model with 671 billion parameters can cost about as much per token as one with 37 billion, what the router actually does, why serving such models is still hard, and how state-space and hybrid models attack the other big cost, long inputs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA mixture-of-experts model stores many specialist sub-networks but sends each token through only a few of them, so you pay compute for the active slice and memory for the whole; state-space hybrids do something similar for context length, replacing most of the attention memory with a fixed-size state.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture an operations floor with 256 specialists: sanctions, trade finance, card disputes, Arabic complaints. A triage clerk reads each case and hands it to the eight best-placed people. The other 248 never touch it. The floor knows a lot, but any one case uses a small part of that knowledge.

    That is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model. A standard transformer (F5.5) sends every token through every parameter. An MoE replaces each layer's big feed-forward block with many small feed-forward "experts" plus a tiny router that picks a few per token. Total parameters hold the knowledge; active parameters per token set the arithmetic you pay for.

    DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion parameters in total and 37 billion active per token (DeepSeek-AI, 2024). Per token it does the arithmetic of a 37B dense model with far more capacity.

    The catch: all 671 billion must sit in fast memory, because the next token might need any expert. MoE trades compute for memory. State-space models and hybrids (Mamba, Jamba, Falcon-H1) make a different trade: a fixed-size state instead of ever-growing attention memory, which matters for very long inputs.

    Hold onto thisCompute follows the active parameters; memory follows the total. Any claim that MoE is cheap or expensive depends on which one you pay for. Misreading to avoid: "37B active" does not mean it fits in 37B of memory.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.1 · The sparse layer, the router and fine-grained experts

    In a transformer block (F5.5, F5.1), attention mixes information across positions, then a feed-forward network (FFN) processes each token alone. The FFN holds most parameters. An MoE layer swaps it for E smaller FFNs (the experts) and a router: one linear layer that scores every expert for the current token. Like a triage clerk scoring each desk, then sending the case to the top few.

    y=∑i∈TopKgi·Experti(x)withs=softmax(Wrx)

    x is the token's hidden vector, Wr the router's weights, s the scores over the E experts, TopK the k highest, and gi their (often renormalised) scores, used as mixing weights. The other E − k experts never run for this token. Origin: Shazeer et al. (2017), made practical by GShard and Switch Transformer.

    Three design choices separate the models you will hear about.

    • Many small experts beat few big ones. Mixtral (2024) used 8 large experts, top-2. DeepSeekMoE split each into several small ones and activated proportionally more, at the same compute. Like a menu: 8 dishes with 2 picks gives 28 meals; 256 dishes with 8 picks gives about 4 × 1014. A token's needs can be met by a precise mix instead of two blunt tools (Simulation 1).
    • Shared experts. One or two experts that every token uses soak up general knowledge (grammar, common facts), so routed experts can specialise instead of each relearning the basics.
    • What is routed. Usually each token chooses its experts ("token choice"). In "expert choice" each expert picks its tokens: balanced by construction, but awkward when generating one token at a time (Zhou et al., 2022).

    Reading names: "Qwen-30B-A3B" means 30B total, 3B active ("A" is active). Cost is visible at a glance.

    Load balancing: the part that makes or breaks training

    Left alone, routers collapse. A few experts get slightly better early on, so the router sends them more tokens, so they improve further, and the rest starve. It is like one popular restaurant: the queue grows, the others empty. Hardware makes it worse: experts live on different GPUs and a step ends when the busiest finishes. An expert with 3× the average load makes the whole cluster wait.

    The classic fix is an auxiliary loss added to the training objective. In Switch Transformer:

    Lbal=α·E·∑i=1Efi·Pi

    fi is the fraction of tokens sent to expert i, Pi its average router probability, α a small coefficient. The sum is smallest when shares are equal. The cost: it competes with the language-modelling objective. Too large hurts quality; too small lets routing collapse.

    DeepSeek-V3 uses auxiliary-loss-free balancing (Wang et al., 2024). Each expert gets a bias added to its score only when choosing the top-k. After each step, an overloaded expert's bias is nudged down and an underloaded one's up. Mixing weights still use the unbiased scores, so no extra gradient interferes. The V3 report gives an update speed of 0.001 and keeps a very small sequence-level balance loss as a safeguard, so it is not entirely loss-free.

    Two more details. Capacity and dropping: older systems gave each expert a fixed buffer and dropped overflow tokens, quietly hurting quality; DeepSeek-V3 reports no dropping. Router stability: routers can produce huge logits and unstable training, so some models add a z-loss on router logits.

    Why the compute saving does not come free: memory, batches and serving

    Decoding is mostly reading weights from memory, not multiplying, like a chef who spends more time fetching ingredients than chopping. Arithmetic intensity is operations divided by bytes read. Below the hardware's ridge point (a few hundred operations per byte) a step is memory-bound: extra compute is free. Above it, it is compute-bound.

    A dense model with batch b reads all weights once and does about 2 operations per parameter per sequence, so intensity is about 2b and crosses the ridge near batch 150. An MoE differs: each token picks its own experts, so a batch touches the union of choices. With uniform routing, the expected share of experts touched by b tokens is:

    1−(1−kE)b

    With E = 256 and k = 8: one token touches 3% of experts, 16 tokens about 40%, 64 tokens 87%. At modest batches an MoE reads nearly all its weights but does only 37B-worth of arithmetic per token, so intensity stays low and the compute saving is not felt as speed. Only at batches in the thousands, across many users, does it become compute-bound.

    In practice:

    • Training is where MoE shines. Huge batches make the compute saving real. DeepSeek reported pre-training V3 on 14.8 trillion tokens in about 2.79 million H800 GPU-hours, far less than a comparable dense model would be expected to need.
    • Serving needs scale. You spread experts across many GPUs (expert parallelism) and batch many users. A bank hosting one model for a few hundred staff will often pay the memory cost without the throughput benefit.
    • Small active, big footprint. A 120B-total, 5B-active MoE can run on one large GPU when quantized (R12.1) and decodes fast at batch 1. Its quality sits between 5B and 120B, not at either.
    • Fine-tuning has quirks. Adapters on attention layers are easy; training the experts risks disturbing routing.

    See R2.1 for how MoE changes the compute-optimal curve and R12.1 for squeezing memory.

    R1.2 · State-space models and hybrids, in brief

    MoE attacks the cost of parameters. A second cost grows with input length. Attention compares each new token with all earlier ones and keeps their keys and values (the KV cache). In the toy 32-layer model that is about 128 KiB per token, so a 128,000-token document costs 16 GiB for one user. Long contracts, credit files and call transcripts hit this wall.

    State-space models (SSMs) work like the recurrent networks of F5.4: read one token at a time and update a fixed-size state, a running summary with room for only so much. Memory stays flat and decoding costs the same at token 100 or 1,000,000. Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023) made the update selective: what to write or forget depends on the current token, so it can ignore filler and keep a sanctioned name. Mamba-2 linked SSMs to a form of attention, enabling faster implementations.

    The weakness is the state's size. Exact retrieval ("what was the second clause's penalty rate?") and copying are easy for attention, which looks straight at any earlier token, and harder for a compressed state. So the practical answer is hybrids: mostly SSM layers with a few attention layers for lookup.

    • Jamba (AI21, 2024): about one attention layer per seven or eight Mamba layers, plus MoE.
    • Nemotron-H (NVIDIA, 2025): mostly Mamba-2, reporting up to about 3× faster inference than comparable transformers.
    • Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi, 2025): a parallel hybrid, with attention and SSM side by side in each block. 0.5B to 34B parameters, up to 256K context, 18 languages, openly licensed. The one to know in the UAE.

    Hybrids are also faster at long lengths, since attention's per-token cost grows with length and an SSM's does not. Attention still wins at exact recall, so designers tune the ratio, and the optimum is still moving.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "671B parameters, but only 37B, so it fits on a small machine"

      Compute is small, memory is not. All experts must be loaded.

      Spot itAsk for the memory footprint in GB at the planned precision, and the GPU count.
    • Quoting the active size as the "real" size

      MoE quality sits between active and total, near a dense model of intermediate size. Neither number is the right comparison.

      Spot itCompare on your own task against a dense model of similar cost.
    • Assuming cheap API prices mean cheap self-hosting

      Prices reflect huge shared batches that keep experts busy. A lightly used private deployment leaves expensive GPUs idle.

      Spot itCompare expected concurrent requests with the batch size where throughput peaks (Simulation 3).
    • Reading "experts" as human-style specialists

      Trained experts tend to specialise by token type or syntax (punctuation, code, certain languages), not clean topics like "finance". There is no "sanctions expert" to switch on.

      Spot itDistrust claims of interpretable domain experts without evidence.
    • Routing collapse and dropped tokens

      Poor balancing leaves experts idle, or drops tokens under tight capacity limits, silently degrading outputs under load.

      Spot itAsk for expert load histograms, whether tokens are dropped, and whether quality was tested at production batch sizes.
    • Quantization and fine-tuning that disturb routing

      Aggressive quantization or full fine-tuning can change router decisions and cause losses dense-model experience would not predict.

      Spot itRe-run your own evaluation after any compression or fine-tuning, on your languages and document types.
    • "Infinite context" claims for SSMs

      A fixed state cannot store everything. Exact retrieval from the middle of a long document exposes this.

      Spot itRun needle-in-a-haystack lookups on your own documents, such as a clause or amount deep in a long file.
    • Treating a benchmark gain as an architecture gain

      A hybrid may beat a transformer because of different or more data, not the layers.

      Spot itLook for controlled comparisons with the same data and training budget.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model is called "Model-80B-A6B". What do the numbers mean, and which sets the arithmetic per token and which the memory?

    80B total, 6B active per token. Active (6B) sets arithmetic; total (80B) sets memory, about 80 GB at 8-bit.

    2. With 64 routed experts and top-6, how many combinations can a token pick in one layer? Why do more combinations help?

    C(64, 6) = 74,974,368, about 75 million. A token's needs can be met by a finer mix of small specialists, so experts specialise more sharply at the same compute.

    3. Why does a shared expert help?

    Every token needs common knowledge. Without a shared expert, many routed experts relearn the same basics. One shared expert holds it once, freeing the routed ones to differ.

    4. With 256 experts and top-8 under uniform routing, what share of experts does a batch of 16 tokens touch?

    1 − (1 − 8/256)16 = 1 − 0.9687516 ≈ 1 − 0.60 = about 40%. Even a small batch reads much of the weights, so MoE does not speed up memory-bound decoding at moderate batches.

    5. Explain auxiliary-loss-free balancing in two sentences.

    Each expert has a bias added to its router score only when choosing the top-k; after every step, overloaded experts' biases go down and underloaded ones' go up. Load evens out without a competing loss term, and the mixing weights still use the unbiased scores.

    6. In the toy model, a transformer stores 128 KiB of KV cache per token. How much for a 200,000-token document, and what happens in a hybrid with one attention layer in eight?

    200,000 × 128 KiB ≈ 24.4 GiB for full attention. One layer in eight gives an eighth, about 3 GiB, plus a fixed SSM state of roughly 7 MiB (28 layers × 256 KiB). Memory per user falls about 8 times.

    7. Why are SSMs weaker than attention at exact recall, and how do hybrids cope?

    An SSM compresses everything into a fixed-size state, so details can be lost; attention looks straight back at any token. Hybrids keep a few attention layers for lookup and use SSM layers for the cheap bulk.

    8. A vendor quotes very low per-token prices for a large MoE. Why might your private deployment cost much more per token?

    Their price assumes large shared batches that keep every expert busy. A private deployment with few users batches little, touches most experts per step for little useful work, and leaves GPUs under-used.
