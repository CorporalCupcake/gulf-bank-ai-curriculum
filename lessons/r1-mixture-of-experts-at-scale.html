    Mixture-of-Experts at Scale, and the Recurrent Challengers

    Why a model with 671 billion parameters can cost about as much per token as one with 37 billion, what the router actually does, why serving such models is still hard, and how state-space and hybrid models attack the other big cost, long inputs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA mixture-of-experts model stores many specialist sub-networks but sends each token through only a few of them, so you pay compute for the active slice and memory for the whole; state-space hybrids do something similar for context length, replacing most of the attention memory with a fixed-size state.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a large bank's operations floor with 256 specialists: sanctions, trade finance, Islamic structures, card disputes, Arabic complaints, and so on. When a case arrives, a triage clerk reads it and hands it to the eight people best placed to deal with it. The other 248 do not touch it. The floor knows a great deal, but any one case uses a small part of that knowledge.

    That is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) language model. In a standard transformer (F5.5), every token passes through every parameter. In an MoE, the big feed-forward block in each layer is replaced by many smaller feed-forward "experts" plus a tiny router that picks a few experts for each token. The model has a large total parameter count, which holds knowledge, and a much smaller active count per token, which sets the arithmetic you pay for.

    Concretely, DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion parameters in total and 37 billion active for each token (DeepSeek-AI, 2024). Per token it does roughly the arithmetic of a 37B dense model, with the capacity of something far larger. That is why DeepSeek-style models are cheap to train and cheap per token to run at scale.

    There is a catch, and it is the most useful thing to understand. All 671 billion parameters must still sit in fast memory, because the next token might need any expert. MoE trades compute for memory. The second half of this lesson is about a different trade: state-space models and hybrids such as Mamba, Jamba and Falcon-H1 trade the ever-growing attention memory for a fixed-size state, which matters when inputs are very long.

    Hold onto thisAn MoE changes which parameters a token uses, not the idea of a transformer. Compute follows the active parameters; memory follows the total. Every claim about MoE being cheap or expensive depends on which of the two you are paying for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.1 · The sparse layer, the router and fine-grained experts

    Inside a transformer block (F5.5, F5.1), attention mixes information across positions and a feed-forward network (FFN) then processes each token on its own. The FFN holds most of the parameters. An MoE layer swaps that single FFN for E smaller FFNs, the experts, and a router: a single linear layer that scores every expert for the current token.

    y=∑i∈TopKgi·Experti(x)withs=softmax(Wrx)

    x is the token's hidden vector, Wr the router's weights, s the router's scores over the E experts, TopK the k experts with the highest scores, and gi their (often renormalised) scores used as mixing weights. The other E − k experts are never run for this token. The design goes back to Shazeer et al. (2017) and was made practical for transformers by GShard and Switch Transformer.

    Three design choices separate the models you will hear about.

    • How many experts, how big. Mixtral (2024) used 8 large experts per layer and picked 2. DeepSeekMoE argued for fine-grained experts: split each big expert into several small ones and activate proportionally more of them, keeping compute the same. The gain is flexibility. With 8 experts and top-2 a token can pick one of 28 combinations; with 256 experts and top-8 it can pick one of roughly 4 × 1014. Simulation 1 shows this directly. More combinations let experts specialise more sharply, because a token's needs can be met by a precise mix instead of two blunt tools.
    • Shared experts. DeepSeek-style models add one or two experts that every token uses. They soak up the general knowledge (grammar, common facts) that all tokens need, so the routed experts are freed to specialise instead of each learning the same basics.
    • What is routed. Usually each token chooses its own experts ("token choice"). The alternative, "expert choice", lets each expert pick its tokens, which balances load by construction but is awkward for generation where tokens arrive one at a time (Zhou et al., 2022).

    A practical point on naming. "Qwen-30B-A3B" means 30B total, 3B active. "A" stands for active. Read model names that way and you can estimate cost at a glance.

    Load balancing: the part that makes or breaks training

    Left alone, routers collapse. Early in training a few experts get slightly better, so the router sends them more tokens, so they get better still. The rest starve. You end up with a large model where only a handful of experts work, which defeats the purpose. There is also a hardware reason to care: experts live on different GPUs, and each step finishes only when the busiest GPU finishes. If one expert gets 3× the average load, the whole cluster waits for it.

    The classic fix is an auxiliary loss added to the training objective. In Switch Transformer it is

    Lbal=α·E·∑i=1Efi·Pi

    fi is the fraction of tokens actually sent to expert i, Pi the average router probability for expert i, and α a small coefficient. The sum is smallest when all experts get equal shares. The cost is that this loss pulls on the router's gradients and competes with the language-modelling objective; too large and it hurts quality, too small and routing collapses.

    DeepSeek-V3 used a different approach, auxiliary-loss-free balancing (Wang et al., 2024). Each expert gets a bias that is added to its score only when choosing the top-k. After each step, the bias of an overloaded expert is nudged down and that of an underloaded expert is nudged up. The mixing weights still use the original, unbiased scores, so no extra gradient interferes with learning. The V3 report reports a speed for this adjustment of 0.001 and says it keeps a very small sequence-level balance loss as a safeguard, so it is not entirely loss-free.     Two further practical details. First, capacity and dropping: older systems gave each expert a fixed buffer and dropped overflow tokens, which quietly hurts quality; DeepSeek-V3 reports no token dropping in training or inference thanks to its balancing. Second, router stability: routers can produce huge logits and unstable training, which is why techniques such as a z-loss on router logits are used in some models.

    Why the compute saving does not come free: memory, batches and serving

    Decoding a token is mostly reading weights from memory, not multiplying. A GPU can do hundreds of arithmetic operations in the time it takes to fetch one byte. The ratio matters: the arithmetic intensity of a step is its floating-point operations divided by the bytes it reads. If that is below the hardware's ridge point (a few hundred operations per byte on a modern accelerator), the step is memory-bound and extra compute is free. If above, it is compute-bound.

    For a dense model, a batch of b sequences reads all weights once and does about 2 operations per parameter per sequence, so intensity is about 2b. It crosses the ridge near a batch of 150. For an MoE, batching has a twist. Each token picks its own experts, so a batch of 64 tokens touches the union of their choices. If routing were uniform, the expected share of experts touched by at least one of b tokens is

    1−(1−kE)b

    With E = 256 and k = 8, a single token touches 8 experts (3%), 16 tokens touch about 40% of them and 64 tokens touch 87%. So at modest batch sizes an MoE reads nearly all its weights while doing only 37B-worth of arithmetic per token. Its intensity stays low and the saving in compute does not turn into speed. Only at very large batches, thousands of tokens across many users, does it become compute-bound and show its advantage.

    What this means in practice:

    • Training is where MoE shines. Training uses huge batches, so the compute saving is real. DeepSeek reported pre-training V3 on 14.8 trillion tokens for about 2.79 million H800 GPU-hours, far less than a dense model of comparable quality would be expected to need.
    • Serving needs scale. To use all the experts efficiently you spread them across many GPUs (expert parallelism) and batch many users together. A big provider can do this; a single bank hosting one model for a few hundred staff will often see the memory cost without the throughput benefit.
    • Small active, big footprint. A 120B-total MoE with 5B active can run well on one large GPU with its weights quantized (R12.1), and decodes quickly at batch 1 because it reads few weights. But its quality tracks something between 5B and 120B, not either number.
    • Fine-tuning has quirks. Adapters on the attention layers are easy. Training the experts themselves risks disturbing routing.

    For why the 37B figure translates to training cost, see scaling laws in R2.1 (MoE changes the compute-optimal curve), and for squeezing the memory side see R12.1.

    R1.2 · State-space models and hybrids, in brief

    MoE attacks the cost of parameters. A second cost grows with input length. In attention, every new token compares itself with all previous tokens and the model must keep their keys and values, the KV cache. For a toy 32-layer model that is about 128 KiB per token, so a 128,000-token document is 16 GiB for one user. Long contracts, whole credit files and months of call transcripts make this the binding constraint.

    State-space models (SSMs) take another route, close to the recurrent networks of F5.4. They read the sequence one token at a time and update a fixed-size state, like a running summary that has room for only so much. Memory does not grow with length, and decoding a token costs the same at position 100 as at position 1,000,000. Early SSMs were weak at precise recall. Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023) made the state update selective: the decision of what to write into the state and what to forget depends on the current token, so the model can ignore filler and hold on to a sanction-list name. Mamba-2 later showed a mathematical link between SSMs and a form of attention, which allowed faster, matrix-multiply-friendly implementations.

    The weakness is the state's limited size. Exact retrieval ("what was the second clause's penalty rate?") and in-context copying are things attention does naturally, because it can look straight at any earlier token, and a compressed state does less well. Pure SSMs trail transformers on these tasks at equal size. So the practical answer has been hybrids: mostly SSM layers with a few attention layers for precise lookup.

    • Jamba (AI21, 2024) interleaves Mamba and attention layers at a ratio of roughly one attention layer per seven or eight, and uses MoE on top, combining both ideas in one model.
    • Nemotron-H (NVIDIA, 2025) replaces most attention layers with Mamba-2 and reports up to about 3× faster inference than comparable transformers.
    • Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi, 2025) is a parallel hybrid: attention and SSM run side by side within each block, and their outputs are combined. It ships from 0.5B to 34B parameters with up to 256K context and 18 languages, openly licensed. It is the one to know in the UAE.

    Memory is only half the story. Hybrids are also faster at long lengths, since attention's cost per token grows with length and an SSM's does not. But attention remains better at exact recall, so hybrid designers tune the ratio, and evidence on where the optimum lies is still moving.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Architecture choices set the cost, speed and deployment options of every language-model project at the bank. These are the links.

    F5.5 · AttentionWhat MoE and SSMs modify

    MoE replaces the feed-forward block; SSMs replace most attention layers. Know the original to reason about the change, especially the KV cache.

    F5.4 · Recurrent sequence modelsSSMs are recurrence, revived

    A fixed-size state updated token by token is the RNN idea. Selectivity and parallel training are what let it scale this time.

    F6.5 · Distributed trainingExpert parallelism

    Experts are spread across GPUs and tokens travel between them. Load balance is a communication and throughput problem as much as a modelling one.

    R2.1 · Scaling lawsActive parameters set the compute

    Scaling-law questions about how big and how long to train change when "size" splits into total and active.

    R2.4 · FP8 and FP4 trainingLow precision makes MoE affordable

    DeepSeek-V3 trained largely in 8-bit arithmetic. Efficiency tricks stack with sparsity.

    R12.1 · R12.3 · Quantization and on-premises modelsFitting the total into the box

    For an on-premises deployment the total parameter count decides the hardware, so quantization and pruning matter more for MoE.

    R12.2 · KV-cache compressionThe other route to long context

    Compressing or sharing the cache is the alternative to swapping attention for state. DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention is one such method.

    R1.3 · R1.4 · Other architecturesMore challengers

    Diffusion language models and multi-token prediction attack the cost of generating one token at a time, a third axis next to parameters and context.

    B7 · Document AI and copilotsWhere length bites

    Trade-finance bundles and credit files are long. Whether a model reads them in one pass or by retrieval is partly an architecture and cost question.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Choosing an open-weight model to host. A "671B" or "1T" model sounds out of reach, but its active size is a fraction. The honest sizing question is memory for the total and how many concurrent users you really have.
    • Vendor cost and latency claims. Cheap per-million-token prices from API providers reflect large shared batches. They do not mean a private deployment will be as cheap.
    • Data residency. Open-weight MoE models make in-country hosting thinkable, with the caveat that regulators' expectations (CBUAE, SAMA) on third-party and model risk apply to whoever operates it. Check current guidance rather than assume.
    • Long documents and call transcripts. Hybrids and SSMs matter where a whole file or a month of Arabic and English call records must be read at once, though retrieval (B7) is often still cheaper and more auditable.
    • Gulf sovereign AI. Falcon-H1 from TII in Abu Dhabi is a locally built hybrid; its Arabic coverage and licence make it a natural candidate to evaluate for local deployment.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    By late 2026, sparse MoE is the default shape of large open-weight language models, and most frontier systems are widely reported to be MoE too, though closed labs rarely confirm. Hybrid SSM-attention models are established at small and medium sizes and are being tested at larger ones. The live arguments: how fine-grained the experts should be, how to balance load without hurting quality, whether pure-SSM models can match transformers, and how best to serve MoE efficiently.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These come up in vendor decks and in loose technical talk. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "671B parameters, but only 37B, so it fits on a small machine"

      Compute is small, memory is not. All experts must be loaded.

      Spot itAsk for the memory footprint in GB at the precision they will run, and the number of GPUs it needs.
    • Quoting the active size as the model's "real" size

      Quality of an MoE sits between its active and total size, closer to a dense model of intermediate size. Neither number is the right comparison.

      Spot itCompare on your own task against a dense model with similar cost, not by headline parameters.
    • Assuming cheap API prices mean cheap self-hosting

      Prices reflect huge shared batches that keep experts busy. A lightly used private deployment leaves expensive GPUs idle.

      Spot itModel utilisation: expected concurrent requests against the batch size at which throughput peaks (Simulation 3).
    • Reading "experts" as human-style specialists

      Experts in trained MoEs tend to specialise by token type or syntax (punctuation, code, certain languages) more than by clean topics like "finance". Do not expect a "sanctions expert" you can switch on.

      Spot itBe wary of any claim that routing gives interpretable, controllable domain experts without evidence.
    • Routing collapse and dropped tokens

      Poor balancing leaves experts idle, wasting capacity, or drops tokens under tight capacity limits, silently degrading outputs under load.

      Spot itAsk for expert load histograms, whether tokens are dropped, and whether quality was tested at production batch sizes.
    • Quantization and fine-tuning that disturb routing

      Aggressive quantization or full fine-tuning can change router decisions, causing quality loss that dense-model experience would not predict.

      Spot itRe-run your own evaluation after any compression or fine-tuning, with languages and document types you care about.
    • "Infinite context" claims for SSMs

      A fixed state cannot store everything. Long-context benchmarks that test exact retrieval from the middle of a document expose this.

      Spot itTest needle-in-a-haystack style lookups on your own documents, such as a specific clause or amount deep in a long file.
    • Treating a benchmark gain as an architecture gain

      A hybrid may beat a transformer because it saw different or more data, not because of the layers.

      Spot itLook for controlled comparisons with the same data and training budget.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These newer models are built like a large team of specialists with a triage desk: each word is handled by a handful of them rather than the whole team. That is why the biggest open models cost a fraction as much to run per word as their size suggests. The catch is that the whole team has to be on site, so hardware cost depends on the total size, and the savings only really appear when many users share the system. For us the question is how many concurrent users we expect, not the headline number.”
    To a technical interviewer or platform engineer
    “MoE replaces the FFN with many experts and a top-k router, so FLOPs follow active parameters and memory follows total. DeepSeek-V3 is 671B total and 37B active, with fine-grained and shared experts and bias-based, auxiliary-loss-free balancing. In decode, a batch touches the union of experts, so intensity stays low until batches reach the thousands, which is why serving needs expert parallelism and large shared batches. For long context, KV cache grows linearly, which is why hybrids like Jamba, Nemotron-H and Falcon-H1 use mostly SSM layers with a few attention layers.”
    “Is a 671B model with 37B active as good as a 671B dense model?”

    No, and it is not as weak as a 37B dense one either. Quality is generally somewhere between. The point is that it is much cheaper to train and, at scale, to serve than a 671B dense model, while knowing more than a 37B one. Judge it by quality per unit cost on your task.

    “Why not run DeepSeek-V3 on one GPU, if only 37B is active?”

    Because the router can send the next token to any expert, so all weights must be resident. At 8-bit that is about 671 GB, which means a multi-GPU node even before the KV cache. The 37B figure tells you the arithmetic per token, not the memory.

    “What actually goes wrong if the router sends everything to a few experts?”

    Two things. The unused experts waste parameters, so you have paid for capacity you do not use. And on a cluster, the GPU holding the popular expert becomes a bottleneck while others wait. Balance losses or bias adjustment during training are there to prevent this.

    “Does Mamba replace the transformer?”

    Not so far. Pure SSMs are cheaper on long inputs but weaker at exact recall. The working compromise is hybrids with a minority of attention layers, which already ship in Jamba, Nemotron-H and Falcon-H1. Whether the ratio keeps shifting toward SSM at larger scale is open.

    “Should we host an open MoE model on premises?”

    It depends on utilisation and sensitivity. Compute per token is low, but we need hardware for the full weights, plus the operational skill for expert-parallel serving. If volume is modest or data permits a vendor, a hosted service may cost less. If data must stay in-country, a smaller dense or low-active-size MoE, quantized, is often the better fit. Either way the model-risk and third-party rules under CBUAE or SAMA still apply and I'd confirm the current requirements with compliance.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A model is called "Model-80B-A6B". What do the numbers mean, and which one sets the arithmetic per token and which sets the memory?

    80B total parameters, 6B active per token. The active figure (6B) sets the arithmetic per token; the total (80B) sets the memory needed to hold the weights, about 80 GB at 8-bit.

    2. With 64 routed experts and top-6, how many expert combinations can a token pick in one layer? Why does having more combinations help?

    C(64, 6) = 74,974,368, about 75 million. More combinations mean a token's needs can be met by a finer mix of small specialists, so experts can specialise more sharply than with a few large ones, at the same compute.

    3. Why does a shared expert help?

    Every token needs some common knowledge. If only routed experts exist, many of them each relearn the same basics. A shared expert that every token passes through holds it once, so the routed experts are free to differ from one another.

    4. With 256 experts and top-8 under uniform routing, roughly what share of experts does a batch of 16 tokens touch?

    1 − (1 − 8/256)16 = 1 − 0.9687516 ≈ 1 − 0.60 = about 40%. So even a small batch reads a large share of the weights, which is why MoE does not speed up memory-bound decoding at moderate batch sizes.

    5. Explain auxiliary-loss-free balancing in two sentences.

    Each expert has a bias added to its router score only when choosing the top-k. After every step, the bias of overloaded experts is lowered and that of underloaded ones raised, so load evens out without adding a competing term to the training loss. The weights that mix expert outputs still use the unbiased scores.

    6. In the toy model, a transformer stores 128 KiB of KV cache per token. How much for a 200,000-token document, and what happens in a hybrid with one attention layer in eight?

    200,000 × 128 KiB ≈ 24.4 GiB for full attention. With one layer in eight the cache is an eighth, about 3 GiB, plus a fixed SSM state of roughly 7 MiB (28 layers × 256 KiB). Memory per user falls by around 8 times.

    7. Why are SSMs weaker than attention at exact recall, and how do hybrids cope?

    An SSM compresses everything it has read into a fixed-size state, so details can be lost, while attention can look back directly at any earlier token. Hybrids keep a few attention layers for exact lookup and use SSM layers for the cheap bulk of the processing.

    8. A vendor quotes very low per-token prices for a large MoE model. Why might your private deployment cost much more per token?

    Their price assumes large shared batches that keep every expert busy. A private deployment with few concurrent users batches little, touches most experts per step for little useful work, and leaves expensive GPUs under-used, so cost per token rises.
