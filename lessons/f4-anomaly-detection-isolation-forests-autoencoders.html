    Anomaly Detection and Dimensionality Reduction: Finding the Odd One Out

    How isolation forests and autoencoders find odd behaviour nobody labelled, and how PCA, t-SNE and UMAP compress many features, with a warning about what their pictures can and cannot tell you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAnomaly detection learns what normal looks like and ranks everything by how badly it fits, which is not the same as finding fraud; PCA is the clean linear version of that idea, while t-SNE and UMAP make useful pictures whose distances, sizes and gaps you must not read as facts.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank has five years of card transactions and a few hundred confirmed frauds, mostly from tactics that have since changed. A new attack will not look like the old ones. So flip the question: not "what does fraud look like?" but "what does normal look like, and what does not fit?" A customer who spends AED 200 at supermarkets and suddenly makes ten AED 4,900 purchases in an hour from a new device is odd, labelled or not. Like a smoke alarm, this reacts to anything unusual, not only fires.

    Two ways to define "does not fit" give the two methods:

    • Odd ones are easy to separate. A stranger alone by the wall is picked out in a couple of questions. That is the isolation forest.
    • Odd ones are hard to describe. Learn a compact summary of normal, rebuild each record from it, and see what rebuilds badly. That is the autoencoder, with PCA as its linear cousin.

    Compressing is also the idea behind dimensionality reduction: if twenty correlated features really move as two or three factors, three numbers describe each customer. t-SNE and UMAP draw such data as a map for humans, valuable but treacherous.

    Hold onto thisAn unsupervised detector finds unusual, not fraud. Whether unusual means fraud is a separate question that only analysts and outcomes can answer.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Isolation forests: counting cuts

    A loner by the wall in a crowded square is picked out with a couple of questions; someone mid-crowd takes many. The forest asks such questions at random: each tree takes a small random sample of rows, picks a random feature and split value, and repeats until every row is alone. A far-out point takes few cuts, because a random cut is likely to fall between it and everyone else. Averaged over about a hundred trees, a short path means odd.

    The path length becomes a 0 to 1 score: around 0.5 is a typical point, near 1 strongly anomalous. No fixed value means "fraud". Two design choices matter.

    • Subsampling is a feature. If every tree saw all rows, similar anomalies would hide behind each other (masking). Small subsamples thin the crowd. Simulation 1 shows this.
    • It is cheap and needs no scaling. Units do not matter. A sensible first detector on tabular banking data.

    The blind spot: a point unremarkable on each feature but odd in combination (high spend, very low income) isolates no faster than its neighbours. The features you give it decide what "anomalous" means. Behavioural features (F3.4) such as "spend today divided by 30-day average" do most of the work. The model gives a ranking, not a verdict: set the number flagged from review capacity (F3.5), not from 0.5.

    from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

iso = IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, max_samples=256, random_state=0).fit(X_train)
raw = iso.score_samples(X_new)      # higher = more normal
order = (-raw).argsort()            # most anomalous first
queue = order[:300]                 # review capacity decides the cut, not 0.5

    Autoencoders: compress, rebuild, measure the miss

    Think of a courier who packs a standard suitcase from a short checklist. Everyday outfits fit the checklist; a snowsuit does not, so it comes out wrong at the other end. An autoencoder is a network trained to output its own input through a narrow middle: an encoder squeezes a record into a short code and a decoder rebuilds it (see F5.1). The code is too short to memorise, so it keeps only the shared structure, such as how spend, frequency and merchant mix usually move together.

    An unusual combination is rebuilt as the nearest normal-looking record, and the gap is large. That reconstruction error is the anomaly score. Large means "does not fit the learned pattern", not "is fraud". With one linear layer each way it learns exactly PCA; Simulation 2 draws that in two dimensions.

    Where practice goes wrong:

    • Capacity is the dial. A bottleneck as wide as the input lets the network copy, so error is near zero for everything, fraud included. Too narrow and normal data rebuilds poorly too.
    • Train on mostly clean data. If fraud is in the training set, the network may learn to rebuild it.
    • Standardise. A feature in fils would define "odd". Per-feature error shows which features did not fit, which helps analyst explanations (F8).

    Simpler alternatives are worth trying too: local outlier factor, one-class SVM, and Mahalanobis distance (a correlation-aware distance from the centre, hard to beat in low dimensions).

    How to tell whether it works

    Without labels you cannot compute recall, so use these:

    • Inject known anomalies into held-out data and count how many rank in the top N. Optimistic, but it catches broken pipelines.
    • Analyst review of the top N. Investigators grade a sample as fraud, unusual-but-legitimate or data error. Track the useful share.
    • Stability. Retrain with another seed or month. If the top 100 changes completely, the ranking is mostly noise.

    Where you have labels, a supervised model (F4.3) usually beats this on fraud you have seen. Anomaly detection earns its place on the unseen: new attacks, data-quality problems, early drift and cold-start portfolios.

    PCA: the cleanest way to compress correlated features

    Banking tables are full of features that move together: balances this month, last month and the month before. Twenty such columns often carry three or four underlying factors, like several shop thermometers that all measure "how hot is it". PCA finds those factors, ordered by how much of the spread each explains, so you keep the first few as the best straight-line summary of each row.

    If the first component carries 90% of the variance, one factor nearly explains the table; at 20%, the features are mostly independent. Do not read "90% of variance" as "90% of the useful information".

    Practical rules:

    • Standardise first, unless features share units. Otherwise the biggest-variance feature wins by default, as the lower box shows.
    • Components are blends, so they are hard to name. A regulator will ask about your inputs, and "PC3" is a weak answer. Use PCA for noise reduction or exploration and keep named features in the scorecard.
    • Fit on training data only. Fitting before the split leaks (F3.1).

    Large variance is not usefulness: the direction that predicts default may be low-variance, and PCA never sees the label.

    from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
import numpy as np

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=0.90)).fit(X_train)   # keep 90% of variance
Z = pipe.transform(X_new)
recon = pipe.named_steps["pca"].inverse_transform(Z)
err = ((pipe.named_steps["standardscaler"].transform(X_new) - recon) ** 2).sum(axis=1)  # anomaly score

    t-SNE and UMAP: maps that look more certain than they are

    PCA is limited to straight lines. Segments, text embeddings or transaction sequences often lie on curved surfaces, and 2-D PCA flattens them into a smear. t-SNE and UMAP draw such data in 2-D so that neighbours stay neighbours. Think of a seating plan that keeps friends at the same table but ignores how far apart the tables are. Their main setting (perplexity, or n_neighbors) is roughly how many neighbours each point cares about.

    Both keep who is near whom and ignore nearly everything else. So:

    • Cluster size means nothing. Each neighbourhood is rescaled to the same size, so tight and diffuse groups look alike.
    • Gaps mean little. Clusters drawn far apart may nearly touch in the real space, or the reverse.
    • Settings and random starts change the story. Low perplexity breaks one population into islands, and noise can be drawn as meaningful blobs.

    Use them to explore, not to prove: do Arabic and English complaint embeddings separate by topic, are there mislabelled or duplicate records. Do not use them as evidence of how many segments exist or how different two segments are. Cluster in the original or PCA space, then draw the result on the map.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Equating "unusual" with "fraud"

      The top alerts turn out to be VIP customers, month-end payroll or a broken feed, and analysts stop trusting the queue. Claims of accuracy with no labels only mean the model agreed with itself.

      Spot itHave investigators grade the top 100 as fraud, unusual-but-legitimate or data error, and report the split.
    • Training on fraud, or too much capacity

      The model learns a recurring rare pattern as normal, or reconstructs everything so nothing stands out.

      Spot itCheck whether known cases score as normal. If they overlap with held-out normal data, clean the training set or shrink the bottleneck.
    • Unscaled features

      Amount in fils dominates PCA or an autoencoder; the "anomaly" is just the largest transaction.

      Spot itCheck which feature carries the error. If it is one large-unit column, standardise or take logs.
    • Drift read as anomaly

      Ramadan spending or salary days push scores up for everyone and flood the queue.

      Spot itPlot the share flagged by week. A smooth rise across all segments is seasonality; a spike in a few accounts is an event.
    • Reading cluster size and gaps off a t-SNE or UMAP plot

      "Segment A is far more diverse than B", or "these groups are far apart".

      Spot itRedraw with another perplexity or seed. If sizes, gaps or cluster count change, the claim was an artefact.
    • Fitting PCA before the split, or naming a component as a fact

      Test-set structure leaks into the directions. And "component 2 is customer wealth" is a weighted blend, not a fact.

      Spot itCheck that scaler and PCA sit inside the training fold. Unstable loadings across resamples mean the label is a story.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why does a point far from the crowd get isolated after fewer random cuts?

    A random cut is likely to land in the empty gap between the outlier and everyone else. A point in a dense region is surrounded, so most cuts keep it with many others.

    2. An isolation forest gives one transaction a score of 0.8 and another 0.5. How do you read that, and how do you choose the cut-off?

    0.8 is isolated in noticeably fewer cuts than a typical point, so it is fairly anomalous; 0.5 is unremarkable. Neither means fraud. Set the cut-off from how many alerts analysts can review.

    3. Why might six near-identical fraud accounts score as normal in an isolation forest, and what could you change?

    They shield each other (masking): a cut that separates one leaves the other five with it. Reduce the subsample size, add features that distinguish them, or use a density or graph method.

    4. An autoencoder's bottleneck is as wide as its input. What happens to anomaly detection?

    It can copy its input, so error is near zero for everyone, fraud included. The bottleneck must be narrow enough that only common structure survives.

    5. A customer is 2 standard deviations above average on card spend and 1.5 below on ATM cash, in data where the two have correlation 0.9. Is she a univariate outlier? Is she an anomaly?

    Not on either feature alone. But normal customers sit near the line where the two move together, and she is far from it, so her reconstruction error is large. The combination is odd.

    6. You have 10 standardised features that are all moderately correlated with each other. Roughly what does PCA tell you, and why might you still need many components to reach 90%?

    The first component picks up the shared movement and carries a large share, perhaps half. The rest is spread fairly evenly as individual noise, so reaching 90% still needs most components. Correlation compresses well only when it is strong.

    7. One feature is in fils and the rest in thousands of dirhams. You run PCA without standardising. What do you see?

    The first component is almost entirely that feature, because it has by far the largest variance. Standardise, or choose units deliberately.

    8. Name three things you must not conclude from a t-SNE plot.

    That a bigger cluster is more diverse; that clusters far apart are more different than closer ones; that the visible cluster count is the real number of segments. Keep to "these points are neighbours".
