    Anomaly Detection and Dimensionality Reduction: Finding the Odd One Out

    How isolation forests and autoencoders find odd behaviour nobody labelled, and how PCA, t-SNE and UMAP compress many features.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAnomaly detection ranks everything by how badly it fits normal, which is not the same as finding fraud; PCA is the clean linear version, while t-SNE and UMAP make pictures whose distances, sizes and gaps you must not read as facts.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Labelled frauds are few and old, and a new attack will not look like them. So flip the question: not "what does fraud look like?" but "what does normal look like, and what does not fit?"

    Two ideas give the two methods. Odd ones are easy to separate: the isolation forest. Odd ones are hard to rebuild from a compact summary of normal: the autoencoder, with PCA as its linear cousin. Compressing is also dimensionality reduction; t-SNE and UMAP draw such data as a map, valuable but treacherous.

    Hold onto thisA detector finds unusual, not fraud. Whether unusual means fraud is a question only analysts and outcomes can answer.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Isolation forests: counting cuts

    A loner by the wall in a crowded square is picked out with a couple of questions; someone mid-crowd takes many. The forest asks such questions at random. Each tree takes a small random sample of rows, picks a random feature and a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum, and repeats until every row is alone. A far-out point takes few cuts, because a random cut is likely to land in the empty gap between it and everyone else. Averaged over many trees, a short path means odd.

    The average path length is turned into a score between 0 and 1: around 0.5 is a typical point, near 1 is strongly anomalous. No fixed value means "fraud". There is no loss function to tune and no need to scale features.

    Three knobs matter. Number of trees: 100 to 200 is plenty; more only smooths the ranking. Subsample size (often 256 rows per tree): this is a feature, not a shortcut. If every tree saw every row, similar anomalies would hide behind each other (masking); small samples thin the crowd, as Simulation 1 shows. Contamination, the expected share of anomalies: it only sets where the library draws the line and never changes the ranking, so ignore it and cut by review capacity instead (F3.5).

    Each tree sees only a small sample, so millions of rows are fine on a laptop. Cuts are made along one feature at a time, so it can miss a point that is ordinary on each feature but odd in combination (high spend, very low income). And the features you give it decide what "anomalous" means: ratios such as today's spend over the 30-day average (F3.4) do most of the work.

    Autoencoders: compress, rebuild, measure the miss

    An autoencoder is a network trained to output its own input through a narrow middle (F5.1), so it keeps only shared structure, like a courier packing from a short checklist.

    An unusual combination is rebuilt as the nearest normal-looking record, and that reconstruction error is the anomaly score. Large means "does not fit", not "is fraud". With linear layers it learns exactly PCA; non-linear layers let it bend that straight line into a curve, which is the whole gain.

    The error can be split by feature, so the alert says which inputs did not fit. That is worth a lot to analysts and to explanation later. To set an alert line, take a high percentile (say the 99th) of the error on held-out normal data, then adjust to review capacity.

    Capacity is the dial. A bottleneck as wide as the input lets the network copy, so error is near zero for everything, fraud included. Too narrow and normal data rebuilds poorly too, which buries the signal in noise.

    Train on mostly clean, standardised data. If a recurring fraud pattern is common in the training set, the network learns to rebuild it and stops flagging it. Categorical fields such as merchant type need encoding, which is where autoencoders get fiddly. A small network on a few hundred thousand rows trains on a CPU in minutes.

    Simpler alternatives deserve a trial first. Mahalanobis distance is a correlation-aware distance from the centre and is hard to beat in low dimensions. Local outlier factor compares a point's local density with its neighbours', which finds outliers next to a dense cluster that a global method misses.

    How to tell whether it works

    Without labels you cannot compute recall. Instead, inject known anomalies into held-out data and see how many rank high, have analysts grade the top N as fraud, unusual-but-legitimate or data error, and retrain with another seed to check the top 100 is stable. Where labels exist, a supervised model (F4.3) wins on seen fraud. This earns its place on the unseen.

    PCA: compressing correlated features

    Banking tables are full of features that move together, like balances this month, last month and the month before. PCA finds the few underlying factors, ordered by how much spread each explains, so you keep the first few as the best straight-line summary. "90% of the variance" is not "90% of the useful information".

    To choose how many components to keep, look at the cumulative-variance curve in Simulation 3, or better, keep the number that gives the best result in the downstream model. The method assumes the structure is linear and that variance means signal. Both can be wrong: a few extreme records can tilt the components, and a low-variance direction may be the one that matters. It costs almost nothing. Rebuilding rows from the kept components also gives an explainable anomaly score.

    Standardise first, unless features share units, or the biggest-variance feature wins by default. Fit on training data only, to avoid leakage (F3.1). Components are blends that are hard to name, so keep named features in a scorecard. Large variance is not usefulness, because PCA never sees the label.

    t-SNE and UMAP: maps that look more certain than they are

    PCA is limited to straight lines. t-SNE and UMAP draw curved or messy data in 2-D so that neighbours stay neighbours, like a seating plan that keeps friends at the same table but ignores how far apart the tables are.

    They keep who is near whom and little else. Cluster size means nothing, gaps mean little, and settings or random starts can change the story.

    The main knob is perplexity in t-SNE, or n_neighbors in UMAP: roughly how many neighbours each point cares about. Low values break one population into islands; high values blur groups together. Try several, from about 5 to 50. UMAP adds min_dist, which controls how tightly points pack. UMAP is faster on large data, keeps somewhat more of the overall layout, and can place new points on an existing map. Standard t-SNE cannot, so a map for monitoring needs UMAP or a re-run.

    Use them to explore, not to prove. Cluster in the original or PCA space, then draw the result on the map.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Finding what nobody labelled. It needs no fraud labels, only a picture of normal, so it can flag a brand-new attack that no supervised model has seen.
    • Cold starts. A new channel has no fraud history, but normal behaviour accumulates fast.
    • Cheap, fast baselines. An isolation forest needs no scaling, trains in seconds and scores millions of rows, because each tree only sees a small sample.
    • Odd combinations. Autoencoders and PCA judge a record against the usual relationships between features, so a customer with ordinary numbers in an unusual mix still stands out.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • You have good labels for the thing you care about. A supervised model (F4.3) learns exactly which unusual patterns are fraud. The detector only knows "rare".
    • Fraud looks normal, or is common. Fraud that mimics typical customers, or a pattern frequent enough to enter the training data, is learned as normal.
    • Unusual is mostly innocent. VIP customers, payroll day and Ramadan all look odd. Precision can be poor, and analysts stop trusting the queue.
    • You must explain each decision to a regulator or customer. "Odd" is a thin reason on its own. Per-feature error helps, but a rule or scorecard is easier to defend.

    Alternatives and how to choose

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Isolation forestTabular data, no labels, you want a fast first detector with little tuningThe oddness lives in feature combinations, or the data is images or text
    AutoencoderMany correlated features, or sequences and embeddings, where the shape of normal is complexSmall data, or you need quick, explainable results
    PCA reconstruction errorCorrelated numeric features and a need for a simple, explainable baselineRelationships are strongly curved, or features are mostly independent
    Mahalanobis or local outlier factorFew features and moderate rows; a quick sanity check, or outliers sitting beside dense clustersDozens of features, or very large tables
    Supervised gradient boostingYou have enough confirmed cases of the targetLabels are tiny, stale or biased toward what you already catch
    t-SNE or UMAPExploring embeddings or segments by eye, and spotting duplicatesProving cluster counts or sizes, or any model input that must be stable

    In practice

    Start with an isolation forest on a few well-built behavioural features. Move to an autoencoder only if both miss something analysts care about. Cut the alert list by review capacity, grade the top 100 each cycle, and log the outcomes. That feedback is the labelled data you lacked, and it lets you move to a supervised model later (F3.5).

    Sensible defaults: 100 to 200 trees and 256 rows per tree; an autoencoder bottleneck of a quarter to a half of the input width; standardised inputs; 90 to 95% of variance for PCA. All run on one machine.

    In a bank this appears as new-pattern card and online-banking fraud monitoring, peer-group outliers in AML transaction monitoring, spotting unusual staff access, and data-quality checks on incoming feeds. PCA shows up in risk work to summarise correlated rates, and UMAP in exploring complaint-text embeddings.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Equating "unusual" with "fraud"

      Top alerts are VIPs, payroll day or a broken feed.

      Spot itHave investigators grade the top 100 as fraud, unusual-but-legitimate or data error.
    • Training on fraud, or too much capacity

      The model learns a recurring rare pattern as normal, or rebuilds everything well.

      Spot itCheck whether known cases score as normal; if so, clean the training set or shrink the bottleneck.
    • Unscaled features

      Amount in fils dominates PCA or an autoencoder, so the "anomaly" is just the largest transaction.

      Spot itCheck which feature carries the error, then standardise.
    • Drift read as anomaly

      Ramadan spending or salary days push scores up for everyone.

      Spot itPlot the share flagged by week. A smooth rise across segments is seasonality.
    • Reading size and gaps off a t-SNE or UMAP plot

      "Segment A is far more diverse than B" is usually an artefact.

      Spot itRedraw with another perplexity or seed. If the claim changes, it was never real.
    • Alerts with no reason attached

      Analysts see a score of 0.93 and nothing else, so they cannot triage and stop trusting the queue.

      Spot itCheck whether each alert shows its top contributing features. If analysts ignore the list, this is why.
    • Fitting the scaler or PCA before the split

      Statistics from future or test data leak in, so offline scores look better than live ones.

      Spot itConfirm the scaler and PCA sit inside the training pipeline and are fitted on training rows only.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why does a far-out point get isolated in fewer random cuts?

    A random cut is likely to land in the empty gap between it and everyone else. A point in a dense region is surrounded, so cuts keep it with many others.

    2. An isolation forest scores one transaction 0.8 and another 0.5. How do you read that, and where do you set the cut-off?

    0.8 is fairly anomalous and 0.5 is unremarkable, but neither means fraud. Set the cut-off from how many alerts analysts can review.

    3. An autoencoder's bottleneck is as wide as its input. What happens?

    It can copy its input, so error is near zero for everyone, fraud included. The bottleneck must be narrow enough that only common structure survives.

    4. A customer is high on card spend and low on ATM cash, where the two normally move together. Is she an anomaly?

    Yes, even though neither number is extreme alone. She is far from the line where normal customers sit, so her reconstruction error is large.

    5. Name two things you must not conclude from a t-SNE plot.

    That a bigger cluster is more diverse, or that clusters far apart are more different. Keep to "these points are neighbours".

    6. A card portfolio has three years of confirmed, labelled fraud. A new buy-now-pay-later channel launches with no labels. Which method do you pick for each, and why?

    Use a supervised model such as gradient boosting for the card portfolio, since labels teach it which unusual patterns are fraud. For the new channel, start with an isolation forest, because there is nothing to train a classifier on.

    7. A steering committee wants one picture of customer segments and a count of how many there are. Would you use t-SNE, UMAP or PCA, and what do you tell them?

    Cluster in the original or PCA space to get the count, and use UMAP or t-SNE only to draw it. PCA is the honest choice if sizes and gaps matter. Tell them the picture shows neighbours, not sizes or distances, and check that it holds under a different seed.
