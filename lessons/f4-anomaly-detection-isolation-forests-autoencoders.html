    Anomaly Detection and Dimensionality Reduction: Finding the Odd One Out

    How isolation forests and autoencoders find fraud nobody labelled, why both are really about "what does normal look like", and how PCA, t-SNE and UMAP compress many features, with a warning about what their pictures can and cannot tell you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAnomaly detection learns what normal looks like and ranks everything by how badly it fits, which is different from finding fraud; PCA is the clean linear version of that idea (and of an autoencoder), while t-SNE and UMAP make useful pictures whose distances, sizes and gaps you must not read as facts.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank has five years of card transactions and only a few hundred confirmed frauds, most of them from tactics that have since changed. A new attack will not look like the old labelled ones. So how do you catch something you have never seen?

    You flip the question. Instead of "what does fraud look like?" you ask "what does normal look like, and what does not fit?" A customer who usually spends AED 200 at supermarkets in Dubai Marina and suddenly makes ten AED 4,900 purchases in an hour from a device she has never used is odd, whether or not anyone has labelled that pattern. This is anomaly detection, also called outlier or novelty detection.

    There are two ways to define "does not fit", and they give the two methods in the title.

    • Odd ones are easy to separate. Picture a crowd in a square. A stranger standing alone by the wall can be picked out with a couple of questions ("is it in the east half? the north quarter?"). Someone in the middle of the crowd takes many more. This is the isolation forest: count how few random cuts it takes to isolate a point.
    • Odd ones are hard to describe. Learn a compact summary of normal data and use it to rebuild each record. Normal records rebuild well. Odd ones rebuild badly, because the summary has no room for what it never saw. This is the autoencoder, and its linear cousin is PCA.

    That second idea, compress then reconstruct, is also the entire story of dimensionality reduction, the second half of this lesson. If twenty correlated features really move together as two or three underlying factors, you can describe each customer with three numbers. PCA does that with a straight-line projection. t-SNE and UMAP do it differently, to draw a map of the data for a human. The maps are valuable and treacherous, for reasons Simulation 4 makes visible.

    Hold onto thisAn unsupervised detector does not find fraud. It finds unusual, defined by its own idea of normal. Whether unusual means fraud is a separate question that only analysts, labels and outcomes can answer. Most failures in this topic come from forgetting that gap.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Isolation forests: counting cuts

    Build a tree the lazy way. Take a small random sample of rows (typically 256). Pick a feature at random, pick a split value uniformly between that feature's minimum and maximum in the current group, and send rows left or right. Repeat inside each branch until every row is alone, or the tree reaches a depth limit of about log2(sample size). No labels, no distances, no loss function. Then build a hundred of these trees.

    Isolating a point that sits far from the pack takes few cuts, because a random cut across the range is likely to fall between it and everyone else. A point in a dense region needs many cuts. So the average path length from root to the point's leaf, over all trees, is a measure of oddness. Short means odd.

    Paths are converted to a score between 0 and 1 so that a threshold means roughly the same thing for any sample size:

    s(x)=2−E[h(x)]c(ψ)

    h(x) is the path length for point x, averaged over trees. c(ψ) is the average path length you would expect for a typical point when each tree sees ψ rows; it is about 2(ln(ψ−1) + 0.577) − 2(ψ−1)/ψ. For ψ = 256, c ≈ 10.2. A point isolated after about 10 cuts scores 0.5 (unremarkable). One isolated after 3 cuts scores 2−3/10.2 ≈ 0.82 (very odd). Scores near 1 are strongly anomalous; scores well below 0.5 are deeply inside the crowd.

    Two design choices explain why it works so well in practice.

    • Subsampling is a feature, not a shortcut. If every tree saw all rows, anomalies would be hidden by their neighbours (masking) and normal points near them would look odd (swamping). Small subsamples thin out the crowd, so a tight group of six frauds becomes one or two per tree and is easier to isolate. Simulation 1 shows this.
    • It is cheap and needs no scaling. Cost grows linearly with data, and because it only compares values within one feature at a time, units do not matter (AED against transaction count). That makes it a sensible first detector on tabular banking data.

    Its blind spots follow from the mechanism. Splits are axis-parallel, so a point that is unremarkable on each feature but odd in combination (high spend, very low income) is isolated no faster than its neighbours unless the trees happen to cut the right way. The extended isolation forest uses randomly tilted cuts to reduce this. It also has no notion of which features matter: add fifty noisy columns and the "odd" signal is diluted. The features you give it decide what "anomalous" means. Hand-built behavioural features (F3.4) such as "spend today divided by 30-day average" do most of the work.

    The remaining decision is the threshold. The model gives a ranking, not a verdict. Like any rare-event detector (F3.5), you choose how many to flag from review capacity or costs, not from a score of 0.5. Some libraries expose a contamination setting; treat it as "how many do I want to see?", not as a belief about fraud prevalence.

    from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

iso = IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, max_samples=256, random_state=0).fit(X_train)
raw = iso.score_samples(X_new)      # higher = more normal (it is the negative of s above)
order = (-raw).argsort()            # most anomalous first
queue = order[:300]                 # review capacity decides the cut, not 0.5

    Autoencoders: compress, rebuild, measure the miss

    An autoencoder is a neural network trained to output its own input, but forced through a narrow middle. The first half (encoder) squeezes the record into a short code, say 8 numbers from 60 features. The second half (decoder) rebuilds the 60 features from the code. Training minimises the reconstruction error, typically the mean squared difference between input and output. See F5.1 for the network machinery and F1.3 for how it trains.

    Because the code is too short to memorise everything, the network keeps only the structure that is common across the training data: the way spend, frequency and merchant mix usually move together. Train it on mostly normal transactions and it gets good at rebuilding normal ones. A transaction with an unusual combination of features gets rebuilt as the nearest normal-looking one, and the gap between input and output is large. That gap is the anomaly score.

    The cleanest way to see this is to strip the network down to a single linear layer each way. That autoencoder learns exactly the same subspace as PCA, and the reconstruction error is the distance from the point to that subspace. Simulation 2 draws it in two dimensions.

    Several things follow, and they are where practice goes wrong.

    • Capacity is the dial. With a bottleneck as wide as the input, the network can copy and the error is near zero for everything, including fraud. Too narrow and normal data rebuilds poorly too. The useful middle is found by watching validation error on held-out normal data. This is why people say autoencoders "generalise too well": a big one can reconstruct anomalies it never saw.
    • Training data should be mostly clean. If fraud is 0.5% of training rows the network may partly learn to rebuild it. Remove known fraud and known odd cases first. Robust and denoising variants reduce the problem but do not remove it.
    • Error is dominated by large-scale features. Standardise inputs (F3.4), or one feature measured in fils will define "odd". Also consider per-feature error: the pieces tell you which features did not fit, which is useful for an analyst's explanation (F8).
    • Categoricals and text need embeddings first. Merchant category, country and device type become learned vectors or are one-hot encoded. For sequences of transactions, the same idea works with a sequence model instead of a feed-forward net.

    Other classical detectors fill gaps. Local outlier factor compares a point's local density with its neighbours' and handles clusters of different density. One-class SVM draws a boundary around the data, but scales poorly and is sensitive to settings. Mahalanobis distance uses the covariance to say how far a point is from the centre in a correlation-aware way, and is hard to beat for simple low-dimensional cases. Simulation 2's "odd mix" would be flagged by Mahalanobis for exactly the same reason it was flagged by the autoencoder.

    How to tell whether it works

    Without labels you cannot compute a recall. This is the hardest practical part, and the honest options are:

    • Inject known anomalies. Plant synthetic or historical fraud patterns in held-out data and measure what share rank in the top N. Optimistic, because injected cases are usually easier than real ones, but it catches broken pipelines.
    • Back-test on confirmed cases. Where a few labelled frauds exist, check where they rank among everyone. A detector that places 60% of known frauds in the top 1% is useful even if it was never trained on them.
    • Analyst review of the top N. Sample the top-ranked alerts and have investigators grade them as "fraud", "unusual but legitimate" or "data error". Track the share that is useful, and feed answers back as labels.
    • Stability. Retrain with a different seed or month. If the top 100 changes completely, the ranking is mostly noise.

    And a plain truth: when you do have labels, a supervised model (F4.3) will usually beat an unsupervised one on the fraud you have seen. Anomaly detection earns its place for the unseen: new attack types, data-quality problems, early signs of drift, and cold-start portfolios with no labels yet. In production the two are combined, with the anomaly score as one feature of the supervised model, or as a separate queue for novelty.

    PCA: the cleanest way to compress correlated features

    Banking tables are full of features that move together: balance this month, last month and the month before; number of debit-card swipes and total debit-card spend. Twenty such columns often carry the information of three or four underlying factors. PCA finds them.

    Geometrically (F1.1–F1.2): centre the data, then find the direction in which the cloud is most spread out. That is the first principal component. The second is the most-spread direction at right angles to it, and so on. Each point can now be described by its coordinates along these directions, and if you keep only the first k you have the best rank-k straight-line summary of the data, in the sense of least squared reconstruction error. That is the same quantity an autoencoder minimises.

    share of variance in component j=λjλ1+…+λp

    λj are the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix: how much variance lies along each component. If the features are standardised, the total is the number of features p, so an eigenvalue of 5.5 among 10 features means that component carries 55% of everything. Plotting eigenvalues from largest to smallest is the scree plot; you keep components until the curve flattens or until a chosen share (often 90%) is reached.

    Practical rules for PCA:

    • Standardise first unless features share units and you want large ones to count more. Otherwise the biggest-variance feature wins by default, as the lower box shows.
    • Components are mixtures, so they are hard to name. Component 1 may be "overall activity level" and component 2 "cash versus card". Loadings tell you the recipe, but a regulator will ask about your inputs, and "PC3" is a weak answer. If interpretability matters, use PCA for noise reduction or exploration and keep named features in the scorecard.
    • Fit on training data only. PCA learns from data; fitting it on the full dataset before splitting leaks (F3.1). Same for scalers.
    • Large variance is not the same as useful. The direction that matters for default may be a low-variance one. PCA never looks at the label.
    • Used for anomalies, the residual after keeping k components is the reconstruction error of Simulation 2. The error in the discarded components can itself be monitored for drift (F7.7).
    from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
import numpy as np

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=0.90)).fit(X_train)   # keep 90% of variance
Z = pipe.transform(X_new)
recon = pipe.named_steps["pca"].inverse_transform(Z)
err = ((pipe.named_steps["standardscaler"].transform(X_new) - recon) ** 2).sum(axis=1)  # anomaly score

    t-SNE and UMAP: maps that look more certain than they are

    PCA is limited to straight lines. Customer segments, text embeddings or transaction sequences often lie on curved surfaces, and a 2-D PCA flattens them into a smear. t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) and UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018) are built to draw such data in 2-D so neighbours stay neighbours.

    t-SNE works in three steps. First it converts distances in the original space into neighbour probabilities: for each point, nearby points get high probability, with a bandwidth chosen so that the effective number of neighbours equals the perplexity you set (roughly "how many neighbours should each point care about", usually 5–50). Second it places the points at random in 2-D and defines similar probabilities there using a heavy-tailed curve. Third it moves the points to make the two sets of probabilities agree, by minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence (F1.6–F1.7), which punishes mapping close points far apart much more than the reverse. UMAP follows the same plan with a different neighbour graph (controlled by n_neighbors) and a different cost, and a min_dist setting for how tightly points may pack. It is faster and, with good initialisation, usually keeps broad layout better.

    The crucial point is what is thrown away. Both methods preserve who is near whom and are indifferent to nearly everything else. This is where the pictures mislead:

    • Cluster size means nothing. A tight cluster and a diffuse one come out similar because each point's neighbourhood is rescaled to the same effective size.
    • Gaps between clusters mean little. Two clusters drawn far apart may be nearly touching in the real space, or the other way around.
    • Settings change the story. Low perplexity breaks one population into many small islands; the right number of clusters depends on a slider.
    • Different runs differ. The start is random, so axes, rotation and sometimes the arrangement of clusters change from run to run.
    • You can see structure that is not there. Random noise can be drawn as apparently meaningful blobs.

    Use them as exploration tools: to inspect whether embeddings of Arabic and English complaints separate by topic, to check whether a model's learned customer representation has structure, to spot a cluster of mislabelled or duplicated records. Do not use them as evidence of how many segments exist, how different two segments are, or as inputs to a downstream model without checking. If you want clusters, cluster in the original or PCA space and then draw the result on a t-SNE map. If you must compare maps, initialise from PCA and fix seeds, as recommended by Kobak and colleagues.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Both halves are one idea, "learn a compact description of the data", pointed at different jobs. Here is where it meets the rest of the curriculum.

    F4.3 · Trees and forestsSame machinery, no labels

    An isolation forest reuses the random-tree idea but splits at random and measures depth instead of purity.

    F1.1 – F1.2 · Vectors and geometryProjection and subspaces

    PCA is a projection onto the best subspace. Reconstruction error is the length of what is left over.

    F5.1 · Neural networksAutoencoders are small networks

    Encoder, decoder and a squared-error loss. Everything about training and overfitting applies.

    F5.7 · Self-supervised learningThe autoencoder's grown-up relatives

    Predicting parts of the input from the rest is the engine behind modern pre-training. Anomaly scores from such models are an active area.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataSame thresholds, same traps

    An anomaly score is a ranking. Choosing the cut, estimating precision and the cost of alerts follow the rules of that lesson.

    F3.4 · Feature engineeringFeatures define "odd"

    Behavioural ratios and scaling choices decide what both methods see. Scaling matters for PCA and autoencoders, not for trees.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringAnomaly scores as a drift alarm

    A rising share of high-error records means the inputs have moved away from training data, whether by attack, new product or pipeline fault.

    F4.9 · HyperparametersTuning without labels is hard

    Bottleneck width, perplexity and subsample size cannot be tuned against recall you cannot measure. Use stability, injected cases and analyst feedback.

    G3 · Independent validationWhat validators will ask

    Unsupervised models have no ground truth to validate against, so expect questions about stability, sensitivity to settings and how alerts are judged.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card and payment fraud. A novelty queue next to the supervised model, catching unfamiliar tactics. See B1 and delayed labels, which is why unsupervised signals matter while labels are still arriving.
    • AML transaction monitoring. Peer-group outliers: an account whose behaviour differs from other accounts of the same type and size. Compare with rules in B2.
    • KYC and customer risk. Unusual combinations in onboarding data, and PCA to compress many risk indicators for review; see B3.
    • Synthetic identities and devices. Clusters of near-identical applications, or devices behaving unlike any normal user (A9, device intelligence).
    • Data quality and operations. The same machinery flags broken feeds, duplicate records and system faults, probably the highest-return use in many banks.
    • Embeddings and text. Projecting document or complaint embeddings with t-SNE or UMAP to explore topics in Arabic and English; compression of high-dimensional embeddings (R10).

    Where the field is now

    The classical methods are settled, and the research questions are now about evidence: which detectors actually win on real data, whether deep methods justify their cost, and how honestly dimension-reduction maps are read.

    1. 1901–33
      PCA

      Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933) set out principal components. Still the default way to compress correlated features.

      Pearson, Phil. Mag., 1901; Hotelling, J. Educ. Psych., 1933
    2. 2006
      Deep autoencoders for dimensionality reduction

      Hinton and Salakhutdinov showed that deep autoencoders, with careful initialisation, give much better low-dimensional codes than PCA on some data. Part of the revival of deep learning.

      Hinton & Salakhutdinov, Science, 2006
    3. 2008
      Isolation forest and t-SNE

      Liu, Ting and Zhou introduced isolation by random partitioning, with linear time and low memory. The same year van der Maaten and Hinton published t-SNE.

      Liu et al., ICDM, 2008 (journal version TKDD, 2012); van der Maaten & Hinton, JMLR, 2008
    4. 2016
      How to read a t-SNE plot

      Interactive demonstrations that cluster sizes, distances and even the number of clusters change with perplexity and that random data can look structured.

      Wattenberg, Viégas, Johnson, Distill, 2016
    5. 2018
      UMAP

      A faster, graph-based alternative that scales to millions of points and became standard for embeddings and single-cell data.

      McInnes, Healy, Melville, arXiv 1802.03426, 2018
    6. 2019–21
      Initialisation and tilted cuts

      Kobak and colleagues showed that much of UMAP's apparent advantage at preserving global layout comes from its default initialisation; t-SNE initialised from PCA with a suitable learning rate does similarly. Meanwhile the extended isolation forest replaced axis-parallel cuts with random hyperplanes to remove artefacts in the score map.

      Kobak & Berens, Nat. Commun., 2019; Kobak & Linderman, Nat. Biotechnol., 2021; Hariri et al., IEEE TKDE, 2021
    7. 2021
      Deep anomaly detection surveyed

      A major review of deep methods (autoencoders, one-class networks, self-supervised approaches) and their open problems, notably that many are hard to tune without labels.

      Pang et al., ACM Computing Surveys, 2021
    8. 2022
      ADBench: a sober benchmark

      A large comparison of dozens of detectors on many datasets found that no unsupervised method wins everywhere, that simple methods such as isolation forest stay competitive with deep ones, and that even a handful of labels (semi-supervised) helps a lot.

      Han et al., NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2022
    9. 2023
      The case against over-reading embeddings

      A widely discussed paper argued that t-SNE and UMAP plots routinely distort distances and local structure, and that conclusions drawn from them should be checked in the original space.

      Chari & Pachter, PLoS Comput. Biol., 2023
    10. 2024–26
      Foundation models meet anomaly detection

      Pre-trained tabular and time-series models, and LLMs reading transaction narratives, are being tested as anomaly scorers. Evidence is early; I have not seen it replace isolation forests in production at scale. Treat claims with the ADBench lesson in mind.

      Active research area; see A8 and R9

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Equating "unusual" with "fraud"

      The top alerts are VIP customers, month-end payroll, a new product launch, or a broken data feed. Analysts stop trusting the queue.

      Spot itHave investigators grade a sample of the top 100 as fraud, unusual-but-legitimate or data error, and report the split.
    • Claiming accuracy with no labels

      "The model detects 95% of anomalies", on data where the anomalies were defined by the model.

      Spot itAsk what the ground truth is and who produced it. If the answer is "the same algorithm", there is no evidence.
    • Training on data that contains the fraud

      The autoencoder or forest learns the rare pattern as normal, especially if it is recurring (a mule account active for months).

      Spot itCheck whether known confirmed cases score as normal. Remove them and old attack periods from training.
    • An autoencoder with too much capacity

      Reconstructs everything, so the error is flat and nothing stands out.

      Spot itCompare reconstruction error on known anomalies with held-out normal data. If the distributions overlap, shrink the bottleneck.
    • Unscaled features

      Amount in fils dominates PCA, an autoencoder or a distance method; the "anomaly" is just the largest transaction.

      Spot itLook at which features carry the error or the first loading. If it is one large-unit column, standardise or take logs.
    • Drift read as anomaly, or anomaly as drift

      Ramadan spending, salary days or a new merchant category pushes scores up for everyone. The alert queue floods.

      Spot itPlot the share flagged by week. A smooth rise across all segments is drift or seasonality; a spike concentrated in a few accounts is an event. Build seasonality into features or baselines.
    • Reading cluster size and gaps off a t-SNE or UMAP plot

      "Segment A is much more diverse than segment B" or "these two groups are far apart".

      Spot itRe-draw with another perplexity or seed. If the sizes, gaps or cluster count change, the original claim was an artefact. Measure in the original space.
    • Fitting PCA or the scaler before the split

      Test-set structure leaks into the learned directions; held-out results look better than they are.

      Spot itCheck that scaler and PCA are fitted inside the training fold, ideally as a single pipeline object.
    • Naming a principal component as if it were a fact

      "Component 2 is customer wealth." It is a weighted blend, rotated arbitrarily if eigenvalues are close.

      Spot itAsk for the loadings and for stability across resamples. Unstable loadings mean the label is a story.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our fraud model knows the attacks we've already seen. An anomaly detector is a second line: it learns what normal customer behaviour looks like and flags what doesn't fit, so it can surface tactics we haven't labelled. It does not know what is fraud, so the alerts go to a separate queue and we judge it by how many an investigator finds useful. I'd start with one analyst reviewing the top fifty a day for a month.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start with an isolation forest on standardised behavioural ratios, because it needs no scaling, is cheap, and ADBench shows it is hard to beat. I'd evaluate with injected anomalies, back-tested confirmed cases and analyst-graded top alerts, and test stability across seeds and months. An autoencoder only goes in if it beats that baseline; I'd keep the bottleneck narrow, train on cleaned data, and monitor error by segment. For visualisation I'd use PCA-initialised UMAP, and make claims about clusters in the original space.”
    “We have no fraud labels. Can we still build something?”

    Yes, an anomaly detector, but be clear what it delivers: a ranking of unusual behaviour, not a fraud probability. Start with isolation forest on good features, have analysts grade the top alerts, and use their answers as the first labels for a supervised model. The unsupervised score then becomes one input to that model.

    “Why an isolation forest rather than an autoencoder?”

    It is fast, needs no scaling, has few settings and performs competitively in benchmarks such as ADBench. An autoencoder adds tuning (architecture, bottleneck, regularisation) and a risk of reconstructing anomalies, and needs standardised inputs. It pays off with rich or sequential inputs and when you want per-feature error as an explanation. I'd try the forest first and justify the autoencoder against it.

    “How do you set the threshold without labels?”

    From capacity and value rather than a score cut. If investigators can look at 200 a day, flag the top 200, and measure the share they find useful. Raise or lower N as that share and the review cost change. The score's numerical value has no business meaning on its own.

    “The t-SNE plot shows five clearly separate customer segments. Can we use them?”

    As a hypothesis, not a finding. t-SNE preserves neighbours, not sizes or gaps, and the number of clusters depends on perplexity and the random start. I'd re-run with different settings, cluster in the original or PCA space, and check the segments differ on measurable business outcomes before using them.

    “Is PCA the same as an autoencoder?”

    A linear autoencoder with one hidden layer learns the same subspace as PCA. Real autoencoders use non-linear activations and several layers, so they can capture curved structure PCA cannot, at the cost of tuning and instability. When the structure is mostly linear, PCA is cheaper and more reliable.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a point far from the crowd get isolated after fewer random cuts?

    A random cut value chosen between a feature's minimum and maximum is likely to fall in the empty gap between the outlier and everyone else, separating it immediately. A point in a dense region is surrounded, so most cuts keep it with many others and it needs many more.

    2. Each tree sees 256 rows, so c is about 10.2. A transaction has an average path length of 4. What is its score, and is it odd?

    s = 2−4/10.2 = 2−0.39 ≈ 0.76. Well above 0.5 (an average point), so it is fairly anomalous; clear outliers tend to score above about 0.8, though the cut should come from review capacity, not from the number.

    3. Why might six near-identical fraud accounts score as normal in an isolation forest, and what could you change?

    They shield each other (masking): a cut that separates one from the crowd leaves the other five with it, so each needs more cuts. Reduce the subsample size so each tree sees only one or two of them, add features that distinguish them, or look for clusters directly with a density or graph method.

    4. An autoencoder's bottleneck is as wide as its input. What happens to anomaly detection?

    It can learn to copy its input, so reconstruction error is near zero for everyone, including fraud. The bottleneck must be narrow enough that only the common structure survives.

    5. A customer is 2 standard deviations above average on card spend and 1.5 below on ATM cash, in data where the two have correlation 0.9. Is she a univariate outlier? Is she an anomaly?

    Not on either feature alone: 2 and 1.5 are unremarkable. But normal customers sit close to the line where the two move together, and she is far from it, so her reconstruction error (and Mahalanobis distance) is large. The combination is what is odd.

    6. You have 10 standardised features, all pairwise correlations 0.5. What share of variance does the first component carry, and how many components for 90%?

    First eigenvalue = 1 + 9 × 0.5 = 5.5, out of a total of 10: 55%. The other nine are 0.5 each (5% each). To reach 9.0 you need 5.5 plus seven more at 0.5, so eight components in total.

    7. One feature is in fils and the rest in thousands of dirhams. You run PCA without standardising. What do you see?

    The first component is almost entirely that one feature, because it has by far the largest variance, and it can explain over 99% of the total. The other features barely influence the result. Standardise, or choose units deliberately.

    8. Name three things you must not conclude from a t-SNE plot.

    That a bigger cluster is a more diverse group; that two clusters far apart are more different than two closer ones; that the number of visible clusters is the real number of segments. Also that it will look the same next run. Keep to "these points are neighbours".
