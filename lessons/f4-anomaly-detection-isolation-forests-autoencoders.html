    Clustering, Anomaly Detection and Dimensionality Reduction

    Part 1

    Learning without answers

    In one sentenceThese methods look for structure in data that has no answer column: groups, odd ones out, and simpler summaries.

    A normal model learns from labelled examples, such as emails marked spam or not spam. That is supervised learning. Here nobody has marked anything. The model only gets the raw rows and has to find patterns itself. That is unsupervised learning.

    Our running example is a shop with a table of customers. Each has two numbers: monthly spend and visits per month. Three jobs, three methods:

    • Clustering: which customers are alike? Sort them into groups.
    • Anomaly detection: which customer is unlike all the rest? Flag the odd one.
    • Dimensionality reduction: can fewer numbers describe each customer almost as well? Shrink the table.
    Part 2

    Clustering: finding groups

    In one sentenceClustering sorts rows into groups of similar rows, where "similar" means close together on the numbers.

    The best-known method is k-means. You choose how many groups you want, called k. Here k = 3:

    The crosses are the group centres. Every customer belongs to the nearest centre.

    How it differs from classification

    A classifier is told the groups in advance (spam, not spam) and learns to spot them. A clustering method is told nothing. It invents the groups, and the labels ("big spenders") are names you add afterwards.

    How k-means works

    1. Drop k centres at random places.
    2. Assign each customer to the nearest centre.
    3. Move each centre to the average of its customers.
    4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until nothing moves.

    How to judge the groups

    • You choose k, and the data won't object. k-means returns k groups even on a shapeless cloud.
    • Silhouette score runs from −1 to 1 and says how cleanly the groups separate. Around 0.7 is strong. Around 0.4 is weak.
    • Stability: run again on a different sample. Real groups reappear. Fake ones don't.
    • Scale first. If spend runs to 40,000 and visits to 15, distance is almost all spend. Standardise the columns, and take a log of heavy-tailed money amounts.

    Segments describe, they don't explain. "Weekend big spenders" says how the algorithm sorted two columns. It does not say why they behave that way, or that they will react alike to an offer. Test that with an experiment.

    Part 3

    Anomaly detection: finding the odd one

    In one sentenceAnomaly detection learns what normal looks like, then flags anything that doesn't fit.

    It is useful when you have few or no examples of the thing you fear. A new fraud attack won't look like old labelled fraud, so flip the question: not "what does fraud look like?" but "what is normal, and what isn't?"

    How it differs from clustering and classifying

    • Clustering describes the crowd. Anomaly detection cares about the one who is not in it.
    • A supervised model learns known fraud and wins when you have good labels. A detector only knows "rare".

    Isolation forest: count the cuts

    Picture a crowded square. A loner by the wall can be picked out with one or two questions. Someone in the middle of the crowd takes many.

    1. Make random cuts. Each tree picks a feature and a random split point, again and again, until every customer is alone.
    2. Count the cuts needed for each customer.
    3. Average over many trees. A short path means odd. A long path means normal.

    The score runs from 0 to 1. Around 0.5 is typical, and near 1 is strongly odd. No value means "fraud". Each tree sees only a small random sample, so it is fast on millions of rows and needs no scaling.

    Autoencoders take another route. A small neural network squeezes each row through a narrow middle and tries to rebuild it. Normal rows rebuild well. An odd mix rebuilds badly, and that reconstruction error is the anomaly score. It needs more care than an isolation forest, so try simple methods first.

    How to read the results

    • Unusual is not fraud. VIP customers, payday and Ramadan all look odd. The score only ranks, and a person decides.
    • Set the alert line by capacity: if analysts can check 100 cases a day, flag the top 100.
    • No labels means no recall. Check it by planting known odd rows and seeing whether they rank high, and by having analysts grade the top cases.
    Part 4

    PCA: fewer numbers, same story

    In one sentencePCA (principal component analysis) replaces many correlated columns with a few blended ones that keep most of the variety.

    Customers who spend more also visit more, so the two columns move together. One line through the cloud describes most of it:

    Each customer's position along the line is one number. It replaces two, and little is lost.

    How it differs from the other two

    • Clustering shrinks many rows into a few groups. PCA shrinks many columns into a few new columns.
    • Feature selection keeps some original columns. PCA keeps blends of all of them, which are harder to name.

    How it works

    1. Standardise the columns so none wins by having bigger numbers.
    2. Find the direction with the most spread. That is the first component.
    3. Find the next direction with the most spread that is at right angles to the first, and so on.
    4. Keep the first few and drop the rest.

    How many to keep? Each component reports the share of spread it carries. Keep enough to reach about 90%, or better, keep the number that gives the best result in the model that follows. "90% of the spread" is not "90% of the useful information", because PCA never sees the answer.

    Maps from t-SNE and UMAP draw messy data in 2-D so that neighbours stay neighbours. They are good for exploring. But cluster sizes and gaps on the map mean little, and settings change the picture. Cluster on the real data, then draw the result on the map.

    Part 5

    Which to use

    MethodQuestion it answersStrong atWeak at
    k-meansWhich rows are alike?Fast first look at groups, millions of rowsRound blobs only, you must pick k
    DBSCAN / HDBSCANWhere are the dense regions?Groups of any shape, labels noiseNeeds a distance scale set
    Isolation forestWhich row is unlike the rest?Fast, no scaling, no labelsMisses odd combinations of ordinary values
    AutoencoderWhich row can't be rebuilt?Odd combinations, says which inputs didn't fitMore tuning, fiddly with categories
    PCACan fewer columns do?Cheap, cuts correlated columnsStraight lines only, blends are hard to name

    These methods are strong at:

    • Finding what nobody labelled, including new attack types.
    • Cold starts, where a new product has no fraud history yet.

    They are weak at:

    • Beating a supervised model when you have good labels.
    • Precision. Most unusual cases are innocent, and analysts lose trust in a noisy queue.
    • Explaining decisions to a regulator or customer. "It looked odd" is rarely enough.

    In practice: standardise first, try k-means with several k, try an isolation forest as a baseline detector, and use PCA to cut correlated columns. Fit all of them on training data only.

    In banking, clustering segments customers, isolation forests and autoencoders surface unfamiliar fraud and data errors for analysts, and PCA compresses piles of correlated balances.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Not scaling the columns

      Distance-based methods and PCA are won by whichever column has the biggest numbers. Standardise first.

    • Believing the groups are real

      k-means always returns k groups. Check silhouette and stability before naming segments.

    • Treating "odd" as "fraud"

      A detector finds rare, not wrong. Have analysts grade the top cases.

    • Trusting a t-SNE or UMAP map

      Sizes and gaps are not honest. Use it to explore, not to prove.

    1. What is the difference between supervised and unsupervised learning?

    Supervised learning learns from labelled answers. Unsupervised learning has no answers and looks for structure in the rows alone.

    2. k-means gives a silhouette of 0.3 for every k you try. What does that suggest?

    There are probably no clear groups. The algorithm is just cutting a continuous cloud into pieces.

    3. Why does an isolation forest score an outlier as odd?

    Random cuts isolate it in very few steps, while normal points need many.

    4. How does PCA differ from clustering?

    Clustering groups similar rows. PCA merges correlated columns into a few new ones.

    5. You have plenty of labelled fraud. Do you still want an anomaly detector?

    A supervised model is better at known fraud. A detector can still add a second net for unfamiliar patterns.
