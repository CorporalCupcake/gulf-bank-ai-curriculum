    F3.5 Day one About 35 minutes

    Imbalanced Data: When the Thing You Want Is Rare

    Why accuracy goes blind at 0.5% fraud, what resampling and class weights really do to your probabilities, why the threshold is the lever that matters, and how few positives you actually have.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceImbalance is a symptom: the real problems are that you have few positive examples, that missing one costs far more than a false alarm, and that the labels arrive late. Fix those with the threshold, honest splits and intervals, and treat rebalancing as a tool that shifts probabilities and must be undone before anyone uses them.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A card-fraud team at a Gulf bank screens 100,000 transactions a day. Five in every thousand, 0.5%, turn out to be fraud. A data scientist builds a model and reports accuracy of 98.9%. The head of fraud is pleased until someone points out that a program with one line, "call everything legitimate", scores 99.5%.

    The program catches no fraud at all. It wins on accuracy because accuracy counts every transaction equally, and 99.5% of transactions are legitimate. Getting those right is easy and worth nothing. The 0.5% you care about hardly moves the number.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Judge a rare-event model on the rare class. What share of fraud did it catch (recall)? What share of its alerts were real (precision)? Both are about the minority. Accuracy mostly measures the majority. F7 builds these properly; here we only need the habit.
    • "Imbalanced" is not the disease. A model is not broken because 99.5% of rows are one class. The trouble is what comes with it: very few positives to learn from, a lopsided cost of errors, and labels that show up weeks late. Most of this lesson is about those three.
    • Rebalancing the data is a tool with a side effect. It can help a weak model find the minority class. It also changes the probabilities the model outputs, and nobody downstream knows unless you tell them. Part 2 shows exactly how far and how to undo it.
    Hold onto thisBefore touching the data, ask three questions. What does a missed positive cost against a false alarm? How many positives do I really have? How long until a label is trustworthy? The answers decide the metric, the threshold and the split. Resampling is usually the last thing to reach for.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why rare events defeat the usual numbers

    The base-rate arithmetic from F1.4 and F7 is the whole reason. Precision, the share of alerts that are real, depends on the fraud rate π as well as on the model:

    precision=π·TPRπ·TPR+(1−π)·FPR

    TPR is recall (share of fraud caught) and FPR is the share of legitimate transactions wrongly flagged. With π = 0.5%, TPR = 70% and FPR = 1%: 0.0035 ÷ (0.0035 + 0.00995) = 26%. Three in four alerts are false, from a model that looks excellent by its rates. The 99.5% legitimate transactions multiply even a small FPR into a large pile of false alarms.

    That is also why the choice between ROC and precision-recall curves matters here. The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR, and neither involves π, so ROC-AUC is unchanged when fraud goes from 5% to 0.05%. The precision-recall curve uses precision, which collapses as π falls, so PR-AUC (average precision) tracks the workload your team will actually face. A useless model has ROC-AUC 0.5 at any prevalence, but PR-AUC roughly equal to π, which is the right floor to compare against. F7 has the simulation and the nuance (ROC is still the right tool for comparing ranking quality across populations with different rates); go there rather than repeat it here.

    One practical consequence: never compare PR-AUC across datasets with different fraud rates, or quote it without the rate beside it. A PR-AUC of 0.30 is a superb result at 0.05% prevalence and a poor one at 20%.

    Your real sample size is the number of positives

    A fraud table with five million rows feels like big data. For learning what fraud looks like, it isn't. Each legitimate row says a little about "normal"; the information about the boundary between fraud and not-fraud comes mostly from the fraud rows. As a rule of thumb, the effective sample size is closer to the number of positives than to the number of rows. In logistic regression this is literal: the uncertainty on the intercept and on most coefficients shrinks with the minority count, and adding more negatives helps little once you have enough of them (King and Zeng, 2001).

    The same holds for evaluation. Recall is a proportion estimated from the positives in your test set, so its standard error is

    SE(recall)≈r(1−r)n1

    r is the measured recall and n1 the number of positives in the test set. A 95% interval is about ±2 standard errors (F2.1). At r = 0.8 this is ±0.78 ÷ √n1: with 100 frauds in the test set you can only say recall is 80% give or take 8 points. The number of legitimate rows does not appear.

    Five million rows and half a million rows give exactly the same precision on recall, because both have 1,000 positives. The AML case is worse: with 40 confirmed cases in the test slice, the difference between two models at 78% and 84% recall is noise. This is the power calculation of F2.7 with positives as the sample size, and it should shape what you promise. Two corollaries:

    • Model complexity is limited by positives, not rows. An old rule of thumb asks for about ten events per candidate variable; newer work gives more careful criteria (Riley et al., 2020). Either way, 200 positives do not support a model with 300 features unless something regularises it hard.
    • Report intervals. Bootstrap the test positives (F2.2) when you compare models, and say "the models are not distinguishable on 40 cases" when that is true.

    Weights, undersampling, oversampling, SMOTE: what each really does

    All the standard fixes try to stop the model ignoring the minority class. They differ in mechanism, and the differences matter more than the folklore suggests.

    Why resampling inflates probabilities, and the fix

    Bayes' rule (F1.4) splits the posterior odds into two pieces: how much the evidence favours fraud (the likelihood ratio), times the prior odds. When you drop legitimate rows at random, regardless of their features, you change the prior odds in your training table and leave the likelihood ratio alone. So the model faithfully learns the likelihood ratio, but it multiplies by the wrong prior. Its odds come out too high by exactly the factor you altered the mix by.

    Keep a fraction β of legitimate rows. The odds the model reports are 1/β times the true odds. Undo it:

    p=βpsβps−ps+1

    ps is the probability from the model trained on the undersampled data, β the share of legitimate rows kept, and p the corrected probability. This is the form given by Dal Pozzolo et al. (2015). In odds terms it is just "multiply by β". For a class weight w on the fraud rows, divide the odds by w: p = pw ÷ (pw + w(1 − pw)). Example: keep 1 in 100 (β = 0.01), model says 33%: odds 0.49, times 0.01 gives 0.0049, so p ≈ 0.5%.

    The picture explains three things you will hear in meetings.

    • AUC did not change and the model was not worse. The ordering of transactions is the same. Only the labels on the probability axis moved. This is the undersampling failure from F7.4 seen from the inside.
    • It bites when the probability is used as a number. A fixed cut such as "alert above 10%", an expected-loss calculation, a pricing or provisioning model (IFRS 9 probability of default), or a blend with another model's score all assume the number means what it says. If you only send the top 300 scores a day to analysts, calibration doesn't matter.
    • The correction assumes a clean mechanism. It is exact when sampling depends on the label alone and the model is well specified. For boosted trees and neural nets under heavy resampling the shift is not a neat constant. The robust habit is to hold out an untouched, representative validation set and recalibrate on it (isotonic or Platt scaling, F7.4), whatever you did in training. Never evaluate on the resampled set: precision there is meaningless.

    On SMOTE specifically. It was a reasonable idea in 2002 for weak learners that cannot find a thin minority region, and it is still widely taught. The evidence since then is not kind to it with modern models: strong classifiers such as gradient boosting with a tuned threshold show little or no gain from it, and plain random oversampling does about as well. It also needs a distance, which is awkward for categorical fields like merchant category or channel, and it invents transactions that never happened. Above all, it must be applied inside each training fold only. Oversample before splitting and synthetic points built from test-set neighbours leak into training, and AUC jumps for entirely the wrong reason.

    The threshold is the real lever

    Most of what people hope to get from rebalancing, a model that "pays attention" to fraud, is available more directly. A model produces a score. You choose where to draw the line. Moving the line trades missed fraud against false alarms along the same curve, and nothing about resampling creates a better curve for a well-fitted model.

    If probabilities are calibrated, the cost-minimising rule is simple: alert when the expected loss avoided exceeds the cost of acting. With AED 400 at stake and AED 40 to review, alert when p × 400 > 40, so p > 10%. If review capacity is the binding limit, alert on the top k scores per day instead. Both are decisions about the threshold, made from business numbers. Neither needs a balanced training set. F7.3 builds this out; the simulation below shows the part that is specific to this lesson.

    The orange line is the trap. A team sets class weights, leaves the old "alert above 10%" rule in place, and quietly floods the review queue, because the same score now maps to a larger shown probability. The remedy is either to move the threshold to the best cut, which depends only on costs and the true score distribution, or to correct the probabilities first so that 10% means 10%. Doing neither is the common failure.

    When might weighting or resampling still help? When the learner is flexible and the minority class is so thin that, unweighted, it spends its capacity on the majority and never finds the small fraud pocket. Then weights act on the shape of the fit and the ranking improves, not just the labels. Treat that as an empirical question: compare against the plain model on an untouched, time-ordered test set, with intervals, using precision at your review capacity as the metric.

    Splitting without fooling yourself

    With few positives, how you split matters as much as what you do to the training data.

    SplitWhat it doesUse it forDanger
    Random splitRows land in train or test by chance.Almost nothing in banking.With 0.05% positives a fold can have too few frauds, and future fraud leaks into the past.
    Stratified split / stratified k-foldKeeps the fraud share equal in every fold.Stable estimates when time does not matter: application scorecards on a snapshot, benchmarks of methods.Fixes the count problem, not the time problem. It will still mix tomorrow into yesterday.
    Temporal splitTrain on months 1–9, validate on 10, test on 11–12.Any live system. Fraud tactics, customer mix and rules change, and the model will face the future.Fewer positives per slice, so wide intervals. Needs an embargo for label delay (next section).
    Group splitAll of a customer's, account's or device's rows go to one side.Whenever one entity has many rows: the same card, the same mule account, the same complaint author.If skipped, the model memorises the entity and test scores are fantasy.

    The usual sound design is temporal and grouped, stratified where it can be, with repeated or rolling windows so you can see variation between periods, not one lucky number. Point-in-time correctness (F3.1) governs which features existed at each date; this is the same discipline applied to the labels.

    Label delay: the fraud you cannot see yet

    A card transaction is labelled fraud when the customer disputes it, an analyst confirms it, or a chargeback arrives. That takes days to months. A loan is labelled defaulted only after 90 days past due. So at any moment, the most recent transactions look cleaner than they are, and the missing frauds are exactly the newest ones, which are the ones the model most needs to learn.

    If you treat "no fraud label yet" as "legitimate", recent weeks teach the model that the new pattern is normal. This is censoring, the same problem that survival analysis (F2.8) handles for time to default. In practice there are three remedies: leave an embargo so that training and evaluation only use periods old enough for most labels to have matured; use techniques that treat recent labels as incomplete; and monitor how the labelled rate of a given week keeps rising for months after it ends.

    This is why a model can look excellent in back-testing and disappoint in its first month. The evaluation period had mature labels; the live period does not, and neither do its monitoring numbers. When the fraud team reports "recall fell in March", ask whether March's labels were still arriving. Dal Pozzolo and colleagues (2018) made this the centre of their fraud-detection design: train separately on fresh analyst feedback and on older delayed labels, and combine them.

    Delay is not only a nuisance in the target. Alert-driven labels are also selective: you only confirm what you alerted on (F2.6, "selective labels"). Keep a small random sample of unflagged transactions reviewed, so you have an unbiased estimate of what you miss.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Imbalance sits where probability, evaluation and process meet. These are the places it comes back.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, thresholds, calibrationThe measuring side of this lesson

    F7 teaches how to measure a rare-event model and how to calibrate it. This lesson is about what you do to the training data and why it changes what F7 measures.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rule and base ratesPrior odds times evidence

    The prior-shift correction is Bayes' rule with a different prior. Resampling edits the prior and leaves the evidence alone.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and the bootstrapPut an interval on recall

    With 40 positives in a test slice, recall is a wide interval. Bootstrap the positives before declaring a winner.

    F2.3 · F2.4 · F2.7 · Tests and powerPower is about positives

    How many frauds do you need to detect a 5-point recall gain? The answer is a count of positives, not of rows.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsThe intercept carries the prior

    In logistic regression, resampling or weighting shifts the intercept and leaves the slopes roughly as they were. That is the algebra behind the correction.

    F2.6 · Selection biasLabels exist where someone looked

    Fraud and AML labels come from alerts. A retrained model inherits the old rules' blind spots unless you keep a random review sample.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisLabel delay is censoring

    A loan that has not defaulted yet is not a good loan, it is an unfinished one. Survival methods use that information properly.

    F3.1 · F3.6 · Leakage and experimentsSplits and proof

    Resampling before splitting is leakage. And the only clean proof that a rebalanced model improved fraud losses is a controlled experiment, not a better offline score.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringPrevalence drift moves calibration

    When the fraud rate shifts, ranking holds and calibration slips. The prior-shift formula is also the quick repair for a changed base rate.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card and payment fraud. Rates of 0.02–0.5%, delayed chargeback labels, capacity-limited analysts. Thresholds and top-k precision dominate.
    • AML transaction monitoring. Confirmed suspicious cases are rarer still and labelled only where an investigator looked. Intervals on tiny positive counts matter most here.
    • Credit default. A few percent bad rate, labels only after 90 days past due, calibration needed for provisioning. Resampling for a scorecard must be corrected before it reaches an expected-loss model.
    • Complaints and KYC text. The one complaint in 400 alleging unauthorised access, in Arabic or English, or the adverse-media hit among thousands of screenings. Rare classes in text models raise the same questions, with weights and thresholds per language.
    • Collections and churn. Moderate imbalance, but the temporal split and the treatment effects of calling (F2.6) matter more than the ratio.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core is settled. The live arguments are about whether the classic fixes are needed at all with modern models, how much they damage calibration, and how to handle label latency in streaming fraud systems.

    Research checked on 1 November 2026. Searches were light; items below are ones I could confirm.

    1. 2001
      Rare events and prior correction in logistic regression

      King and Zeng showed that standard logistic regression underestimates the probability of rare events in small samples, and gave the prior correction and weighting for samples where positives were deliberately over-collected.

      King & Zeng, Political Analysis, 2001
    2. 2002
      SMOTE

      Synthetic minority oversampling by interpolation between neighbours. One of the most cited methods in machine learning, and the template for a large family of variants.

      Chawla et al., JAIR, 2002
    3. 2015
      The PR plot over ROC for imbalanced data, and the undersampling correction

      Saito and Rehmsmeier argued, with simulations, that the precision-recall plot is more informative than ROC on imbalanced data. The same year, Dal Pozzolo et al. gave the probability correction after undersampling used in Simulation 2.

      Saito & Rehmsmeier, PLoS ONE, 2015; Dal Pozzolo et al., IEEE SSCI, 2015
    4. 2017–19
      Loss-level answers for deep nets

      Focal loss down-weights easy examples so that a flood of easy negatives does not dominate training. The class-balanced loss reweights by an "effective number" of samples per class that accounts for overlap between examples.

      Lin et al., ICCV 2017; Cui et al., CVPR 2019
    5. 2018
      Fraud detection with delayed feedback, on real card data

      A realistic model of fraud streams: investigators label alerts quickly, other transactions are labelled late. The proposed strategy trains separate classifiers on feedback and on delayed labels and aggregates them. Tested on over 75 million transactions across three years.

      Dal Pozzolo et al., IEEE TNNLS, 2018
    6. 2022
      Imbalance corrections harm calibration and do not help discrimination

      In simulations with logistic regression, random under- and oversampling and SMOTE all led to poor calibration with strong overestimation of minority-class probabilities, and gave no gain in AUC. Simply shifting the threshold gave similar sensitivity and specificity trade-offs.

      van den Goorbergh et al., JAMIA, 2022
    7. 2022
      Do strong classifiers need balancing?

      Across 73 datasets, balancing did not help strong, consistent classifiers such as gradient boosting. It still helped weaker ones, and simple random oversampling worked about as well as SMOTE-style methods.

      Elor & Averbuch-Elor, arXiv 2201.08528
    8. 2026
      The same finding at scale

      A preprint testing SMOTE, random under- and oversampling on ten clinical datasets with over 600,000 patients found no positive effect on predictive performance, and consistently worse calibration, with shifted calibration intercepts and slopes. It is a preprint and medical rather than banking; the lesson transfers because the mechanism is the same.

      arXiv 2603.00208, 2026 (preprint; check for a published version)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Reporting accuracy, or F1 with no threshold stated

      "The model is 99.4% accurate." Against a baseline of 99.5%, it is worse than nothing.

      Spot itAsk for the do-nothing baseline's accuracy, and for precision and recall at a named threshold or alert volume.
    • Resampling before the split

      SMOTE or duplication on the full dataset, then a train-test split. Copies and neighbours of test rows sit in training. AUC jumps, live performance does not.

      Spot itAsk where in the pipeline resampling happens. It must be inside the training fold only. An AUC above 0.99 on fraud is a prompt to check for this.
    • Evaluating on the resampled set

      Precision looks wonderful because the test set has 50% fraud.

      Spot itCheck the fraud rate in the test table. It should be the real rate, or a correctly weighted version of it.
    • Using resampled probabilities as probabilities

      Feeding them to a 10% alert rule, an expected-loss calculation, or a provisioning model.

      Spot itCompare the average predicted probability on a representative holdout with the actual fraud rate. If the model's average is many times higher, the correction is missing.
    • Random or stratified CV on drifting data

      Neat folds, tight spread, then a poor first quarter live.

      Spot itAsk for a forward-in-time test. If results drop sharply, the random split was borrowing from the future.
    • Treating missing labels as negatives

      Recent transactions with no fraud flag yet are labelled legitimate and used for training or monitoring.

      Spot itPlot the labelled rate by week. If the last weeks slope down towards today, labels are still arriving. Add an embargo.
    • Comparing models on a handful of positives

      Model B beats model A by 3 points of recall on 60 cases.

      Spot itAsk for the number of positives and an interval. At 60 cases the interval is about ±10 points.
    • Splitting rows when entities repeat

      The same customer, device or mule account appears in train and test.

      Spot itCheck whether any customer ID appears on both sides of the split.
    • Comparing PR-AUC across different base rates

      "Our complaints classifier has PR-AUC 0.45; the fraud model only 0.30, so it is worse."

      Spot itAsk for the prevalence beside each PR-AUC, and compare each to its own no-skill floor.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Fraud is about one in two hundred transactions, so a headline accuracy of 99% tells us nothing; doing nothing scores 99.5%. What matters is how much fraud we stop and how many genuine customers we bother. Those are set by where we draw the alert line, using what a missed fraud costs against what a review costs. I'd show you both numbers at the line we'd run in practice, with a margin of error, because we have only a few hundred confirmed cases to test on.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start with a strong gradient-boosted baseline and no resampling, evaluated forward in time with an embargo for label maturity, on precision and recall at the operating point plus precision at review capacity. Thresholds come from the cost ratio. If weights or undersampling beat that baseline with bootstrap intervals, I'd keep them but recalibrate on an untouched, representative validation set, or apply the prior correction, so downstream probabilities mean what they say. Resampling happens inside training folds only.”
    “Should we balance the classes before training?”

    Not by default. For a strong model, a tuned threshold usually does what balancing is meant to do, and balancing damages calibration. I'd try the plain model first, then weights or undersampling as experiments judged on a time-ordered test set. If I use them, I must undo the probability shift before anyone applies a threshold or computes expected loss.

    “Why not use SMOTE? Everyone does.”

    It helps weak learners that cannot find a thin minority region. With boosted trees, studies across many datasets find little or no gain, and plain oversampling does about as well. It invents transactions, struggles with categorical fields, and when done before the split it leaks. I'd only keep it if it beats the baseline with intervals, and then recalibrate.

    “The AUC is 0.96 and the fraud team says the alerts are useless.”

    AUC ignores the fraud rate. At 0.5% fraud, a 1% false positive rate means about 995 false alerts per 100,000 against maybe 350 real, so roughly three quarters of alerts are false. Show the team precision and alert volume at the operating point, and check the threshold was set from costs, not left at 0.5 or at a rebalanced default.

    “We only have 300 confirmed AML cases. Can we build a model?”

    A simple, regularised one, yes, with a plain statement of its uncertainty: a 60-case test slice gives recall to about ±10 points. I'd keep the feature set small, use grouped temporal validation, consider unlabelled-data methods for ranking, and plan for more labels by feeding analyst outcomes back with a random review sample so we are not only learning the old rules.

    “Recall dropped last month. Is the model decaying?”

    Maybe, but first check label maturity. Last month's fraud is still being confirmed, so its recorded recall is biased low until chargebacks land. Compare against the same age of label in earlier months, or re-evaluate once the labels have matured. Then look at drift in the inputs and the fraud rate.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A model has recall 70% and a false positive rate of 1%. Fraud is 0.5% of transactions. What fraction of alerts are fraud, and why can "never flag anything" beat it on accuracy?

    Precision = 0.0035 ÷ (0.0035 + 0.00995) ≈ 26%. Its error rate is 0.29π + 0.01 = 1.145%, against 0.5% for the do-nothing rule. The model only beats do-nothing on accuracy when the fraud rate is above about 1.4%.

    2. You trained on all fraud rows and a random 1 in 50 of the legitimate rows. The model says a transaction has a 40% fraud probability. What is the real probability?

    Odds shown = 0.4 ÷ 0.6 = 0.667. Multiply by β = 1/50: 0.01333. Probability = 0.01333 ÷ 1.01333 ≈ 1.3%. Using the formula: 0.02 × 0.4 ÷ (0.02 × 0.4 − 0.4 + 1) = 0.008 ÷ 0.608 ≈ 1.3%.

    3. A model trained with class weight 20 on fraud shows 30%. What does this correspond to without the weight?

    p = 0.3 ÷ (0.3 + 20 × 0.7) = 0.3 ÷ 14.3 ≈ 2.1%. The weight multiplied the odds by 20, so divide by 20.

    4. You have 2,000 rows of fraud in 10 million. You keep 25% for testing. How precisely can you measure recall around 80%, and does adding 100 million more legitimate rows help?

    The test set has about 500 frauds: ±0.78 ÷ √500 ≈ ±3.5 points. More legitimate rows do not change the number of frauds, so they leave the recall interval unchanged. They would narrow the false-positive-rate estimate.

    5. Someone reports that SMOTE raised the AUC from 0.88 to 0.99. What do you ask first?

    Whether oversampling was done before the train-test split or on the training fold only. A jump like this is the signature of synthetic points built from test neighbours, which leak the answer. Then ask whether the test set has the real fraud rate, and for a forward-in-time result.

    6. The average label delay on chargebacks is six weeks. You evaluate on transactions from the last two weeks and recall looks poor. What is a likely cause and what do you do?

    Most of those weeks' frauds have not been labelled yet, so they count as legitimate in the data, and the model's catches appear as false positives while its misses go unseen. Leave out weeks younger than the label-maturity point (an embargo), or re-evaluate later, and track recall by label age.

    7. When do you not need to correct probabilities after resampling?

    When only the ranking is used, for example sending the top 300 scores to analysts each day. Ordering is preserved. As soon as a number is compared with a fixed threshold, multiplied by an exposure, or blended with another model, correct or recalibrate.
