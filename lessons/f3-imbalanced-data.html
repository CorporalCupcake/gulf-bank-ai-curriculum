    Imbalanced Data: When What You Want Is Rare

    Part 1

    What imbalanced data is

    In one sentenceData is imbalanced when the thing you want to find is rare, so a model that never finds it still looks accurate.

    Our running example is card payments. Out of every 200 payments, 1 is fraud. Here are 200 payments, with the fraud in red:

    That one-line "model" is right 199 times out of 200, and it is useless. This is the accuracy trap: when almost every case is the same, accuracy mostly measures how easy the common case is.

    How it differs from ordinary data

    • Balanced data has roughly equal groups, so accuracy is a fair score and the model has plenty of examples of each.
    • Imbalanced data has a tiny group you care about (fraud, default, disease, churn). The model sees few examples of it, and accuracy hides failure.
    • Small data is a different problem. You can have millions of rows and still be short of data, because what counts is how many rare cases you have, not how many rows.
    Part 2

    What to measure instead

    Take 10,000 payments, of which 50 are fraud (0.5%). A decent model catches 35 of the 50 frauds, and wrongly flags 1% of the legitimate payments, about 100. Sort every payment into one of four boxes:

    • Recall is the share of real fraud you catch: 35 out of 50 is 70%.
    • Precision is the share of your alerts that are real: 35 out of 135 alerts is only 26%.
    • Accuracy here is about 98.9%, which looks superb and tells you nothing.

    How to read it

    • Low precision is normal on rare events, not a sign of a bad model. Good customers outnumber fraud 200 to 1, so even a tiny false-alarm rate produces many false alarms.
    • Precision depends on how common fraud is, not just on the model. The same model at 5% fraud would show far higher precision. So always test on data with the real fraud rate.
    • Your real sample size is the number of rare cases. With 100 frauds in the test set, recall is only good to about 8 points either way. Say "70%, give or take 8" and avoid declaring a winner between 78% and 84% on 40 cases.
    • A high ROC-AUC can still mislead. It ignores how common fraud is, so a model with 0.95 can still drown the alert team. The next lesson covers precision-recall curves.
    Part 3

    What to do about it

    Work down this list and stop when the model is good enough. Resampling comes last, not first.

    1. Pick the right score. Use recall and precision, or the money lost, never plain accuracy.
    2. Keep the test set at the real rate. Never balance it. Split off the test set before you resample anything.
    3. Move the threshold. A model gives each payment a score, and you choose the line above which it raises an alert. This often does more than any rebalancing trick.
    4. Add class weights. Tell the model that a missed fraud counts several times as much as a false alarm. No data is thrown away or invented.
    5. Resample only if still needed. Change the training data so fraud looks more common, using one of the methods below.
    6. Fix the probabilities if you used step 4 or 5, because both inflate them.

    The resampling methods

    • Undersampling drops most of the common cases at random. It trains faster on huge tables but throws information away.
    • Oversampling copies the rare cases. It is cheap, but a flexible model can memorise the copies. Copy only after the train/test split.
    • SMOTE invents new rare cases by blending a real one with its nearest rare neighbours. It suits numeric features and struggles with categories or overlapping classes.

    The side effect: resampling makes fraud look more common to the model, so its probabilities come out too high. Keep 1 legitimate payment in 100 and the odds come out about 100 times too high, so a displayed 33% is really about 0.5%. The ranking of payments is unchanged, but any rule that uses the probability itself (such as "alert above 10%") breaks. Fix it by scaling the odds back down, or by recalibrating on untouched data.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ApproachWhat it doesStrong atWeak at
    Just move the thresholdChanges the alert line onlySimple, no data changed, probabilities stay honestCannot help if the model never learned the rare cases
    Class weightsMakes rare mistakes cost moreEasy, loses nothingInflates probabilities
    UndersamplingDrops common rowsFast on huge dataDiscards information, noisier fit
    Oversampling or SMOTEAdds rare rowsCan help weak modelsMemorising, blurred edges, little gain for strong models

    In practice: start with a good score, a real-rate test set and a tuned threshold. Add class weights if the model still ignores the rare class. Resample last, and recalibrate afterwards. If you do nothing else, remember that more rare cases beat any trick: doubling the months of data helps far more than doubling the legitimate rows.

    Show the formulas

    Recall = frauds caught ÷ all real frauds. Precision = frauds caught ÷ all alerts raised.

    In banking, fraud, default and money-laundering models are all rare-event problems, so regulators and risk teams expect precision, recall and cost to be reported at the real rate, never plain accuracy.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting accuracy

      99.5% sounds brilliant when "never flag anything" scores the same. Always compare with that do-nothing baseline.

    • Balancing the test set

      Precision measured on a 50/50 sample says nothing about live precision. Test at the real rate.

    • Resampling before splitting

      Copies or invented neighbours leak into the test set and scores jump for the wrong reason. Split first, resample inside training only.

    • Using resampled probabilities as they are

      They are inflated. Correct or recalibrate before using any fixed cut-off or expected-loss figure.

    1. Fraud is 0.5% of payments. A model scores 99% accuracy. Is it good?

    Not necessarily. "Never flag anything" scores 99.5%, so this model may be worse than doing nothing. Check recall and precision.

    2. What is the difference between precision and recall?

    Recall: of all real frauds, how many did you catch? Precision: of all your alerts, how many were real?

    3. Why is precision low even for a good model on rare events?

    Good payments vastly outnumber fraud, so a small false-alarm rate still creates more false alarms than there are frauds to catch.

    4. You have 5 million rows but only 80 frauds. How big is your data really?

    About 80, for the purpose that matters. Keep the model simple, and report ranges rather than single numbers.

    5. What do you do first: SMOTE or moving the threshold?

    Move the threshold. It changes nothing in the data and often gives most of the benefit. Resampling is the last resort.
