    Imbalanced Data: When the Thing You Want Is Rare

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A fraud model scores 98.9% accurate. A one-line program, "call everything legitimate", scores 99.5%, because only 0.5% of daily card transactions are fraud. It catches nothing.

    Accuracy counts every transaction equally, so the easy 99.5% swamps the 0.5% you care about. Judge rare-event models on recall (share of fraud caught) and precision (share of alerts that are real).

    "Imbalanced" is a symptom, not the disease. The real problems are few positives, misses that cost far more than false alarms, and labels that arrive late. Rebalancing also inflates the probabilities.

    Hold onto thisAsk first: what does a miss cost against a false alarm, how many positives do I have, and how late are labels? Resampling comes last.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why rare events defeat the usual numbers

    Precision depends on how common fraud is, not just the model. Like a metal detector on a coin-poor beach, even a good one beeps mostly at bottle caps: a model catching 70% of fraud with a 1% false-alarm rate has only about 26% real alerts at 0.5% fraud. ROC-AUC ignores the fraud rate; PR-AUC tracks alert workload, and a useless model scores about the fraud rate. Never quote PR-AUC without the fraud rate beside it. See F7.

    Choose the headline metric from the decision. If analysts can review a fixed number of cases a day, report precision and recall at that alert volume. If a miss and a false alarm have known costs, report expected cost at the chosen threshold. ROC-AUC is fine for a quick ranking comparison, but its false-alarm axis is a share of legitimate transactions. A 1% rate looks tiny until you remember legitimate rows outnumber fraud 200 to 1.

    Your real sample size is the number of positives

    Five million rows feel like big data, but the information comes from the fraud rows: 20 shots tell you more about a footballer's finishing than 5,000 passes. Positives, not rows, limit model complexity and test precision. With 100 test frauds, recall is good to only about 8 points either way, so report intervals.

    This sets how complex a model you can afford. With 200 positives, 300 features invite overfitting: use strong regularisation (penalising big coefficients), shallow trees, a low learning rate and a minimum number of positives per leaf. It also sets how you compare models. Bootstrap the test positives (F2.2) and say "not distinguishable on 40 cases" when true: 78% against 84% recall on 40 cases is noise. Doubling the legitimate rows changes little. Doubling the months of data, and so the positives, changes a lot.

    Weights, undersampling, oversampling, SMOTE

    All of these stop the model ignoring the rare class. The default to beat is doing nothing and choosing the threshold.

    Class weights tell the loss that a missed fraud counts as several missed legitimate rows. No data is lost or invented, and most libraries expose it (class_weight, scale_pos_weight). Use it first if you use anything. Undersampling drops legitimate rows at random. It speeds up training on huge tables, but throws away information and makes the fit noisier; averaging several models, each trained on a different undersample, recovers much of that. Oversampling duplicates fraud rows. It is cheap, but a flexible model can memorise the copies, so duplicate only after the split. SMOTE invents new frauds by interpolating between a fraud and its nearest fraud neighbours. It suits numeric features, and struggles with categories, many dimensions, and classes that overlap, where it blurs the boundary.

    The side effect is inflated probabilities. Resampling changes how common fraud looks in training, not how well the model reads evidence. Keep 1 in 100 legitimate rows and the odds come out about 100 times too high: a shown 33% is really about 0.5%. The fix is to scale the odds back down (prior correction) or recalibrate on untouched data.

    AUC did not change. The ordering is the same; only the labels on the probability axis moved. It bites only when the number is used: a fixed 10% cut, expected loss, IFRS 9, or a blend with another score. Recalibrate (F7.4) on untouched data, and never evaluate on the resampled set.

    SMOTE adds little for strong models. Use it inside each training fold only, or AUC jumps for the wrong reason.

    The threshold is the real lever

    A model gives a score; you choose where to draw the line, like smoke-alarm sensitivity. Moving it trades misses against false alarms along the same curve. Set it from business numbers: with a miss at AED 400 and a review at AED 40, alert above 10%. If capacity is the limit, alert on the top k scores a day (F7.3).

    The 10% is simply review cost divided by cost of a miss, applied to a calibrated probability. Choose the cut on a validation set that looks like live data, and re-choose it when fraud rates, costs or analyst capacity change. Many banks use tiers rather than one line: block above a high score, ask for step-up authentication in the middle, queue for review below that, and let the rest pass.

    The orange line is the trap: set class weights, leave "alert above 10%" in place, and the review queue floods. Move the threshold or correct the probabilities first.

    Splitting without fooling yourself

    Random splits mix tomorrow into yesterday: fine for benchmarks, rarely for live systems. Temporal splits match real use, at the cost of fewer positives per slice. Group splits keep a customer or device on one side, or the model memorises it. A sound design is walk-forward: train on months 1 to 9, validate on 10, then roll on, and keep one final forward slice untouched until the end. Stratifying (keeping fraud counts steady per fold) is worth doing inside that.

    Label delay: the fraud you cannot see yet

    Fraud is labelled days to months late, like exam scripts still being marked: the newest look better than they are. Treating "no label yet" as "legitimate" teaches the model the new pattern is normal. The remedy is an embargo: use only periods old enough for labels to mature.

    This is why a model can shine in back-testing and disappoint in its first month: the test period had mature labels, the live period does not. When recall "falls", ask whether the labels have arrived. Also review a small random sample of unflagged transactions, since you confirm only what you alerted on.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Rebalancing is strong at:

    • Helping flexible models notice small pockets of fraud. Weights change the loss, so a tiny cluster of frauds matters to the fit instead of being averaged away.
    • Cutting training time on tables with hundreds of millions of legitimate rows. Undersampling keeps the frauds and a slice of the rest.
    • Rescuing weak or unstable learners, such as a plain decision tree or a small neural network, that otherwise predict "legitimate" every time.

    Weak at, or don't use when:

    • You need honest probabilities. Provisioning, expected loss and fixed alert rules all break unless you correct or recalibrate.
    • Your model is already strong (gradient boosting with a tuned threshold). Gains are usually small, and the threshold does the real work.
    • You have very few positives. Copies and synthetic points add no new information; 30 frauds stay 30 frauds.
    • Labels are late or noisy. Resampling amplifies the noise, and cannot fix an unlabelled attack.

    Alternatives and how to choose:

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Do nothing, tune the thresholdDefault for gradient boosting or logistic regression; ranking or cost-based alertsThe model ignores the rare class entirely
    Class weightsFlexible model, small fraud pockets, no data to spareYou feed raw probabilities to a fixed rule
    Undersampling (with bagging)Huge tables, slow training, 1,000+ positivesPositives are few; discarded rows hold the signal
    Oversampling or SMOTEWeak learners, numeric features, tiny datasets as an experimentMany categories, overlapping classes, or any leakage risk
    Anomaly detectionAlmost no labels, or novel attacks unlike past fraudYou have enough labels; supervised models win

    In practice: train the plain model, pick the threshold from costs or capacity, then try weights and compare on a time-ordered test set with intervals. Keep resampling only if it wins by more than the interval. Most of the gain comes from better features and honest splits, not from resampling. In card fraud, rates of 0.1% to 0.5% are common, and teams often train on months of data with gradient boosting, recalibrated on a recent holdout. In AML, positives are rarer still and labels slower, so ranking for investigators matters more than calibration. In credit default, calibration matters most, because probabilities feed provisioning, so correct any resampling.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reporting accuracy, or F1 with no threshold

      "99.4% accurate" is worse than the 99.5% do-nothing baseline.

      Spot itAsk for precision and recall at a named threshold.
    • Resampling before the split, or testing on the resampled set

      Copies of test rows sit in training, or the test set is 50% fraud. AUC looks wonderful; live results do not.

      Spot itAsk where resampling happens and the test fraud rate.
    • Using resampled probabilities as probabilities

      Fed to a 10% alert rule or provisioning, they are many times too high.

      Spot itCompare average predicted probability on a representative holdout with the actual fraud rate.
    • Random splits on drifting data, or repeating entities

      Neat folds, then a poor first quarter live.

      Spot itAsk for a forward-in-time test.
    • Treating missing labels as negatives

      Recent transactions with no flag yet are used as legitimate.

      Spot itPlot labelled rate by week. If the last weeks slope down, labels are still arriving.
    • Comparing models without positive counts or intervals

      Model B beats A by 3 points of recall on 60 cases. That is noise.

      Spot itAsk for the positive count and an interval. At 60 cases, recall is good to about 10 points.
    • Adding SMOTE or weights because "the data is imbalanced"

      The strong model gains little, and the probabilities are now wrong.

      Spot itAsk for the plain-model baseline with a tuned threshold, on the same untouched test set.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model catches 70% of fraud and flags 1% of legitimate transactions. Fraud is 0.5% of volume. Roughly what share of alerts are real, and why can "never flag anything" beat it on accuracy?

    About a quarter, because legitimate transactions outnumber fraud 200 to 1. Its errors outnumber the 0.5% error of do-nothing, so do-nothing wins on accuracy while catching nothing.

    2. You trained on all fraud rows and 1 in 50 legitimate rows. The model says 40%. Is the real probability higher or lower?

    Much lower, nearer 1%. Legitimate rows were 50 times rarer in training, so fraud odds are overstated about 50 times. Correct them or recalibrate on untouched data.

    3. Average chargeback delay is six weeks. You evaluate on the last two weeks and recall looks poor. Likely cause?

    Most of those frauds are not yet labelled, so they count as legitimate. Embargo weeks younger than label maturity, or re-evaluate later.

    4. When do you not need to correct probabilities after resampling?

    When only ranking is used, such as sending the top 300 scores to analysts daily. Once a number meets a fixed threshold, an exposure or another model's score, correct it.

    5. You have 5 million transactions and 80 confirmed frauds. A colleague proposes SMOTE plus a deep network. Which would you choose and why?

    A simple, regularised model (logistic regression or small boosted trees), a threshold from costs, and wide intervals. 80 positives limit complexity; SMOTE adds no new information, only interpolated copies. Spend effort on getting more labels and better features.

    6. Your bank wants every transaction above 10% fraud probability blocked. The model was trained with class weight 20. Do you retrain with resampling, move the threshold, or recalibrate? Which would you choose and why?

    Recalibrate on an untouched, representative holdout (or apply the weight's prior correction), then keep the 10% rule. Moving the threshold also works for ranking, but the probability would still be wrong for provisioning or other uses. Retraining changes nothing about the miscalibration.
