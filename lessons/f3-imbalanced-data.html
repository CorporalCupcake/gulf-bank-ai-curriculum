    Imbalanced Data: When the Thing You Want Is Rare

    Why accuracy goes blind at 0.5% fraud, what resampling and class weights really do to your probabilities, why the threshold is the lever that matters, and how few positives you actually have.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceImbalance is a symptom: the real problems are that you have few positive examples, that missing one costs far more than a false alarm, and that the labels arrive late. Fix those with the threshold, honest splits and intervals, and treat rebalancing as a tool that shifts probabilities and must be undone before anyone uses them.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A fraud model scores 98.9% accurate. A one-line program, "call everything legitimate", scores 99.5%, because only 0.5% of the bank's 100,000 daily card transactions are fraud. The program catches no fraud at all.

    It is like a smoke alarm that never rings: right on every day without a fire. Accuracy counts all transactions equally, so the 99.5% that are easy swamp the 0.5% you care about.

    • Judge a rare-event model on the rare class. Recall: what share of fraud did it catch? Precision: what share of its alerts were real? F7 builds these properly.
    • "Imbalanced" is a symptom, not the disease. The real problems are few positives to learn from, a missed fraud that costs far more than a false alarm, and labels that arrive weeks late.
    • Rebalancing is a tool with a side effect. It can help a weak model, but it inflates the probabilities the model outputs. Part 2 shows how to undo it.
    Hold onto thisBefore touching the data, ask: what does a miss cost against a false alarm? How many positives do I really have? How long until a label is trustworthy? Those answers set the metric, threshold and split. Resampling comes last.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why rare events defeat the usual numbers

    Precision (the share of alerts that are real) depends on the fraud rate π, not just the model. Think of a metal detector on a beach with almost no coins: even a good one beeps mostly at bottle caps.

    precision=π·TPRπ·TPR+(1−π)·FPR

    TPR is recall; FPR is the share of legitimate transactions wrongly flagged. With π = 0.5%, TPR = 70%, FPR = 1%: 0.0035 ÷ (0.0035 + 0.00995) = 26%. Three in four alerts are false, from a model that looks excellent by its rates. Do not read precision as a fixed trait of the model: halve π and it drops sharply.

    ROC-AUC uses TPR and FPR, neither of which involves π, so it does not change when fraud falls from 5% to 0.05%. PR-AUC (average precision) uses precision, so it tracks the alert workload you will face. A useless model scores ROC-AUC 0.5 at any rate but PR-AUC of about π, which is the floor to compare against. So never quote PR-AUC without the fraud rate beside it: 0.30 is superb at 0.05% prevalence and poor at 20%. See F7 for the full treatment.

    Your real sample size is the number of positives

    Five million rows feel like big data, but the information about where fraud starts comes from the fraud rows. It is like judging a footballer's finishing: 5,000 passes tell you little, 20 shots tell you something. The effective sample size is close to the number of positives. Extra legitimate rows help little once you have enough (King and Zeng, 2001). The same applies to testing. Recall is a proportion measured on the test positives, so:

    SE(recall)≈r(1−r)n1

    r is measured recall, n1 the positives in the test set. A 95% interval is about ±2 SE (F2.1). At r = 0.8 that is ±0.78 ÷ √n1: with 100 frauds, recall is 80% give or take 8 points. Legitimate rows do not appear.

    • Model complexity is limited by positives, not rows. A rule of thumb is about ten events per candidate variable; newer criteria are more careful (Riley et al., 2020). 200 positives do not support 300 features without heavy regularisation.
    • Report intervals. Bootstrap the test positives (F2.2) and say "not distinguishable on 40 cases" when true.

    Why resampling inflates probabilities, and the fix

    Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds (F1.4). Dropping legitimate rows at random changes the prior in your training table but not the likelihood ratio. The model learns the evidence correctly and multiplies by the wrong prior. Think of a referee told that most players on the pitch are fouling: every call comes out too harsh. Keep a fraction β of legitimate rows and the model's odds are 1/β times the true odds. Undo it:

    p=βpsβps−ps+1

    ps is the model's probability, β the share of legitimate rows kept, p the corrected probability (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015). In odds terms: multiply by β. For a class weight w, divide the odds by w: p = pw ÷ (pw + w(1 − pw)). Example: keep 1 in 100, model says 33%: odds 0.49 × 0.01 = 0.0049, so p ≈ 0.5%.

    • AUC did not change and the model was not worse. The ordering is the same; only the labels on the probability axis moved.
    • It bites when the probability is used as a number: a fixed "alert above 10%" cut, an expected-loss calculation, IFRS 9 probability of default, or a blend with another score. If you only send the top 300 scores a day to analysts, calibration does not matter.
    • The correction assumes a clean mechanism. It is exact when sampling depends on the label alone and the model is well specified. Under heavy resampling, boosted trees and neural nets shift in a messier way. The robust habit: recalibrate (isotonic or Platt, F7.4) on an untouched, representative validation set. Never evaluate on the resampled set; precision there is meaningless.

    On SMOTE: a reasonable 2002 idea for weak learners. Since then, strong classifiers such as gradient boosting with a tuned threshold show little or no gain, and plain oversampling does about as well. It needs a distance (awkward for merchant category or channel) and invents transactions that never happened. Apply it inside each training fold only: oversample before splitting and synthetic points built from test neighbours leak, and AUC jumps for the wrong reason.

    The threshold is the real lever

    A model gives a score; you choose where to draw the line, like setting the sensitivity on a smoke alarm. Moving the line trades missed fraud against false alarms along the same curve. For a well-fitted model, resampling does not create a better curve.

    With calibrated probabilities, alert when the expected loss avoided exceeds the cost of acting. With AED 400 at stake and AED 40 to review, alert when p × 400 > 40, so p > 10%. If review capacity is the limit, alert on the top k scores per day. Both come from business numbers, not a balanced training set (F7.3).

    The orange line is the trap: a team sets class weights, leaves "alert above 10%" in place, and floods the review queue, because the same score now shows a larger probability. Either move the threshold to the best cut (it depends only on costs and the true score distribution) or correct the probabilities first.

    Weighting can still help when a flexible learner would otherwise spend its capacity on the majority and miss a small fraud pocket. Then it changes the shape of the fit and improves ranking. Test it: compare with the plain model on an untouched, time-ordered test set, with intervals, using precision at review capacity.

    The sound design is temporal and grouped, stratified where possible, with rolling windows so you see variation between periods. This is point-in-time correctness (F3.1) applied to labels.

    Label delay: the fraud you cannot see yet

    Fraud is labelled when the customer disputes it, an analyst confirms it, or a chargeback lands: days to months. A loan is labelled defaulted only after 90 days past due. It is like exam results still being marked: the newest scripts look better than they are. The missing frauds are the newest, which the model most needs to learn.

    Treating "no label yet" as "legitimate" teaches the model that the new pattern is normal. This is censoring (F2.8). Remedies: an embargo (use only periods old enough for labels to mature), methods that treat recent labels as incomplete, and tracking how a week's labelled rate keeps rising for months.

    This is why a model can shine in back-testing and disappoint in its first month: the test period had mature labels, the live period does not. When someone says "recall fell in March", ask whether March's labels have arrived. Dal Pozzolo et al. (2018) train separately on fresh analyst feedback and older delayed labels, then combine them.

    Labels are also selective: you only confirm what you alerted on (F2.6). Keep a small random sample of unflagged transactions reviewed, for an unbiased estimate of what you miss.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reporting accuracy, or F1 with no threshold stated

      "99.4% accurate" against a 99.5% do-nothing baseline is worse than nothing.

      Spot itAsk for the do-nothing accuracy, and precision and recall at a named threshold or alert volume.
    • Resampling before the split

      Copies and neighbours of test rows sit in training. AUC jumps, live performance does not.

      Spot itAsk where resampling happens. It must be inside the training fold. AUC above 0.99 on fraud is a prompt to check.
    • Evaluating on the resampled set

      Precision looks wonderful because the test set is 50% fraud.

      Spot itCheck the test fraud rate. It should be the real rate, or correctly weighted.
    • Using resampled probabilities as probabilities

      Fed to a 10% alert rule, an expected-loss calculation, or provisioning.

      Spot itCompare average predicted probability on a representative holdout with the actual fraud rate. Many times higher means the correction is missing.
    • Random or stratified CV on drifting data

      Neat folds, tight spread, then a poor first quarter live.

      Spot itAsk for a forward-in-time test. A sharp drop means the split borrowed from the future.
    • Treating missing labels as negatives

      Recent transactions with no flag yet are used as legitimate.

      Spot itPlot labelled rate by week. If the last weeks slope down towards today, labels are still arriving. Add an embargo.
    • Comparing models on a handful of positives

      Model B beats A by 3 points of recall on 60 cases.

      Spot itAsk for the positive count and an interval. At 60 cases it is about ±10 points.
    • Splitting rows when entities repeat

      The same customer, device or mule account is in train and test.

      Spot itCheck whether any customer ID appears on both sides.
    • Comparing PR-AUC across different base rates

      "Complaints classifier PR-AUC 0.45; fraud model only 0.30, so it is worse."

      Spot itAsk for the prevalence beside each PR-AUC and compare each to its own no-skill floor.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model has recall 70% and FPR 1%. Fraud is 0.5% of transactions. What fraction of alerts are fraud, and why can "never flag anything" beat it on accuracy?

    Precision = 0.0035 ÷ (0.0035 + 0.00995) ≈ 26%. Its error rate is 0.29π + 0.01 = 1.145%, against 0.5% for do-nothing. The model beats do-nothing on accuracy only when fraud is above about 1.4%.

    2. You trained on all fraud rows and a random 1 in 50 legitimate rows. The model says 40%. What is the real probability?

    Odds shown = 0.4 ÷ 0.6 = 0.667. Times β = 1/50 gives 0.01333. Probability = 0.01333 ÷ 1.01333 ≈ 1.3%. By the formula: 0.008 ÷ 0.608 ≈ 1.3%.

    3. A model trained with class weight 20 on fraud shows 30%. What is that without the weight?

    p = 0.3 ÷ (0.3 + 20 × 0.7) = 0.3 ÷ 14.3 ≈ 2.1%. The weight multiplied the odds by 20, so divide by 20.

    4. You have 2,000 frauds in 10 million rows and keep 25% for testing. How precisely can you measure recall near 80%, and do 100 million more legitimate rows help?

    About 500 test frauds: ±0.78 ÷ √500 ≈ ±3.5 points. More legitimate rows do not change the fraud count, so the recall interval is unchanged. They would narrow only the FPR estimate.

    5. Someone reports SMOTE raised AUC from 0.88 to 0.99. What do you ask first?

    Whether oversampling was done before the split or inside the training fold only. A jump like this is the signature of synthetic points built from test neighbours. Then ask whether the test set has the real fraud rate, and for a forward-in-time result.

    6. Average chargeback delay is six weeks. You evaluate on the last two weeks and recall looks poor. Likely cause and fix?

    Most of those frauds are not yet labelled, so they count as legitimate: the model's catches look like false positives and its misses go unseen. Apply an embargo for weeks younger than label maturity, or re-evaluate later, and track recall by label age.

    7. When do you not need to correct probabilities after resampling?

    When only ranking is used, such as sending the top 300 scores to analysts daily. Ordering is preserved. Once a number is compared with a fixed threshold, multiplied by an exposure or blended with another model, correct or recalibrate.
