    Learning by Trying, Listening to Signals, and the Word-Count Baseline

    How a bank can learn which offer works while still selling, why sequences of decisions change the answer, what a sampling rate does to a phone call, and why a TF-IDF text classifier is the model every language model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBandits and reinforcement learning learn from their own decisions, so they must balance trying new things against using what works and cannot be judged on logs alone; signal processing explains what survives when sound or sensors are turned into numbers; and TF-IDF with a linear classifier is a cheap, readable text baseline that tells you what a fancier model has really earned.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Bandits and RL (F9.7). A bank has three SMS offers and doesn't know which converts best. An A/B test splits customers evenly, then picks a winner, but everyone who got a weak offer meanwhile was a lost sale. A bandit shifts traffic towards the winner while learning, like a diner who mostly orders the best dish but sometimes tries another. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the same when today's action changes tomorrow's situation: a harsh collections call may recover money now and lose the customer next month.

    Signal processing (F9.8). A phone call is a pressure wave, measured thousands of times a second to store it. Which sounds survive, and which turn into something else?

    Classic NLP (F9.9). A complaint containing "unauthorised" and "card" is probably fraud. TF-IDF weights word counts so rare, telling words beat "the" and "my"; a linear model gives each word a readable weight. It is fast and often lands within a few points of much heavier models.

    Hold onto thisYou only see the outcome of what you chose, so a bandit explores deliberately. A word-count model gives a baseline you can explain in one slide. For any model, know what it can observe and what it is compared with.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Bandits: explore, exploit, regret

    Picture a row of slot machines with unknown payouts. A bank faces the same with several actions (offers, messages, collection scripts): you see the reward only for the action you chose. Never send offer B and you never learn how good B is.

    Regret is the price of not knowing: the reward from always playing the best action, minus what you got. A flat regret curve means you've settled on the best; a steadily rising line means you keep paying the same loss per customer.

    • Explore-then-commit (the A/B test): split evenly for a fixed period, then give everyone the winner. If the winner is mistaken or the world changes, you stay wrong.
    • Epsilon-greedy: with probability ε pick randomly, otherwise the best so far. You waste ε of traffic for ever.
    • Thompson sampling: keep a belief about each action's true rate, draw one plausible rate per action, play the highest. Little-known actions have wide beliefs and sometimes draw high, so they get tried; as evidence builds, the spread narrows. Exploration tracks uncertainty with no setting to tune.

    Contextual bandits choose the action given customer features (age band, balance, past response). Delayed rewards are the norm: a loan offer's payoff arrives weeks later, so feed the bandit in batches, like label delay in F3.5.

    Reinforcement learning: when today's action changes tomorrow

    A bandit assumes your action doesn't change the customer's future. Often it does. In collections, a customer in early arrears who gets a gentle SMS may cure; with nothing, they may slip to 30 days late, where cure is much harder. RL is choosing actions in a loop: observe a state, act, get a reward, land in a new state.

    The goal is a policy, a rule from state to action, that maximises total reward over time. A discount γ between 0 and 1 sets how much the future counts: near 0 is short-sighted, near 1 is patient. The core idea is that an action is worth its immediate reward plus the value of where it leads. Q-learning learns this by nudging estimates towards what happened. A bandit is the one-state special case.

    The table is a toy, but its difficulties are real.

    • The reward must be what you want. Reward "AED recovered this month" and the policy over-contacts distressed customers. Put conduct and customer outcomes in the reward, or enforce them as hard constraints.
    • You cannot just try things. Each bank action touches a real customer, so banks mostly use offline RL, learning from logged decisions.
    • Scoring a new policy from old logs. Logs hold only the old policy's choices, so you reweight logged outcomes by how likely the new policy was to take each action. If the old policy never tried an action in some situation, no method can say what it would have done. This is the selection bias of F2.6 and F9.6 again.

    In language models, RL means rewarding outputs a human or checker prefers; see from RLHF to DPO and process reward models and verifiers.

    Signal processing: what survives being turned into numbers

    A signal is a quantity measured over time: a voice, a vibration sensor in an ATM, a corporate account's minute-by-minute balance. To store it you sample it, recording its value a fixed number of times a second. How fast is fast enough?

    The Fourier transform rewrites a signal as a sum of sine waves, each with a strength: a chord split back into its notes. The result, the spectrum, often shows patterns that are tangled in time but clean in frequency, such as a 50 Hz mains hum or a voice between about 300 and 3,400 Hz.

    The rule of thumb: sampling at a given rate faithfully captures frequencies up to half that rate (the Nyquist frequency). Anything above folds back as a different, lower frequency: aliasing, like a wagon wheel that seems to turn backwards on film. Telephone audio is sampled at 8,000 per second, so it holds nothing above 4,000 Hz. Fine for words, poor for the high-frequency detail some voice-clone detectors use.

    • Spectrograms. Cut audio into short windows, take each spectrum, stack them: frequency over time, like sheet music for sound. Modern speech recognisers and voice-fraud detectors feed these to a neural network, which is why convolutions and attention apply to sound.
    • Calls are compressed. Contact-centre audio is narrowed and compressed. A model trained on studio speech can fail on it, so train and test on the same channel.

    Mostly background; it becomes live work in voice biometrics, deepfake-voice detection and ATM sensor monitoring.

    TF-IDF: counting words so that the telling ones count more

    A bag of words ignores order and counts each word: one row per document, one column per vocabulary word, mostly zeros. Raw counts are swamped by "my", "the" and "account". TF-IDF fixes this by multiplying two things: how often the word appears in this document, and how rare it is across all documents. A word in every document gets a weight near zero; one in 2 of 1,000 gets a big one.

    Analogy: in a pub quiz, "the" tells you nothing about the topic, "murabaha" tells you a lot. A high weight means "frequent here and rare elsewhere": the word that characterises this document. BM25 keyword search is the same idea, refined.

    Arabic is harder: prefixes attach to words ("بطاقتي" is "my card" in one token) and spelling and dialect vary. Real pipelines normalise letters and use sub-word units (see tokenisation). The demo below does not.

    Linear text classifiers: a weight per word

    Feed TF-IDF rows to a logistic regression (F2.5) or linear SVM and each word gets a weight, like a checklist where each word adds or removes points. A positive weight pushes towards the class, a negative one away, so every prediction is explained by the words that mattered (F8.1, with no extra tooling).

    This is not a toy. For complaint routing, adverse-media triage or intent detection with enough labels, it lands close to far larger models, trains in seconds, and behaves stably. Its weakness is what a bag discards: order, negation, context. Unseen synonyms ("compromised", "hacked") carry no weight.

    Build this baseline first. It takes an afternoon and tells you what an upgrade buys. If a fine-tuned transformer or an LLM is 2 points better on a held-out set, you know what the extra cost buys. If it is no better, you saved a deployment (see C1).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging a new policy from logs or a simulator

      The system did not record the probability of each choice, or the simulator was built from the same biased data and the policy exploits its errors. Nobody can say how a new policy would really do.

      Spot itDo we store the probability of each logged action? How often did the old policy pick what the new one would? What randomised test follows?
    • Optimising the wrong reward

      Click-through rises, long-term value falls. Recoveries rise, and so do complaints.

      Spot itAsk what is not in the reward. Track a counter-metric (complaints, attrition, 90-day balance).
    • Non-stationary world, stationary learner

      A bandit that averages all history keeps favouring an offer that has stopped working, like the committed strategy in Simulation 1.

      Spot itCompare each arm's recent rate with its lifetime rate. Use discounting or a sliding window.
    • Aliasing and channel mismatch in audio

      A model trained on 16 kHz studio speech runs on 8 kHz compressed calls and quality collapses, or a high-frequency cue vanishes.

      Spot itCheck sampling rate and codec of training and live audio. Evaluate on production-channel audio.
    • Leaking the future or the test set

      A spectrum or smoothing computed over the whole series, or TF-IDF values fitted before the split, lets test information into training.

      Spot itCheck every filter and vectoriser is fitted on training data only (F3.1).
    • Trusting a bag of words, or an LLM with no baseline

      "I did not report any fraud" is flagged as fraud, and new scam wording scores zero. Claiming "the LLM gets 91%" means little if a TF-IDF model scored 89%.

      Spot itRead errors, not scores. Add negation cases to the test set. Ask what a linear model scores on the same split and what each costs per thousand messages.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Three offers convert at 4%, 5.5% and 7%. Why does showing them evenly cost you, and does the cost shrink as you get more customers?

    An even mix converts at the average (5.5%), so every customer who sees a weaker offer is a lost chance against the 7% offer. The loss per customer never shrinks, so the total grows steadily with volume. Only shifting traffic towards the winner stops it.

    2. Why does Thompson sampling explore less over time without anyone lowering a setting?

    Beliefs narrow as data arrives. A narrow belief on a weak arm rarely draws above the best arm's, so it is chosen less. A wide belief on an under-tested arm often draws high, so it gets tried.

    3. What is the difference between a bandit and full reinforcement learning?

    In a bandit your action does not change the next situation: one state, immediate reward. In RL actions change the state and rewards can be delayed, so you must value where an action leads. A bandit is RL with one state.

    4. Why can't you evaluate a new collections policy by averaging the outcomes in your logs?

    Logs hold outcomes only for actions the old policy chose, in the situations it chose them. Customers who got a call differ from those who didn't. Reweight by recorded propensities, and you need overlap between the policies. Even then, a randomised trial is the final check.

    5. A phone line is sampled at 8,000 times a second and a tone sits above 4,000 Hz. What happens to it, and why does it matter for a voice-fraud model?

    It does not vanish; it folds back and shows up as a lower, wrong frequency (aliasing). A model trained on richer audio can lose cues or misread calls, so check the sampling rate of training and live audio.

    6. Why does a word in 900 of 1,000 complaints get almost no weight, while one in 2 of them gets a lot?

    The first word tells you nothing about which complaint you are reading; the second is a strong clue. IDF down-weights common words and up-weights rare, telling ones.

    7. Why does a TF-IDF classifier call "I have not seen any unauthorised transactions" a fraud report, and what are two ways to improve it?

    Bag of words discards order and negation: "unauthorised" has a positive weight wherever it appears. Fixes: add word pairs (bigrams) such as "not seen", or use a model that reads context (fine-tuned transformer, prompted LLM). Test either on a set with negations.
