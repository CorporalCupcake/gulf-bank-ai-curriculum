    Learning by Trying, Listening to Signals, and the Word-Count Baseline

    How a bank can learn which offer works while still selling, why sequences of decisions change the answer, what a sampling rate does to a phone call, and why a TF-IDF text classifier is the model every language model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBandits and reinforcement learning learn from their own decisions, so they must balance trying new things against using what works and cannot be judged on logs alone; signal processing explains what survives when sound or sensors are turned into numbers; and TF-IDF with a linear classifier is a cheap, readable text baseline that tells you what a fancier model has really earned.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    All three topics ask: what can you learn, and what does it cost to learn it?

    Bandits and RL (F9.7). A bank has three SMS offers and doesn't know which converts best. An A/B test splits customers evenly, waits, picks a winner, but everyone who got a weak offer meanwhile was a lost sale. A bandit shifts traffic towards the winner while learning, like a diner who keeps ordering the best dish but occasionally tries another. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the same when today's action changes tomorrow's situation: a harsh collections call may recover money now and lose the customer next month.

    Signal processing (F9.8). A phone call is a pressure wave, measured thousands of times a second to store it. Which sounds survive, and which turn into something else? Often the best description is "how much of each frequency" rather than "what value at each moment". Background for most bank roles, but it explains why voice models behave as they do.

    Classic NLP (F9.9). A complaint containing "unauthorised" and "card" is probably fraud. TF-IDF weights word counts so rare, telling words beat "the" and "my"; a linear model gives each word a readable weight. It runs in milliseconds and often lands within a few points of much heavier models.

    Hold onto thisLearning from your own decisions has a trap: you only see the outcome of what you chose, so a bandit explores deliberately. A word-count model gives a baseline you can explain in one slide. For any model, know what it can observe and what it is compared with.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Bandits: explore, exploit, regret

    Picture a row of slot machines with unknown payouts. Each pull pays and teaches. A bank faces the same with K actions (offers, messages, collection scripts): you see the reward only for the action you chose. Never send offer B and you never learn how good B is.

    Regret is the price of not knowing: the reward from always playing the best action, minus what you got. A flat regret curve means you've settled on the best; a steadily rising line means you keep paying the same loss per customer. It says nothing about whether you picked the right winner, only what finding it cost. Three strategies cover most cases.

    • Explore-then-commit (the A/B test): split evenly for a fixed period, then give everyone the winner. Regret rises during the test, then flattens. If the winner is mistaken or the world changes, you stay wrong.
    • Epsilon-greedy: with probability ε pick randomly, otherwise the best so far. Simple and robust, but you waste ε of traffic for ever.
    • Thompson sampling (1933): keep a distribution over each action's true rate, draw one plausible rate per action, play the highest. Like asking each offer to make its best case, with unproven ones making wilder claims. Little-known actions have wide distributions and sometimes draw high, so they get tried; as evidence builds, the spread narrows. Exploration tracks uncertainty with no setting to tune. UCB, which plays the highest optimistic estimate, has similar guarantees.

    For conversion rates the belief is Beta(1 + s, 1 + f) after s conversions and f failures (Bayesian updating, F1.4). Theory (Lai and Robbins, 1985) says regret can grow only like the log of customers, not linearly.

    Contextual bandits choose the action given customer features (age band, balance, past response); the reward rate becomes a function of features, usually a regularised linear or tree model (Li et al., 2010). Delayed rewards are the norm: a loan offer's payoff arrives weeks later, so feed the bandit in batches, like label delay in F3.5.

    Reinforcement learning: when today's action changes tomorrow

    A bandit assumes your action doesn't change the customer's future. Often it does. In collections, a customer in early arrears who gets a gentle SMS may cure; with nothing, they may slip to 30 days late, where cure is much harder. Chess is the same: a move matters for the positions it leads to. RL is choosing actions in a loop: observe a state, act, get a reward, land in a new state.

    Pieces: states s, actions a, rewards r (here, negative cost), transition probabilities, and a discount γ between 0 and 1 for how much the future counts. γ near 0 means short-sighted; near 1 means patient. The goal is a policy (a rule from state to action) maximising total discounted reward. Its core object is the Bellman equation:

    Q*(s,a)=r(s,a)+γ∑s′P(s′|s,a)maxa′Q*(s′,a′)

    Value of action a in state s = immediate reward + discounted value of the best move afterwards, averaged over where you land. With known transitions, solve by repeated sweeps (value iteration). Otherwise Q-learning nudges estimates towards what happened: Q ← Q + α (r + γ max Q(s′, ·) − Q). Deep RL swaps the table for a neural network. A bandit is the one-state special case.

    The table is a toy, but its difficulties are real.

    • The reward must be what you want. Reward "AED recovered this month" and the policy over-contacts distressed customers. Put conduct and customer outcomes in the reward, or enforce them as hard constraints on actions.
    • You cannot just try things. A game agent plays millions of episodes; each bank action touches a real customer. So banks mostly use offline RL, learning from logged decisions.
    • Off-policy evaluation. Logs hold only the old policy's choices. To score a new policy, reweight logged outcomes by how much more or less likely the new policy was to take each logged action (inverse propensity scoring). Doubly robust estimators add a reward model to cut variance (Dudík et al., 2011). If the old policy never tried an action in some situation, no method can say what it would have done. This is the selection bias of F2.6 and the causal reasoning of F9.6 again.

    In language models, RL means rewarding outputs a human or checker prefers; see from RLHF to DPO and process reward models and verifiers.

    Signal processing: what survives being turned into numbers

    A signal is a quantity measured over time: a voice, a vibration sensor in an ATM, a corporate account's minute-by-minute balance. To store it you sample it, recording its value fs times a second. The question: how fast is fast enough?

    The Fourier transform rewrites a signal as a sum of sine waves, each with a strength. Analogy: a chord played on a piano, split back into its notes. A pure 3 Hz wave rises and falls three times a second; a vowel is a stack of frequencies tied to the voice's pitch. The FFT (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) turns samples into strengths per frequency, the spectrum. Patterns tangled in time are often clean in frequency: a 50 Hz mains hum, or a voice between about 300 and 3,400 Hz.

    The sampling theorem (Nyquist, Shannon): sampling at fs faithfully captures frequencies below fs/2, the Nyquist frequency. Anything above it folds back as a different, lower frequency: aliasing, like a wagon wheel that seems to turn backwards on film. Telephone audio is sampled at 8,000 per second, so it holds nothing above 4,000 Hz. Fine for understanding words, poor for the high-frequency detail some voice-clone detectors use.

    What a bank data scientist needs beyond the picture:

    • Spectrograms. Cut audio into short overlapping windows, take each spectrum, stack them: frequency over time, like sheet music for sound. Speech models use a perceptual (mel) scale, classically compressed into MFCCs (Davis and Mermelstein, 1980). Modern recognisers and voice-fraud detectors feed log-mel spectrograms to a neural network, which is why convolutions and attention apply to sound.
    • Filtering. Removing a band (line hum, hiss) is multiplication in the frequency domain. A moving average is a low-pass filter.
    • Seasonality. Weekly and monthly cycles show as peaks. A spectrum is a quick check before modelling time series.
    • Calls are compressed. Contact-centre audio is narrowed and compressed. A model trained on studio speech can fail on it, so train and test on the same channel.

    Mostly background knowledge; it becomes live work in contact-centre analytics, voice biometrics, deepfake-voice detection and ATM sensor monitoring.

    TF-IDF: counting words so that the telling ones count more

    A bag of words ignores order and counts each word: one row per document, one column per vocabulary word, mostly zeros. Raw counts are swamped by "my", "the" and "account". TF-IDF fixes this with two factors.

    • Term frequency (TF): how often the word appears in this document.
    • Inverse document frequency (IDF): how rare the word is across all documents, usually ln(documents ÷ documents containing the word). A word in every document gets weight near zero; a word in 2 of 1,000 gets a big one (Spärck Jones, 1972).

    Analogy: in a pub quiz, "the" tells you nothing about the topic, "murabaha" tells you a lot. Weight = TF × IDF, with each row scaled to length one so long documents don't dominate. A high weight means "frequent here and rare elsewhere": the word that characterises this document. BM25 keyword search is the same idea with better handling of length and repetition.

    Pre-processing matters: lower-casing, stripping punctuation, and for English stemming ("transfers", "transferred" become "transfer"). Arabic is harder: prefixes and suffixes attach to words ("بطاقتي" is "my card" in one token), spelling varies with alef forms and diacritics, and dialect differs from formal text. Real pipelines normalise letters and use a morphological analyser or sub-word units (see tokenisation). The demo below does none of this.

    Linear text classifiers: a weight per word

    Feed TF-IDF rows to a logistic regression (F2.5) or linear SVM and each word gets a coefficient. Think of a checklist where each word adds or removes points. The score is the sum of weight × TF-IDF value per word, plus an intercept. A positive coefficient pushes towards the class, a negative one away, so every prediction is explained by the words that mattered (F8.1, with no extra tooling).

    This is not a toy. For complaint routing, adverse-media triage or intent detection with enough labels, it lands close to far larger models, trains in seconds, and behaves stably. Its weakness is what a bag discards: order, negation, context. "Unauthorised" in "I did not see any unauthorised transactions" counts as in a fraud report. Unseen synonyms ("compromised", "hacked") carry no weight.

    Build this baseline first. It takes an afternoon. Like timing a walk before buying a car, it tells you what the upgrade buys. If a fine-tuned transformer or an LLM is 2 points better on a held-out set, you know what the extra cost buys. If it is no better, you saved a deployment (see C1).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A bandit with no logged propensities

      The system chose offers but did not record the probability of each choice. Nobody can later estimate how a new policy would do.

      Spot itFor each logged decision, do we store the probability the policy gave to the action it took?
    • Optimising the wrong reward

      Click-through rises, long-term value falls. Recoveries rise, and so do complaints.

      Spot itAsk what is not in the reward. Track a counter-metric (complaints, attrition, 90-day balance).
    • Non-stationary world, stationary learner

      A bandit that averages all history keeps favouring an offer that has stopped working, like the committed strategy in Simulation 1.

      Spot itCompare each arm's recent rate with its lifetime rate. Use discounting or a sliding window.
    • Judging a new RL policy by a simulator or old logs

      The simulator was built from the same biased data, and the policy exploits its errors.

      Spot itAsk for overlap: in how many logged cases did the old policy pick what the new one would? Ask what randomised test follows.
    • Aliasing and channel mismatch in audio

      A model trained on 16 kHz studio speech runs on 8 kHz compressed calls and quality collapses, or a high-frequency cue vanishes.

      Spot itCheck sampling rate and codec of training and live audio. Evaluate on production-channel audio.
    • Leaking information through the window

      A spectrum or smoothing computed over the whole series, future included, then used to predict it.

      Spot itCheck every filter and feature uses only data available at prediction time (F3.1).
    • TF-IDF fitted on all the data before the split

      IDF values are learned from test documents. Small in size, serious in principle.

      Spot itFit the vectoriser inside the training fold, then apply it to the test fold.
    • Trusting a bag of words with negation, sarcasm and new vocabulary

      "I did not report any fraud" flagged as fraud. New scam wording scores zero.

      Spot itRead errors, not scores. Add negation cases to the test set and retrain as vocabulary drifts.
    • Comparing an LLM to nothing

      "The LLM classifies complaints with 91% accuracy", with no baseline. A TF-IDF model may have scored 89%.

      Spot itWhat does a linear model on the same split score, and what does it cost per thousand messages?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Three offers convert at 4%, 5.5% and 7%. You show them evenly to 10,000 customers. How many conversions do you lose against always showing the best, and what is that worth at AED 300 each?

    The even mix converts at (4 + 5.5 + 7) ÷ 3 = 5.5%. The best converts at 7%. Gap: 1.5 points = 150 conversions = AED 45,000.

    2. Why does Thompson sampling explore less over time without anyone lowering a setting?

    Beliefs narrow as data arrives. A narrow belief on a weak arm rarely draws above the best arm's, so it is chosen less. A wide belief on an under-tested arm often draws high, so it gets tried.

    3. What is the difference between a bandit and full reinforcement learning?

    In a bandit your action does not change the next situation: one state, immediate reward. In RL actions change the state and rewards can be delayed, so you must value future states (Bellman equation). A bandit is RL with one state.

    4. Why can't you evaluate a new collections policy by averaging the outcomes in your logs?

    Logs hold outcomes only for actions the old policy chose, in the situations it chose them. Customers who got a call differ from those who didn't. Reweight by recorded propensities, and you need overlap between the policies. Even then, a randomised trial is the final check.

    5. A tone at 70 Hz is sampled at 100 samples per second. Where does it appear?

    Nyquist is 50 Hz. The tone is above it, so it folds: |70 − 100| = 30 Hz.

    6. A word appears in 2 of 1,000 complaints; another in 900 of them. Using IDF = ln(N ÷ df), which gets the larger weight and by roughly how much?

    ln(1000 ÷ 2) = ln 500 ≈ 6.2; ln(1000 ÷ 900) ≈ 0.11. The rare word's IDF is about 59 times larger.

    7. Why does a TF-IDF classifier call "I have not seen any unauthorised transactions" a fraud report, and what are two ways to improve it?

    Bag of words discards order and negation: "unauthorised" has a positive weight wherever it appears. Fixes: add word pairs (bigrams) such as "not seen", or use a model that reads context (fine-tuned transformer, prompted LLM). Test either on a set with negations.
