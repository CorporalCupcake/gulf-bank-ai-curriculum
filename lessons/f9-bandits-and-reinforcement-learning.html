    Bandits and Reinforcement Learning

    Part 1

    What a bandit is

    In one sentenceA bandit is a method for choosing between options when you only learn how good an option is by trying it, so you must balance trying new things against using what already works.

    Say a website has three versions of an advert: A, B and C. You don't know which gets the most clicks. Each visitor sees one advert, and you see only whether that advert was clicked.

    If you never show B, you never learn how good B is. So every choice is a trade: exploit (show the advert that looks best so far) or explore (show another, in case it is better). The name comes from a gambler facing a row of slot machines, each called a "one-armed bandit".

    How it differs from other methods

    • An A/B test splits visitors evenly, waits, then picks a winner. Clean evidence, but half the visitors keep seeing the weaker advert for the whole test.
    • A bandit shifts visitors towards the winner while it learns. Fewer wasted views, but a less clean estimate of how much worse the losers are.
    • A prediction model learns from outcomes that already happened. A bandit also decides what to try next, so it shapes the data it learns from.
    Part 2

    Three ways to explore

    Suppose the true click rates are A 4%, B 5.5% and C 7%. You don't know that yet. Regret is the price of not knowing: the clicks you would have got by always showing the best advert, minus the clicks you actually got.

    1. Explore, then commit (an A/B test). Show all three evenly for, say, 1,000 visitors, then show only the winner. Simple, but if the early result was luck, or the world changes, you stay wrong.
    2. Epsilon-greedy. A small share of the time (epsilon, say 10%) show a random advert. Otherwise show the best so far. That 10% is wasted for ever, even once you are sure.
    3. Thompson sampling. For each advert, keep a tally of clicks and non-clicks. For each visitor, draw a plausible click rate from each tally and show the advert with the highest draw. Uncertain adverts get tried, and as evidence builds the exploring fades by itself.

    Showing the three evenly for ever would lose about 75 clicks over 5,000 visitors. Thompson sampling typically loses far fewer, because it quickly stops showing the weak adverts.

    How to read it

    • Regret that flattens means the bandit has settled on the best option.
    • Regret that keeps rising means a steady loss per visitor: you are still showing weaker options too often.
    • Close options need lots of traffic. Telling 5.5% from 7% takes thousands of visitors per advert. With a few hundred, a bandit mostly wanders.
    • A world that changes (a new season, a new audience) breaks a bandit that averages all history. Down-weight old data, or use only a recent window.

    Contextual bandits go one step further: they pick the best advert for this visitor, using features such as age band or past behaviour. Always log the probability with which each option was shown, or the logs are much less useful later.

    Part 3

    Reinforcement learning: when today changes tomorrow

    In one sentenceReinforcement learning (RL) is a bandit where each action also changes the situation you face next, so you must plan over many steps.

    Showing an advert doesn't change which advert is best tomorrow. Many problems are not like that. Ignore a small damp patch on a wall and it spreads. Wipe it and it may clear. Repair it properly and it is gone, at a higher price today.

    The words you need:

    • State: what you know now (how far the damp has spread, what you did last time).
    • Action: what you can do (ignore, wipe, repair).
    • Reward: the score after each step (cost, or benefit).
    • Policy: the rule that maps each state to an action. This is what RL learns.
    • Discount (gamma): how much the future counts. Near 0 is short-sighted; near 1 is patient.

    Why the discount matters: a short-sighted planner ignores the damp, because waiting is free today. A patient one repairs it. A bandit is simply the one-state case of RL.

    How it learns: Q-learning keeps a score for each action in each state, and nudges it towards what actually happened plus the value of where it ended up. Deep RL replaces that table with a neural network. RL needs a great deal of trial and error, so it usually learns in a simulator or from logged data.

    A quick note on text baselines

    When you need a first model for text, try TF-IDF (term frequency, inverse document frequency) with logistic regression. TF-IDF scores each word by how often it appears in a message, down-weighted if it appears in every message. It trains in seconds and every prediction traces to its words. It misses negation ("this is not a prize offer") and paraphrase, so treat it as the baseline any fancier model must beat.

    Part 4

    Which one to use

    MethodUse it whenAvoid it when
    A/B testOne-off decision, two or three options, you need a clean effect size and audit trailMany options, or weak options are costly to show
    Thompson banditMany options, fast feedback, thousands of visitors a weekSlow outcomes or low traffic
    Contextual banditThe best option differs by customer and you log probabilitiesLittle data per customer group
    Full RLActions change later situations over a long horizonOne-step problems, no simulator, no usable logs

    Bandits are strong at:

    • Wasting fewer views than an A/B test when there are many options.
    • Adapting as results come in, with no fixed test length.

    They are weak at:

    • Slow or tiny feedback. If rewards take months, or you have a few hundred customers, options can't be told apart.
    • Clean effect sizes. Weak options get little traffic, so you learn little about how much worse they are.
    • Judging a new policy from old logs. The logs only hold outcomes for actions the old policy chose.

    In practice: start with Thompson sampling, a minimum share of traffic per option, and daily batch updates. Frame a problem as a bandit first, and move to full RL only if today's action demonstrably changes tomorrow's situation.

    In banking, bandits suit SMS and push-notification offers and message timing, inside suitability and conduct rules. Full RL is rare in production.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Optimising the wrong reward

      Clicks rise while long-term value or satisfaction falls. Track a counter-metric such as complaints.

    • Exploring without guardrails

      A bandit may try a loan offer on a customer it is unsuitable for. Filter the allowed actions with suitability rules before the learner picks.

    • A changing world, a learner that never forgets

      Averaging all history keeps favouring an offer that has stopped working. Compare recent rates with lifetime rates.

    • Using RL when a bandit would do

      Full RL adds cost and fragility. If the action doesn't change the future, use a bandit or a plain prediction model plus rules.

    1. Why does showing three adverts evenly cost you clicks?

    An even mix converts at the average rate, so every visitor shown a weaker advert is a lost chance. The loss per visitor never shrinks, so the total grows with volume.

    2. What is the difference between exploring and exploiting?

    Exploiting shows the option that looks best so far. Exploring shows another option to learn whether it is better.

    3. How does a bandit differ from reinforcement learning?

    In a bandit, your action doesn't change the next situation. In RL it does, so you must plan over several steps.

    4. Why can't you judge a new collections policy by averaging outcomes in your logs?

    The logs only show outcomes for actions the old policy chose, and customers who got a call differ from those who didn't. Reweight by recorded probabilities and confirm with a randomised trial.

    5. You have a few hundred customers a week and want to compare two offers cleanly. Bandit or A/B test?

    An A/B test. With low traffic a bandit can't separate close options, and you want a clean effect size.
