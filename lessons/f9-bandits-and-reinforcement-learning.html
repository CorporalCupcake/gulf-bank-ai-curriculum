    Learning by Trying, Listening to Signals, and the Word-Count Baseline

    How a bank learns which offer works while still selling, what sampling does to a phone call, and why a word-count text classifier is the baseline to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBandits and RL learn from their own choices, so they must explore; sampling rate decides what survives when sound becomes numbers; TF-IDF is the cheap, readable text baseline.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Bandits and RL. A bank has three SMS offers and doesn't know which converts best. A bandit shifts traffic towards the winner while it learns, like a diner who mostly orders the best dish but sometimes tries another. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the same when today's action changes tomorrow's situation, as when a harsh collections call recovers money now and loses the customer later.

    Signals and text. A phone call is sampled thousands of times a second, and that rate decides which sounds survive. TF-IDF weights words by how telling they are.

    Hold onto thisYou only see the outcome of what you chose, so a bandit must explore on purpose. For any model, know what it can observe and what it is compared with.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Bandits: explore, exploit, regret

    You see the reward only for the action you chose, so never send offer B and you never learn how good it is. Regret is the price of not knowing: the best action's reward minus what you got. A flat curve means you have settled; a rising line means a steady loss per customer.

    • Explore-then-commit (the A/B test): split evenly, then give everyone the winner. If it is wrong or the world changes, you stay wrong.
    • Epsilon-greedy: pick randomly a share ε of the time, otherwise the best so far. That share is wasted for ever.
    • Thompson sampling: draw a plausible rate for each action from your beliefs and play the highest. Uncertain actions get tried, and exploration fades as evidence builds.

    For conversion rates, Thompson sampling keeps a simple belief per offer: a tally of successes and failures, starting flat. Each customer nudges one belief. Two limits apply. Telling 5.5% from 7% takes thousands of customers per offer, so on low traffic a bandit just wanders. And they assume the world holds still, so down-weight old data, or use a sliding window, to notice a decayed offer.

    Contextual bandits choose by customer features such as age band, balance and past response. A model predicts each offer's reward for this customer, with randomness added for exploration. Log the probability with which each offer was shown, or the logs are far less useful later. Delayed rewards are the norm, since a loan offer pays off weeks later, so update in batches and decide in advance which signal counts as the reward.

    Reinforcement learning: when today's action changes tomorrow

    A bandit assumes your action doesn't change the customer's future. Often it does: in collections, a gentle SMS may cure early arrears, while doing nothing lets the customer slip further. RL is a loop of state, action, reward, new state.

    The goal is a policy (state to action) that maximises reward over time. A discount γ sets how much the future counts: near 0 is short-sighted, near 1 is patient. A bandit is the one-state special case.

    The moving parts. The state must hold what matters for the next decision (days late, balance, past contacts), on the assumption that the present state summarises the past. The value of a state is the reward you expect from there on, discounted. Q-learning nudges its estimate of each action's worth towards what happened, and deep RL swaps the table for a neural network. RL is sample-hungry, often needing millions of steps, so it thrives in simulators and struggles with real customers.

    • The reward must be what you want. Reward "AED recovered" and the policy over-contacts distressed customers. Put conduct in the reward.
    • You cannot just try things on real customers, so banks mostly use offline RL from logged decisions, usually with methods that stay close to what the old policy actually did.
    • Scoring a new policy from old logs means reweighting by action probabilities, and no method can judge actions the old policy never took. The reweighting is fair on average but noisy, because a few rare, heavily weighted cases can dominate. This is the selection bias of F9.6 again.

    In language models, see from RLHF to DPO.

    Signal processing: what survives being turned into numbers

    To store a signal such as a voice you sample it a fixed number of times a second. The Fourier transform rewrites it as a sum of sine waves, like a chord split into notes; this spectrum shows patterns that are tangled in time.

    Sampling captures frequencies only up to half the rate (the Nyquist frequency). Anything higher folds back as a lower, wrong frequency: aliasing, like a wagon wheel that seems to spin backwards. Telephone audio at 8,000 samples a second holds nothing above 4,000 Hz.

    Two practical knobs. A recorder puts a low-pass filter before the sampler to remove frequencies it cannot keep, because aliasing cannot be undone afterwards, and resampling 8 kHz audio up to 16 kHz adds no information. Long analysis windows separate close pitches but blur timing; short ones do the reverse. The same thinking helps with business series. Weekly and monthly cycles in card spending show up as clear peaks, and sine and cosine terms at those cycles give a forecasting model its seasonality.

    • Spectrograms stack short-window spectra into frequency over time; the mel version spaces frequencies the way human hearing does. Speech and voice-fraud models feed them to neural networks (convolutions, attention).
    • Calls are compressed. A model trained on studio speech can fail on contact-centre audio, so train and test on the same channel.

    TF-IDF: counting words so that the telling ones count more

    A bag of words ignores order and counts each word. Raw counts are swamped by "my" and "the", so TF-IDF multiplies how often a word appears in this document by how rare it is across all documents. "The" tells you nothing about a document; "murabaha" tells you a lot. BM25 is the same idea, refined.

    Arabic is harder: prefixes attach to words, so real pipelines use sub-word units (see tokenisation).

    The knobs that matter. N-grams add word pairs such as "not seen", which recovers a little local order. A minimum document frequency drops words seen in only a handful of documents, cutting typos and noise. Character n-grams, chunks of three to five letters, cope with typos and Arabic prefixes, at the cost of a bigger matrix. The matrix is stored sparsely, so a 50,000-word vocabulary is no problem. Fit the vocabulary and IDF on training data only.

    Linear text classifiers: a weight per word

    Feed TF-IDF rows to a logistic regression (F2.5) and each word gets a weight, like a checklist where words add or remove points, so every prediction is explained by its words. For complaint routing it lands close to far larger models. It discards order and negation, and unseen synonyms carry no weight.

    The main knob is regularisation, a penalty that keeps weights small so the model does not memorise rare words. Tune its strength by cross-validation. For rare classes such as fraud reports, weight the rare class up and pick the decision threshold from the precision and recall you need, not from 0.5. Read weights with care: when two words always appear together, credit is split between them arbitrarily.

    Build this baseline first. It takes an afternoon and tells you what an LLM upgrade really buys (see C1).

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Learning while earning (bandits). Traffic moves to better options as evidence grows, so you pay far less for learning than with a fixed even split.
    • Trading cost now for value later (RL). It values where an action leads, so it can spend AED 35 on a call today to avoid an AED 8,000 default, which a one-step model cannot see.
    • Making hidden structure visible (Fourier). Cycles, hums and pitch tangled in time become a few clear peaks.
    • Cheap, stable, readable text models (TF-IDF with a linear classifier). They train in seconds on a CPU, rarely surprise you, and trace every prediction to its words. They suit short, keyword-driven text with thousands of labels.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • Slow or tiny feedback. If rewards take months, or traffic is a few hundred customers, bandits cannot separate the options.
    • You need a clean effect size. A bandit starves weak arms of traffic, so it estimates how much worse they are poorly. For a committee sign-off, run an A/B test.
    • The action does not change the future. Full RL then adds cost and fragility. Use a bandit, or a prediction model plus rules.
    • You cannot try things or log propensities. Without a safe simulator or logs with overlap, you cannot learn or even score a policy.
    • Meaning matters more than words. A bag of words misses negation, paraphrase and new scam wording, and struggles with few labels.

    Alternatives and how to choose

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    A/B testOne-off decision, two or three options, clean effect size and audit trail neededMany options, or weak options are costly to show
    Thompson banditMany offers or creatives, fast feedback, thousands of customers a weekSlow outcomes, low traffic, or a clean effect estimate is the goal
    Contextual banditBest offer differs by customer and propensities are loggedLittle data per segment
    Offline or full RLActions change later states: collections, limit management, long horizonsOne-step problems, no overlap in logs, no simulator
    Spectral features + simple modelAudio or sensor data with little labelled data, or seasonal seriesLarge labelled audio sets, where a neural net wins
    TF-IDF + linearBaseline, routing, triage, explanation needed, low latencyNegation, paraphrase or multilingual meaning decides the label

    In practice

    • Bandits. Start with Thompson sampling, a minimum share per offer and daily batch updates. The knobs that matter are the reward definition, the update delay and how fast old data is forgotten. Expect thousands of customers per option.
    • RL. Frame the problem as a bandit first. Move to RL only if today's action demonstrably changes tomorrow's state.
    • Signals. Check the sampling rate and codec first, then try mel spectrograms before anything exotic.
    • Text. Try unigrams and bigrams, a minimum document frequency of 2 to 5 and logistic regression with a tuned penalty. A few hundred labels per class is a start. It trains in seconds on a laptop.

    In banking, bandits suit SMS and push offers and message timing, inside suitability and conduct rules. Full RL is rare in production. TF-IDF models do complaint routing, adverse-media triage and transaction categorising. Spectral methods sit behind voice biometrics and deepfake-voice detection.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging a new policy from logs or a simulator

      Action probabilities were not recorded, or the simulator shares the data's bias.

      Spot itWhat randomised test follows?
    • Optimising the wrong reward

      Click-through rises, long-term value falls.

      Spot itTrack a counter-metric such as complaints.
    • Non-stationary world, stationary learner

      A bandit averaging all history keeps favouring an offer that has stopped working.

      Spot itCompare recent with lifetime rates.
    • Aliasing and channel mismatch in audio

      A model trained on 16 kHz studio speech collapses on 8 kHz compressed calls.

      Spot itCheck the sampling rate of training and live audio.
    • Leaking the future or the test set

      A vectoriser, filter or smoother fitted on the whole dataset lets test information into training.

      Spot itCheck every transform is fitted on training data only.
    • Exploring without guardrails

      A bandit tries a loan offer on a customer for whom it is unsuitable, or a harsh message on one in distress.

      Spot itDo suitability rules filter the allowed actions before the learner picks?
    • Trusting a bag of words, or an LLM with no baseline

      "I did not report any fraud" is flagged as fraud. "The LLM gets 91%" means little if TF-IDF scored 89%.

      Spot itAdd negation cases to the test set.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Three offers convert at 4%, 5.5% and 7%. Why does showing them evenly cost you?

    An even mix converts at the average, so every customer who sees a weaker offer is a lost chance. The loss per customer never shrinks, so the total grows with volume.

    2. Why can't you evaluate a new collections policy by averaging outcomes in your logs?

    Logs hold outcomes only for actions the old policy chose, and customers who got a call differ from those who didn't. Reweight by recorded probabilities, and confirm with a randomised trial.

    3. A phone line is sampled at 8,000 times a second and a tone sits above 4,000 Hz. What happens?

    It folds back as a lower, wrong frequency (aliasing). Check the sampling rate of training and live audio.

    4. Why does a TF-IDF classifier call "I have not seen any unauthorised transactions" a fraud report?

    A bag of words discards order and negation, so "unauthorised" counts wherever it appears. Add word pairs or use a model that reads context, and test on negations.

    5. You must choose between two collections-message wordings, once, and a conduct committee will review the result. A/B test or bandit, and why?

    An A/B test. It is a one-off choice between two options, and the committee needs a clean effect size. A bandit gains little here. For ten SMS variants with thousands of customers a day, choose a bandit.

    6. You have 5,000 labelled complaints, 12 queues, a latency limit and an audit requirement. Do you start with TF-IDF and logistic regression or an LLM?

    TF-IDF and logistic regression first. It is fast, cheap and explainable, and 5,000 labels is plenty. Move to an LLM only if it beats the baseline on a held-out set by enough to justify its cost and weaker audit trail.

    7. What separates a bandit from full reinforcement learning, and when is the extra machinery worth it?

    In a bandit your action does not change the next situation. In RL it does. Use RL only when today's action demonstrably alters tomorrow's state, as with arrears stages.
