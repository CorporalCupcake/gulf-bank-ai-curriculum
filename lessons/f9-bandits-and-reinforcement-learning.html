    Learning by Trying, Listening to Signals, and the Word-Count Baseline

    How a bank can learn which offer works while still selling, why sequences of decisions change the answer, what a sampling rate does to a phone call, and why a TF-IDF text classifier is the model every language model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBandits and reinforcement learning learn from their own decisions, so they must balance trying new things against using what works and cannot be judged on logs alone; signal processing explains what survives when sound or sensors are turned into numbers; and TF-IDF with a linear classifier is a cheap, readable text baseline that tells you what a fancier model has really earned.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    These three topics look unrelated. They share one question: what can you learn, and what does it cost you to learn it?

    Bandits and reinforcement learning (F9.7). A bank has three SMS offers for a personal-finance top-up. It does not know which converts best. The usual approach is an A/B test: split customers evenly, wait, pick the winner. But every customer who got a weak offer during the test was a lost sale. A bandit shifts traffic towards the winner while it is still learning. The name comes from a row of slot machines: you must decide which arm to pull, and each pull both pays and teaches you something. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the same idea when today's action changes tomorrow's situation. A harsh collections call may recover money today and lose the customer next month.

    Signal processing (F9.8). A phone call is a pressure wave. To store it, a bank's recorder measures it thousands of times a second. Which sounds survive that measuring, and which turn into something else? A signal is a quantity changing over time, and its most useful description is often not "what value at each moment" but "how much of each frequency". This is background knowledge for most bank roles, but it explains why voice models behave as they do.

    Classic NLP (F9.9). Before neural language models, text was classified by counting words. A complaint containing "unauthorised" and "card" is probably about fraud. TF-IDF is a way of weighting those counts so that rare, telling words count more than "the" and "my". A linear model on top assigns each word a weight you can read. It runs in milliseconds, costs nothing, and often gets within a few points of much heavier models.

    Hold onto thisLearning from your own decisions creates a trap: you only see the outcome of what you chose. A bandit handles that deliberately by exploring. A word-count model removes the trap of complexity: it gives you a baseline you can explain in one slide. Between them sits the rule for any model: know what it can observe, and know what it is being compared with.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Bandits: explore, exploit, regret

    The setting is simple. There are K actions (offers, messages, collection scripts). Each time a customer arrives you choose one and observe a reward: converted or not. You see the reward only for the action you chose. That is the whole difficulty. If you never send offer B, you never learn how good B is.

    The yardstick is regret: the reward you would have collected by always playing the best action, minus what you collected. A strategy is good if regret grows slowly. Three strategies cover most of what you will meet.

    • Explore-then-commit (the classic A/B test): split evenly for a fixed period, then switch everyone to the winner. Regret grows steadily during the test, then flattens. But if the winner is mistaken, or the world changes, you stay wrong.
    • Epsilon-greedy: with probability ε pick a random action, otherwise the best so far. Simple and robust. The cost is that you keep wasting ε of traffic for ever.
    • Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933): keep a probability distribution over each action's true rate, draw one plausible rate per action from it, and play the action whose draw is highest. An action you know little about has a wide distribution and sometimes draws high, so it gets tried. As evidence builds, the distribution narrows and trials fade. Exploration is proportional to uncertainty, with no extra setting to tune. The usual alternative with similar guarantees is UCB, which plays the action with the highest optimistic estimate.

    For conversion rates, the distribution is a Beta. After s conversions and f non-conversions the belief is Beta(1 + s, 1 + f), the same Bayesian updating as in F1.4. The theory (Lai and Robbins, 1985) says regret can be made to grow only like log of the number of customers, instead of linearly. The simulation below shows the trade.

    Two refinements matter in practice. Contextual bandits choose the action given what you know about the customer (age band, balance, past response): the reward rate becomes a function of features, usually a regularised linear or tree model, and the exploration idea carries over. This is how personalised offers and notification timing are optimised (Li et al., 2010). And delayed rewards are the norm in a bank: a loan offer's payoff arrives weeks later, so the bandit must be fed in batches and the delay planned for, like label delay in F3.5.

    Reinforcement learning: when today's action changes tomorrow

    A bandit assumes your action does not change the customer's future. For many bank decisions that is false. In collections, a customer in early arrears who gets a gentle SMS may cure; one who gets nothing may slip to 30 days late, where cure is much harder. The action alters the state. RL is the framework for choosing actions in a loop: observe a state, act, receive a reward, land in a new state.

    Its pieces are a set of states s, actions a, rewards r (here, negative cost), transition probabilities, and a discount factor γ between 0 and 1 saying how much the future counts. The goal is a policy, a rule from state to action, that maximises total discounted reward. The central object is the value of a state, which obeys the Bellman equation:

    Q*(s,a)=r(s,a)+γ∑s′P(s′|s,a)maxa′Q*(s′,a′)

    The value of taking action a in state s is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the best you can do afterwards, averaged over where you land. If the transition probabilities are known you can solve this by repeated sweeps (value iteration). If they are not, Q-learning nudges each estimate towards what actually happened: Q ← Q + α (r + γ max Q(s′, ·) − Q). Deep RL replaces the table with a neural network. A bandit is the special case with one state.

    The table is a toy, but its structure is the real one, and so are its difficulties.

    • The reward must be what you want. Reward "recovered AED this month" and the policy will happily over-contact distressed customers. Conduct rules and customer outcomes need to be in the reward, or enforced as hard constraints on the action set.
    • You cannot just try things. In a game, RL plays millions of episodes. In a bank each exploratory action touches a real customer. So banks mostly use offline or batch RL, learning from logged decisions, and that brings the central difficulty below.
    • Off-policy evaluation. Logs only contain the old policy's choices. To estimate how a new policy would do, you reweight the logged outcomes by how much more (or less) likely the new policy was to take each logged action: inverse propensity scoring. Doubly robust estimators add a model of the reward to cut variance (Dudík et al., 2011). If the old policy never tried an action in some situation, no method can tell you what it would have done there. This is the selection-bias problem of F2.6 and the causal reasoning of F9.6 in a new costume.

    RL also means something different in current language models: the model is rewarded for outputs a human or a checker prefers. The mechanics are related; the lessons from RLHF to DPO and process reward models and verifiers pick that up.

    Signal processing: what survives being turned into numbers

    A signal is a quantity measured over time: a voice, a heartbeat, a vibration sensor in an ATM, the minute-by-minute balance of a corporate account. To put it in a computer you sample it, recording its value fs times a second. The question is how fast you need to sample.

    The central tool is the Fourier transform. Any signal can be written as a sum of sine waves of different frequencies, each with its own strength. A pure 3 Hz wave is a slow rise and fall three times a second; a speech vowel is a stack of frequencies related to the pitch of the voice. The discrete Fourier transform (computed quickly by the FFT, Cooley and Tukey, 1965) turns a list of samples into a list of strengths per frequency, the spectrum. Patterns that are tangled in time are often clean in frequency: a hum at 50 Hz, or a voice that sits between about 300 and 3,400 Hz.

    The sampling theorem (Nyquist, Shannon) gives the limit. Sampling at fs can faithfully capture frequencies below fs/2, the Nyquist frequency. A component above it does not disappear. It folds back and shows up as a different, lower frequency: aliasing. Wagon wheels that seem to turn backwards on film are the same effect. Telephone audio is sampled at 8,000 times a second, so it holds nothing above 4,000 Hz. That is fine for understanding words and poor for hearing the high-frequency detail some voice-clone detectors use.

    What a bank data scientist needs from this, beyond the picture:

    • Spectrograms. Cut audio into short overlapping windows, take the spectrum of each, and stack them: a picture of frequency over time. Speech models use a variant on a perceptual (mel) scale; classically, compressed into MFCC features (Davis and Mermelstein, 1980). Modern speech recognisers and voice-fraud detectors take log-mel spectrograms as input to a neural network, which is why convolutions and attention apply to sound.
    • Filtering. Removing a band of frequencies (line hum, high-frequency noise) is multiplication in the frequency domain. Smoothing a noisy series with a moving average is a low-pass filter.
    • Seasonality. A series with weekly and monthly cycles has peaks at those frequencies. A spectrum is a quick check for cycles before you model them, and links to time series.
    • Calls are compressed. The audio stored by a contact-centre system has been narrowed and compressed. A model trained on studio-quality speech can fail on it. Always train and test on audio from the same channel.

    In most bank roles this stays background knowledge. It becomes live work in contact-centre analytics, voice biometrics, deepfake-voice detection, and branch or ATM sensor monitoring.

    TF-IDF: counting words so that the telling ones count more

    The oldest useful way to turn text into numbers is the bag of words: ignore order, count each word. Each document becomes a long row, one column per word in the vocabulary, mostly zeros. Raw counts have a flaw: "my", "the" and "account" appear everywhere and swamp the signal. TF-IDF fixes it with two factors.

    • Term frequency (TF): how often the word appears in this document.
    • Inverse document frequency (IDF): how rare the word is across all documents, usually the logarithm of (number of documents ÷ number containing the word). A word in every document gets weight near zero; a word in two documents out of a thousand gets a big weight (Spärck Jones, 1972).

    The weight is TF × IDF, and each document row is then scaled to length one so that long documents do not dominate. The result: distinctive words stand out. The same weighting idea sits inside BM25 keyword search, which is TF-IDF with better handling of document length and repeated words.

    Pre-processing choices change results more than people expect. Lower-casing, removing punctuation, and for English stemming ("transfers", "transferred" to "transfer"). Arabic is harder: prefixes and suffixes attach to words ("بطاقتي" is "my card" in one token), spelling varies with the form of alef and with diacritics, and dialect differs from formal text. Real Arabic pipelines normalise letters and often use a morphological analyser or sub-word units (see tokenisation). The small demo below does none of that and says so.

    Linear text classifiers: a weight per word

    Feed TF-IDF rows to a logistic regression (F2.5) or a linear SVM and each word gets a coefficient. The score of a message is the sum of its words' contributions, each word's weight times its TF-IDF value, plus an intercept. Positive coefficients push towards the class, negative ones away. Because it is a sum, every prediction can be explained by listing the words that mattered. That is global and local explanation (F8.1) in its simplest form, with no extra tooling.

    This is not a toy. For many classification problems with enough labels (complaint routing, adverse-media triage, intent detection), a TF-IDF linear model lands close to far larger models, trains in seconds on a laptop, and has stable behaviour. Its weaknesses are in what a bag of words discards: word order, negation, context and meaning. "Unauthorised" in "I did not see any unauthorised transactions" counts exactly as in a fraud report. Synonyms the model never saw in training ("compromised", "hacked") carry no weight at all.

    A good habit: always build this baseline first. It takes an afternoon. If a fine-tuned transformer or an LLM prompt is 2 points better on a held-out set, you know exactly what the extra cost buys. If it is no better, you have saved yourself a deployment. This is the "baseline first" rule of C1 applied to text.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Three topics, many neighbours.

    F1.4 · Bayes and distributionsBeta beliefs

    Thompson sampling is Bayesian updating of a Beta belief followed by one random draw per action.

    F3.6 · F2.3 · A/B testingThe test a bandit replaces

    A bandit trades clean inference for lower regret. When you need to be sure which offer is better and by how much, run the experiment; when you only need to earn while learning, use a bandit.

    R8 · Modern A/B testingAdaptive experiments

    Adaptive allocation makes ordinary p-values and intervals misleading. Modern methods handle that.

    F9.6 · Causal inferenceEvaluating a policy from logs is causal inference

    Off-policy evaluation estimates what would have happened under a different decision rule, using propensities and overlap.

    F9.x · Recommenders and rankingOffers are a recommendation problem

    Next-best-offer systems score a catalogue; bandits decide how much to explore it. Both suffer from feedback loops, since you learn only about what you showed.

    F9.1 · Time seriesSpectra and seasonality

    Cycles in balances and transactions are frequencies. The tools for seeing them are the ones in this lesson.

    F5.3 · F5.5 · Convolutions and attentionModels that read spectrograms

    Speech systems turn audio into a spectrogram, then apply the same network families used for images and text.

    F2.5 · F8.1 · Regression and explanationA linear text model is a GLM

    Its coefficients are explanations for free, in contrast to explaining a black box after the fact.

    M3 · SearchBM25 is TF-IDF grown up

    Retrieval for RAG still leans on keyword weighting, mixed with embeddings.

    A3 · DeepfakesSound as evidence

    Voice-clone detection lives on spectral cues, and on whether the call channel kept them.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Offer and message optimisation. Which SMS wording, which time of day, which cashback tier. Bandits suit short-horizon, high-volume choices with fast feedback and low stakes per decision.
    • Collections strategy. A sequential problem: contact channel, timing, restructuring offers. Typically solved with simple rules and A/B tests, with RL a research-and-pilot direction, held back by conduct rules and off-policy evaluation.
    • Credit-limit and pricing decisions. Sequential with long delays and regulatory limits. Treat any "learning" policy as a model needing validation (G3), and any exploration as something Compliance must approve.
    • Contact-centre speech. Transcription, call routing, voice authentication and deepfake checks. Spectral features and channel effects matter.
    • Complaint routing, KYC and adverse-media triage. Short, labelled text, Arabic and English: the home territory of the TF-IDF baseline.
    • Islamic finance product text. Product names and terms (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq) are rare tokens with high IDF; keyword baselines handle them well, and general language models sometimes do not.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    All three areas are mature in their classic forms. What changes is their role: bandits and off-policy evaluation became standard tools for product teams; RL moved to the centre of language-model training; speech and text signals are mostly handled by large pretrained models, with the old methods as baselines and preprocessing.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • A bandit with no logged propensities

      The system chose offers but did not record the probability with which it chose them. Later nobody can estimate how a new policy would perform.

      Spot itAsk: for each logged decision, do we store the probability the policy gave to the action it took?
    • Optimising the wrong reward

      Click-through rises, long-term value falls. In collections, recoveries rise while complaints rise too.

      Spot itAsk what is not in the reward. Track a counter-metric (complaints, attrition, 90-day balance) beside it.
    • Non-stationary world, stationary learner

      A bandit that averages all history keeps preferring an offer that has stopped working, as the committed strategy in Simulation 1 did.

      Spot itCompare each arm's recent rate against its lifetime rate. Use discounting or a sliding window.
    • Judging a new RL policy by a simulator or by old logs

      The simulator was built from the same biased data. The policy exploits its errors.

      Spot itAsk for overlap: in how many logged cases did the old policy choose the action the new policy would? Ask what randomised test follows.
    • Aliasing and channel mismatch in audio

      A model trained on 16 kHz studio speech is run on 8 kHz compressed calls and quality collapses. Or a high-frequency cue disappears.

      Spot itCheck the sampling rate and codec of training and live audio. Evaluate on audio from the production channel.
    • Leaking information through the window

      Spectrum or smoothing computed over the whole series, including the future, then used to predict it.

      Spot itCheck that every filter and feature uses only data available at the prediction time (F3.1).
    • TF-IDF fitted on all the data before the split

      The IDF values are learned from the test documents. Minor in size, serious in principle, and it breaks point-in-time discipline when the vocabulary drifts.

      Spot itThe vectoriser must be fitted inside the training fold, then applied to the test fold.
    • Trusting a bag of words with negation, sarcasm and new vocabulary

      "I did not report any fraud" flagged as a fraud report. New scam wording scores zero.

      Spot itRead errors, not scores. Add negation cases to the test set, and schedule retraining as vocabulary drifts.
    • Comparing an LLM to nothing

      "The LLM classifies complaints with 91% accuracy" with no baseline. A TF-IDF model may have scored 89%.

      Spot itAsk: what does a linear model on the same split score, and what does it cost per thousand messages?
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a marketing head · 30 seconds
    “A classic A/B test sends equal traffic to every offer until it ends, so some customers always get the weaker one. A bandit moves traffic toward the winner while it learns. In our case that would have kept most of the margin an even split gives up. The price is that results are less clean for reporting, so I'd keep a small fixed holdout to measure the true uplift against doing nothing.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For text classification I'd start with TF-IDF and a regularised logistic regression, fitted inside the training fold, evaluated on a time-ordered split with the metrics at the operating point. It gives a floor, a readable explanation per message, and a cost per message for the LLM or fine-tuned model to beat. For sequential decisions I'd log propensities from day one, evaluate any new policy off-policy with a doubly robust estimator, check overlap, and then run a small randomised trial before it touches customers.”
    “Why not just run an A/B test?”

    For a decision that is one-off, expensive to get wrong or needs a clean, defensible estimate of the effect, run the test. A bandit is better when the choice is repeated many times, feedback comes quickly, the cost of a weak option is real, and the answer may drift. A bandit gives you earnings during the experiment, a test gives you the cleanest inference. I'd often do both: a bandit for allocation with a small randomised holdout for measurement.

    “Could reinforcement learning set our collections strategy?”

    In principle: it is a sequential decision where today's action changes tomorrow's state. In practice, three things hold it back. The reward must include conduct and customer outcomes, not only recovery. We can't explore freely on distressed customers. And evaluating a new policy from historical logs needs overlap and propensities we may not have recorded. I'd start by logging properly, building a simple policy with transparent rules, and running small randomised comparisons.

    “What is the Nyquist limit and why would I care?”

    It is half the sampling rate: the highest frequency a recording can hold faithfully. Anything higher gets folded into a false lower frequency. Phone audio at 8 kHz holds nothing above 4 kHz, so a voice model, or a deepfake detector, trained on wider-band audio may rely on cues the call channel has already removed. I'd check the channel before trusting a benchmark.

    “Why would we use TF-IDF when we have LLMs?”

    As the baseline. It is fast, almost free, explainable word by word, runs inside our perimeter, and often lands close to heavier models. The LLM has to show what it adds in accuracy or in handling of negation, new wording and Arabic dialect, relative to its cost and the extra validation burden. If the gain is small, we use the simple one. If it is large, we can say precisely what it is worth.

    “Does the Arabic text need special handling?”

    Yes. Arabic attaches prefixes and suffixes to words, spelling varies with letter forms and diacritics, and dialect differs from formal Arabic. A bag of words needs normalisation and stemming, or character n-grams, to be competitive, and I would test on real dialect samples from our customers. I'm cautious about quoting an accuracy before that.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Three offers convert at 4%, 5.5% and 7%. You show them evenly to 10,000 customers. How many conversions do you lose against always showing the best, and what is that worth at AED 300 each?

    The even mix converts at (4 + 5.5 + 7) ÷ 3 = 5.5%. The best converts at 7%, so the gap is 1.5 points: 150 conversions, worth AED 45,000.

    2. Why does Thompson sampling explore less over time without anyone lowering a setting?

    Each arm's belief narrows as data accumulates. A narrow belief on a weaker arm rarely produces a draw that beats the best arm's, so that arm is chosen less. A wide belief on an under-tested arm often draws high, so it gets tried.

    3. What is the difference between a bandit and full reinforcement learning?

    In a bandit your action does not change the next situation: one state, immediate reward. In RL actions change the state and rewards can be delayed, so you must value future states (the Bellman equation). A bandit is RL with a single state.

    4. Why can't you evaluate a new collections policy by averaging the outcomes in your logs?

    The logs hold outcomes only for actions the old policy chose, in the situations it chose them. Customers who got a call differ from those who didn't. To estimate the new policy you reweight by action probabilities (propensities), which must have been recorded, and you need overlap between the two policies. Even then a randomised trial is the final check.

    5. A tone at 70 Hz is sampled at 100 samples per second. Where does it appear?

    Nyquist is 50 Hz. The tone is above it, so it folds: |70 − 100| = 30 Hz.

    6. A word appears in 2 of 1,000 complaints; another in 900 of them. Using IDF = ln(N ÷ df), which gets the larger weight and by roughly how much?

    ln(1000 ÷ 2) = ln 500 ≈ 6.2; ln(1000 ÷ 900) ≈ 0.11. The rare word's IDF is about 59 times larger, so it dominates the document's vector unless it appears rarely in that document.

    7. Why does a TF-IDF classifier call "I have not seen any unauthorised transactions" a fraud report, and what are two ways to improve it?

    Bag of words discards order and negation: "unauthorised" has a positive weight wherever it appears. Improvements: add word pairs (bigrams) such as "not seen", or use a model that reads context, such as a fine-tuned transformer or a prompted LLM. Either must be tested on a set with negations.
