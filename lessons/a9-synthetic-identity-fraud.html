    Customers Who Never Existed, and Banks That Learn Together

    How a fabricated person is grown into a credit line and then abandoned, why the signal is in the links between applications rather than in any one of them, and how banks can pool what they know about crime without handing each other their customers.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA synthetic identity is invisible one application at a time and obvious as a cluster, and criminal networks span banks while each bank sees one slice, so the frontier is detecting clusters across institutions: by sharing models, not data, and by being honest about what that still leaks.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a customer file. The account opened fourteen months ago. A small credit card, used for groceries and a phone bill, paid in full every month. The bank raised the limit four times, automatically, because the behaviour was so tidy. Last month the card was drawn to its limit in three weeks, mostly in cash-equivalent purchases, then went silent. The collections team phones the number on file. No one answers. The address is a flat that houses six other "customers". There is no person to find.

    The loss is booked as credit default, because that is how it looks in the ledger. It was never credit risk. Nobody ever intended to repay. This is a synthetic identity: a customer assembled from real and invented parts (a genuine national ID number or phone with a made-up name and date of birth, or a wholly invented person with a forged document) and then raised like a plant, with patience, until the credit line was worth stealing.

    Two things make this hard, and they are the two halves of this lesson.

    • No victim complains. In ordinary identity theft a real person finds an unknown loan and shouts. A synthetic identity has no owner. The label (fraud) arrives late, if ever, and usually in the wrong category (credit loss), which poisons every model trained on it. See late labels and feedback loops.
    • No bank sees the whole ring. The same group of operators can open cards at four banks, using the same phones and the same drop address. Each bank sees one application that looks fine. The pattern exists only in the union of what they know.

    Read the economics, not the technique. The ring invests in patience because the bank rewards patience: good behaviour automatically produces bigger limits. The attack targets the bank's own model of "a good customer". It also shows the lever on the defence side. Early detection costs the criminals their whole investment, and slower, evidence-based limit growth shrinks the prize. Both are cheaper than chasing the debt.

    Hold onto thisA synthetic identity is a fact about relationships: shared phones, devices, addresses, timings and document templates. It is rarely a fact about one application. And the criminal network is wider than your bank. Everything in this lesson follows from those two sentences.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A9.3 · The anatomy of a synthetic identity

    The term covers a family of attacks. Keeping them apart matters, because each leaves different traces.

    TypeHow it is madeWho gets hurtWhat gives it away
    FabricatedEvery field invented, plus a forged or AI-generated document and face.Only the bank.Document and selfie forensics, device and network traces, no history anywhere else.
    Manipulated / hybridA real identifier (national ID number, phone, child's or dead person's data) glued to invented details.The bank, plus a real person whose record gets contaminated.Mismatches between sources: name against ID number, age against credit history.
    Authorised-user piggybackA new identity is added as a user on a real person's good card (sometimes paid for) to inherit a track record.The bank. The cardholder is often a willing seller.An odd tradeline with no spend of its own; many unrelated "users" on one account.
    Rented or mule identityA real person lends their genuine identity for a fee. Everything verifies.The bank; later the person, when it goes wrong.Behaviour after opening: who controls the device, where the money goes. Not the application.

    The common life cycle has four stages. Seed: assemble the identity and open the first account, often at a lender with light checks. Season: use small amounts, pay on time, ask for increases, add a second and third product so that a credit bureau starts to see a "history". Bust out: draw everything within days across all products and cash it out, often through mule accounts (A9.1–A9.2). Abandon: the phone dies, the address is empty, the debt is eventually charged off as credit loss.

    Generative AI changed the first stage most. Producing a plausible name, a consistent document, a face that passes a basic liveness check, and a believable email history used to be skilled work. Now it is cheap and can be repeated hundreds of times with small variations (A3.5). The cost of a seed fell. The cost of seasoning did not. That is where defenders still have time.

    The Gulf picture, and an honest caveat. In the UAE and Saudi Arabia, identity is anchored in state systems (Emirates ID and UAE Pass; national ID, Absher and Nafath), and credit is visible through bureaus (AECB in the UAE, SIMAH in Saudi Arabia). A wholly fabricated person is therefore harder to onboard than in markets where an identifier can be self-asserted. My judgement, not a published statistic: the Gulf variants to worry about most are rented and mule identities, hybrid cases that use a real document with changed contact details, fake employers and salary letters, and shell companies, rather than pure inventions. I have not seen public Gulf loss figures for this, so treat anything that quotes one with care.

    The fairness trapA genuine new arrival in the country has a thin file, a new phone, a shared flat and no local history. Those are the same surface features as a synthetic identity. A model tuned only to catch the ring will reject thousands of honest expatriate newcomers and the bank will lose real customers. Always report the false-positive group by segment (G2).

    A9.3 · Finding the ring: links, clusters and their traps

    Single-application checks are weak because each field was chosen to pass. The better signal is reuse. Criminals recycle: a phone number across ten identities, one device submitting twelve applications, one address receiving eight cards. Build a graph in which nodes are applications or customers and an edge means "shares a value of some attribute", then look at connected clusters. It is the entity-resolution idea from B3.3 turned from "are these two records the same person?" to "are these records plausibly independent people?"

    The weakness is that honest customers share things too. Families share an address and a phone plan. Workers in shared accommodation share a postal address. Everyone with a common email pattern shares something. A rule of "any shared value is a link" produces a giant, mostly innocent cluster. The three controls below are the real design decisions, and the simulation lets you feel each.

    • Which attributes count. A shared device is much rarer, and stronger evidence, than a shared city.
    • How many must agree (k). Requiring two shared attributes cuts accidental links, and also cuts the ring's weakest connections.
    • Ignoring values that are too common. If 20 unrelated people have the same generic email pattern, that match tells you almost nothing. This is the inverse-document-frequency idea from text search: the rarer the shared value, the more it means.

    What the exercise shows is a limit, not a trick. Exact-match graph rules cannot separate a family from a ring when both share an address and a phone. Better features can: when the applications arrived (a family applies over years, a ring over a weekend), the velocity of new accounts per device, how similar the documents and selfies are to each other (AI-generated sets share artefacts), and the behaviour after opening. Graph neural networks (F9.2) learn such combinations from labelled clusters rather than hand rules, but they inherit the labelling problem from Part 1: confirmed synthetic cases are few and arrive late (F3.5).

    Detection also sits at three different moments, with different costs: at application (cheapest, but least information), during seasoning (rich behavioural data, still no loss: the best target) and at bust-out (too late, but fast velocity rules can limit the damage). Simulation 1 priced the middle one.

    A9.4 · Why one bank is never enough

    A criminal network does not respect institution boundaries. Layering money through accounts at five banks, or opening synthetic cards at six, spreads the evidence so that each bank sees something that looks ordinary. Anti-money-laundering (AML) systems are especially exposed: a monitoring model at one bank sees the part of each flow that touches its own customers. If the chain is A → B → C → D and your bank is B, you see money arrive and leave and know nothing about its origin or destination.

    This is why regulators and industry groups have pushed for private-sector information sharing. It is also hard, for good reasons. Banks may not hand over customer data to competitors, data-protection law restricts it, tipping off a suspect is an offence in many regimes, and a leak would be a catastrophe. So the interesting engineering question is how to get the benefit of pooled intelligence while sharing as little as possible. There is a ladder of options, from least to most technical.

    ApproachWhat is sharedWhat it buysWhat still leaks or is hard
    Typology and alert sharingWritten descriptions of patterns, or named suspects, through a legal gateway.Simple. Already used in several jurisdictions.Slow, manual, legal gateway needed. Not a model.
    Hashed or encrypted matching (private set intersection)Only the overlap: "these 40 accounts appear at both banks".Finds shared entities without revealing the non-shared ones.Hashes of low-entropy values (phones, ID numbers) can be brute-forced unless done with proper cryptography and keys.
    Clean room / secure enclaveRaw data goes into a neutral hardware- or contract-controlled space; only agreed outputs leave.Flexible analysis on joined data.Trust in the operator and the enclave. See A11.3.
    Federated learningEach bank trains locally and sends model updates; a server averages them.One model learned from all banks; raw rows never move.Updates can leak; participants can poison; legal status of "model derived from other banks' data" is unsettled.
    Secure multi-party computation / homomorphic encryptionEncrypted values, computed on without decryption.Strong guarantees against the other parties.Slow and complex; limited to simpler models today.

    A9.4 · Federated learning: how the averaging works

    The idea is old and simple. Each bank k holds its own data. A server sends everyone the current model weights. Every bank improves the model for a few steps on its own data and sends back only the new weights (or the change). The server combines them, usually by a weighted average, and starts the next round. This is federated averaging (FedAvg; McMahan et al., 2017).

    wt+1=∑k=1Knknwkt+1

    Next-round weights wt+1 are the average of each bank's locally updated weights, each counted in proportion to the number of rows nk it holds (out of n in total). If every bank trained on identical data this would equal ordinary training. Where banks differ, the average is only an approximation to training on the pooled data, and the gap is what Simulation 3 measures.

    Three facts decide whether this works for AML and fraud.

    • Banks are not alike (non-IID data). One bank sees remittance-heavy retail customers, another trade finance, another private banking. Their positives follow different typologies. Local models then specialise, and a plain average can wobble. This is the main technical difficulty, and also the main reason to collaborate at all: the more different the banks, the more each can learn from the others.
    • The positives are few. Fifty confirmed cases at one bank is not enough to learn from; two hundred across four is better. Recall from F3.5 that your effective sample size is the number of positives, so pooling is worth most exactly where labels are scarcest.
    • Features must line up. Banks have different schemas, different definitions of "suspicious", different label quality. Agreeing a shared feature definition (a common feature spec for transactions and customers) is usually harder than the learning algorithm.

    A9.4 · What still leaks, and the defences

    "The data never leaves" is true and not the whole story. A model update is a function of the data, and functions can sometimes be inverted.

    • Gradient and update inversion. With small batches, researchers have reconstructed training examples from the shared gradients (Zhu et al., 2019). The risk falls with larger batches, many local steps, and aggregation, and rises with a curious server.
    • Membership inference. Given the final model, an attacker may tell whether a particular person's record was in training. For a bank, "this customer was in the suspect set of bank X" is itself sensitive, and revealing it risks tipping off.
    • Poisoning. A dishonest or compromised participant can send updates that plant a backdoor, for instance teaching the shared model to treat one pattern as clean. A criminal who controls a small institution in the consortium has an obvious motive. See G1.3.
    • Free-riding and unequal gain. A bank with more positives subsidises one with fewer. Consortia need an explicit settlement of who contributes and who benefits.

    The standard defences stack. Secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., 2017) lets the server see only the sum of the updates, never any single bank's. Differential privacy (clip each update, add calibrated noise) gives a formal limit on what any one record can change, at a cost in accuracy that Simulation 3 shows in miniature. With only a handful of participants and a few hundred positives, a strong guarantee costs a lot, which is a genuine open difficulty. Robust aggregation and anomaly checks on updates reduce poisoning. And governance does the rest: who is admitted, what is audited, who is liable when the shared model is wrong. See A11.3–A11.4 for the machinery and A12 for the Gulf regulatory expectations.

    One regulatory point to hold with humility. Whether a UAE or Saudi bank may share suspicious-activity intelligence with another bank, through a model or otherwise, depends on the specific legal gateway, the regulator's position and the data-protection regime. I have not found a published Gulf framework that I can describe confidently, so treat any claim that "it is allowed" or "it is banned" as something to confirm with Legal and Compliance before building anything.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    The two topics share a root: crime is a network, and the evidence about it is spread across places that cannot easily talk.

    B3.1 – B3.3 · CDD, eKYC, entity resolutionThe front door

    Synthetic identities are onboarding failures. Entity resolution gives the "who is really the same" machinery that cluster detection reuses.

    A3.5 · Deepfakes against eKYCCheaper seeds

    Generated faces and documents make the first stage easy; the defence moves to linkage and behaviour.

    A9.1 – A9.2 · Scams and mule detectionThe cash-out path

    Bust-out money leaves through mule accounts. Graph methods for mules and for synthetic clusters are close cousins.

    A9.5 · Behavioural biometrics and device intelligenceDevice and behaviour signals

    One device behind many "people" is among the strongest links. Typing and touch patterns catch the rented identity.

    F9.2 · Graph analytics and graph MLLearning on the graph

    From hand rules over shared attributes to graph neural networks that learn which patterns of sharing matter.

    B2.1 · B2.2 · Transaction monitoring and typologiesWhat federated AML would improve

    Per-bank monitoring sees one leg of layering. Collaborative models are meant to see the chain.

    B1.3 · Late labels and feedback loopsFraud booked as credit loss

    Bust-out losses carry the wrong label for months. Clean them before training or the model learns the wrong lesson.

    A11.3 · A11.4 · Privacy technology and federated learningThe machinery

    Clean rooms, differential privacy, homomorphic encryption, and federated learning across group entities, in depth.

    G1.3 · Data poisoningA hostile participant

    In a consortium, the training data is partly controlled by parties you cannot audit. Poisoning is a first-order risk.

    A8.2 · Synthetic tabular dataAnother route to sharing

    Sharing generated data instead of real data, with its own privacy risks. Note the name collision: this "synthetic" is a tool, the one in this lesson's title is a crime.

    A12.1 · A12.2 · Regulators and data protectionWhat you may share

    The legal gateway for sharing intelligence is the gating item for every collaborative approach in the table above.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Retail credit and cards. Application fraud and bust-outs, booked as credit loss. The loss is in provisioning too (IFRS 9 models trained on contaminated defaults).
    • Digital onboarding. Neobank and app-based account opening, where there is no branch conversation and the device is the only witness.
    • AML and sanctions. Mule and shell structures need cross-bank views; this is where collaborative learning has the clearest case.
    • Group and consortium settings. Federated learning may be easier first inside one banking group, across subsidiaries in different jurisdictions, than across competitors (A11.4).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Synthetic identity fraud is now widely described as the fastest-growing identity threat, driven by generative AI. Collaborative AML has moved from papers to pilots, but public evidence of live results is thin, and none that I could find is in the Gulf.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Training on mislabelled losses

      Bust-out losses sit in the data as ordinary credit defaults, so the model learns that "good early behaviour then default" is credit risk, not fraud.

      Spot itPull defaults whose first 12 months were spotless and whose contact details are dead. Check how many were later confirmed as fraud, and relabel.
    • Mistaking newcomers for synthetic identities

      Thin file, new phone, shared flat: all true of a legitimate new resident. The model rejects them in volume.

      Spot itBreak results down by length of residence, nationality group and employer type. Look for a segment with a sharply higher flag rate and a low confirmation rate.
    • Giant clusters from common values

      A very common phone prefix, a generic email pattern, an employer's address or a branch address links thousands of people.

      Spot itLook at the largest cluster. If it has hundreds of members, a common value is doing the linking. Cap value frequency, as in Simulation 2.
    • Cutting links the ring will route around

      Once phone reuse is policed, rings buy more SIMs. The model's first success teaches the attacker.

      Spot itTrack which link types catch cases month by month. A fall in one while total fraud is flat suggests substitution.
    • "Federated" taken to mean "private"

      The data stays home, so the project declares privacy solved, and skips the analysis of what updates reveal.

      Spot itAsk three questions: what exactly leaves the building, who can see it, and what could they infer from it. If there is no answer, there is no privacy argument.
    • Evaluating on one participant's view

      A federated model is judged on the bank that built it. It performs well there and poorly at partners with different typologies.

      Spot itAsk for results broken out per bank, and for a held-out bank that did not contribute.
    • Averaging hides one bank's poison or noise

      One participant with poor data, or a hostile one, drags the shared model.

      Spot itLook at each participant's update norm and its effect on a clean validation set. An update that moves the model a lot, on a bank with few cases, deserves scrutiny.
    • Quoting synthetic-data gains as production gains

      "Detection doubled" in a sandbox on generated data, repeated in a board paper as if it were live.

      Spot itAsk what the data was, who generated it, and how realistic the planted patterns are. Any model trained to find the generator's own patterns will find them.
    • Tipping off through the system

      A shared alert or a model score that reveals a customer is under suspicion.

      Spot itTrace who sees any output about a named person, and whether anything reaches the customer or a front-line employee without Compliance approval.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Synthetic identity losses look like credit losses, so we undercount them. The ring invests months to earn bigger limits, then disappears. The defence is spotting the links between applications early: shared devices, phones and addresses, and applications that arrive in bursts. We must do that without rejecting honest newcomers, so I'd track false positives by customer segment. For the wider network, banks can learn together without sharing customer records, but I'd treat that as a two-year programme and start with legal and compliance sign-off, not with the algorithm.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For synthetic identity I'd build an identity graph with frequency-capped attribute links, require more than one agreeing attribute, add timing and velocity features, and train a graph model on confirmed clusters with bust-out losses relabelled as fraud. Evaluate forward in time with precision at review capacity and the flag rate by segment. For collaborative AML I'd consider federated averaging with secure aggregation and clipped, noised updates, evaluated on a held-out participant, with an explicit threat model for inversion, membership inference and poisoning.”
    “What actually makes an identity synthetic rather than just stolen?”

    There is no victim. A stolen identity belongs to a real person who eventually notices. A synthetic identity is partly or wholly invented, so nobody complains, the loss is booked as credit default, and the fraud is noticed late, if at all. That changes both detection (look for relationships, not complaints) and the labels used for training.

    “Can't we just stop deepfake documents at onboarding?”

    Document and liveness checks matter and must keep improving, but they are one layer in an arms race and are getting cheaper to beat. The ring's costly asset is the season, not the seed. So I'd add linkage across applications, early behaviour monitoring and slower limit growth, which work even when the document looks perfect.

    “Would this hit legitimate customers?”

    Yes, if designed carelessly. New residents have thin files and shared accommodation, which look like the criminals' pattern. I'd require two agreeing attributes, ignore very common values, route borderline cases to a light verification step rather than a decline, and report the flag rate for each segment to check it is not concentrated on newcomers.

    “What is federated learning, in one minute, and why use it for AML?”

    Each bank trains the same model on its own data and sends back only the model's updates. A server averages them and sends back an improved model. Raw customer records never move. It matters for AML because laundering crosses banks while each bank sees only a slice, and confirmed cases are few at any one bank. Pooling the learning raises the effective sample size, especially when banks see different typologies.

    “Is federated learning private?”

    It is more private than centralising rows, but not private by itself. Updates can leak training examples, and a final model can reveal whether a record was used. The usual safeguards are secure aggregation so that no individual update is visible, clipping and noise for differential privacy, and access controls. With few participants and few positives, strong guarantees cost accuracy, so we need to decide the acceptable trade-off with Risk and Legal.

    “Is any of this allowed in the UAE or Saudi Arabia?”

    I would not assert it either way. It depends on the legal gateway for sharing intelligence about suspected crime, the regulator's stance and the data-protection regime. I'd take the question to Compliance, Legal and the regulator early, and start with the lower rungs of the ladder (internal group sharing, hashed overlap checks) while that is settled.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A card opens at AED 5,000 and its limit grows 10% a month. After 10 months the ring draws 95%. What is the take per identity, and what would 5% growth give?

    At 10%: 5,000 × 1.110 = 5,000 × 2.594 = AED 12,969; 95% is about AED 12,321. At 5%: 5,000 × 1.0510 = 5,000 × 1.629 = AED 8,144; 95% is about AED 7,737. Slower growth cuts the prize by about 37%.

    2. Why is a synthetic identity typically labelled as a credit loss, and why is that harmful?

    Because the account stopped paying, and no victim reports fraud. The loss enters the credit-default data. Models trained on it learn that the pattern "perfect early behaviour, then default" is credit risk, and fraud models never receive those positives. Provisioning models also see inflated defaults.

    3. Twenty unrelated customers share the email pattern "firstname.lastname@commonprovider". Why shouldn't that create a link?

    A shared value is evidence only to the extent that it is rare. If 20 people share it by chance, the match carries almost no information, and treating it as a link joins unrelated people into one cluster. Cap the frequency of values, or weight links by inverse frequency.

    4. Why can't an exact-match graph tell a family from a fraud ring, and what could?

    Both share addresses, phones and sometimes devices. What differs is timing (a family applies over years, a ring in a burst), document and photo similarity, velocity per device, and behaviour after opening. These need extra features or a learned model.

    5. Four banks each have 50 confirmed cases. Banks' typologies are very different. Why might pooling help a lot, and when would it not?

    Each bank sees only a quarter of the patterns and has few examples. Pooled, the model sees all of them with 200 positives. If the banks' cases were drawn from the same mix, each bank alone would already see most patterns, and the gain would shrink to the extra sample size only (Simulation 3 with difference at 0).

    6. Write FedAvg in words. What does it do when banks have very different amounts of data?

    The new global weights are the average of each bank's locally updated weights, weighted by that bank's number of rows. A big bank counts for more. That is statistically sensible but gives large participants more influence, including over any poisoning, and may disadvantage a small bank whose pattern is rare.

    7. A vendor says "our solution is privacy-safe because data never leaves your bank". List two things you would still ask.

    What do the model updates reveal, and to whom (inversion and membership inference)? Is there secure aggregation or differential privacy, with a stated budget? Also acceptable: how are malicious participants detected, who is liable for a wrong shared score, and which legal gateway covers the sharing.

    8. Swift reports detection roughly doubled in its federated experiment. What do you need to know before using that number?

    That it was on a synthetic data set and reported by the organiser. The patterns in generated data may be easier than real ones, the bank mix was chosen, and no false-positive rate or live results were published. It shows the idea can work, not what the gain would be at our bank.
