    Customers Who Never Existed, and Banks That Learn Together

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A card opened fourteen months ago. Small grocery and phone-bill spend, paid in full every month. The bank raised the limit four times, automatically. Then the card was drawn to its limit in three weeks and went silent. Collections phone the number on file: no answer. The address is a flat housing six other "customers". There is no person to find.

    This is a synthetic identity: a customer built from real and invented parts (a genuine ID number with a made-up name, or a wholly invented person with a forged document), then raised like a plant until the credit line was worth stealing. Two things make it hard:

    • No victim complains. The loss is booked as credit default, so the fraud label arrives late, if ever, and poisons every model trained on it. See late labels and feedback loops.
    • No bank sees the whole ring. One group can open cards at four banks with the same phones and drop address. Each bank sees one fine-looking application, like a jigsaw split across four tables.

    Read the economics, not the technique. The bank rewards patience with bigger limits, so the ring invests in patience. Early detection costs the criminals their whole investment, and slower, evidence-based limit growth shrinks the prize.

    Hold onto thisA synthetic identity is a fact about relationships (shared phones, devices, addresses, timings), rarely about one application. And the criminal network is wider than your bank.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A9.3 · The anatomy of a synthetic identity

    The term covers four attacks, each with different traces.

    TypeHow it is madeWho gets hurtWhat gives it away
    FabricatedEvery field invented, plus a forged or AI-generated document and face.Only the bank.Document forensics, device and network traces, no history elsewhere.
    Manipulated / hybridA real identifier (ID number, phone, a child's or dead person's data) glued to invented details.The bank, and a real person whose record is contaminated.Mismatches between sources: name against ID number, age against credit history.
    Authorised-user piggybackA new identity is added to a real person's good card to inherit its track record.The bank.A tradeline with no spend of its own; many unrelated "users" on one account.
    Rented or mule identityA real person lends their genuine identity for a fee. Everything verifies.The bank, and later the person.Behaviour after opening: who controls the device, where the money goes.

    The life cycle has four stages. Seed: assemble the identity. Season: small spend, on-time payments, limit requests. Bust out: draw everything within days and cash out through mule accounts (A9.1–A9.2). Abandon: the debt is charged off.

    Generative AI made the seed cheap (A3.5). It did not make seasoning cheap. That is where defenders still have time.

    The Gulf picture. Identity is anchored in state systems and credit in bureaus (AECB, SIMAH), so pure fabrication is harder. My judgement, not a published statistic: worry most about rented and mule identities, fake employers and shell companies.

    The fairness trapA genuine new arrival has a thin file, a new phone, a shared flat and no local history: the same surface features as a synthetic identity. A model tuned only to catch rings will reject honest newcomers. Report false positives by segment (G2).

    A9.3 · Finding the ring: links, clusters and their traps

    Every field of a synthetic application was chosen to pass, so single checks are weak. The signal is reuse: one phone across ten identities, one device submitting twelve applications. Build a graph where nodes are applications and an edge means "shares an attribute value", then look at connected clusters. It is the entity-resolution idea from B3.3 flipped from "same person?" to "plausibly independent people?"

    The trap: honest customers share things too (families share addresses and phone plans; workers share accommodation). "Any shared value is a link" yields one giant, mostly innocent cluster. Three controls are the real design decisions:

    • Which attributes count. A shared device is stronger evidence than a shared city.
    • How many must agree. Requiring two cuts accidental links, and also the ring's weakest connections.
    • Ignoring values that are too common. The rarer the shared value, the more it means.

    Exact-match rules cannot separate a family from a ring when both share an address and a phone. Better features can: when applications arrived (a family over years, a ring over a weekend), velocity of new accounts per device, and document or selfie similarity. Graph neural networks learn such combinations but inherit the labelling problem: confirmed cases are few and late (F3.5).

    Detection can happen at application (cheapest, least information), during seasoning (rich behaviour, still no loss: the best target) or at bust-out (too late).

    A9.4 · Why one bank is never enough

    Laundering through five banks, or opening synthetic cards at six, spreads the evidence so each bank sees something ordinary. Sharing is hard for good reasons: competitors, data-protection law, tipping-off offences and leak risk. So the aim is pooled intelligence while sharing as little as possible. The options, from least to most technical:

    ApproachWhat is sharedWhat it buysWhat still leaks or is hard
    Typology and alert sharingWritten patterns or named suspects, through a legal gateway.Simple; already used in places.Slow and manual. Not a model.
    Hashed or encrypted matchingOnly the overlap: "these 40 accounts appear at both banks".Finds shared entities without revealing the rest.Hashes of phones and IDs can be brute-forced without proper cryptography.
    Clean room / secure enclaveRaw data enters a neutral space; only agreed outputs leave.Flexible analysis on joined data.Trust in the operator. See A11.3.
    Federated learningEach bank trains locally; a server averages the updates.One model from all banks; raw rows never move.Updates can leak; participants can poison.
    Secure multi-party computation / homomorphic encryptionEncrypted values, computed on without decryption.Strong guarantees against other parties.Slow; simple models only today.

    A9.4 · Federated learning: how the averaging works

    Think of four chefs improving one recipe: each tweaks it using their own kitchen and sends back only the tweaks. The server averages them (bigger banks count for more) and starts the next round. This is federated averaging. Three facts decide whether it works:

    • Banks are not alike. Local models specialise and a plain average can wobble, but the more different the banks, the more each learns from the others.
    • Positives are few. Fifty confirmed cases at one bank is too few; two hundred across four is better. Pooling helps most where labels are scarcest (F3.5).
    • Features must line up. Agreeing a common feature spec and definition of "suspicious" is usually harder than the algorithm.

    A9.4 · What still leaks, and the defences

    "The data never leaves" is not the whole story: an update is a function of the data, and functions can sometimes be inverted.

    • Gradient inversion. Researchers have reconstructed training examples from shared updates (Zhu et al., 2019). Risk rises with a curious server.
    • Membership inference. An attacker may tell whether a person was in training. "This customer was in bank X's suspect set" is itself sensitive and risks tipping off.
    • Poisoning. A compromised participant can teach the model that one pattern is clean (G1.3).

    Defences stack. Secure aggregation: the server sees only the sum of updates. Differential privacy: clip updates and add noise, at an accuracy cost (Simulation 3 shows it). Robust aggregation reduces poisoning. Governance covers admission, audit and liability. See A11.3–A11.4 and A12.

    Whether a UAE or Saudi bank may share suspicious-activity intelligence depends on the legal gateway and regulator position. I know of no published Gulf framework, so confirm with Legal and Compliance first.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training on mislabelled losses

      Bust-outs sit in the data as credit defaults, so the model learns "good early behaviour then default" means credit risk.

      Spot itPull defaults with spotless first 12 months and dead contact details. Check how many were later confirmed as fraud, and relabel.
    • Mistaking newcomers for synthetic identities

      Thin file, new phone, shared flat: all true of a legitimate new resident.

      Spot itBreak results down by length of residence and employer type. Look for a segment with a high flag rate and low confirmation rate.
    • Giant clusters from common values

      A common phone prefix, email pattern or branch address links thousands of people.

      Spot itCheck the largest cluster. Hundreds of members means a common value is linking. Cap value frequency.
    • "Federated" taken to mean "private"

      Data stays home, so privacy is declared solved without analysing what updates reveal.

      Spot itAsk what leaves the building, who sees it, and what they could infer.
    • Evaluating on one participant's view

      The model looks good at the bank that built it and poor at partners with different typologies.

      Spot itAsk for per-bank results and a held-out bank that did not contribute.
    • Quoting synthetic-data gains as production gains

      "Detection doubled" in a sandbox, repeated in a board paper as if live.

      Spot itAsk what the data was and who generated it. A model will find the generator's own patterns.
    • Tipping off through the system

      A shared alert or score reveals a customer is under suspicion.

      Spot itTrace who sees any output about a named person, and whether it reaches the front line without Compliance approval.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why does slower automatic limit growth help the bank even if the ring is never caught?

    The ring's take is a share of the limit at bust-out, and limits compound with each increase. Slower growth means a much smaller prize for the same upkeep cost. In Simulation 1, moving from 12% to 4% growth cuts the take sharply and can make the ring unprofitable.

    2. Why is a synthetic identity typically labelled as a credit loss, and why is that harmful?

    The account stopped paying and no victim reports fraud. Credit models learn "perfect early behaviour, then default" is credit risk, and fraud models never receive the positives.

    3. Twenty unrelated customers share one common email pattern. Why shouldn't that create a link?

    A shared value is evidence only to the extent it is rare. Twenty people sharing it by chance joins strangers into one cluster. Cap value frequency.

    4. Why can't an exact-match graph tell a family from a fraud ring, and what could?

    Both share addresses and phones. What differs is timing (years versus a burst), document similarity, velocity per device and behaviour after opening.

    5. Four banks each have 50 confirmed cases and very different typologies. Why might pooling help a lot, and when would it not?

    Each bank sees a quarter of the patterns with few examples; pooled, the model sees all with 200 positives. If the banks' cases came from the same mix, each alone would see most patterns and the gain would only be extra sample size (Simulation 3, difference at 0).

    6. In plain words, how does federated averaging combine the banks' models, and what is the catch when banks differ greatly in size?

    The server averages each bank's updated model, with bigger banks counting for more. That is sensible statistically, but it gives large participants more influence (including over poisoning) and may disadvantage a small bank with a rare pattern.

    7. A vendor says "our solution is privacy-safe because data never leaves your bank". What two things would you still ask?

    What do the updates reveal, and to whom? Is there secure aggregation or differential privacy with a stated budget? Also acceptable: how poisoning is detected, and who is liable for a wrong shared score.

    8. Swift reports detection roughly doubled in its federated experiment. What do you need to know before using that number?

    It was on a synthetic data set. Generated patterns may be easier than real ones, and no false-positive rate or live results were published. It shows the idea can work, not the gain at our bank.
