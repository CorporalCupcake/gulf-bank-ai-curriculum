    Synthetic Identities and Shared Fraud Intelligence

    Part 1

    What a synthetic identity is

    In one sentenceA synthetic identity is a customer built from real and invented parts, then raised patiently until its credit line is worth stealing. There is no real person behind it to complain.

    Picture a card opened fourteen months ago. Small grocery and phone spend, paid in full every month. The bank raised the limit four times, automatically. Then the card was drawn to its limit in three weeks and went silent. Collections phone the number on file: no answer. The address is a flat housing six other "customers".

    How it differs from other fraud

    • No victim complains. The loss is booked as a credit default, so the fraud label arrives late, if ever, and misleads every model trained on it.
    • No bank sees the whole ring. One group can open cards at four banks with the same phones and drop address. Each bank sees one fine-looking application, like a jigsaw split across four tables.
    • Patience is the weapon. The bank rewards good behaviour with bigger limits, so the ring invests in patience. Catching it early costs the criminals their whole investment.
    Part 2

    How to find the ring

    Every field of a synthetic application was chosen to pass, so single checks are weak. The signal is reuse: one phone across ten identities, one device submitting twelve applications.

    1. Draw a graph. Each application is a dot. Draw a line between two dots that share an attribute (phone, device, address, employer).
    2. Pick which attributes count. A shared device is stronger evidence than a shared city.
    3. Ignore very common values. The rarer the shared value, the more it means. A common phone prefix would link strangers.
    4. Look at the clusters of connected dots, and flag the ones that are large or arrived in a burst.
    5. Check timing and behaviour. A family opens accounts over years; a ring over a weekend. Also look at how many new accounts each device opens and whether documents or selfies look alike.

    Why one bank is never enough

    Laundering through five banks, or opening synthetic cards at six, spreads the evidence so each bank sees something ordinary. Sharing is hard: competitors, data-protection law, tipping-off offences and leak risk. So the aim is pooled intelligence while sharing as little as possible.

    Federated learning is like four chefs improving one recipe. Each tweaks it in their own kitchen and sends back only the tweaks. A server averages them (bigger banks count for more) and starts the next round. Raw customer rows never move.

    But "the data never leaves" is not the whole story. An update is a function of the data, and researchers have reconstructed training examples from shared updates (Zhu et al., 2019). Defences include secure aggregation (the server sees only the sum), differential privacy (clip updates and add noise, at an accuracy cost) and robust averaging against poisoning.

    Part 3

    How to read the signs

    Defaults with spotless first 12 months and dead contact details
    Classic bust-out. Pull these from your "credit default" data and check how many were later confirmed as fraud.
    One cluster with hundreds of members
    A common value (phone prefix, email pattern, branch address) is doing the linking. Cap how common a shared value may be.
    High flag rate, low confirmation rate in one segment
    Probably honest newcomers. A genuine new arrival has a thin file, a new phone and a shared flat: the same surface features as a synthetic identity.
    Slower limit growth
    The ring's take is a share of the limit at bust-out, and limits compound with each increase. Growing limits more slowly shrinks the prize, even if the ring is never caught.

    Four kinds of attack hide under the one name:

    • Fabricated: every field invented, with a forged or AI-generated document.
    • Hybrid: a real identifier glued to invented details. A real person's record is contaminated too.
    • Authorised-user piggyback: a fake user is added to a real person's good card to inherit its history.
    • Rented identity: a real person lends their genuine identity for a fee. Everything verifies.

    Which approach to use

    ApproachWhat is sharedStrong atWeak at
    Single-bank link graphNothingRings inside your own bookRings spread over several banks
    Alert sharingWritten patterns or named suspects, through a legal gatewaySimple, already used in placesSlow, manual, not a model
    Hashed matchingOnly the overlap between banksFinds shared entities cheaplyHashes of phones and IDs can be brute-forced

    • Strong at (pooling): few confirmed cases per bank. Fifty at one bank is too few; two hundred across four is better.
    • Weak at (pooling): banks that look alike gain little, and agreeing a shared feature list and a definition of "suspicious" is often harder than the algorithm.
    • Best moment to detect: during seasoning. Application checks have the least information, and by bust-out it is too late.

    In banking, whether a UAE or Saudi bank may share suspicious-activity intelligence depends on the legal gateway and regulator position. Confirm with Legal and Compliance first, and make sure no shared alert reveals to the front line that a named customer is under suspicion.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Training on mislabelled losses

      Bust-outs sit in the data as credit defaults, so the model learns that "good early behaviour then default" is credit risk. Relabel confirmed fraud.

    • Mistaking newcomers for synthetic identities

      A model tuned only to catch rings rejects honest new residents. Report false positives by length of residence and employer type.

    • Treating "federated" as "private"

      Ask what leaves the building, who sees it, and what they could infer from it.

    • Judging on one bank's view

      A model can look good at the bank that built it and poor at partners with different patterns. Ask for per-bank results and a held-out bank.

    1. Why is a synthetic identity usually labelled a credit loss, and why does that hurt?

    The account stopped paying and no victim reported fraud. Credit models then learn "perfect start, then default" as normal credit risk, and fraud models never receive the positives.

    2. Twenty unrelated customers share one common email pattern. Should that create a link?

    No. A shared value is evidence only in proportion to how rare it is. Cap value frequency so strangers are not joined into one cluster.

    3. How can you tell a family from a ring when both share an address and phone?

    Timing (years versus a burst), accounts per device, document or selfie similarity, and behaviour after opening.

    4. When does pooling across banks help most?

    When each bank has few confirmed cases and their patterns differ. If all banks see the same mix, the gain is only extra sample size.

    5. In plain words, how does federated averaging work?

    Each bank improves the model on its own data and sends back the update. The server averages the updates, with bigger banks counting for more, and repeats.
