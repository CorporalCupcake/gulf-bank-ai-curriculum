    Drift, Uncertainty and Stress: Knowing When a Model Has Stopped Being Right

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model is a bet that tomorrow looks like the training data, like a satnav with last year's map. Four tools check the bet:

    • Drift (PSI, KS): has the population moved? Fast, no labels needed.
    • Performance monitoring: has the model got worse? Needs labels, which arrive late.
    • Uncertainty: how sure is this prediction?
    • Stress testing: what if conditions turn sharply?
    Hold onto thisDrift is about inputs. Decay is about performance. Neither implies the other, so a green drift dashboard does not mean healthy and a red one does not mean broken.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring a moving population: PSI and the KS statistic

    PSI measures how different today's mix is from a reference mix, by comparing the share of customers in each score bin. Below 0.10 is stable, 0.10 to 0.25 is worth a look, above 0.25 is a major shift. These are conventions. PSI says how much the mix moved, not whether the model got worse.

    The mechanics are simple. Cut the reference data into ten equal-sized bins (deciles), then compare the share of today's customers falling in each. A bin that gains or loses share adds to the total, and the sum is PSI. A small shift across many bins and a big shift in one bin can score the same. Empty bins break the calculation, so practitioners floor them at a tiny share. PSI also has a noise floor that rises as samples shrink: on a few hundred rows, two draws from the same population already show a small non-zero PSI, so the usual bands mean little there.

    KS asks whether there is any real difference, using the largest gap between two cumulative curves. Its significance test is its trap: with millions of rows, a harmless shift looks "significant". So KS says "is it different?" and PSI with a threshold says "different enough to care?". Do not confuse it with the scorecard KS, which uses the same maximum gap but between good and bad customers to measure discrimination.

    Other drift metrics fill gaps. Chi-square suits categories and, like KS, flags tiny shifts at large sample sizes. Wasserstein distance is the average distance the distribution must move, in the variable's own units, so it is easy to explain. For embeddings or text, train a classifier to tell old from new: an AUC near 0.5 means no drift. Whatever you choose, fix the threshold in advance and say what action each level triggers.

    What you bin on matters. Watch the score, key features and the target rate, because the score can stay still while features move in opposite directions. Missing values are their own bin, so a broken feed shows there first.

    The reference matters too. Comparing with last month hides slow drift, because each step is small. Comparing with development data shows total drift but alarms for ever once the world has changed for good. Use both.

    Drift is not decay: monitoring performance

    Covariate shift changes the mix of inputs and often hurts little. Concept drift changes the link between inputs and outcomes, and hurts silently because the inputs can look identical. A pipeline change, such as a new currency unit, shows up sharply and feeds the model nonsense. A fourth kind, prior shift, moves the base rate (fraud attempts double in a campaign): ranking holds but calibration breaks.

    Performance monitoring is the ground truth: Gini, calibration by band, precision and recall. The catch is time. Labels arrive late, so the newest cohorts look better than they are; compare cohorts at equal age. Fast proxies such as first-payment default fill the gap.

    Labels are also selective: you see repayment only for approved customers, so a shift among the rejected goes unseen. The practical answer is layers. Drift statistics answer in days, proxies such as early delinquency in weeks, true outcomes in quarters. Concept drift is the one validators fear most, because without labels nothing can hear it.

    Drift triggers investigation, not a verdict. Every monitor needs an owner, an action and a plan to adapt. Recalibrating is cheap and fixes a level shift. Refitting on recent data fixes a changed relationship. Falling back to a rule is the safety net. Capital models need formal validation, so changes take months.

    Uncertainty quantification: a model that knows what it does not know

    A point prediction hides how far to trust it. Aleatoric uncertainty is noise in the world: two identical customers can still differ, and a PD of 3% already says so. Epistemic uncertainty is ignorance. Drift is an epistemic problem. A calibrated classifier already handles the aleatoric part. The hard part is "I have not seen anything like this".

    Four tools cover most needs. Ensembles and bootstrap refit the model several times on resampled data and read disagreement as doubt, at the cost of refitting, and they underestimate ignorance far outside the data. Quantile regression predicts the 5th and 95th percentiles directly, with no guarantee beyond the model's own quality. Abstention refuses low-confidence cases and sends them to a human, but needs a trustworthy confidence signal. Conformal prediction is below.

    Conformal prediction wraps any model and sizes intervals from its past errors, so "90%" means about nine in ten outcomes land inside, provided the future resembles the past. Drift breaks that proviso and coverage falls silently, so check realised coverage. R8 develops it fully.

    Uncertainty enables a three-lane decision: confident approve, confident decline, and an analyst in the middle. Lane width is a business choice, the cost of review against the cost of error. Language models are harder, because next-word probability is not the probability the answer is right. Compare agreement across several sampled answers, and treat a verbal "I am 90% sure" as evidence to test.

    Robustness and stress testing: when conditions change sharply

    Stress testing asks what happens if the road turns. Portfolio tests impose a severe scenario (oil down 40%, property down 25%) and read the loss and capital. Robustness tests add noise or drop a feed to see whether the model stays stable. Four cheap checks suit every ML model. Perturbation: add small noise and re-score; large swings mean a brittle fit. Broken inputs: send a null, a zero, a wrong unit; confident scores on garbage are a failure. Monotonic sanity: raise arrears and confirm risk rises. Segment slices: fine on average can hide a small segment doing much worse.

    Borrowers fail together, like umbrellas all opening in the rain. In a bad year defaults rise faster than a flat average PD suggests, so a model fitted on calm years understates losses exactly when it matters. The Basel-style model behind the simulation says each borrower's fortune is one shared economic factor plus individual luck. More correlation means a steeper tail.

    Good stress tests are severe, plausible and varied. They include reverse tests, which start from the failure ("what would breach this limit?") and work back. Say loudly where the model leaves its experience: a PD model fitted on a calm sample has no recession data, so its extrapolation is an assumption. Good tests also include second-round effects such as concentrations, collateral prices falling as defaults rise, and liquidity drying up. The single-factor model cannot see these.

    Drift monitoring, uncertainty and stress testing are one idea at three speeds: stress testing asks in advance, uncertainty says in real time, and drift monitoring says afterwards.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Early warning without labels. PSI and KS compare inputs only, so they react within days, while defaults take months to show.
    • Localising a cause. Per-feature and per-bin PSI show which variable moved and where in its range, which separates a new channel from a broken feed.
    • Cheap and model-agnostic. A few lines of code run on any scorecard, tree ensemble or neural net, which is why regulators and validators expect them.
    • Honest intervals via conformal. It uses real past errors, so the promise is testable and needs no distributional assumption.
    • Stress tests expose the tail. Correlation makes losses nonlinear in the shock. A scenario shows that, where an average-year view cannot.

    Weak at, or do not use when

    • Concept drift. Inputs look the same, so drift metrics stay green while the model decays.
    • Small samples. The PSI noise floor swamps the conventional bands on a few hundred rows.
    • Huge samples with KS. Everything is significant, so the test stops discriminating.
    • Changing populations by design. A fast-growing product alarms every month. Monitor against a moving reference instead.
    • Stress tests as forecasts. They are conditional on assumptions, and a single-factor model ignores second-round effects.

    In practice. Start with PSI on the score, the top 10 to 20 features, the missing share and the target rate, monthly. Fix a threshold, an owner and an action before the first alarm. Compute a noise-floor PSI from two halves of the same period so you know what quiet looks like. Add realised-coverage checks for any interval, and a reverse stress test for any portfolio model. The compute is trivial; the effort is in governance.

    In banking, PSI is standard scorecard monitoring for retail credit, and the same ideas guard fraud and AML models, where fraudsters adapt deliberately and the base rate swings. Stress testing is a supervisory requirement for the portfolio, and Gulf banks run oil and property scenarios. Conformal intervals suit forecasts such as deposit outflows (as with delivery times, the range is sized from past errors).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with a quick check.

    • "PSI is green, so the model is fine"

      Stable inputs say nothing about the input-outcome relationship, so concept drift passes straight through.

      Spot itAsk for outcome metrics alongside PSI, and their lag.
    • "PSI is red, so retrain now"

      The shift may be benign, temporary or a feed bug. Retraining on a blip bakes it in.

      Spot itAsk which feature moved and why. Check the feed before the model.
    • Significance mistaken for importance

      A KS test on millions of rows flags every month as drift. Likewise, thresholds of 0.1 and 0.25 behave differently on 300 rows and on 20 million.

      Spot itCompute PSI between two random halves of the same period: that is your noise floor.
    • Immature labels, or only the average

      Last quarter's default rate looks low because defaults have not happened yet. Overall AUC can stay flat while a small segment collapses.

      Spot itAsk for metrics by segment and vintage, compared at equal ages.
    • A stress test that is a bigger version of history

      "2008 plus 20%" misses a different kind of shock with different correlations. Averages alone also hide the tail.

      Spot itAsk what the scenario assumes about correlations, and for the breaking point.
    • Intervals and stress tests never checked

      A "90% interval" is quoted at calibration and never tested. Likewise, five models trained alike can be wrong together, so low ensemble disagreement is not certainty.

      Spot itAsk what fraction of actuals fell inside last quarter, and ask for a reverse stress test.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can a KS test flag drift while PSI stays tiny?

    KS comes with a significance test whose power grows with sample size, so with millions of rows it detects tiny shifts. PSI measures size. Significant is not the same as important.

    2. Name a case where PSI is zero but the model is failing, and one where PSI is huge but the model is fine.

    Zero PSI, failure: concept drift, where inputs look the same but risk at each input has risen. Huge PSI, fine: covariate shift into a well-covered region, such as a new channel bringing more of an existing customer type.

    3. Your 12-month default model's newest cohort shows a very low default rate. Is the model improving?

    No: the cohort has not had time to default. Compare equal months on book, and use early-delinquency proxies for recent cohorts.

    4. A 90% conformal interval only covered 74% of actuals last quarter. What happened?

    The future stopped resembling the calibration data: errors grew or became biased, so the margin is too small. Recalibrate and investigate.

    5. A book has average PD 3%. Why might a stressed default rate be 15% rather than 6%?

    Borrowers fail together through the economy, so in a bad year the tail rate is a multiple of the average. A flat PD fitted on calm years misses this.

    6. Your retail PD model's monthly PSI is green but you have no labels for six months. You can run one extra monitor. Which would you choose and why?

    An early-delinquency proxy by vintage, such as first-payment default. PSI cannot hear concept drift, so you need something tied to outcomes that arrives in weeks. A target-rate and calibration-by-band check on the proxy catches level shifts early.

    7. A pizza shop wants an uncertainty range on its delivery-time forecast. Would you choose quantile regression, an ensemble or conformal prediction, and why?

    Conformal, wrapped on the existing forecaster. It needs no model change, sizes intervals from real past errors and gives a coverage claim you can test each quarter. Quantile regression and ensembles carry no such guarantee. Either way, monitor realised coverage, because drift breaks the guarantee.
