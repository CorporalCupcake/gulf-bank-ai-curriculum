    Drift and Monitoring: Knowing When a Model Stops Working

    Part 1

    What drift and monitoring are

    In one sentenceMonitoring is checking a live model at four levels, from "is the system up?" to "are its predictions still right?", and drift is the third level: today's data no longer looks like the data the model learned from.

    Running example: a model that predicts whether a loan customer will miss a payment. It is a bet that tomorrow's customers look like the ones it was trained on, like a satnav with last year's map.

    How drift differs from decay

    • Drift is about the inputs: the mix of customers has changed. You can see it quickly, with no outcomes needed.
    • Decay is about performance: the model is actually getting worse. You only see it once real outcomes arrive, which takes months for loans.
    • Neither implies the other. A green drift dashboard does not mean the model is healthy, and a red one does not mean it is broken.
    Part 2

    The four layers

    1. System: latency, error rate, whether the nightly job ran, how fresh the scores are. Tells you the service is up, nothing about whether answers are right.
    2. Data quality: share of missing values, out-of-range values, row counts, changed columns. Inputs are well-formed. This is the most common cause of sudden failure.
    3. Drift: has the mix of inputs, or of scores, moved from training? An early warning, but it cannot say the model is worse.
    4. Performance: precision, recall and calibration against real outcomes. The only proof of harm, and the slowest to arrive.

    Layers 1 and 2 are cheap and should be near-complete. Layer 3 warns early. Layer 4 is the truth, but late. Also watch how people use the model: a rising rate of analysts overriding it shows lost trust long before the metrics move.

    Part 3

    How drift is measured

    The most common measure is PSI, the population stability index. It asks how different today's mix of scores is from a reference mix.

    1. Cut the reference scores into 10 bins with equal numbers of customers, so each holds 10%.
    2. Score today's customers and count the share that falls in each bin.
    3. Compare each bin: a bin that gains or loses share adds to the total.
    4. Add them up. That sum is the PSI.

    How to read it

    • Below 0.10: stable. Carry on.
    • 0.10 to 0.25: worth a look. The figure above sits here.
    • Above 0.25: a major shift. Investigate now.
    • These bands are conventions, not laws. PSI says how much the mix moved, not whether the model got worse.
    • Small samples are noisy: on a few hundred rows, two draws from the same population already give a small non-zero PSI, so the bands mean little.

    The KS test asks a different question: is there any real difference at all? It uses the biggest gap between two cumulative curves. With millions of rows, even a harmless shift looks "significant", so KS says "is it different?" and PSI with a threshold says "different enough to care?". Do not confuse it with the scorecard KS, which measures the gap between good and bad customers.

    Show the formula

    PSI = sum over bins of (today's share − reference share) × ln(today's share ÷ reference share).

    Each term is zero when a bin's share has not moved and grows as it moves, up or down. Empty bins break the logarithm, so practitioners floor them at a tiny share.

    What to compare against

    • Watch several things: the score, key inputs and the actual default rate. The score can stay still while two inputs move in opposite directions. Missing values get their own bin, so a broken feed shows there first.
    • Last month as reference hides slow drift, because each step is small.
    • Training data as reference shows total drift, but alarms for ever once the world has changed for good. Use both.
    Part 4

    Four ways the world moves

    • Covariate shift: the mix of inputs changes, for example more young customers. The model often copes, because the link between inputs and outcomes is unchanged.
    • Concept drift: the link itself changes, for example a rate rise makes the same customer riskier. It hurts silently because the inputs can look identical, so PSI stays at zero. Validators fear it most, because without outcomes nothing can detect it.
    • Pipeline change: a new currency unit or a renamed field feeds the model nonsense. It shows up sharply in layer 2.
    • Prior shift: the base rate moves, for example fraud attempts double during a campaign. Ranking holds, but the predicted probabilities become wrong.

    Outcomes are also late and selective. Labels arrive slowly, so the newest cohorts look better than they are: compare cohorts of equal age. And you only see repayment for customers you approved, so a shift among those you rejected goes unseen.

    What to do when an alarm fires

    • Every alarm needs an owner and an action, agreed in advance. Drift triggers an investigation, not a verdict.
    • Recalibrate (adjust the probabilities) to fix a level shift. It is cheap.
    • Refit on recent data to fix a changed relationship.
    • Fall back to a simple rule as the safety net while you fix it.

    Which check to use

    • Strong at: the layers together give fast warnings and a slow, true verdict.
    • Weak at: drift statistics cannot see concept drift. Only outcomes can.

    In banking, capital and credit models need formal validation when they change, so a fix can take months. That is why early warnings matter.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading drift as decay

      A high PSI means the inputs moved, not that the model got worse. Investigate before retraining.

    • Trusting KS on huge samples

      With millions of rows almost any shift is "significant". Judge by size with PSI.

    • Alarms with no owner

      An alert nobody must act on is noise. Name an owner and an action for each.

    • Judging new cohorts too early

      Recent customers have had less time to default, so they look better. Compare at equal age.

    1. Which layer is the only proof that a model is harming customers, and why is it slow?

    Performance. It needs real outcomes, and outcomes such as defaults arrive months later.

    2. PSI is 0.04 but defaults are rising. What might be happening?

    Concept drift: the inputs look the same, but the link between inputs and defaults has changed. PSI cannot see that.

    3. A PSI of 0.18 appears. What do you do?

    It is in the "worth a look" band: investigate which inputs moved and why. It is not proof the model is worse.

    4. Why compare against both last month and the training data?

    Last month hides slow drift because each step is small. Training data shows the total drift but keeps alarming once the change is permanent.

    5. Why is the number of rows important when reading PSI or KS?

    Small samples give noisy PSI even with no change, and huge samples make KS flag trivial shifts.
