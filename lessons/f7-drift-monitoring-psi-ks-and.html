    Drift, Uncertainty and Stress: Knowing When a Model Has Stopped Being Right

    How PSI and the KS statistic measure a shifting population, why drift in the inputs is not the same as decay in performance, how a model can say how sure it is, and what happens to a portfolio when conditions turn sharply.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceEvery model is a bet that tomorrow resembles the training data: drift statistics tell you the population moved, performance monitoring tells you whether it matters (usually months later, when labels land), uncertainty estimates tell you which predictions to distrust today, and stress tests tell you what happens when the bet fails badly.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model is a bet that tomorrow looks like the training data. It is a satnav with last year's map: still confident after the roads change. A credit-card score built in 2023 now meets more self-employed applicants and a new app channel. Nobody changed the model. Is it still right? Four questions, four tools:

    • Has the population moved? Drift detection (PSI, KS). Needs no labels, so it is fast. The smoke alarm: it can ring for harmless reasons, or stay silent.
    • Has the model got worse? Performance monitoring. Needs labels, which for defaults arrive 90 days or more later. The damage report: accurate but late.
    • How sure is this prediction? Uncertainty quantification. Route doubtful cases to a human, or refuse.
    • What if conditions turn sharply? Stress testing. The fire drill: push the model and portfolio outside the range they have seen.
    Hold onto thisDrift is about inputs. Decay is about performance. Neither implies the other: inputs can move a lot with no harm, and the input-outcome relationship can change while inputs look identical. A green drift dashboard does not mean healthy, and a red one does not mean broken.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring a moving population: PSI and the KS statistic

    PSI (Population Stability Index) answers: how different is today's mix from the reference mix? Picture a seating plan for ten tables: if guests still sit 10% per table, PSI is 0; the more they pile into some tables, the higher it climbs. Cut the score into ten bins using the reference data, then compare the share of today's population in each bin. It comes from credit scorecards.

    Reading a value. Below 0.10 is stable, 0.10 to 0.25 is worth a look, above 0.25 is a major shift. These are conventions, not laws. Calibrate them to your own variables and sample sizes, and write them into the monitoring policy.

    What a change means. Going from 0.05 to 0.15 means more customers have moved bins, not that the model is worse. PSI says how much the mix moved, not which way or whether it matters. Common misreading: treating PSI as a performance measure. It is a prompt to investigate.

    KS (Kolmogorov-Smirnov) answers: is there any real difference at all? Plot each sample's cumulative curve and take the largest vertical gap, D, between 0 and 1. It is the widest point at which one runner leads the other. D = 0.05 means that at the worst value, 5 percentage points more of one sample sit below it. No bins needed. Its strength is a proper significance test, and that is also its trap: with a million rows each side, a harmless shift is "significant". KS answers "is it different?", PSI with a threshold answers "is it different enough to care?" With few rows the opposite happens: PSI picks up sampling noise, so even two samples from the same population show a small non-zero PSI.

    Do not confuse this with the scorecard KS: the same maximum gap, but between good and bad customers. That measures discrimination, not drift. Say which one you mean.

    What you bin on matters more than the metric:

    • Score, features and target rate. The model score summarises everything; each important feature shows the cause; a rising target rate means more defaults or fraud whatever the inputs say. The score can stay still while two features move in opposite directions, so watch all three.
    • Missing values are a bin of their own. A broken feed shows as a jump in the missing share before any score moves.
    • Choose the reference deliberately. Last month hides slow drift. Development data shows total drift but alarms permanently once the world has changed for good. Use both.

    Other metrics exist: chi-square for categories (flags tiny shifts at large n, like KS), Wasserstein distance (the average shift, in the variable's own units, so easy to explain) and an old-versus-new classifier for embeddings or text (AUC near 0.5 means no drift). Whatever the metric: pick it, state the threshold in advance, and say what action each level triggers.

    Drift is not decay: monitoring performance

    The world can change in four ways, each with a different effect:

    TypeWhat changesBank exampleDrift metric sees it?Hurts the model?
    Covariate shiftThe mix of inputs. The rule linking inputs to outcomes is intact.More young, thin-file applicants through a new app.YesOften not much, unless the new inputs fall where the model never saw data.
    Prior (label) shiftThe base rate of the outcome.Fraud attempts double during a campaign.Only if the target rate is trackedRanking holds, calibration breaks (F7.4).
    Concept driftThe relationship between inputs and outcome.Customers with the same income and debt now default more because of a cost-of-living change.No: inputs can look identical.Yes, and silently.
    Data pipeline changeThe meaning of a field.A feed changes currency units or a null becomes zero.Usually, sharplyYes. The model gets nonsense.

    Concept drift worries validators most, because the smoke alarm cannot hear it: without labels you cannot measure a change in the relationship.

    Performance monitoring is the ground truth. Use the F7 metrics: AUC or Gini and KS for ranking, calibration by band (F7.4), precision and recall at the operating point. Read a fall in Gini as worse ranking, and predicted PD of 3% against actual 4.5% as slipped calibration. The problems are about time:

    • Labels are late. A 12-month default flag takes 12 months, so the newest cohorts look better than they are. Compare cohorts at the same age (vintage analysis).
    • Labels are selective. You see repayment only for approved customers (F2.6), so monitoring can look fine while a rejected-population shift goes unseen.
    • Proxies fill the gap. First-payment default, 30-day delinquency, analyst overrides, complaints: noisy but quick. Stack them: drift (days), proxies (weeks), outcomes (quarters).

    Three rules for running monitoring:

    • Drift triggers investigation, not a verdict. Diagnose which feature moved, why (new channel? broken feed?), and whether the model extrapolates safely there. Retraining on a blip can cement it.
    • Every monitor needs an owner and an action. "PSI above 0.25 for two months: owner investigates within ten working days." Your bank's policy and regulator set the details.
    • Decide in advance how to adapt. Recalibrate (cheap, fixes level shifts), refit on recent data, rebuild, or fall back to a rule. Capital models need formal validation, so changes take months.

    Uncertainty quantification: a model that knows what it does not know

    A point prediction ("PD 3.2%", "deposits next week AED 480m") hides how far to trust it. Weather makes the split clear: "30% chance of rain" is honest noise, "never seen weather like this" is ignorance.

    • Aleatoric is noise in the world. Two customers with identical files can still differ, and no data fixes it. A PD of 3% is itself an aleatoric statement.
    • Epistemic is ignorance: little data in this region, or an input unlike anything seen. Drift and extrapolation are epistemic problems.

    A calibrated classifier already reports aleatoric uncertainty (F7.4). The hard part is "I haven't seen anything like this". Main tools:

    MethodHow it worksCatch
    Bootstrap / ensembles (F2.2)Refit several times on resampled data or random starts; disagreement signals doubt.Cost of refitting; underestimates out-of-range ignorance.
    Quantile regressionPredict the 5th and 95th percentile directly (F6.1, F9.1).Intervals are only as good as the model; no guarantee.
    Conformal predictionWrap any model: measure its errors on held-out data, then use them to size intervals with a coverage guarantee.The guarantee needs the future to look like the past, which drift breaks.
    AbstentionRefuse when confidence is low; send to a human.Needs a trustworthy confidence signal.

    Conformal prediction turns "trust me" into a testable promise. It is sizing a margin from your past forecast misses. "90%" means about nine in ten future outcomes land inside, whatever the model, provided the future resembles the calibration data. A wider interval means larger past errors. R8 develops it fully.

    Drift breaks that proviso. If errors grow or become biased after calibration, the interval is too narrow and coverage falls silently. Conformal intervals are calibrated against the past, so you still need to monitor drift itself.

    Uncertainty underpins safe automation: a three-lane decision of confident approve, confident decline, and a middle lane to an analyst. Lane width is a business choice, the cost of review against the cost of error (F7.3). Language models are harder, because next-word probability is not the probability the answer is right. Compare agreement across several sampled answers, and treat a verbal "I'm 90% sure" as evidence to test, not a measurement.

    Robustness and stress testing: when conditions change sharply

    Drift monitoring watches the road you are on. Stress testing asks what happens if the road turns. Two families:

    • Portfolio stress testing. Impose a severe but plausible scenario (oil down 40%, property down 25%, rates up 300 basis points), push it through the PD, LGD and exposure models, and read loss, capital and liquidity. Supervisors require it. It asks about the bank.
    • Model robustness testing. Add noise, drop a feed, feed extreme values. It asks about the model: is it stable and safe outside its training range?

    Why a calm-period model underestimates a crisis

    Borrowers fail together, like umbrellas all opening when it rains. The model behind Basel capital (Vasicek) says each borrower's fortune depends on one shared economic factor plus individual luck. In a bad year the default rate rises faster than linearly, and faster still when borrowers are more correlated. A model fitted on calm years has seen little of that tail, so its flat average PD understates losses exactly when it matters.

    Good stress tests:

    • Severe, plausible and varied. Historical (a past oil collapse), hypothetical (regional conflict, property correction) and reverse tests, which start from the failure ("what would breach this limit?") and work back. Reverse tests often reveal most.
    • Mind the model's validity. A PD model fitted on a calm sample has no recession data, so its extrapolation is an assumption. Say loudly where the model leaves its experience.
    • Include second-round effects. Concentrations, collateral falling as defaults rise, liquidity drying up. The single-factor model cannot see these.

    Robustness tests are cheap, and every ML model needs them:

    TestWhat you doWhat failure looks like
    PerturbationAdd small noise or rounding to numeric inputs; re-score.Large score swings from tiny changes: a brittle fit.
    Missing and broken inputsBlank a feature, send a null, a zero, a wrong unit.Confident scores on garbage, or crashes in production.
    Monotonic and sanity checksRaise one feature that should only raise risk (arrears) and confirm the score rises (F8).Score falls as arrears grow: the model learned noise.
    Segment slicesScore by nationality group, product, channel, age band (G2).Fine on average, much worse for a small segment.

    Drift monitoring, uncertainty and stress testing are one idea at three speeds. Stress testing asks in advance how far the model can be pushed. Uncertainty says in real time that this input is beyond what it knows. Drift monitoring says afterwards that the population has wandered towards the edge.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • "PSI is green, so the model is fine"

      Stable inputs say nothing about the input-outcome relationship. Concept drift passes straight through.

      Spot itAsk for outcome metrics alongside PSI, and their lag.
    • "PSI is red, so retrain now"

      The shift may be benign, temporary or a feed bug. Retraining on a blip bakes it in.

      Spot itAsk which feature moved, why, and whether the new region is inside the training range. Check the feed before the model.
    • Thresholds copied without thought, or significance mistaken for importance

      0.1 and 0.25 on 300 rows, or 20 million, behave completely differently. A KS test on millions of rows flags every month as "drift".

      Spot itCompute PSI between two random halves of the same period: that is your noise floor. Read the effect size, not the p-value.
    • Monitoring only the average, or reading immature labels as performance

      Overall AUC is flat while a small segment collapses. Last quarter's default rate looks low because the defaults have not happened yet.

      Spot itAsk for metrics by segment and vintage, with sample sizes and months on book. Compare equal ages only.
    • Interval with a coverage claim and no coverage check

      "90% interval" quoted at calibration, never tested. Drift silently breaks the promise. Likewise, five models trained alike can all be wrong in the same way, so low ensemble disagreement is not certainty.

      Spot itAsk what fraction of actuals fell inside in the last three months. Test uncertainty on a known out-of-range slice: does it rise there?
    • A stress test that is just a bigger version of history

      "2008 plus 20%" when the next shock is a different kind of event with different correlations. Averages alone also hide the tail.

      Spot itAsk what the scenario assumes about correlations, and ask for the loss range, the breaking point and a reverse stress test.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A tenth of the population moves from the lowest bin to the highest; everything else is unchanged. Would you expect PSI to be in the "major shift" band?

    No. PSI comes out around 0.055, which is stable by the usual bands, even though a tenth of customers moved between the extremes. PSI needs a broad movement across many bins to climb, so a small, targeted shift can hide under the threshold. That is why you also watch individual features and the target rate.

    2. Why can a KS test flag drift while PSI stays tiny?

    KS comes with a significance test whose power grows with sample size, so with millions of rows it detects tiny shifts. PSI measures size, and a threshold on it asks "does it matter". Significant is not important.

    3. Name a case where PSI is zero but the model is failing, and one where PSI is huge but the model is fine.

    Zero PSI, failure: concept drift, where inputs look the same but risk at each input has risen. Huge PSI, fine: covariate shift into a well-covered region with the same input-outcome relationship, such as a new channel bringing more of an existing customer type. Simulation 2 shows both.

    4. Your 12-month default model's newest cohort shows a very low default rate. Is the model improving?

    Almost certainly not: the cohort has not had time to default. Compare equal months on book, and use early-delinquency proxies for recent cohorts.

    5. A 90% conformal interval was calibrated on 200 forecast errors. Last quarter only 74% of actuals fell inside. Causes and action?

    Exchangeability has failed: errors got larger or biased (regime change, new products, data change), so the calibrated margin is too small. Check whether the centre shifted or the spread widened, recalibrate on recent errors or use an adaptive variant, and investigate the cause rather than only widening the band.

    6. Distinguish aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty with a banking example of each.

    Aleatoric: two identical-looking borrowers, one defaults. Irreducible noise; the model should say 3%, not 0% or 100%. Epistemic: a first-ever applicant type, such as a new visa class, with almost no history. More data helps, and an ensemble would disagree there.

    7. A book has average PD 3%. Why might the stressed default rate be 15% or more rather than 6%?

    Defaults are correlated through the economy. In a bad year many borrowers fail together, so the tail rate is a multiple of the average. A flat PD fitted on calm years misses this (Simulation 4 shows the curve).

    8. What is a reverse stress test and why is it valuable?

    You fix the bad outcome (capital breached, loss limit hit) and find the conditions that produce it. It forces a discussion of plausibility, exposes hidden concentrations, and avoids testing only the scenarios people already worry about.
