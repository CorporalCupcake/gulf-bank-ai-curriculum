    Drift, Uncertainty and Stress: Knowing When a Model Has Stopped Being Right

    How PSI and the KS statistic measure a shifting population, why drift in the inputs is not the same as decay in performance, how a model can say how sure it is, and what happens to a portfolio when conditions turn sharply.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceEvery model is a bet that tomorrow resembles the training data: drift statistics tell you the population moved, performance monitoring tells you whether it matters (usually months later, when labels land), uncertainty estimates tell you which predictions to distrust today, and stress tests tell you what happens when the bet fails badly.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model is a bet that tomorrow looks like the training data. It is a satnav with last year's map: still confident after the roads change. A credit-card score built in 2023 on salaried customers now meets more self-employed applicants, a new app channel and different rates. Nobody changed the model. Is it still right? Four questions, four tools:

    • Has the population moved? Drift detection (PSI, KS). Needs no labels, so it is fast. The smoke alarm: it can ring for harmless reasons, or stay silent.
    • Has the model got worse? Performance monitoring. Needs labels, which for defaults arrive 90 days or more later (F3.5). The damage report: accurate but late.
    • How sure is this prediction? Uncertainty quantification. Route doubtful cases to a human, widen a range, or refuse.
    • What if conditions turn sharply? Stress testing. The fire drill: push the model and portfolio outside the range they have seen.
    Hold onto thisDrift is about inputs. Decay is about performance. Neither implies the other: inputs can move a lot with no harm, and the input-outcome relationship can change while inputs look identical. A green drift dashboard does not mean healthy, and a red one does not mean broken.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Measuring a moving population: PSI and the KS statistic

    PSI (Population Stability Index) answers: how different is today's mix from the reference mix? Picture a seating plan for ten tables: if guests still sit 10% per table, PSI is 0; the more they pile into some tables, the higher it climbs. Take a reference sample (usually development data), cut the variable or score into ten bins by reference quantiles (10% each), and compare the share of today's population in each bin. Borrowed from credit scorecards (Siddiqi, 2006).

    PSI=∑i(ai−ei)·lnaiei

    ei is the expected (reference) share in bin i, ai the actual (current) share. Each term is zero when shares match and positive otherwise. PSI is a symmetric KL divergence (F1.7). A mean shift of δ standard deviations gives PSI of about δ² with fine bins, a bit less with ten.

    Reading a value. Below 0.10 is stable, 0.10 to 0.25 is worth a look, above 0.25 is a major shift. These are scorecard conventions, not laws, and roughly match the mean moving 0.3 and 0.5 standard deviations. Calibrate them to your own variables and sample sizes and write them into the monitoring policy.

    What a change means. Going from 0.05 to 0.15 means more customers have moved bins, not that the model is worse. PSI says how much the mix moved, not which way or whether it matters. Even a tenth of the population moving between the extremes gives only 0.055 (self-check 1). Common misreading: treating PSI as a performance measure. It is a prompt to investigate.

    KS (Kolmogorov-Smirnov) answers: is there any real difference at all? Plot each sample's cumulative curve (share of customers at or below each value) and take the largest vertical gap, D, between 0 and 1. It is the widest point at which one runner leads the other. D = 0.05 means that at the worst value, 5 percentage points more of one sample sit below it. No bins needed. Its strength is a proper significance test: for samples of size n and m, the shift is detectable at 5% when

    D>1.36n+mnm

    With a million rows on each side the threshold is about 0.002, so a harmless shift is "significant". KS answers "is it different?", PSI with a threshold answers "is it different enough to care?" With few rows the opposite problem appears: PSI picks up sampling noise. Two samples from the same population give expected PSI of roughly (bins − 1) × (1/n + 1/m), so 10 bins and 500 rows per period gives a noise floor near 0.036 before anything changes.

    Do not confuse this with the scorecard KS: the same maximum gap, but between the score distributions of good and bad customers. That measures discrimination, not drift. Say which one you mean.

    Practical details that matter more than the formula:

    • What you bin on. The model score (one number summarising everything), each important feature (where the cause shows), and the target rate if labels exist. A rising target rate means more defaults or fraud than before, whatever the inputs say. The score can stay still while two features move in opposite directions, so do all.
    • Categorical features. Bin by category, merging rare ones. A category absent from the reference gives an empty expected bin: add a small constant to avoid dividing by zero, and treat the appearance itself as an alert.
    • Missing values are a bin of their own. A broken feed shows as a jump in the missing share before any score moves.
    • Choose the reference deliberately. Last month hides slow drift (each step is small). Development data shows total drift but alarms permanently once the world has changed for good. Use both.

    Other drift metrics, each with how to read it:

    MetricWhat it meansHow to read it
    Chi-squareFor categories: do the category shares differ from the reference by more than chance?Like KS, it flags tiny shifts at large n. Pair it with an effect size such as PSI.
    Wasserstein ("earth-mover")The cost of shovelling one distribution into the shape of the other, in the variable's own units.A value of 2,000 on income means values would have to move about 2,000 on average to match. Easy to explain, because it has units.
    Jensen-Shannon divergenceA smoothed, bounded cousin of KL divergence.0 means identical; the maximum means no overlap. Handy when bins are empty.
    Old-versus-new classifierFor embeddings or text: train a classifier to tell old from new.If it can, they differ. AUC near 0.5 means no drift; near 1 means a large one.

    Whatever the metric: pick it, state the threshold in advance, and say what action each level triggers.

    Drift is not decay: monitoring performance

    The world can change in four ways, each with a different effect (Gama et al., 2014; Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2009):

    TypeWhat changesBank exampleDrift metric sees it?Hurts the model?
    Covariate shiftDistribution of inputs P(x). The rule linking inputs to outcomes P(y | x) is intact.More young, thin-file applicants through a new app.YesOften not much, unless the new inputs fall where the model never saw data.
    Prior (label) shiftThe base rate P(y).Fraud attempts double during a campaign.Only if the target rate is trackedRanking holds, calibration breaks (F3.5, F7.4).
    Concept driftThe relationship P(y | x) itself.Customers with the same income and debt now default more because of a cost-of-living change. Fraudsters learn to avoid the model's tells.No: inputs can look identical.Yes, and silently.
    Data pipeline changeThe meaning of a field.A feed changes currency units or a null becomes zero.Usually, sharplyYes. The model gets nonsense.

    Concept drift worries validators most, because the smoke alarm cannot hear it: without labels you cannot measure a change in P(y | x). Rabanser, Günnemann and Lipton (2019, "Failing Loudly") say models should fail loudly rather than silently, but you can only be loud about what you measure.

    Performance monitoring is the ground truth. Use the F7 metrics: AUC or Gini and KS for discrimination, calibration by band (F7.4), precision and recall at the operating point, and the F7.5 error metrics for forecasts. Read a fall in Gini as worse ranking, and predicted PD of 3% against actual 4.5% as slipped calibration, even if ranking holds. The problems are about time:

    • Labels are late. A 12-month default flag takes 12 months, so the newest cohorts look better than they are (F3.5, Simulation 4). Compare cohorts at the same age (vintage analysis).
    • Labels are selective. You see repayment only for approved customers and fraud only for alerted transactions (F2.6). Monitoring can look fine while a rejected-population shift goes unseen.
    • Proxies fill the gap. First-payment default, 30-day delinquency, alert-rejection rate, analyst overrides, complaints: noisy but quick. Stack them: drift (days), proxies (weeks), outcomes (quarters).
    • Without labels. Under covariate shift, reweight old labelled data to resemble the new inputs. Under label shift, black-box shift estimation (Lipton et al., 2018) estimates the new base rate from predictions. Both fail under concept drift: screening aids, not proof.

    Three rules for running monitoring:

    • Drift triggers investigation, not a verdict. Diagnose which feature moved, why (new channel? broken feed?), and whether the model extrapolates safely there. Retraining on a blip can cement it.
    • Every monitor needs an owner and an action. "PSI above 0.25 for two months: owner investigates within ten working days; and Gini down 5 points: escalate to the model risk committee." Supervisory guidance (for example US SR 11-7, widely used as a reference in Gulf banks) expects ongoing monitoring with defined thresholds. Your bank's policy and regulator set the details.
    • Decide in advance how to adapt. Recalibrate (cheap, fixes level shifts), refit on recent data, rebuild, or fall back to a rule. Fraud, where adversaries adapt, retrains regularly. Capital models need formal validation, so changes take months. Know that cost before relying on the model.

    Uncertainty quantification: a model that knows what it does not know

    A point prediction ("PD 3.2%", "deposits next week AED 480m") hides how far to trust it. Weather makes the split clear: "30% chance of rain" is honest noise, "never seen weather like this" is ignorance. Two kinds of uncertainty:

    • Aleatoric is noise in the world. Two customers with identical files can still differ, and no data fixes it. A PD of 3% is itself an aleatoric statement.
    • Epistemic is ignorance: little data in this region, or an input unlike anything seen. More data, or knowing you are out of range, reduces it. Drift and extrapolation are epistemic problems.

    A calibrated classifier already reports aleatoric uncertainty (F7.4). The hard part is "I haven't seen anything like this". Main tools:

    MethodHow it worksCatchesCatch
    Bootstrap / bagging (F2.2)Refit on resampled data; the spread of predictions shows sampling uncertainty.Epistemic, from limited data.Cost of refitting; underestimates out-of-range ignorance.
    Deep ensemblesTrain 5 to 10 networks from different random starts; disagreement signals doubt (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).Epistemic. A strong, simple baseline.5 to 10 times the training and serving cost.
    MC dropoutKeep dropout on at prediction time and average many passes (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016).An approximation to the above, cheaper.Quality depends on the setup; often too confident.
    Quantile regression / predictive distributionsPredict the 5th and 95th percentile directly, or a full distribution (F6.1, F9.1).Aleatoric spread, including skew.Intervals are only as good as the model; no guarantee.
    Conformal predictionWrap any model: measure its errors on held-out calibration data, then use the error quantile to build intervals or sets with guaranteed coverage (Vovk et al.; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021).Calibrated intervals with a finite-sample guarantee.The guarantee needs exchangeability, which drift breaks. See below.
    Abstention / selective predictionRefuse when confidence is low; send to a human.Operational use of any score above.Needs a trustworthy confidence signal.

    Conformal prediction turns "trust me" into a testable promise. It is sizing a margin from your past forecast misses. Hold out a calibration set and take the model's absolute errors on it. For a 90% interval from n points, take the error quantile at level ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/(n+1) and add and subtract it around each new prediction. Reading it: "90%" means about nine in ten future outcomes land inside, whatever the model and error shape, provided calibration and future data are exchangeable (drawn from the same stable process). A wider interval means larger past errors. R8 develops it fully.

    Drift destroys exchangeability. If errors grow or become biased after calibration, the interval is too narrow and coverage falls silently. Conformal intervals are calibrated against the past. Adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs and Candès, 2021) and reweighting for known covariate shift (Barber et al., 2023) help, but you still need to monitor drift itself.

    Uncertainty underpins safe automation: a three-lane decision of confident approve, confident decline, and a middle lane to an analyst. Lane width is a business choice, the cost of review against the cost of error, as in F7.3. Language models are harder, because next-word probability is not the probability the answer is right. Compare agreement across several sampled answers, check support from retrieved documents (A4), or use calibrated verifiers. Treat verbal "I'm 90% sure" as evidence to test, not a measurement.

    Robustness and stress testing: when conditions change sharply

    Drift monitoring watches the road you are on. Stress testing asks what happens if the road turns. Two families:

    • Portfolio stress testing (macro scenarios). Impose a severe but plausible scenario (oil down 40%, property down 25%, rates up 300 basis points, unemployment doubled), push it through the PD, LGD and exposure models, and read loss, capital and liquidity. Supervisors require it (ICAAP and ILAAP style; your regulator sets the details). It asks about the bank.
    • Model robustness testing. Add noise, drop a feed, shift a feature, feed extreme values. It asks about the model: is it stable, monotonic where it should be, and safe outside its training range?

    Why a calm-period model underestimates a crisis

    Borrowers fail together, like umbrellas all opening when it rains. The Vasicek single-factor model behind Basel capital captures this: each borrower's fortune depends on one shared macro factor z (how bad the economy is) and individual luck. With through-the-cycle default probability PD and asset correlation ρ, in a downturn of severity z:

    PD(z)=Φ(Φ−1(PD)+ρ·z1−ρ)

    Φ is the standard normal distribution function, Φ−1 its inverse. z = 0 is an average year; z = 3.09 is the one-in-a-thousand bad year in the Basel IRB formula (BCBS, 2005). Reading it: the default rate rises faster than linearly as z worsens, and faster for higher ρ. A model fitted on calm years has seen little of the bad tail, so its flat average PD understates losses exactly when it matters.

    Good stress tests:

    • Severe, plausible and varied. Historical (a past oil collapse, 2020), hypothetical (regional conflict, property correction) and reverse tests, which start from the failure ("what would breach this limit?") and work back. Reverse tests often reveal most, because they force the question the committee avoids.
    • Mind the model's validity. A PD model fitted on a calm sample has no recession data, so its extrapolation is an assumption. Remedies: macro-linked satellite models, longer histories or other markets, expert overlays, and saying loudly where the model leaves its experience.
    • Include second-round effects. Concentrations (a few large developers, one sector), collateral falling as defaults rise, liquidity drying up. The single-factor formula cannot see wrong-way risk.

    Robustness tests are cheap, and every ML model needs them:

    TestWhat you doWhat failure looks like
    PerturbationAdd small noise or rounding to numeric inputs; re-score.Large score swings from tiny changes: a brittle, high-variance fit.
    Missing and broken inputsBlank a feature, send a null, a zero, a wrong unit, a string.Confident scores on garbage, or crashes in production.
    Extreme valuesIncome 100 times the maximum, tenure of −3 years.Non-monotonic or absurd scores outside the training range.
    Monotonic and sanity checksRaise one feature that should only raise risk (arrears, utilisation) and confirm the score rises (F8, partial dependence).Score falls as arrears grow: the model learned noise or an artefact.
    Segment and subgroup slicesScore by nationality group, product, channel, region, age band (G2).Fine on average, much worse for a small segment.
    Distribution-shift stressReweight or simulate a population shift (more thin-file customers); also text and image corruptions for those models (Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019).Performance falls off a cliff instead of gently.
    Adversarial and manipulation testsFraud: tweak the features a criminal can control. LLMs: paraphrases, transliterated Arabic, injection attempts (G1, A3).Easy evasions; model relies on features that are cheap to fake.

    Drift monitoring, uncertainty and stress testing are one idea at three speeds. Stress testing asks in advance how far the model can be pushed. Uncertainty says in real time that this input is beyond what it knows. Drift monitoring says afterwards that the population has wandered towards the edge.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • "PSI is green, so the model is fine"

      Stable inputs say nothing about the input-outcome relationship. Concept drift passes straight through.

      Spot itAsk for outcome metrics alongside PSI, and their lag. PSI alone is blind to concept drift.
    • "PSI is red, so retrain now"

      The shift may be benign, temporary or a feed bug. Retraining on a blip bakes it in.

      Spot itAsk which feature moved, why, and whether the new region is inside the training range. Check the feed before the model.
    • Thresholds copied without thought

      0.1 and 0.25 on a variable with 300 rows, or 20 million, where noise floor and sensitivity differ completely.

      Spot itCompute PSI between two random halves of the same period. That is your noise floor.
    • Statistical significance mistaken for importance

      A KS test on millions of rows flags every month as "drift".

      Spot itRead the effect size (D, PSI, mean shift in standard deviations), not the p-value, and tie the alarm to performance impact.
    • Bins fixed at development time, never refreshed

      Reference deciles from a 2022 score now bunched in two bins. PSI reads huge for months, then everyone ignores it.

      Spot itCompare the current distribution's shape with the bin edges. Rebase the reference through the formal change process, never silently.
    • Monitoring only the average

      Overall AUC is flat while a small segment, say new-to-bank customers, collapses.

      Spot itAsk for metrics by segment and vintage, with sample sizes.
    • Reading immature labels as performance

      Last quarter's default rate looks low because the defaults have not happened yet.

      Spot itCheck months-on-book for each cohort. Compare equal ages only.
    • Interval with a coverage claim and no coverage check

      "90% interval" quoted at calibration, never tested. Drift silently breaks the promise.

      Spot itAsk what fraction of actuals fell inside in the last three months. Far below the promise means the interval is stale.
    • Ensemble spread read as the whole truth

      Five models trained alike can all be wrong in the same way, so low disagreement is not certainty.

      Spot itTest on a known out-of-range slice: does uncertainty rise there? If not, it is not measuring what you need.
    • A stress test that is just a bigger version of history

      "2008 plus 20%" when the next shock is a different kind of event with different correlations.

      Spot itAsk what the scenario assumes about correlations and model behaviour outside its training data. Ask for a reverse stress test.
    • Averages that hide the tail

      Expected loss under stress reported with no sense of the distribution around it.

      Spot itAsk for the loss range, the breaking point and which assumption moves it most.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Reference shares are 10% in each of ten bins. Today the lowest bin holds 5% and the highest 15%; the rest stay at 10%. Compute the PSI.

    Low bin: (−0.05)(−0.693) = 0.0347. High bin: 0.05 × 0.405 = 0.0203. PSI ≈ 0.055: stable by the usual bands, although a tenth of the population moved between the extremes.

    2. Why can a KS test flag drift while PSI stays tiny?

    KS comes with a significance test whose power grows with sample size, so with millions of rows it detects tiny shifts. PSI measures size, and a threshold on it asks "does it matter". Significant is not important.

    3. Name a case where PSI is zero but the model is failing, and one where PSI is huge but the model is fine.

    Zero PSI, failure: concept drift, where inputs look the same but risk at each input has risen. Huge PSI, fine: covariate shift into a well-covered region with the same input-outcome relationship, such as a new channel bringing more of an existing customer type. Simulation 2 shows both.

    4. Your 12-month default model's newest cohort shows a very low default rate. Is the model improving?

    Almost certainly not: the cohort has not had time to default. Compare equal months on book, and use early-delinquency proxies for recent cohorts.

    5. A 90% conformal interval was calibrated on 200 forecast errors. Last quarter only 74% of actuals fell inside. Causes and action?

    Exchangeability has failed: errors got larger or biased (regime change, new products, data change), so the calibrated quantile is too small. Check bias against variance (shifted centre versus wider spread), recalibrate on recent errors or use an adaptive variant, and investigate the cause rather than only widening the band.

    6. Distinguish aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty with a banking example of each.

    Aleatoric: two identical-looking borrowers, one defaults. Irreducible noise; the model should say 3%, not 0% or 100%. Epistemic: a first-ever applicant type, such as a new visa class, with almost no history. More data helps, and an ensemble would disagree there.

    7. A book has average PD 3%. Why might the stressed default rate be 15% or more rather than 6%?

    Defaults are correlated through the economy. In a bad year many borrowers fail together, so the tail rate is a multiple of the average. With PD 3% and ρ = 0.12, the one-in-a-thousand year gives about 19% (Simulation 4). A flat PD fitted on calm years misses this.

    8. What is a reverse stress test and why is it valuable?

    You fix the bad outcome (capital breached, loss limit hit) and find the conditions that produce it. It forces a discussion of plausibility, exposes hidden concentrations, and avoids testing only the scenarios people already worry about.
