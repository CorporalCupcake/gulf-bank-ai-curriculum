    Causal Inference and Bayesian Thinking: Did It Work, and How Sure Are We?

    Why "customers who got the offer stayed more" proves nothing, how confounding, randomisation, adjustment and difference-in-differences each try to recover the effect, and how Bayesian reasoning lets you say something honest from a small pilot.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA causal claim is a comparison with what would have happened otherwise, which you never observe, so every method is an argument for why some group stands in for that missing world; Bayesian thinking is the matching habit of stating what you believed before the data, how far the data moved you, and how much doubt remains.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank sends a fee-waiver offer to 30,000 customers. A year later, 82% of those who took it up are still active, against 74% of those who did not. The slide says: "The campaign lifted retention by 8 points."

    Who took up the offer? Customers who open the app and care about their account, who were likely to stay anyway. Like a gym: members who attend often are fitter, but the gym did not necessarily make them so.

    Prediction asks "what will happen, given what I see?" Causation asks "what would happen if I did something?" The second needs this same customer without the offer, a world you never see. Every causal method borrows a stand-in for it and argues the stand-in is fair.

    • Correlation answers a different question. The 8 points truly describe who stayed. They just do not isolate the offer.
    • Every causal estimate rests on an assumption you cannot fully test. Make it plausible and check what you can.
    • Small data needs prior knowledge stated openly. Bayesian reasoning combines a pilot with what you believed before, and shows both.
    Hold onto thisBefore accepting any "X caused Y" slide, ask: compared with what? Who would have ended up in each group anyway?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Potential outcomes: the missing world

    Each customer has two potential outcomes: whether they stay if offered, and whether they stay if not. The causal effect is the difference, but you only ever see one. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference: like a fork in the road, you only learn the path you took. So we target averages, either over everyone (ATE), over just the treated (ATT, "did it help the people we contacted?"), or for particular customer types ("for whom does it work?").

    The naive treated-minus-untreated gap equals the true effect plus selection bias: how different the treated would have been even without treatment. Randomisation sets selection bias to zero by construction. Every other method tries to remove it without randomising.

    One hidden assumption to watch: no interference. One customer's treatment should not change another's outcome, which fails with referrals, fraud rings and bank runs.

    Confounders, colliders and the causal graph

    A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Engagement drives both take-up and staying, so correlation flows even at zero effect. Close it by comparing within levels of the confounder (engaged takers with engaged non-takers), which is what the "adjust" switch did.

    The wrong adjustment creates bias:

    • Collider. Caused by both treatment and outcome. Analysing only customers flagged "high-value this quarter", when the flag depends on both offer and staying, creates a spurious link. A credit model trained only on approved applicants is the same trap (F2.6).
    • Mediator. A step on the causal path. If the offer works by raising card usage, adjusting for usage removes the effect you wanted.
    • Post-treatment variable. Anything measured after the offer. Control only for things fixed before it (F3.1).

    "Throw every variable in" is fine for prediction but unsafe for causal questions.

    Adjustment, matching and propensity scores

    If you measured all the confounders, the methods share one idea: make treated and untreated look alike, then compare.

    • Regression adjustment. Model the outcome from treatment and covariates; the treatment coefficient is the adjusted effect (F2.5).
    • Propensity scores. Model each customer's probability of being treated, then compare people with equal propensity. Weighting does the same: a rare type of treated customer speaks for many similar untreated ones, so counts more.

    Two catches. Overlap: if some customers are almost never treated, there is nothing to compare them with and weights explode. If the propensity histograms for the two groups barely touch, the data cannot answer the question for those customers. And no method fixes an unmeasured confounder; sensitivity analysis asks how strong a hidden factor would need to be to erase the effect.

    Machine-learning versions of these ideas (double ML, causal forests) show how the effect varies by customer, the basis of uplift modelling.

    Randomisation and natural experiments

    When you choose who gets treated, randomise. A coin flip is independent of everything, including factors you never measured. At a bank, hold back a random 5-10% from a campaign, limit increase or collections script (F3.6, R8.3). Analyse by assignment, not by who took it up, or selection returns.

    When you cannot randomise, find a natural experiment: something outside customers' control that changed who got treated.

    • Difference-in-differences (DiD). A policy hits one branch or emirate, not another. Compare the change in each. Needs parallel trends: without treatment, both would have moved alike.
    • Regression discontinuity. A cut-off rule: score 600 gets the product, 599 does not. Customers either side are alike except for treatment. The effect holds only near the cut-off.
    • Synthetic control. Blend untreated units that tracked the treated one before the change. See R8.5.

    The before-after number credits the launch with everything that changed, including the background decline. DiD removes that background using the comparison region, trading one assumption (nothing else changed in the treated region at launch) for another (parallel trends), which you can partly inspect in the pre-launch months.

    Bayesian thinking: prior, data, posterior

    Causal methods make the comparison fair. Bayesian thinking says how sure you are of the number it gave. Like a weather forecaster who starts from the seasonal norm and updates with today's radar, you start with what you believed (the prior), update with data, and end with a full distribution of plausible values (the posterior).

    Think of the prior as imaginary earlier customers: a 3% prior "worth 100 customers" blends with a pilot like 100 more observations. Little data and the prior dominates; lots of data and the data does.

    Why it matters at a bank:

    • Small samples. A 200-customer pilot, 40 AML cases. You get an honestly wide distribution, and extreme rates shrink toward a sensible centre instead of reporting 0 of 12 as "0%".
    • Direct decision statements. "88% probability the response rate beats our 3% break-even" is what the business wants. A credible interval says this, given your prior and model; a confidence interval does not.
    • Sequential decisions. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, the engine of bandits.

    The prior is a choice: state it, justify it, and show how the answer moves when it changes. And a posterior is only as causal as the comparison behind it. Run Simulation 1 through a Bayesian model and you get a well-calibrated posterior around the wrong lift. Ask is the comparison fair? first, then how sure are we of its number?

    Can you claim causation? Four worked cases

    The meeting skill is naming what is wrong or missing. Decide, then open the answer.

    Case A. A wealth-management RM team calls 5,000 clients; their deposits rose 12% in the next quarter versus 4% for uncalled clients. "Calls drive deposits."

    Not yet. RMs call promising or familiar clients, so selection is built in. Ask how the 5,000 were chosen, adjust for pre-period balances and tenure, and caveat unmeasured relationship strength. Best: randomise who is called next quarter.

    Case B. A new credit-limit policy applied to Dubai branches only from 1 March. Spend per card rose; Abu Dhabi did not change. A colleague compares March spend in the two emirates.

    The gap mixes the policy with permanent emirate differences. Use DiD on the change from earlier months, and plot several pre-months to check parallel trends. Check nothing else hit Dubai in March, and remember two regions give little independent evidence.

    Case C. A model predicts churn well. The business wants to "target the customers with the highest churn score with a retention offer".

    That targets who will leave, not who can be persuaded to stay. The causal quantity is uplift, the change in staying probability with versus without the offer. Start with a randomised holdout on the next offer.

    Case D. Of 14 AML alerts from a new rule on a niche corridor, 6 were confirmed. The rule's owner says "43% hit rate, ten times better than the old rule's 4%."

    Fourteen alerts is tiny. A prior around the old rule's few-percent rate would pull the estimate well below 43% and leave it wide. Ask for a larger window, and whether the 14 were hand-picked.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Comparing takers with non-takers

      "Customers who used the feature churn less." Feature users are the engaged ones.

      Spot itAsk how people ended up in each group. If they chose, or a person or model chose, it is not fair.
    • Adjusting for everything

      Controlling for a mediator, collider or post-treatment variable and reading the coefficient as the effect.

      Spot itFor each control: was it fixed before treatment, and could treatment or outcome have caused it?
    • A causal claim from a predictive model

      "Feature importance says the call centre contact reduces churn." Importance describes the model, not the world.

      Spot itAny "if we change X then Y" claim from an importance chart needs an experiment or a proper causal design.
    • No overlap, or untested parallel trends

      Comparison groups nothing like the treated, or a DiD with one pre-period that visibly diverges.

      Spot itPlot propensity histograms by group. Ask for several pre-launch months and what else changed at launch.
    • Too few clusters

      Two regions, thousands of customers, a tiny p-value. The independent units are the regions.

      Spot itAsk what the unit of assignment was and whether standard errors account for it.
    • Priors chosen to get the answer, or a posterior mistaken for proof

      A confident prior from an enthusiastic sponsor, or a neat credible interval around an effect from confounded data.

      Spot itAsk for the prior and its source, and the answer under weaker and stronger priors. Then ask how the comparison was made.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. In Simulation 1 with the slider high, why is the naive lift far above the true 5 points?

    Takers skew towards engaged customers, who would have stayed anyway. The naive comparison credits the offer with that head start. Adjusting for engagement removes it.

    2. What does randomising assignment achieve that adjustment cannot?

    It makes treatment independent of every other factor, including ones you never measured. Adjustment handles only the confounders you included and got right.

    3. A DiD gives +4 points, but the treated group was already drifting down faster than the comparison before launch. Is the true effect bigger or smaller than 4?

    Bigger. If the faster fall would have continued, the DiD understated the effect. The correction extrapolates the trend, which is itself an assumption, so show the pre-period and be honest about it.

    4. You start with a prior worth 100 customers at 3%, then see 6 responses in 100 customers. Where does the posterior mean land, and why?

    Halfway, at 4.5%, because prior and data carry equal weight. With a smaller pilot it would sit nearer 3%; with a larger one, nearer the observed rate.

    5. Name a confounder, a mediator and a collider in "a fee waiver raises retention".

    Confounder: engagement (drives take-up and retention). Mediator: card or app usage, if the waiver works by raising it. Collider: a "high-value this quarter" flag that depends on both waiver and retention. Adjust for the first only.

    6. Why is a churn model a poor tool for choosing who to send a retention offer to?

    It ranks by likelihood to leave, not by how much the offer changes that. Some high-risk customers leave regardless; some low-risk ones are unaffected or irritated. Target uplift, estimated from experimental or well-adjusted data.

    7. A 95% confidence interval and a 95% credible interval both run from 2% to 7%. What is the difference in what each claims?

    Credible: given the prior and model, 95% probability the true rate is in the range. Confidence: the procedure captures the true value in 95% of repeated samples. With weak priors and plenty of data they are close.
