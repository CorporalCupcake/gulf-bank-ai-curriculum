    Causal Inference and Bayesian Thinking: Did It Work, and How Sure Are We?

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A school offers an optional revision app to 30,000 students. At the end of the year, 82% of the students who used it passed their exam, against 74% of those who did not, and the slide says the app lifted the pass rate by 8 points.

    Probably not. The students who chose the app are the motivated ones, who would have done well anyway, like gym members who are fitter before they join. Prediction asks what will happen given what I see; causation asks what would happen if I did something, which needs the same student without the app, a world you never see. Every method borrows a stand-in for it.

    Hold onto thisBefore accepting any "X caused Y" slide, ask: compared with what? Who would have ended up in each group anyway?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Potential outcomes: the missing world

    Each student has two potential outcomes, passing with the app and passing without it, but you only see one. That is the fundamental problem of causal inference: like a fork in the road, you only learn the path you took. So we estimate averages instead. The ATE is the average effect over everyone, the ATT is the average over those actually treated ("did it help the students who used it?"), and effects for sub-groups ask "for whom does it work?".

    The naive gap equals the true effect plus selection bias, how different the treated would have been anyway. Randomisation removes it by construction; other methods try without.

    One hidden assumption is no interference: one customer's treatment should not change another's outcome. It fails if students share the app with friends. In banking it fails with referrals, fraud rings and bank runs.

    Confounders, colliders and the causal graph

    A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Motivation drives both app use and passing, so they correlate even at zero effect. In banking the same role is played by customer engagement. Compare within levels of it, as the adjust switch did.

    Wrong adjustment creates bias. Three traps:

    • Collider. Caused by both treatment and outcome, like a "placed in the top set" flag, which depends on both app use and exam results. Analysing only flagged students creates a spurious link.
    • Mediator. A step on the causal path. If the app works by getting students to practise past papers, adjusting for practice removes the very effect you wanted.
    • Post-treatment variable. Anything measured after the app was introduced. Control only for things fixed before it.

    "Throw every variable in" is fine for prediction but unsafe for causal questions. Sketch the causal graph with the business team first.

    Adjustment, matching and propensity scores

    If you measured all the confounders, every method here shares one idea: make treated and untreated look alike, then compare.

    • Regression adjustment. Model the outcome from treatment plus covariates; the treatment coefficient is the adjusted effect. Simple and transparent, but it relies on the model form being right.
    • Propensity scores. Model each customer's probability of being treated, then compare people with equal propensity, by matching or by weighting. In weighting, a rare type of treated customer speaks for many similar untreated ones, so counts more.
    • Doubly robust and ML versions. Combine an outcome model with a propensity model, so the answer survives if either is right (double ML, causal forests). They also show how the effect varies by customer, the basis of uplift modelling.

    Two catches. Overlap: if some customers are almost never treated, there is nothing to compare them with and weights explode. If the propensity histograms for the two groups barely touch, the data cannot answer the question for those customers. And no method fixes an unmeasured confounder. Sensitivity analysis asks how strong a hidden factor would need to be to erase the effect, which tells you how much to worry.

    Randomisation and natural experiments

    When you choose who is treated, randomise: a coin flip is independent of everything, including what you never measured. Hold back a random 5-10% of any campaign, limit increase or collections script (F3.6, R8.3) and analyse by assignment, not by who took it up, or selection returns. Otherwise look for a natural experiment, something outside customers' control that changed who got treated:

    • Difference-in-differences. A policy hits one region, town or branch, not another; compare the change in each. Needs parallel trends: without treatment, both would have moved alike.
    • Regression discontinuity. A cut-off rule (score 600 gets the product, 599 does not). Customers either side are alike except for treatment, so it is close to a local experiment. The effect holds only near the cut-off, and it breaks if people can game the score.
    • Synthetic control. Blend untreated units that tracked the treated one before the change. Useful with one treated unit, such as a single branch or country. See R8.5.

    Before-after credits the launch with everything that changed, including the background decline. DiD uses the comparison town to remove that background, trading one assumption for another (parallel trends), which you can partly check in the pre-launch months.

    Bayesian thinking: prior, data, posterior

    Causal methods make the comparison fair; Bayesian thinking says how sure you are of its number. You start with what you believed (the prior), update with data, and end with a range of plausible values (the posterior). Think of the prior as imaginary earlier trials: a 3% prior "worth 100 leaflets" blends with a pilot like 100 more observations. With little data the prior dominates, with lots the data does.

    Why it matters at a bank:

    • Small samples. A 200-customer pilot, 40 AML cases. You get an honestly wide distribution, and extreme rates shrink toward a sensible centre instead of reporting 0 of 12 as "0%".
    • Direct decision statements. "88% probability the response rate beats break-even" is what the business wants. A credible interval says this, given your prior and model. A classical confidence interval instead describes how often the procedure captures the truth over repeated samples. With weak priors and plenty of data the two are close.
    • Sequential decisions. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, the engine of bandits.

    The prior is a choice: state it, justify it from past campaigns, and show how the answer moves under a weaker and a stronger one. A posterior is also only as causal as the comparison behind it. Run Simulation 1 through a Bayesian model and you get a well-calibrated posterior around the wrong lift. So ask is the comparison fair? first, then how sure are we?

    Can you claim causation? Four worked cases

    Decide, then open the answer.

    Case A. An RM team calls 5,000 clients; their deposits rose 12% next quarter versus 4% for uncalled clients. "Calls drive deposits."

    Not yet. RMs call promising clients, so selection is built in. Adjust for prior balances and tenure, or randomise who is called next quarter.

    Case B. A credit-limit policy applies to Dubai only from 1 March. A colleague compares March spend in Dubai and Abu Dhabi.

    That mixes the policy with permanent emirate differences. Use DiD on the change from earlier months, and check pre-trends.

    Case C. A model predicts churn well. The business wants to send the retention offer to the customers with the highest churn score.

    That targets who will leave, not who can be persuaded to stay. The causal quantity is uplift: the change in staying probability with versus without the offer. Start with a randomised holdout on the next offer.

    Case D. Of 14 AML alerts from a new rule on a niche corridor, 6 were confirmed. The owner says "43% hit rate, ten times better than the old rule's 4%".

    Fourteen alerts is tiny. A prior around the old rule's few-percent rate would pull the estimate well below 43% and leave it wide. Ask for a larger window, and whether the 14 were hand-picked.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Answering "what if we do this?" Prediction models describe patterns; causal designs compare treated customers with a credible stand-in for the same customers untreated, so the number is about the action, not the correlation.
    • Randomised holdouts need no modelling. A coin flip balances measured and unmeasured factors alike, so a simple difference in means is an unbiased answer and easy to defend to a risk committee.
    • Natural experiments use data you already have. When a policy hits one emirate or a cut-off rule splits customers, DiD and regression discontinuity give credible effects without running a new test.
    • Bayesian methods stay honest with small data. The prior regularises noisy rates, and the posterior gives a direct probability of beating break-even, which suits go or no-go decisions on pilots.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • Unmeasured confounding. If the thing that drove selection is not in your data, no amount of adjustment fixes it. Sensitivity analysis only tells you how worried to be.
    • No overlap. If nearly everyone with some profile gets treated, there is no comparison group for them, and the estimate is extrapolation.
    • Untestable assumptions. Parallel trends, no interference and "no hidden confounder" can be probed but never proved.
    • Priors used to steer. A strong prior on a tiny sample mostly returns your opinion. Bayesian output on confounded data is a neat interval around a biased number.
    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Randomised holdoutYou control who is treated; stakes justify a clean answerWithholding is unethical or regulated, or the volume is too small to detect the effect
    Adjustment / propensity scoresTreatment already happened; you measured the main drivers of selection; groups overlapSelection depends on things you cannot observe, such as RM judgement
    Difference-in-differencesA policy hit some units not others, and you have several pre-launch periodsPre-trends diverge, or something else changed in the treated unit
    Regression discontinuityA sharp cut-off decides treatment and customers cannot game itYou need the effect far from the cut-off, or there are few customers near it
    Bayesian estimationSmall samples, sequential decisions, or you need "probability it beats break-even"There is no defensible prior and plenty of data, where classical methods suffice

    In practice. Try randomising first: even a 5% holdout on every campaign builds a trusted evidence base. If you cannot, write down the selection story, draw the graph, and choose adjustment or a natural experiment accordingly. The knobs that matter are the control set, the overlap check, the number of pre-periods in DiD, and the prior. Detecting a few points of lift on a retention rate usually needs thousands per arm; regression discontinuity needs far more. Compute is rarely the constraint; careful thinking is.

    In banking you will see this in campaign and offer measurement, pricing and limit-policy evaluation, branch or policy roll-outs judged by DiD, uplift targeting for retention, and pilot decisions for new AML rules or fraud controls using Bayesian rates.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Comparing takers with non-takers

      App users pass more because they were already the motivated ones. In banking, feature users churn less because they were already the engaged ones.

      Spot itAsk how people ended up in each group. If they or a model chose, it is not fair.
    • Adjusting for everything

      Controlling for a mediator, collider or post-treatment variable.

      Spot itFor each control, ask whether it was fixed before treatment.
    • A causal claim from a predictive model

      Feature importance describes the model, not the world.

      Spot itAny "if we change X then Y" claim needs an experiment or causal design.
    • No overlap, or untested parallel trends

      Comparison groups nothing like the treated, or a DiD with one pre-period that visibly diverges.

      Spot itPlot propensity histograms by group. Ask for several pre-launch months and what else changed at launch.
    • Too few clusters

      Two towns, thousands of households, a tiny p-value. The independent units are the towns.

      Spot itAsk what the unit of assignment was and whether standard errors account for it.
    • Priors chosen to get the answer, or a posterior mistaken for proof

      A neat credible interval around an effect from confounded data.

      Spot itAsk for the prior and its source, then how the comparison was made.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. In Simulation 1, why is the naive lift far above the true 5 points?

    App users skew towards motivated students, who would have passed anyway. Adjusting for motivation removes that head start.

    2. What does randomising achieve that adjustment cannot?

    It makes treatment independent of every factor, including ones you never measured. Adjustment only handles confounders you included and got right.

    3. A DiD gives +4 points, but the treated group was already drifting down faster before launch. Is the true effect bigger or smaller?

    Bigger, since that faster fall would probably have continued. Correcting for it is itself an assumption, so show the pre-period.

    4. Name a confounder, a mediator and a collider in "a revision app raises exam pass rates".

    Confounder: motivation. Mediator: practice on past papers, if the app works through it. Collider: a "top set" flag caused by both app use and results. Adjust for the first only. In a banking offer, the equivalents are engagement, card usage and a "high-value" flag.

    5. Why is a churn model a poor way to choose who gets a retention offer?

    It ranks who will leave, not who the offer will change. Target uplift, estimated from experimental or well-adjusted data.

    6. You can randomise a new limit-increase policy across 200,000 customers, but a colleague suggests comparing past takers instead because it is quicker. Which would you choose and why?

    Randomise, with a small holdout. Past takers are self-selected, and an unmeasured driver such as an RM's judgement cannot be adjusted away. Adjustment is the fallback when the action has already happened.

    7. A new fraud rule fires on 20 transactions and 5 are confirmed. Would you report 25%, or use a Bayesian estimate with a prior from the old rule? Why?

    Use a prior from the old rule, and show the sensitivity. Twenty cases is too few to trust 25%, and the prior shrinks it toward a plausible rate with an honestly wide interval. State the prior and its source, and drop it if the history is not comparable.
