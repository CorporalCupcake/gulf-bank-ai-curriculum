    Causal Inference and Bayesian Thinking: Did It Work, and How Sure Are We?

    Why "customers who got the offer stayed more" proves nothing, how confounding, randomisation, adjustment and difference-in-differences each try to recover the effect, and how Bayesian reasoning lets you say something honest from a small pilot.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA causal claim is a comparison with what would have happened otherwise, which you never observe, so every method is an argument for why some group stands in for that missing world; Bayesian thinking is the matching habit of stating what you believed before the data, how far the data moved you, and how much doubt remains.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank sends a fee-waiver offer to 30,000 customers. A year later, 82% of those who took it up are still active, against 74% of those who did not. The slide says: "The campaign lifted retention by 8 points."

    Who took up the offer? Customers who open the app and care about their account, who were likely to stay anyway. The 8 points mix what the offer did with what kind of customer says yes. Compare it to a gym: members who attend often are fitter, but the gym did not necessarily make them so.

    Prediction asks "what will happen, given what I see?" Causation asks "what would happen if I did something?" The second needs this same customer without the offer, a world you never see. Every causal method borrows a stand-in for it and argues the stand-in is fair.

    • Correlation answers a different question. The 8 points truly describe who stayed. They just do not isolate the offer.
    • Every causal estimate rests on an assumption you cannot fully test. "Engagement is the only thing driving both take-up and staying" is one. Make it plausible and check what you can.
    • Small data needs prior knowledge stated openly. A pilot on 200 customers cannot speak for itself. Bayesian reasoning combines the pilot with what you believed before, and shows both.
    Hold onto thisBefore accepting any "X caused Y" slide, ask: compared with what? Who would have ended up in each group anyway? What must be true for this comparison to be fair?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Potential outcomes: the missing world

    Give each customer two potential outcomes: Y(1), whether they stay if offered, and Y(0), whether they stay if not. Their causal effect is the difference, but you only ever see one. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference (Holland, 1986). Like a fork in the road, you only learn the path you took. So we target averages:

    ATE=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]

    ATE is the average effect over everyone. The ATT averages over only the treated ("did it help the people we contacted?"), often what a campaign review wants. The CATE conditions on customer features ("for whom does it work?"). They differ when effects vary across people.

    The naive treated-minus-untreated gap equals the true effect plus selection bias: how different the treated would have been even without treatment. In Simulation 1 it is the engaged customers' extra 30 points of baseline retention, weighted by their larger share among takers. Randomisation sets selection bias to zero by construction. Every other method tries to remove it without randomising.

    Two hidden assumptions. No interference: one customer's treatment does not change another's outcome (fails with referrals, fraud rings, bank runs). Consistency: "treated" means one well-defined thing, so an SMS to some and a call to others is not one treatment.

    Confounders, colliders and the causal graph

    A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Engagement drives both take-up and staying, so it opens a "back door" path and correlation flows even at zero effect. Close it by comparing within levels of the confounder (engaged takers with engaged non-takers, then average), which is what the "adjust" switch did. On a causal graph (a DAG, from Pearl), the rule is mechanical: adjust for variables that block every back-door path and open no new ones.

    The wrong adjustment creates bias:

    • Collider. Caused by both treatment and outcome. Analysing only customers flagged "high-value this quarter", when the flag depends on both the offer and staying, creates a spurious link. Selection into a dataset is a collider too: a credit model trained only on approved applicants (F2.6).
    • Mediator. A step on the causal path. If the offer works by raising card usage, adjusting for usage removes the effect you wanted.
    • Post-treatment variable. Anything measured after the offer. Control only for things fixed before treatment, as with point-in-time correctness (F3.1).

    So "throw every variable in" is fine for prediction but unsafe for causal questions. Which variables to include is a question about the world, not about fit.

    Adjustment, matching and propensity scores

    If you measured all the confounders (unconfoundedness), the methods share one idea: make treated and untreated look alike, then compare.

    • Regression adjustment. Model the outcome from treatment and covariates; the treatment coefficient is the adjusted effect (F2.5). Transparent, but only right if the model form is.
    • Matching and stratification. Pair each treated customer with similar untreated ones, or compare within strata. Easy to show a sceptic.
    • Propensity scores. Model the probability of treatment given covariates, e(x). Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) showed that comparing people with equal propensity removes all measured confounding. Match on it, or weight by it: inverse probability weighting gives each treated customer weight 1/e.
    ATE≈1n∑TiYie(xi)−(1−Ti)Yi1−e(xi)

    A rare type of treated customer speaks for many similar customers who were not treated, so counts more. In Simulation 1 an unengaged taker has e = 0.2 and counts five times. Untreated outcomes are up-weighted when treatment was likely.

    Two catches. Overlap (positivity): if some customers are almost never treated, there is nothing to compare them with and weights explode. Plot the propensity score by group; if the histograms barely touch, the data cannot answer the question for those customers. And no method fixes an unmeasured confounder. Sensitivity analysis (E-values: how strong would a hidden factor need to be to erase the effect?) turns "maybe there is a hidden factor" into a quantity a reviewer can weigh.

    Double (debiased) machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) uses ML to predict both outcome and treatment from covariates, then regresses leftover outcome on leftover treatment. With cross-fitting, you can use gradient boosting and still get a valid confidence interval. Meta-learners and causal forests extend this to how the effect varies by customer, the basis of uplift modelling.

    Randomisation and natural experiments

    When you choose who gets treated, randomise. A coin flip is independent of everything, including factors you never measured, which no adjustment can match. At a bank, hold back a random 5–10% from a campaign, limit increase or collections script. Design is in F3.6, modern variants in R8.3. Randomise what you actually decide (the offer) and analyse by assignment (intention to treat), not by who took it up, or selection returns.

    When you cannot randomise, find a natural experiment: something outside customers' control that changed who got treated.

    • Difference-in-differences (DiD). A policy hits one branch or emirate, not another. Compare the change in each. Fixed group differences and shared shocks cancel. Needs parallel trends: without treatment, both would have moved alike.
    • Regression discontinuity. A cut-off rule: score 600 gets the product, 599 does not. Customers either side are alike except for treatment. The effect holds only near the cut-off.
    • Instrumental variables. Something that nudges treatment but affects the outcome only through it, such as agents who randomly differ in how often they offer a product. Gives the effect for "compliers", a narrower group than everyone.
    • Synthetic control. One treated unit (one product in one market): build a weighted blend of untreated units that tracked it before the change. See R8.5.

    The before-after number credits the launch with everything that changed, including the background decline. DiD removes that background using the comparison region, trading one assumption (nothing else changed in the treated region at month 6) for another (parallel trends), which you can partly inspect in the pre-launch months. When groups are treated at different times, the simple two-way regression can mislead; newer estimators (Callaway and Sant'Anna; Goodman-Bacon) compare each group only with not-yet-treated or never-treated groups.

    Bayesian thinking: prior, data, posterior

    Causal methods make the comparison fair. Bayesian thinking says how sure you are of the number it gave. Like a weather forecaster who starts from the seasonal norm and updates with today's radar, you start with what you believed, update with data, and end with a distribution (Bayes' rule, F1.4, applied to a parameter).

    p(θ∣data)∝p(data∣θ)·p(θ)

    θ is the unknown, say a response rate. The prior p(θ) is which values were plausible beforehand. The likelihood p(data | θ) is how well each value explains what we saw (F1.6). Their rescaled product is the posterior: updated belief as a full distribution.

    For a rate, a Beta(a, b) prior plus k responses in n trials gives Beta(a + k, b + n − k). Read a and b as imaginary earlier responses and non-responses: a 3% prior worth 100 customers is Beta(3, 97). The posterior mean is an evidence-weighted average of prior mean and observed rate. Little data: the prior dominates. Lots: the data does.

    Why it matters at a bank:

    • Small samples. A 200-customer pilot, 40 AML cases, 80 loans in a new segment. You get an honestly wide distribution, and extreme rates shrink toward a sensible centre instead of reporting 0 of 12 as "0%".
    • Direct decision statements. "88% probability the response rate beats our 3% break-even" is what the business wants. A 95% confidence interval does not say that; it describes the procedure's long-run behaviour. A credible interval does, given your prior and model.
    • Hierarchies. With 60 branches, thin ones borrow strength from the group (partial pooling), beating both separate and fully pooled estimates. It is the shrinkage idea of regularisation (F4.1).
    • Sequential decisions. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, the engine of Thompson sampling in bandits.

    Two cautions. The prior is a choice: state it, justify it (past campaigns), and show how the answer moves when it changes. And a posterior is only as causal as the comparison behind it. Run Simulation 1 through a Bayesian model and you get a well-calibrated posterior around the wrong lift. Ask is the comparison fair? first, then how sure are we of its number?

    Beyond one rate, write the model in Stan or PyMC, which sample the posterior by MCMC. Follow the Bayesian workflow (Gelman et al., 2020): prior predictive check, fit, check the sampler (R-hat near 1, no divergences, enough effective samples), posterior predictive check.

    Can you claim causation? Four worked cases

    The meeting skill is naming what is wrong or missing. Decide, then open the answer.

    Case A. A wealth-management RM team calls 5,000 clients; their deposits rose 12% in the next quarter versus 4% for uncalled clients. "Calls drive deposits."

    Not yet. RMs call promising or familiar clients, so selection is built in. Ask how the 5,000 were chosen. If a score cut-off picked them, use regression discontinuity. Otherwise adjust for pre-period balances, tenure and holdings, check overlap, and caveat unmeasured relationship strength. Best: randomise who is called next quarter.

    Case B. A new credit-limit policy applied to Dubai branches only from 1 March. Spend per card rose; Abu Dhabi did not change. A colleague compares March spend in the two emirates.

    The gap mixes the policy with permanent emirate differences. Use DiD on the change from earlier months, with at least six pre-months plotted for parallel trends. Check nothing else hit Dubai in March (Ramadan timing, Dubai Shopping Festival). Cluster standard errors by branch or region, and note two regions give little independent evidence.

    Case C. A model predicts churn well. The business wants to "target the customers with the highest churn score with a retention offer".

    That targets who will leave, not who can be persuaded to stay. High-risk customers may be lost causes; some low-risk ones may be annoyed by the offer. The causal quantity is uplift, the change in staying probability with versus without the offer. Start with a randomised holdout on the next offer and fit uplift models to it.

    Case D. Of 14 AML alerts from a new rule on a niche corridor, 6 were confirmed. The rule's owner says "43% hit rate, ten times better than the old rule's 4%."

    Fourteen alerts is tiny. With a prior around the old rule's few-percent rate worth even 30 alerts, the posterior is far below 43% and wide. Ask for a larger window and a comparable-period comparison. Ask whether the 14 were the first batch or hand-picked, and whether the customer mix differs from the old rule's.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Comparing takers with non-takers

      "Customers who used the feature churn less." Feature users are the engaged ones.

      Spot itAsk how people ended up in each group. If they chose, or a person or model chose, it is not fair.
    • Adjusting for everything

      Controlling for a mediator, collider or post-treatment variable and reading the coefficient as the effect.

      Spot itFor each control: was it fixed before treatment, and could treatment or outcome have caused it? Draw the graph.
    • A causal claim from a predictive model

      "Feature importance says the call centre contact reduces churn." Importance and SHAP describe the model, not the world.

      Spot itAny "if we change X then Y" claim from an importance chart needs an experiment or explicit causal design.
    • No overlap

      Weights of 200 on a few customers, or comparison groups nothing like the treated.

      Spot itPlot propensity histograms by group and the largest weights. Trim or restrict the population, and say so.
    • Untested parallel trends

      A DiD with one pre-period, or a pre-period that visibly diverges.

      Spot itAsk for several pre-launch months, and what else changed in the treated group at launch.
    • Too few clusters

      Two regions, thousands of customers, a tiny p-value. The independent units are the regions.

      Spot itAsk what the unit of assignment was and whether standard errors account for it.
    • Priors chosen to get the answer

      A confident prior from an enthusiastic sponsor, or "uninformative" priors that are not (flat on the wrong scale).

      Spot itAsk for the prior, its source, the posterior under weaker and stronger priors, and a prior predictive check.
    • Treating a posterior as proof of cause

      A neat credible interval around an effect from confounded data.

      Spot itSeparate the questions: how was the comparison made, then how uncertain is the estimate?
    • Unconverged sampling

      MCMC results reported without diagnostics.

      Spot itAsk for R-hat, effective sample size, divergence count and trace plots.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. In Simulation 1 with the slider at 60%, take-up is 20% among the less engaged and 56% among the engaged. Roughly why is the naive lift well above 5 points?

    Takers skew engaged: 22,400 of 34,400 (65%) against 40% of the population. Engaged customers stay 30 points more anyway, so takers start about 0.25 × 30 ≈ 7.5 points ahead. Naive lift is roughly 5 + 11.5 = 16.5 points (the exact figure shown).

    2. What does randomising assignment achieve that adjustment cannot?

    It makes treatment independent of every other factor, including ones you never measured. Adjustment handles only the confounders you included and got right.

    3. A DiD gives +4 points. The treated group was falling 0.3 points a month faster than the comparison group before launch. The post-period averages six months later than the pre-period. What might the true effect be?

    The continuing faster fall contributes −0.3 × 6 = −1.8 points, so the true effect is about 4 + 1.8 = 5.8 points. The estimate is biased, and the correction extrapolates the trend, itself an assumption.

    4. You use a Beta(3, 97) prior for a response rate, then see 6 responses in 100 customers. What is the posterior and its mean?

    Beta(3 + 6, 97 + 94) = Beta(9, 191). Mean = 9 ÷ 200 = 4.5%, halfway between prior (3%) and observed (6%) because each is worth 100 customers.

    5. Name a confounder, a mediator and a collider in "a fee waiver raises retention".

    Confounder: engagement (drives take-up and retention). Mediator: card or app usage, if the waiver works by raising it. Collider: a "high-value this quarter" flag that depends on both waiver and retention. Adjust for the first only.

    6. Why is a churn model a poor tool for choosing who to send a retention offer to?

    It ranks by likelihood to leave, not by how much the offer changes that. Some high-risk customers leave regardless; some low-risk ones are unaffected or irritated. Target uplift, estimated from experimental or well-adjusted data.

    7. A 95% confidence interval and a 95% credible interval both run from 2% to 7%. What is the difference in what each claims?

    Credible: given the prior and model, 95% probability the true rate is in the range. Confidence: the procedure captures the true value in 95% of repeated samples, with no probability statement about this interval. With weak priors and plenty of data they are close.
