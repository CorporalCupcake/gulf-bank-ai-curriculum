    Causal Inference: Did It Actually Work?

    Part 1

    What causal inference is

    In one sentenceCausal inference is working out what an action actually changed, by comparing what happened with what would have happened without it.

    A school offers an optional revision app. At year end, 82% of students who used it passed, against 74% of those who did not. The slide says "the app lifted the pass rate by 8 points". Is that true?

    Probably not. The students who chose the app are the motivated ones, who would have done well anyway. It's like gym members being fitter before they join.

    Motivation is a confounder: a hidden common cause of both the "treatment" (using the app) and the outcome (passing). It makes the two move together even if the app does nothing.

    How it differs from prediction

    • Prediction asks "what will happen, given what I can see?". Users pass more, so use is a fine predictor.
    • Causation asks "what would happen if I did something?". That needs the same student with and without the app, and you never see both.
    • A/B testing is the cleanest causal method, but only when you control who gets the treatment. The other methods here are for when you couldn't.
    Part 2

    How to estimate an effect

    Every method does the same thing: find a fair stand-in for the missing world. In practice you follow these steps.

    1. State the question. "What did the app do to the pass rate of students who used it?"
    2. Write down who got treated and why. Here, motivated students chose the app. Sketch a simple arrow diagram like the one above with the business team.
    3. Pick a stand-in. Randomise, adjust for what you measured, or use a natural experiment (below).
    4. Compare treated with the stand-in and report the difference with a range, not a single number.
    5. Stress-test it. Ask what hidden factor would have to exist to erase the result.

    The main stand-ins

    • Randomised holdout. Flip a coin to decide who gets the app. A coin is unrelated to motivation or anything else, so the groups are alike and the plain gap is the effect. The best option when you can use it: hold back a random 5-10% of any campaign.
    • Adjustment. Compare motivated students with motivated students, and the rest with the rest. Averaging within groups removes the head start. It only works for factors you measured.
    • Propensity scores. Model each student's chance of using the app, then compare students with the same chance. It's adjustment squeezed into one number.
    • Difference-in-differences. A change hits one group but not a similar one. Compare how much each group changed, so the background trend cancels out.
    • Regression discontinuity. A cut-off decides treatment, such as score 600 gets the product and 599 does not. People just either side are nearly identical, so it is close to a local experiment.

    Both towns drift down for reasons that have nothing to do with the scheme. The dashed line assumes the scheme town would have drifted just as the comparison town did. The gap between the dashed line and reality is the effect. This needs parallel trends: before the scheme, the two lines must have moved alike. Check the months before launch.

    Part 3

    How to read a causal claim

    Before accepting "X caused Y", ask compared with what? Who would have ended up in each group anyway?

    • Plain gap, 8 points. Includes the motivated students' head start. In the school example, motivated students pass 90% of the time and others 60%, with or without the app.
    • Adjusted gap, 5 points. Compares like with like. This is the app's own contribution.
    • A range, not a point. "5 points, give or take 2" is a usable answer. "5 points" alone hides how unsure you are.

    Variables not to adjust for

    "Control for everything" is fine for prediction and wrong for causation. Three traps:

    • Mediator. A step on the causal path. If the app works by getting students to practise past papers, adjusting for practice removes the very effect you want.
    • Collider. Something caused by both treatment and outcome, such as a "top set" flag that depends on app use and exam results. Analysing only flagged students invents a link.
    • After-the-fact variable. Anything measured after treatment started. Control only for things fixed before it.

    Two more cautions. Overlap: if nearly everyone with some profile is treated, there is nobody to compare them with. Unmeasured confounders: if the thing that drove selection is not in your data, no method fixes it.

    Part 4

    Which method to use

    MethodUse it whenAvoid it when
    Randomised holdoutYou control who is treatedWithholding is unethical or regulated, or numbers are too small
    Adjustment / propensityIt already happened and you measured the main reasons for selectionSelection depends on things you can't see, such as a manager's judgement
    Difference-in-differencesA change hit some units, not others, and you have several earlier periodsThe lines were already diverging, or something else changed too
    Regression discontinuityA sharp cut-off decides treatment and nobody can game itYou need the effect far from the cut-off

    Causal methods are strong at:

    • Answering "what if we do this?" The number is about the action, not about a correlation.
    • Being defensible. A randomised holdout needs no modelling, so it's easy to explain to a risk committee.

    They are weak at:

    • Hidden assumptions. Parallel trends and "no hidden confounder" can be probed, never proved.
    • Small effects. Detecting a few points of lift usually needs thousands of people per group.

    In practice: try to randomise first. If you can't, write down who got treated and why, then choose adjustment or a natural experiment to match.

    Show the formula

    Plain gap = true effect + selection bias. Selection bias is how different the treated group would have been anyway. Randomising removes it by design. The other methods try to estimate and subtract it.

    In banking, this shows up when measuring campaigns, credit-limit changes and branch roll-outs, and above all when targeting retention offers at customers who can be persuaded rather than those most likely to leave (called uplift modelling).

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Comparing takers with non-takers

      People who opt in differ from those who don't. The gap is partly who they are, not what you did.

    • Controlling for everything

      Adjusting for mediators or colliders creates bias instead of removing it.

    • Mistaking a good prediction for an effect

      A churn model finds who will leave, not who an offer will keep. The target customer is the one whose behaviour changes because of the offer.

    • Trusting a clever model on a flawed comparison

      A sophisticated model around an unfair comparison gives a precise answer to the wrong question.

    1. Relationship managers call 5,000 clients and their deposits rise 12%, against 4% for the others. Do calls drive deposits?

    Not yet. Managers call promising clients, so selection is built in. Adjust for prior balances and tenure, or randomise who is called next quarter.

    2. Why does a coin flip fix confounding?

    A coin is unrelated to everything, measured or not, so the two groups end up alike apart from the treatment.

    3. A credit-limit policy hits Dubai only. Is comparing Dubai with Abu Dhabi afterwards enough?

    No. It mixes the policy with permanent differences between the emirates. Compare each one's change from earlier months, and check the earlier trends matched.

    4. What is a mediator, and why not adjust for it?

    A step on the path from treatment to outcome. Adjusting for it removes part of the effect you are trying to measure.

    5. What can no adjustment method fix?

    An unmeasured confounder. If the reason for selection isn't in your data, you can only judge how worried to be.
