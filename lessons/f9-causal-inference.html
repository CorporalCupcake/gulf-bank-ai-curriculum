    Causal Inference and Bayesian Thinking: Did It Work, and How Sure Are We?

    Why "customers who got the offer stayed more" proves nothing, how confounding, randomisation, adjustment and difference-in-differences each try to recover the effect, and how Bayesian reasoning lets you say something honest from a small pilot.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA causal claim is a comparison with what would have happened otherwise, which you never observe, so every method is an argument for why some group stands in for that missing world; Bayesian thinking is the matching habit of stating what you believed before the data, how far the data moved you, and how much doubt remains.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The retention team at a Gulf bank sends a fee-waiver offer to 30,000 customers. A year later, 82% of those who took it up are still active, against 74% of those who did not. The slide says: "The campaign lifted retention by 8 points."

    Who took up the offer? Customers who opened the app, read the message and cared about their account. Those people were more likely to stay anyway. The 8 points mix two things: what the offer did, and what kind of customer says yes to offers. Prediction models are happy with that mix, since "took the offer" is a fine predictor of staying. A decision about whether to run the campaign again is not happy with it at all.

    This is the gap between prediction and causation. Prediction asks "what will happen, given what I see?" Causation asks "what would happen if I did something?" The second needs a comparison with a world that did not occur: this same customer, without the offer. You can never see both worlds for one person. Every causal method is a way of borrowing a stand-in for the missing one, and an argument about why the stand-in is fair.

    Three ideas carry the rest of the lesson.

    • Correlation is not wrong, it is answering a different question. The 8 points are a true description of who stayed. They just do not isolate the offer.
    • A causal estimate always rests on an assumption you cannot test fully. "Engagement is the only thing that drives both take-up and staying" is an assumption. The best you can do is make it plausible, and check what you can.
    • Small data needs a way to say what you already knew. A pilot on 200 customers cannot speak for itself. Bayesian reasoning is the discipline of combining what the pilot says with what you reasonably believed before, and showing both.
    Hold onto thisBefore accepting any "X caused Y" slide, ask: compared with what? Who would have ended up in each group anyway, and why? What would have to be true for this comparison to be fair? The method matters less than whether you can answer those three.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Potential outcomes: the missing world

    Give each customer two potential outcomes: Y(1), whether they stay if offered, and Y(0), whether they stay if not. The causal effect for that person is the difference. We see only one of the two, whichever matches what actually happened. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference (Holland, 1986). The effects we can aim at are averages:

    ATE=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]

    The average treatment effect over everyone. The ATT is the same average over only those who were treated, which is often what a campaign review really wants ("did it help the people we contacted?"). The CATE conditions on customer features, and answers "for whom does it work?". These are different numbers when the effect varies across people.

    The naive comparison, mean outcome of treated minus mean outcome of untreated, equals the true effect plus selection bias: how different the treated would have been from the untreated even without treatment. In Simulation 1 the selection bias is the engaged customers' extra 30 points of baseline retention, spread over the larger share of them among takers. Randomisation sets selection bias to zero by construction, which is why it is the gold standard. Everything else in this section is an attempt to remove selection bias without randomising.

    Two assumptions are hidden in all of this. No interference: one customer's treatment does not change another's outcome (fails with referral schemes, fraud rings, bank runs, anything with network effects). Consistency: "treated" means one well-defined thing. A "campaign" that was an SMS to some and a call to others is not one treatment.

    Confounders, colliders and the causal graph

    A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome: engagement drives both take-up and staying. It opens a "back door" path between them, so correlation flows even with zero effect. You close it by comparing within levels of the confounder, which is what the "adjust" switch did: compare engaged takers with engaged non-takers, low with low, then average. With a graph (a directed acyclic graph, DAG, from Pearl's work), the rule is mechanical: adjust for a set of variables that blocks every back-door path and opens no new ones.

    The "opens no new ones" clause matters. Adjusting for the wrong variable can create bias:

    • Collider. A variable caused by both treatment and outcome. If you analyse only customers flagged "high-value this quarter" and that flag depends on both the offer and on staying, you create a spurious link between them. Selection into a dataset is a collider too: a credit model trained only on approved applicants sees a distorted world (the selection bias of F2.6).
    • Mediator. A step on the causal path. If the offer works by raising card usage, adjusting for card usage removes the very effect you wanted to measure.
    • Post-treatment variable. Anything measured after the offer. Control only for things fixed before treatment. This is the same discipline as point-in-time correctness (F3.1).

    This is why "throw every variable into the regression" is not a safe default for causal questions, however sensible it is for prediction. Which variables to include is a question about the world, not about fit.

    Adjustment, matching and propensity scores

    When you have measured the confounders and believe that is all of them (the unconfoundedness or "no hidden confounders" assumption), three families of method follow. They are variations on one idea: make treated and untreated look alike, then compare.

    • Regression adjustment. Model the outcome from treatment and covariates. The treatment coefficient is the adjusted effect (F2.5). Simple and transparent, but only right if the model form is.
    • Matching and stratification. Pair each treated customer with similar untreated ones, or compare within strata. Intuitive and easy to show a sceptic.
    • Propensity scores. Model the probability of being treated given covariates, e(x). Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) showed that comparing people with the same propensity is enough to remove all measured confounding. You can match on it or weight by it: inverse probability weighting gives each treated customer weight 1/e, so the treated group is reweighted to look like everyone.
    ATE≈1n∑TiYie(xi)−(1−Ti)Yi1−e(xi)

    Each treated outcome is up-weighted when that customer was unlikely to be treated (small e), since they speak for many similar customers who were not. Each untreated outcome is up-weighted when treatment was likely. In Simulation 1, an unengaged taker has e = 0.2 and counts five times.

    The catch is overlap (positivity). If some kinds of customer are almost never treated, there is nothing to compare them with, and weights explode. Always plot the propensity score for treated and untreated. If the histograms barely touch, the data cannot answer the question for those customers, whatever the method claims. The other catch is the one that no method fixes: a confounder you did not measure. Sensitivity analyses (such as E-values, or asking "how strong would a hidden factor need to be to erase this effect?") do not remove the worry, but they turn "maybe there is a hidden factor" into a quantified statement a reviewer can weigh.

    Modern practice combines flexible machine learning with these ideas. Double (debiased) machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) uses ML models to predict both the outcome and the treatment from covariates, then regresses the leftover outcome on the leftover treatment. It lets you use gradient boosting for the nuisance parts and still get a valid confidence interval for the effect, provided you use cross-fitting. Related "meta-learners" and causal forests estimate how the effect varies across customers, the basis of uplift modelling.

    Randomisation and natural experiments

    When you can choose who gets treated, randomise. A coin flip is independent of everything, including the factors you never thought to measure. That is the one thing no adjustment can offer. For a bank it is often feasible and cheap: randomly hold back a 5–10% control group from a campaign, a limit increase or a collections script. Design and analysis are in F3.6 and the modern variants in R8.3. Two points belong here. First, randomise the thing you actually decide, offering, and analyse by assignment (intention to treat), not by who took up the offer, or you reintroduce selection. Second, a control group is not a luxury: without it the lift in Simulation 1 cannot be separated from the engagement effect.

    When you cannot randomise, look for a natural experiment: something outside the customers' control that changed who got treated.

    • Difference-in-differences (DiD). A policy hits one branch, segment or emirate and not another. Compare the change in the treated group with the change in the comparison group. Fixed differences between groups cancel, and shared shocks (a holiday, rate move) cancel. It rests on parallel trends: without treatment, both groups would have moved alike.
    • Regression discontinuity. A rule with a cut-off: a credit score of 600 gets the product, 599 does not. Customers just either side are alike except for treatment. It estimates the effect only near the cut-off.
    • Instrumental variables. Something that nudges treatment but affects the outcome only through it, for example random assignment of call-centre agents who differ in how often they offer a product. It gives the effect for customers whose behaviour the nudge changes (the "compliers"), which is a narrower group than everyone.
    • Synthetic control. When only one unit is treated (one new product in one market), build a weighted blend of untreated units that tracks it before the change. See R8.5.

    Notice what the before-after number in the middle does wrong: it credits the launch with everything that changed, including the background decline. DiD removes the background by using the comparison region. It then trades one assumption (nothing else changed in the treated region at month 6) for another (parallel trends), which you can partly inspect by looking at the months before launch. Recent work shows that when different groups are treated at different times, the simple two-way regression version can mislead, and newer estimators fix this (Part 4).

    Bayesian thinking: prior, data, posterior

    Everything above is about the structure of a comparison. The Bayesian half of this lesson is about how to reason about a number once you have it. The idea is Bayes' rule (F1.4) applied to a parameter: start with what you believed, update with data, end with a distribution.

    p(θ∣data)∝p(data∣θ)·p(θ)

    θ is the unknown (say, a campaign response rate). The prior p(θ) says which values were plausible beforehand. The likelihood p(data | θ) says how well each value explains what we saw (F1.6). Their product, rescaled, is the posterior: your updated belief, a full distribution rather than a single estimate.

    The friendliest case is a rate. With a Beta(a, b) prior and k responses in n trials, the posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k). Read a and b as imaginary earlier responses and non-responses: a prior centred on 3% worth "100 customers of evidence" is Beta(3, 97). The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed rate, with weights proportional to their evidence. With little data the prior dominates; with a lot, the data does, and sensible priors agree.

    Why this matters at a bank:

    • Small, costly samples. A pilot of 200 customers, 40 confirmed AML cases, a new segment with 80 loans. The Bayesian answer to "what is the rate?" is a distribution that is honestly wide, and it shrinks extreme rates towards a sensible centre rather than reporting 0 out of 12 as "0%".
    • Direct decision statements. "There is an 88% probability that the response rate beats our 3% break-even" is what the business wants to hear. A 95% confidence interval does not say that: it describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not the probability of this claim. A Bayesian credible interval does say it, conditional on your prior and model.
    • Hierarchies. Estimate default rates for 60 branches: those with few loans borrow strength from the group (partial pooling), which beats both treating every branch alone and ignoring branch differences. This is the same shrinkage idea as regularisation (F4.1) and is how credit and pricing teams often work with thin data.
    • Sequential decisions. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. This is the engine of Thompson sampling in bandits.

    Two honest cautions. The prior is a choice, and a bad one can mislead, so state it, justify it (from past campaigns, say) and show how the answer moves when you change it. And "Bayesian" is not a magic way to get a causal answer: the posterior for an effect is only as causal as the comparison that produced the data. Run the Simulation 1 numbers through a Bayesian model and you get a beautifully calibrated posterior around the wrong lift. The two halves of this lesson answer different questions: is the comparison fair? and how sure are we of the number it gave?

    In practice you rarely do the algebra by hand. For anything beyond a single rate you write the model in a probabilistic programming tool such as Stan or PyMC, which samples the posterior by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). Then you follow the Bayesian workflow (Gelman et al., 2020): check what the prior implies before seeing data (prior predictive check), fit, check the sampler (R-hat near 1, no divergences, enough effective samples), and check the model against the data (posterior predictive check).

    Can you claim causation? Four worked cases

    The skill that gets tested in meetings is not the algebra. It is looking at an analysis and naming what is wrong or what is needed. Open each case, decide, then read the answer.

    Case A. A wealth-management RM team calls 5,000 clients; their deposits rose 12% in the next quarter versus 4% for uncalled clients. "Calls drive deposits."

    Not yet. RMs call clients who look promising or whom they know well, so selection is built in. Ask how the 5,000 were chosen. If the call list came from a score or a rule with a clear cut-off, a regression discontinuity around it is possible. Otherwise adjust for pre-period balances, tenure and product holdings, check overlap, and report an effect with an honest caveat about unmeasured relationship strength. Best: randomise which eligible clients are called next quarter.

    Case B. A new credit-limit policy applied to Dubai branches only from 1 March. Spend per card rose; Abu Dhabi did not change. A colleague compares March spend in the two emirates.

    The post-period gap mixes the policy with permanent differences between emirates. Use difference-in-differences: compare the change from the months before March in both. Plot at least six prior months to examine parallel trends. Check nothing else happened in Dubai in March (Ramadan timing, a merchant promotion, the Dubai Shopping Festival calendar). Cluster standard errors by branch or region and be wary that two regions give very little independent evidence.

    Case C. A model predicts churn well. The business wants to "target the customers with the highest churn score with a retention offer".

    That targets who is likely to leave, not who is likely to be persuaded to stay. High-risk customers may be lost causes, and some low-risk customers might be annoyed by the offer. The causal quantity is uplift: the difference in staying probability with and without the offer, which needs experimental or well-adjusted data on past offers. Start with a randomised holdout on the next offer and fit uplift models to that.

    Case D. Of 14 AML alerts from a new rule on a niche corridor, 6 were confirmed. The rule's owner says "43% hit rate, ten times better than the old rule's 4%."

    Fourteen alerts is a tiny sample. A Bayesian view helps: with a prior around the old rule's few-percent rate worth even 30 alerts of evidence, the posterior for the new rule is far below 43% and the interval is wide. That is not to dismiss the rule; it is to ask for a larger window and a comparable-period comparison. Also ask whether the 14 were the first batch or hand-picked, and whether the old rule's 4% covers a different customer mix. The comparison is not like with like.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Causal and Bayesian thinking touch most of the curriculum, because most business questions are really "what happens if we do this?" and "how sure are we?".

    F2.6 · Confounding, Simpson's paradox, selectionThe problem this lesson solves

    F2.6 shows how confounding and selection mislead. This lesson shows the tools: randomisation, adjustment, natural experiments.

    F3.6 · ExperimentsRandomise when you can

    A control group is the strongest causal tool a bank has. Design, power and write-up are there.

    F1.4 · F1.6 · Bayes and likelihoodThe engine of Bayesian updating

    Posterior is prior times likelihood. The base-rate logic of F1.4 is the same rule applied to a parameter.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · IntervalsConfidence versus credible

    Both express uncertainty. They answer slightly different questions, and with plentiful data and weak priors they almost coincide.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsAdjustment by regression

    A treatment coefficient is causal only if the controls are the right ones. Regularised regression is a Bayesian prior in disguise.

    B6.1 – B6.2 · Churn, propensity, upliftWho to treat

    Churn prediction is not uplift. Treatment-effect modelling needs the causal framing here.

    F9.7 · BanditsThompson sampling

    Sampling from a posterior to decide which offer to show next. The Bayesian update running live.

    R8.2 · R8.5 · FrontierDML, heterogeneous effects, synthetic control

    The research frontier of the methods introduced here: modern DiD and synthetic control, off-policy evaluation.

    C1.3 · BaselinesThe counterfactual, in business terms

    "What happens without the model?" is the counterfactual question. A baseline is the control group of an AI project.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Campaign and retention measurement. The headline case. Hold-out groups, attribution of lift, and refusing to credit the campaign for what engaged customers do anyway.
    • Pricing and limits. Did the new rate change cause the attrition, or did competitors move at the same time? Regression discontinuity and DiD are the usual tools.
    • Fraud and AML rule changes. Rule A replaced rule B and alerts fell. Was it better detection or a changed population? Small counts make Bayesian intervals useful.
    • Credit decisions. Reject inference is a causal missing-data problem: you never see how rejected applicants would have repaid. Be explicit about any assumption used to fill that gap.
    • Evaluating AI tools. Did the staff copilot cut handling time, or were the early adopters the fastest staff anyway? Same selection logic, new setting.
    • Islamic finance and Shariah-based products. New products usually launch to small, self-selected groups. The same cautions apply, with thin data that suits Bayesian treatment.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Comparing takers with non-takers

      "Customers who used the feature churn less." Customers who use features are the engaged ones.

      Spot itAsk how people ended up in each group. If they chose, or were chosen by a person or a model, it is not a fair comparison.
    • Adjusting for everything

      Controlling for a mediator or a collider, or for any post-treatment variable, and reading the coefficient as the effect.

      Spot itFor each control, ask: was it fixed before treatment, and could treatment or outcome have caused it? Draw the graph on a whiteboard.
    • A causal claim from a predictive model

      "The feature importance says the call centre contact reduces churn." Importance and SHAP values describe the model, not the world.

      Spot itAny "if we change X then Y will..." claim from a feature-importance chart needs an experiment or an explicit causal design behind it.
    • No overlap

      Weights of 200 on a handful of customers, or comparison groups that look nothing like the treated.

      Spot itPlot propensity histograms by group and the largest weights. Trim or restrict the population, and say so.
    • Untested parallel trends

      A DiD with one pre-period, or a pre-period that visibly diverges.

      Spot itAsk to see at least several pre-launch months. Check what else changed in the treated group at launch.
    • Too few clusters

      Two regions, thousands of customers, and a tiny p-value. The independent units are the regions.

      Spot itAsk what the unit of treatment assignment was, and whether the standard errors account for it.
    • Priors chosen to get the answer

      A confident prior from an enthusiastic sponsor, or "uninformative" priors that are not (flat on the wrong scale).

      Spot itAsk for the prior, its source, and the posterior under a weaker and a stronger prior. Ask for a prior predictive check.
    • Treating a posterior as proof of cause

      A neat credible interval around an effect estimated from confounded data.

      Spot itSeparate the questions: how was the comparison made, and then how uncertain is the estimate?
    • Unconverged sampling

      MCMC results reported without diagnostics.

      Spot itAsk for R-hat, effective sample size, divergence count and trace plots.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The 8-point gap tells us customers who took the offer stayed more. It doesn't tell us the offer made them stay, because the customers who say yes are the ones who were likely to stay anyway. To know what the campaign is worth, we need a comparison group that didn't get it, ideally a random 5% we hold back next time. That costs us very little revenue and lets us put a real AED number on the benefit.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start from the estimand: ATT for contacted customers. If there's a randomised holdout I analyse by assignment. Without one, I'd adjust for pre-treatment covariates only, using a doubly robust or cross-fitted estimator, show overlap and balance, and run a sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding. For a rollout by region I'd use a staggered-robust difference-in-differences with the pre-trend plotted. For small samples I'd use a hierarchical Bayesian model, report the prior and its sensitivity, and check R-hat and posterior predictive fit.”
    “Why can't we just control for everything in a regression?”

    Because more controls is not always less bias. Controlling for something the treatment causes, such as card usage when the offer works through usage, removes part of the effect. Controlling for a collider creates a false link. The right controls come from a view of how the data arose, and they must be fixed before treatment. Predictive modelling rewards fit; causal modelling rewards the right structure.

    “Can machine learning find the causes for us?”

    Not from observational data alone. ML is excellent at the prediction pieces inside a causal estimator, as in double machine learning, and at finding who responds differently once the design is sound. It cannot tell you which variables are confounders versus consequences; that takes domain knowledge or an experiment. Causal discovery algorithms and LLM-proposed graphs are best treated as hypotheses to test.

    “We can't run an experiment: it's regulated, or senior people won't allow a control group.”

    Then use a natural experiment and be upfront about its assumptions: a rollout order, a rule with a cut-off, a staggered start across branches. Also note that randomisation can often be done on something we are free to vary, like the timing of an offer, a message variant, or a cap on how many get it when capacity is limited. A small randomised holdout is usually cheaper than the weeks of argument about a confounded result.

    “Bayesian or frequentist: which do you use?”

    Whichever answers the question with the data I have. For large samples with simple questions they agree. I reach for Bayesian methods when data is thin, when I have real prior information, when branches or segments should borrow strength from each other, or when the business wants a direct statement like the probability that a rate beats break-even. I always show how sensitive the answer is to the prior.

    “The new fraud rule caught 6 of 14 alerts. Is it better than the old one at 4%?”

    Probably, but not by tenfold. With 14 alerts the data is thin. A Bayesian estimate that starts from the old rule's performance pulls the new rule's rate well below 43%, and the interval is wide. I would ask for a longer window, a comparable population, and check the 14 were not hand-picked. Then decide on the probability it beats the break-even hit rate.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. In Simulation 1 with the slider at 60%, take-up is 20% among the less engaged and 56% among the engaged. Roughly why is the naive lift well above 5 points?

    Takers are mostly engaged customers: of 34,400 takers, 22,400 (65%) are engaged, against 40% of the population. The engaged stay 30 points more often anyway, so the treated group starts about 0.25 × 30 ≈ 7.5 points ahead. The naive lift is therefore roughly 5 + 11.5 = 16.5 points on the simulated numbers (the exact figure shown is 16.5).

    2. What does randomising assignment achieve that adjustment cannot?

    It makes treatment independent of every other factor, including ones you never measured or thought of. Adjustment only handles the confounders you included and got right.

    3. A DiD gives +4 points. The treated group was falling 0.3 points a month faster than the comparison group before launch. The post-period averages six months later than the pre-period. What might the true effect be?

    If that faster fall would have continued, it contributes −0.3 × 6 = −1.8 points to the DiD, so the true effect is about 4 + 1.8 = 5.8 points. The estimate is biased, and the correction depends on an extrapolation of the trend, which is itself an assumption.

    4. You use a Beta(3, 97) prior for a response rate, then see 6 responses in 100 customers. What is the posterior and its mean?

    Beta(3 + 6, 97 + 94) = Beta(9, 191). Mean = 9 ÷ 200 = 4.5%, halfway between the prior mean (3%) and the observed rate (6%) because the prior and the pilot were each worth 100 customers.

    5. Name a confounder, a mediator and a collider in "a fee waiver raises retention".

    Confounder: customer engagement (drives take-up and retention). Mediator: card or app usage if the waiver works by raising usage. Collider: being flagged "high-value this quarter" if that depends on both the waiver and on retention. Adjust for the first, not the other two.

    6. Why is a churn model a poor tool for choosing who to send a retention offer to?

    It ranks by likelihood to leave, not by how much the offer would change that. Some high-risk customers will leave regardless and some low-risk ones would be unaffected or irritated. The right target is uplift, estimated from experimental or well-adjusted treatment data.

    7. A 95% confidence interval and a 95% credible interval both run from 2% to 7%. What is the difference in what each claims?

    The credible interval says that, given the prior and model, there is a 95% probability the true rate lies in the range. The confidence interval says the procedure that produced it captures the true value in 95% of repeated samples; it makes no probability statement about this one interval. In practice, with weak priors and enough data, the numbers are close, and the decision is the same.
