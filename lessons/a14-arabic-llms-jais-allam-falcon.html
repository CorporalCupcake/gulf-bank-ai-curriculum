    Arabic LLMs and the Language Customers Actually Type

    What Jais, ALLaM and Falcon Arabic are, when they beat a frontier model and when they do not, and why Gulf dialect, Arabizi and mixed Arabic-English messages break models that look fine on Arabic benchmarks.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentence"Best Arabic model" is not a ranking you look up: it depends on which Arabic your customers write (formal, Gulf dialect, Arabizi, mixed with English), what the model must do, and whether the data may leave the bank, so you settle it with a small test set built from your own traffic.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer writes to a Gulf bank's WhatsApp channel: ابي احول فلوس لحسابي بس التطبيق معلق. Or in Latin letters: "abi ahawil flous la hisabi bas el app m3allag". Or: "my transfer مو راضي يتم". All three mean "I want to transfer money to my account but the app is stuck." A person in Dubai reads each without effort.

    Models learn mostly from Modern Standard Arabic (MSA): the formal register of news, books and government text that nobody speaks at home. Customers write dialect, with its own words, no standard spelling, and a habit of drifting into English or into Latin script with digits standing in for letters. It is like a student who aces the textbook exam and struggles to follow a street conversation. A model can score well on MSA-based Arabic exams and still stumble on the messages that matter.

    A14.1 covers models built to be good at Arabic and whether they beat a general frontier model. A14.2 covers the text that defeats most of them.

    • Arabic is four test sets, not one. MSA, Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text behave differently. A single "Arabic accuracy" hides which one was measured.
    • Arabic-specific models are not a guaranteed upgrade. They bring better tokenisation, more Arabic training data and local control. Frontier models bring more raw capability and reasoning. Like a local specialist versus a big-city generalist, which wins depends on the job.
    • Your own messages are the only benchmark that counts. Public leaderboards are mostly MSA, often translated from English, and may sit in training data. They shortlist; they do not decide.
    Hold onto thisAsk four things before choosing. Which Arabic does our traffic use? What must the model do: classify, extract, answer, draft? May the data leave our perimeter? How will we measure on our own sample? The answers usually pick the model, and it is often not the top leaderboard one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The Arabic model families, and how they were built (A14.1)

    All these models are transformers as in M1. They differ in data, tokeniser, starting point and who controls them. There are three routes to a good Arabic model:

    • Train from scratch with a balanced mix. Pretrain on Arabic, English and code with an Arabic-aware tokeniser. The first Jais models (2023, Inception/G42, MBZUAI, Cerebras; 13B and 30B) did this. It is expensive, but everything is Arabic-aware.
    • Adapt an existing model. Take a strong open base, add Arabic pieces to its vocabulary and continue pretraining on Arabic plus English. Like retraining a good chef on a new cuisine, it is cheaper and keeps the base skills. Later Jais, ALLaM (SDAIA, Saudi Arabia) and others do this. Mixing English back in stops the model forgetting earlier skills.
    • New architecture, then add Arabic. Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi, 2025) mixes attention and state-space (Mamba) layers to handle long inputs cheaply. Falcon-Arabic (2025) and Falcon-H1 Arabic (announced January 2026; 3B, 7B, 34B) apply continued training to it.

    Post-training (M1.2), meaning instruction and preference tuning, is where Arabic models differ most, and where data is thinnest. Teams often translate English datasets, which brings translated phrasing and Western cultural assumptions. Local teams invest in culturally grounded data (religion, law, customs, local news) so the model sounds regional, not translated.

    Tokenisation: where an English-centric model starts with a handicap

    A tokeniser learns its vocabulary from its training text. Think of a phrasebook written for English speakers: common English words get one entry, while Arabic words must be spelled out letter by letter. If Arabic is a sliver of the text, common Arabic words get no token of their own and are chopped into letters, or bytes. Arabic is also morphologically rich: لحسابي ("to my account") packs a preposition, a noun and a possessive in one word. Without Arabic stems it splits into meaningless pieces (Simulation 1).

    Three consequences:

    • Cost. API pricing is per token, so the same message costs more.
    • Capacity. An 8,000-token window holds fewer Arabic words, which matters for statements and contracts (M1.3).
    • Quality. With letters as units, the model must relearn spelling and word structure.

    Petrov et al. (2023) documented large cross-language "tokenisation premiums". Recent frontier models have much better multilingual vocabularies, so the gap is smaller than in 2023. The lasting habit is to measure it.

    Script noise remains even with a good tokeniser. Arabic has several alef forms (ا أ إ آ), ya and alef maqsura (ي ى), ta marbuta and ha (ة ه), and writers mix them freely. Add optional vowel marks, stretching characters (tatweel) and two digit systems (٠١٢٣ and 0123). Normalising these before search, matching or classification removes a surprising amount of error. Amounts like "AED 1,250.50" inside right-to-left text are a classic source of display and parsing bugs.

    When a specialist beats a frontier model, and when it does not (A14.1)

    A specialist is strongest where any of these hold:

    • Data cannot leave the perimeter. Open weights run on the bank's GPUs or a sovereign cloud (A11, P4). What is allowed depends on regulator, data class and the bank's risk decisions; ask compliance.
    • Volume is high and the task narrow. Classifying, routing and extracting from millions of messages is cheaper on a small self-served model, especially fine-tuned on your labels (M2.2, P3.1).
    • The language is the hard part. Cultural and religious context, local entities, formal Arabic drafting.
    • Control matters. You can freeze a version so behaviour does not change under you, a model-risk virtue (G3.3).

    A frontier model is strongest for reasoning, tool use and long, complicated instructions, as in agents (M4.1) or a 60-page credit file. A model that writes lovely Arabic but cannot follow a five-step policy is a poor agent. Frontier APIs also bring better safety work and need no GPUs. The sensible design often mixes both: a small Arabic model for high-volume classification and normalisation, a frontier model for hard cases.

    Read the interval like a weather forecast's margin: 82% versus 84% on 100 items overlap, so you cannot call a winner; at 2,000 items the gap would be clear. The number of annotated bank messages you hold matters more than which leaderboard you quote (A4.1).

    Be wary of leaderboards. The Open Arabic LLM Leaderboard and ArabicMMLU are valuable but mostly MSA, include items translated from English, test knowledge rather than "customer wrote half a sentence in Khaleeji", and may leak into training sets. Dialect benchmarks (AraDiCE, DialectalArabicMMLU) are more relevant but young and academic. None is a Gulf retail-banking test.

    Gulf dialects: the Arabic nobody wrote textbooks in (A14.2)

    "Gulf Arabic" (Khaleeji) is a family: Emirati, Saudi (Najdi and Hejazi differ a lot), Kuwaiti, Qatari, Bahraini and Omani share a core but differ in vocabulary and sounds. In chat that core departs from MSA in ways models trip on:

    Code-switching is routine: "في issue في my card", English nouns carrying Arabic grammar. Speech adds dialect, expatriate accents, noise and numbers read aloud in mixed languages. Speech-to-text trained on MSA and broadcast audio makes more errors on Gulf phone calls (B7.2). There is no public, widely accepted Gulf call-centre benchmark I know of, so test any vendor claim on your own recordings, measuring word error rate on a labelled sample.

    Dialect also changes meaning in risk work. A phrase that looks like a threat in MSA may be idiom. A complaint classifier trained on formal text can score a polite dialect escalation as neutral. Names matter too: Mohammed, Muhammad and Mohamad are one person, which is the heart of sanctions name matching (B2.2).

    Arabizi: Arabic in Latin letters, with digits as letters

    Arabizi (also "Franco-Arab", "3arabizi") is Arabic typed in Latin letters by sound, with digits borrowed for sounds English lacks: 3 is ع, 7 is ح, 5 is خ, 2 is the glottal stop ء, 9 is ق (some regions ص), 6 is ط, 8 is غ. It has no standard, and short vowels, usually omitted in Arabic script, are written inconsistently.

    It defeats models three ways:

    • It looks like noise. "m3allag" is not English, and digits inside words confuse tokenisers and amount extraction.
    • It is ambiguous. One Latin letter maps to several Arabic ones (t to ت or ط; s to س or ص; d to د or ض), and a written vowel may not exist in Arabic.
    • It has almost no training data. Nobody publishes books in it.

    Darwish (2014) framed the standard approach: detect, transliterate to Arabic script, then process normally. Simulation 3 shows why transliteration is hard.

    The arithmetic multiplies: a five-letter word with three ambiguous positions gives dozens of spellings, a ten-letter word thousands. The dictionary prunes them; what remains is true semantic ambiguity. So modern pipelines hand the whole message to a capable model with an instruction such as "this is Gulf Arabic in Arabizi; read it as Arabic and answer", rather than mapping letters by rule. Strong frontier models manage common phrases and do worse on rare words, names and merchant slang. Arabizi research is thin and published evaluations are small.

    A practical pipeline for messy Gulf text

    Teams typically build these steps, in order:

    1. Detect the form. Script (Arabic, Latin, mixed), share of digits inside words, language ID per message or span. Route accordingly.
    2. Normalise the script. Unify alef and ya forms, strip tatweel and vowel marks, unify digits. Keep the original alongside; the audit trail needs it.
    3. Transliterate Arabizi or let the model read it. A rule or dictionary step, or a prompt telling a capable model what it sees. Keep the confidence.
    4. Extract hard facts with code. Amounts, dates, card endings and account numbers need validated patterns (B7); a model reading AED 1,250 in Arabic-Indic digits may round or reorder it.
    5. Classify and answer with a model chosen per Simulation 2, tested on your labelled sample split by form (MSA, dialect, Arabizi, mixed) so a weak slice cannot hide in a strong average.
    6. Escalate low confidence to a human or stronger model, and feed corrections back into the test set.

    Retrieval has the same problem. Keyword search (M3.1) misses dialect variants, and embeddings trained on English or MSA place a dialect query far from the MSA policy that answers it. Test retrieval on dialect questions and consider rewriting queries into MSA (M3.4).

    Decision scenarios

    Pick a design for each situation. The answer explains the trade-off; there is rarely one right choice.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing by leaderboard rank

      "Top of the Arabic leaderboard" measures MSA knowledge questions, not customers' messages.

      Spot itAsk what share of the benchmark is dialect or chat, and for the score on 200 of our own labelled messages.
    • One blended Arabic accuracy

      93% overall can hide 60% on Arabizi, which is 15% of traffic.

      Spot itAsk for results split by MSA, dialect, Arabizi and mixed, with counts.
    • Translated test sets

      Machine-translated evaluations test translationese, not how people write, and models may have seen the English original.

      Spot itCheck whether items were natively written, and who annotated them.
    • Silent code-switching failure

      The model answers the Arabic half of a message and ignores the English half, or the reverse. The reply is confidently incomplete.

      Spot itInclude mixed messages in the test set and score completeness, not only fluency.
    • Numbers and names mangled

      Arabic-Indic digits misread, amounts reordered in right-to-left text, a name swapped for a "more common" one.

      Spot itExtract amounts and identifiers with validated code, compare against the raw text, and test digits in both systems.
    • Fluent, wrong, and culturally off

      Polished Arabic that misstates a Shariah ruling or local rule. Fluency hides the error from non-expert reviewers.

      Spot itHave a qualified Arabic-speaking expert grade a sample; for Islamic finance, require a Shariah-board-approved source.
    • Normalisation that destroys evidence

      Stripping diacritics and unifying letters helps matching, but if the raw text is lost, investigators cannot see what the customer wrote.

      Spot itCheck that raw and normalised text are both stored.
    • Specialist model, weak reasoning

      A small Arabic model dropped into an agent that needs tool calls and multi-step policy fails quietly.

      Spot itRun agent evals (M5.3) on it, not just language tests.
    • Trusting a vendor's dialect claim

      "Supports all Arabic dialects" can mean only that it produces some output.

      Spot itAsk for benchmark, sample, dialects and WER or accuracy, then pilot on your data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a model that tops an Arabic leaderboard still perform badly on your WhatsApp channel?

    The leaderboard is mostly MSA knowledge questions, sometimes translated. The channel is Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text with non-standard spelling. Different distribution, different task. Only a test on your own messages answers it.

    2. Name two costs of a tokeniser that has seen little Arabic.

    More tokens per word, so higher API cost and fewer words per window; and worse modelling, since pieces carry no meaning. Also slower generation.

    3. Name three situations that favour a regional open model over a frontier API.

    Data that must stay on the bank's or an in-country platform; high-volume narrow tasks where a small fine-tuned model is cheaper; and the need to freeze a version for model risk. Cultural or religious content is a fourth.

    4. The word "7awel" has been through a dictionary and two Arabic words remain. What does this tell you about a rule-based transliterator?

    The ambiguity is real, not a flaw in the rules: "try" and "transfer" share one Latin spelling. Only the sentence resolves it, so you need a model that reads context, or a human for low-confidence cases.

    5. An Arabizi word has 3 letters with two options each and 2 letters with three options each. How many spellings?

    2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 72, before the dictionary prunes them. Counts multiply, so long words explode.

    6. Your classifier gets 92% overall. Arabizi is 12% of traffic. What else do you need before trusting it?

    Accuracy per slice, with counts and intervals. If Arabizi is at 60%, it contributes about 4.8 points of the 8 points of error, the largest single source, invisible in the average.

    7. Why keep the raw text after normalising Arabic script?

    Normalisation is lossy: alef and ya variants, diacritics and tatweel disappear. Investigators, auditors and later better models need the original, which is also evidence of what the customer wrote.

    8. With 100 test items, model A scores 82% and model B 84%. Can you say B is better?

    No. The interval at 100 items is about ±7.5 points each, so a 2-point gap is well inside the noise. You need hundreds or thousands of items, or a paired test on the same items.
