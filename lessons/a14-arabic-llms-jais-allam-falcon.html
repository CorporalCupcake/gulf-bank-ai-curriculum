    Arabic LLMs and the Language Customers Actually Type

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer writes to a Gulf bank's WhatsApp channel: ابي احول فلوس لحسابي بس التطبيق معلق. Or in Latin letters: "abi ahawil flous la hisabi bas el app m3allag". Or: "my transfer مو راضي يتم". All three mean "I want to transfer money to my account but the app is stuck." A person in Dubai reads each without effort.

    Models learn mostly from Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), the formal Arabic of news, books and government text. Customers write dialect, with no standard spelling, drifting into English or Latin letters. It is like a student who aces the textbook exam and struggles to follow a street conversation.

    A14.1 asks whether models built for Arabic beat a general frontier model. A14.2 covers the text that defeats most of them.

    • Arabic is four test sets, not one. MSA, Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text behave differently. A single "Arabic accuracy" hides which one was measured.
    • Arabic-specific models are not a guaranteed upgrade. They bring better tokenisation and local control; frontier models bring more reasoning. Like a local specialist versus a big-city generalist, the job decides.
    • Your own messages are the only benchmark that counts. Public leaderboards are mostly MSA, often translated from English. They shortlist; they do not decide.
    Hold onto thisAsk four things before choosing. Which Arabic does our traffic use? What must the model do? May the data leave our perimeter? How will we measure on our own sample? The answers usually pick the model, and it is often not the top leaderboard one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The Arabic model families, and how they were built (A14.1)

    These are all transformers as in M1. They differ in data, tokeniser and who controls them. There are three routes:

    • Train from scratch with a balanced mix. The first Jais models (2023) did this. Expensive, but everything is Arabic-aware.
    • Adapt an existing model. Take a strong open base, add Arabic to its vocabulary and keep training. Like retraining a good chef on a new cuisine, it is cheaper. Later Jais and ALLaM do this.
    • New architecture, then add Arabic. Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi) handles long inputs cheaply; Falcon-Arabic models add Arabic on top.

    Post-training (M1.2) is where they differ most: translated English data brings Western assumptions, while local teams build culturally grounded data.

    Tokenisation: where an English-centric model starts with a handicap

    A tokeniser learns its vocabulary from its training text. Think of a phrasebook written for English speakers: English words get one entry, Arabic words are spelled out letter by letter. Arabic also packs a lot into one word: لحسابي ("to my account") holds three parts, and without Arabic stems it splits into meaningless pieces (Simulation 1).

    Three consequences: cost (pricing is per token, so the same message costs more), capacity (a fixed window holds fewer Arabic words, M1.3) and quality (the model must relearn word structure). Recent frontier models have better multilingual vocabularies, so the gap is smaller than in 2023. Measure it.

    Script noise remains even with a good tokeniser: several forms of alef and ya, optional vowel marks and two digit systems (٠١٢٣ and 0123). Normalising these before search or classification removes a surprising amount of error.

    When a specialist beats a frontier model, and when it does not (A14.1)

    A specialist is strongest where any of these hold:

    • Data cannot leave the perimeter. Open weights run on the bank's GPUs or a sovereign cloud (A11, P4).
    • Volume is high and the task narrow. Classifying millions of messages is cheaper on a small self-served model fine-tuned on your labels (M2.2).
    • Control matters. You can freeze a version so behaviour does not change under you, a model-risk virtue (G3.3).

    A frontier model is strongest for reasoning, tool use and long, complicated instructions, as in agents (M4.1) or a 60-page credit file. A sensible design often mixes both.

    Read the interval like a weather forecast's margin: 82% versus 84% on 100 items overlap, so you cannot call a winner. How many labelled bank messages you hold matters more than which leaderboard you quote (A4.1).

    Be wary of leaderboards: they are mostly MSA, include items translated from English, and may leak into training sets. None is a Gulf retail-banking test.

    Gulf dialects: the Arabic nobody wrote textbooks in (A14.2)

    "Gulf Arabic" (Khaleeji) is a family of dialects that share a core but differ by country. In chat that core departs from MSA in ways models trip on:

    MeaningMSATypical Gulf writingWhat goes wrong
    I wantأريدابي / أبغى / اباIntent classifiers trained on MSA miss the request verb.
    Moneyالمالفلوس / مصاريWords differ by country; keyword rules miss variants.
    Spelling of the same wordone standardseveral, by speakerNo standard spelling: ابي, أبي and ابغى are all common.

    Code-switching is routine: "في issue في my card", English nouns carrying Arabic grammar. Speech-to-text trained on broadcast MSA makes more errors on Gulf phone calls (B7.2); test any vendor claim on your own recordings.

    Dialect also changes meaning in risk work: a complaint classifier trained on formal text can score a polite dialect escalation as neutral. Name spelling matters too: Mohammed, Muhammad and Mohamad are one person, the heart of sanctions name matching (B2.2).

    Arabizi: Arabic in Latin letters, with digits as letters

    Arabizi is Arabic typed in Latin letters by sound, with digits for sounds English lacks: 3 is ع, 7 is ح, 5 is خ. It has no standard spelling.

    It defeats models three ways. It looks like noise: "m3allag" is not English, and digits inside words confuse tokenisers and amount extraction. It is ambiguous: one Latin letter maps to several Arabic ones (t to ت or ط; s to س or ص). It has almost no training data: nobody publishes books in it.

    Each ambiguous letter multiplies the possible spellings, and a dictionary prunes most; what remains only the sentence can settle. So modern pipelines hand the whole message to a capable model ("this is Gulf Arabic in Arabizi; read it as Arabic and answer") rather than mapping letters by rule.

    A practical pipeline for messy Gulf text

    Teams typically build these steps, in order:

    1. Detect the form (Arabic script, Latin or mixed) and route accordingly.
    2. Normalise the script, but keep the original alongside for the audit trail.
    3. Transliterate Arabizi or let the model read it.
    4. Extract hard facts with code. Amounts, dates and account numbers need validated patterns (B7), not a model's reading.
    5. Classify and answer with a model chosen per Simulation 2, tested split by form so a weak slice cannot hide in the average.
    6. Escalate low confidence to a human or stronger model.

    Retrieval has the same problem: keyword search (M3.1) misses dialect variants, and embeddings can place a dialect query far from the policy that answers it. Test it on dialect questions, and consider rewriting queries into MSA (M3.4).

    Decision scenarios

    Pick a design for each situation. The answer explains the trade-off; there is rarely one right choice.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Choosing by leaderboard rank

      "Top of the Arabic leaderboard" measures MSA knowledge questions, often translated, not customers' messages.

      Spot itAsk what share of the benchmark is native dialect or chat, and for the score on 200 of our own labelled messages.
    • One blended Arabic accuracy

      93% overall can hide 60% on Arabizi, which is 15% of traffic.

      Spot itAsk for results split by MSA, dialect, Arabizi and mixed, with counts.
    • Silent code-switching failure

      The model answers the Arabic half of a message and ignores the English half, or the reverse. The reply is confidently incomplete.

      Spot itInclude mixed messages in the test set and score completeness, not only fluency.
    • Numbers, names and evidence mangled

      Arabic-Indic digits misread, amounts reordered, a name swapped for a "more common" one, or raw text lost after normalising.

      Spot itExtract identifiers with validated code, compare against the raw text, and check both raw and normalised text are stored.
    • Fluent, wrong, and culturally off

      Polished Arabic that misstates a Shariah ruling or local rule. Fluency hides the error from non-expert reviewers.

      Spot itHave a qualified Arabic-speaking expert grade a sample; for Islamic finance, require a Shariah-board-approved source.
    • Specialist model, weak reasoning, or an unchecked vendor claim

      A small Arabic model in an agent that needs tool calls fails quietly. "Supports all dialects" can mean only that it produces some output.

      Spot itRun agent evals (M5.3), and ask vendors for benchmark, dialects and WER, then pilot on your data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a model that tops an Arabic leaderboard still perform badly on your WhatsApp channel?

    The leaderboard is mostly MSA knowledge questions, sometimes translated. The channel is Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text. Different distribution, different task. Only a test on your own messages answers it.

    2. Name two costs of a tokeniser that has seen little Arabic.

    More tokens per word, so higher cost and fewer words per window; and worse modelling, since pieces carry no meaning.

    3. Name three situations that favour a regional open model over a frontier API.

    Data that must stay on the bank's or an in-country platform; high-volume narrow tasks where a small fine-tuned model is cheaper; and the need to freeze a version for model risk.

    4. The word "7awel" has been through a dictionary and two Arabic words remain. What does this tell you about a rule-based transliterator?

    The ambiguity is real, not a flaw in the rules: "try" and "transfer" share one Latin spelling. Only the sentence resolves it, so you need a model that reads context, or a human for low-confidence cases.

    5. Why does a long Arabizi word cause far more trouble than a short one?

    Each ambiguous letter multiplies the possible spellings rather than adding to them, so the candidates explode with length. A dictionary prunes most, but the sentence has to settle what is left.

    6. Your classifier gets 92% overall, and Arabizi is 12% of traffic. What else do you need before trusting it?

    Accuracy per slice, with counts. A weak slice such as Arabizi can be the largest single source of error and still be invisible in the average.

    7. Why keep the raw text after normalising Arabic script?

    Normalisation is lossy: letter variants, diacritics and tatweel disappear. Investigators, auditors and later better models need the original, which is also evidence of what the customer wrote.

    8. With 100 test items, model A scores 82% and model B 84%. Can you say B is better?

    No. With only 100 items each score could easily be several points off either way, so a 2-point gap is well inside the noise. You need hundreds or thousands of items, or a paired test on the same items.
