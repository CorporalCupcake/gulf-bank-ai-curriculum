    Arabic LLMs and the Language Customers Actually Type

    What Jais, ALLaM and Falcon Arabic are, when they beat a frontier model and when they do not, and why Gulf dialect, Arabizi and mixed Arabic-English messages break models that look fine on Arabic benchmarks.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentence"Best Arabic model" is not a ranking you look up: it depends on which Arabic your customers write (formal, Gulf dialect, Arabizi, mixed with English), what the model must do, and whether the data may leave the bank, so you settle it with a small test set built from your own traffic.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer writes to a Gulf bank's WhatsApp channel: ابي احول فلوس لحسابي بس التطبيق معلق. Or the same thing in Latin letters: "abi ahawil flous la hisabi bas el app m3allag". Or: "my transfer مو راضي يتم". All three mean roughly "I want to transfer money to my account but the app is stuck." A person in Dubai reads each without effort.

    The Arabic that models learn from is mostly different. The web, books, news and government text are written in Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), a formal register nobody speaks at home. Customers write in their dialect, which has its own words, its own spelling (there is no standard one), and often drifts into English mid-sentence or into Latin script with digits standing in for letters. A model can score well on Arabic exams, which are written in MSA, and still stumble on the messages that matter to the bank.

    That gap drives both topics. A14.1 is about models built to be good at Arabic: who made them, how, and whether they beat a general frontier model. A14.2 is about the text that defeats most of them.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Arabic is not one thing to a model. MSA, Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text are four different test sets. A single "Arabic accuracy" number hides which one you measured.
    • Arabic-specific models are an answer to a real problem, not a guaranteed upgrade. They offer better tokenisation, more Arabic in training and local control. Frontier models have more raw capability and reasoning. Which wins depends on the task.
    • Your own messages are the only benchmark that counts. Public leaderboards are mostly MSA, often translated from English, and may be in the training data. They shortlist; they do not decide.
    Hold onto thisBefore choosing a model, ask four things. Which Arabic does our traffic use? What does the model need to do: classify, extract, answer, draft? May the data leave our perimeter? How will we measure on our own sample? The answers usually pick the model, and it is often not the one with the best leaderboard rank.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The Arabic model families, and how they were built (A14.1)

    All the models below are transformer language models of the kind described in M1. What differs is the data, the tokeniser, the starting point and who controls them. Three routes exist for making a model good at Arabic:

    • Train from scratch with a balanced mix. Pretrain on Arabic, English and code together with a tokeniser designed for Arabic. The first Jais models (2023, from Inception/G42, MBZUAI and Cerebras) took this route, at 13B and 30B parameters, with a vocabulary trained on both languages. Expensive, but the model's whole vocabulary and statistics are Arabic-aware.
    • Adapt an existing model. Start from a strong open base, extend its vocabulary with Arabic pieces, and continue pretraining on Arabic and English. This is much cheaper and inherits the base model's skills. The later Jais family, ALLaM (from SDAIA in Saudi Arabia) and several others use variants of it. Mixing English back in during continued training matters: without it the model forgets earlier skills.
    • Build a new architecture, then add Arabic. Falcon-H1 (TII, Abu Dhabi, 2025) is a hybrid of attention and state-space (Mamba) layers, built to handle long inputs at lower cost. Falcon-Arabic (2025) and Falcon-H1 Arabic (announced January 2026, in 3B, 7B and 34B sizes) apply the same continued-training idea to those models.

    After pretraining comes post-training (M1.2): instruction tuning and preference tuning. This is where Arabic models differ most in practice, and where the data is thinnest. High-quality Arabic instruction and preference data is scarce, so teams translate English datasets, which brings translated phrasing and Western cultural assumptions with it. Local teams invest in culturally grounded data (religion, law, customs, local news) so the model answers like a regional assistant, not like a translated one.

    Tokenisation: where an English-centric model starts with a handicap

    Simulation 1 shows the mechanism. A tokeniser learns its vocabulary from its training text. If Arabic is a sliver of that text, common Arabic words never get their own token and are chopped into letters, or at worst into bytes. Arabic is also morphologically rich: one written word like لحسابي ("to my account") packs a preposition, a noun and a possessive. A tokeniser without Arabic stems splits it into pieces that carry no meaning of their own.

    Three consequences follow. Cost: API pricing is per token, so the same message costs more. Capacity: a context window of 8,000 tokens holds fewer Arabic words, which matters for long statements or contracts (M1.3). Quality: with letters as units the model has to relearn spelling and word structure, and long-range sense gets harder. Petrov et al. (2023) documented large cross-language "tokenisation premiums" in popular tokenisers. The most recent frontier models have much better multilingual vocabularies, so the gap is smaller than in 2023; the habit of measuring it is what lasts.

    Script details add noise even when the tokeniser is good. Arabic has several forms of alef (ا أ إ آ), of ya and alef maqsura (ي ى), of ta marbuta and ha (ة ه). Writers mix them freely. There are optional vowel marks, stretching characters (tatweel) and two digit systems (٠١٢٣ and 0123). A normalisation step that unifies these before search, matching or classification removes a surprising amount of error. Amounts such as "AED 1,250.50" inside right-to-left text are a classic source of display and parsing bugs.

    When a specialist beats a frontier model, and when it does not (A14.1)

    The case for an Arabic-specialised model is strongest where one or more of these hold:

    • Data cannot leave the perimeter. Open weights can run on the bank's own GPUs or a sovereign cloud (A11, P4). Many customer-data rules and internal policies push towards this, though what is allowed depends on the regulator, the data class and the bank's own risk decisions; check with compliance rather than assume.
    • Volume is high and the task is narrow. Classifying, routing and extracting from millions of messages is cheaper on a small model you serve yourself, especially after fine-tuning on your labels (M2.2, P3.1).
    • The language is the hard part. Cultural and religious context, local entities, Arabic poetry-adjacent phrasing, or formal Arabic drafting where fluency matters.
    • Control matters. You can freeze a version, so behaviour does not change under you, which is a model-risk virtue (G3.3).

    The case for a frontier model is strongest when the task needs reasoning, tool use or long, complicated instructions, as in agents (M4.1) or analysing a 60-page credit file. Smaller Arabic models are weaker there, and a model that writes beautiful Arabic but cannot follow a five-step policy is a poor agent. Frontier APIs also arrive with better safety work and fast improvement, and cost no GPUs to run. Often the sensible design mixes both: a small Arabic model for high-volume classification and normalisation, a frontier model for the hard cases.

    Notice what the interval does. With 100 test items, the frontier model's 82% and the large Arabic model's 84% overlap; with 2,000 items the gap would be clear. Before a procurement meeting, the number of annotated bank messages you hold matters more than which leaderboard you quote (A4.1).

    Leaderboards deserve some suspicion. The Open Arabic LLM Leaderboard and benchmarks such as ArabicMMLU are valuable but mostly cover MSA, include many items translated from English, and cover knowledge questions rather than "customer wrote a half-sentence in Khaleeji." Their questions can leak into training sets. Dialect-focused benchmarks (AraDiCE, DialectalArabicMMLU) exist and are more relevant but are young and academic. None is a Gulf retail-banking test.

    Gulf dialects: the Arabic nobody wrote textbooks in (A14.2)

    "Gulf Arabic" (Khaleeji) is a family, not a single dialect. Emirati, Saudi (Najdi and Hejazi differ a lot), Kuwaiti, Qatari, Bahraini and Omani speech share a core and differ in vocabulary and sounds. Written in chat, they share a core that differs from MSA in ways models trip on:

    Other features matter for a bank's text and speech. Code-switching is routine: "في issue في my card", with English nouns carrying Arabic grammar. Speech adds a second layer: call-centre audio has dialect, accents of expatriate staff and customers, background noise, and numbers read aloud in a mix of languages. Speech-to-text models trained mostly on MSA and broadcast audio make more errors on Gulf telephone speech (B7.2). I am not aware of a public, widely accepted Gulf call-centre benchmark, so any vendor claim should be tested on your own recordings with word error rate measured on a labelled sample.

    Dialect also changes meaning for risk work. A phrase that looks like a threat in MSA may be idiom in dialect. A complaint classifier trained on formal text can score a polite dialect message as neutral when it is a clear escalation. And names: one person's name can be spelled several ways in Latin letters (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamad), which is the heart of sanctions name matching (B2.2).

    Arabizi: Arabic in Latin letters, with digits as letters

    Arabizi (also "Franco-Arab" or "3arabizi") is how many people write Arabic on phones. Latin letters stand in for Arabic ones by sound, and where Arabic has a sound English lacks, a digit that looks like the letter is borrowed. 3 is ع, 7 is ح, 5 is خ, 2 is the glottal stop ء, 9 is ق (in some regions ص), 6 is ط, and 8 is غ. It is written fast, with no standard, and short vowels, which Arabic script usually omits, are written out in Latin, but not consistently.

    Why this defeats models. First, it looks like noise to an English-centric model: "m3allag" is not an English word, and digits inside words confuse both tokenisers and amount extraction. Second, it is ambiguous. One Latin letter maps to several Arabic ones (t to ت or ط; s to س or ص; d to د or ض), and a written vowel may or may not exist in the Arabic. Third, it has almost no training data: nobody publishes books in it. Darwish (2014) framed the standard approach, which is detection, then transliteration to Arabic script, then ordinary Arabic processing. Simulation 3 shows why the middle step is hard.

    Look at the arithmetic. A five-letter word with three ambiguous positions gives dozens of spellings; a ten-letter word gives thousands. The dictionary prunes it. What remains is the true semantic ambiguity, and that is why modern pipelines hand the whole message to a capable language model with an instruction such as "this is Gulf Arabic written in Arabizi; read it as Arabic and answer" rather than mapping letters by rule. Strong frontier models do this reasonably on common phrases and worse on rare words, names and merchant slang. Research on Arabizi is thin compared with its everyday use, and published evaluations are small.

    A practical pipeline for messy Gulf text

    What teams actually build, in order, and why:

    1. Detect the form. Script (Arabic, Latin, mixed), proportion of digits inside words, and a language-ID label per message or per span. Route accordingly.
    2. Normalise the script. Unify alef and ya forms, strip tatweel and optional vowel marks, convert Arabic-Indic digits to a single digit system, and keep the original text alongside. Never throw away the raw message: the audit trail needs it.
    3. Transliterate Arabizi or let the model read it. Either a rule or dictionary step, or a prompt telling a capable model what it is looking at. Keep the confidence.
    4. Extract the hard facts with code, not with prose. Amounts, dates, card endings and account numbers need validated patterns and checks (B7), because a model that "reads" AED 1,250 in Arabic-Indic digits may round or reorder it.
    5. Classify and answer with a model chosen per Simulation 2, tested on your labelled sample, split by form (MSA, dialect, Arabizi, mixed) so a weak slice cannot hide behind a strong average.
    6. Escalate low confidence to a human or to a stronger model, and feed the corrected cases back into the test set.

    Retrieval has the same problem in a different place. Keyword search (M3.1) misses dialect variants of the same word, and embedding models trained mostly on English or MSA place a dialect query far from the MSA policy document that answers it. Test retrieval on dialect questions specifically, and consider query rewriting into MSA (M3.4).

    Decision scenarios

    Pick a design for each situation. The answer explains the trade-off; there is rarely a single right choice.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Customer service chat and WhatsApp. Intent detection, routing, suggested replies and summaries across dialect, Arabizi and English.
    • Call-centre analytics. Transcripts in a code-switched mix; complaint and vulnerability detection; quality monitoring.
    • Complaints and regulatory reporting. Classifying free-text complaints in Arabic and English into the categories a regulator expects.
    • Staff copilots. Searching Arabic and English policies from either language (B7.3).
    • Islamic finance content. Product explanations, fatwa and Shariah-board references, where terminology must be exact and a fluent but wrong answer is costly.
    • KYC and adverse media. Screening Arabic news and names across spellings.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Choosing by leaderboard rank

      "It is top of the Arabic leaderboard." The leaderboard measures MSA knowledge questions, not customers' messages.

      Spot itAsk what share of the benchmark is dialect or chat, and for the score on 200 of our own labelled messages.
    • One blended Arabic accuracy

      93% overall, hiding 60% on Arabizi, which is 15% of traffic.

      Spot itAsk for results split by MSA, dialect, Arabizi and mixed, with counts.
    • Translated test sets

      An Arabic evaluation machine-translated from English tests translationese, not how people write. Models can also have seen the English original.

      Spot itCheck whether items were natively written, and who annotated them.
    • Silent code-switching failure

      A model answers the Arabic half of a message and ignores the English half, or the reverse, and the reply is confidently incomplete.

      Spot itInclude mixed messages in the test set and score completeness of the answer, not only fluency.
    • Numbers and names mangled

      Arabic-Indic digits misread, amounts reordered inside right-to-left text, a name changed to a "more common" one.

      Spot itExtract amounts and identifiers with validated code and compare against the raw text; test with digits in both systems.
    • Fluent, wrong, and culturally off

      Polished Arabic that misstates a Shariah ruling or a local rule. Fluency hides the error from non-expert reviewers.

      Spot itHave a qualified Arabic-speaking subject expert grade a sample; for Islamic finance content, require a Shariah-board-approved source.
    • Normalisation that destroys evidence

      Stripping diacritics and unifying letters improves matching, but if the raw text is lost, an investigator cannot see what the customer wrote.

      Spot itCheck that raw and normalised text are both stored.
    • Specialist model, weak reasoning

      The small Arabic model is dropped into an agent that needs tool calls and multi-step policy, and fails quietly.

      Spot itRun the agent evals (M5.3) on the Arabic model, not just language tests.
    • Trusting a vendor's dialect claim

      "Supports all Arabic dialects." Support can mean that the model produces some output.

      Spot itAsk for the benchmark, the sample, the dialects, the word error rate or accuracy, and run it on your data in a pilot.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There is no single best Arabic model. The regional models, Jais, ALLaM and Falcon, are strong for keeping data in-country and for cheap high-volume work. The big international models are stronger at complicated reasoning. What decides it is how our customers actually write: formal Arabic, Gulf dialect, Latin-letter Arabic, or mixed with English. I'd test two or three candidates on a few hundred of our own anonymised messages before committing, and expect to use more than one model.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd build an evaluation set from real, de-identified traffic, labelled natively by Gulf-dialect speakers, stratified into MSA, dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text, with enough items per slice for intervals. Pipeline: language ID, script normalisation with raw text retained, validated extraction for numbers and names, then a model chosen per slice and per task. Specialist open models for sovereign or high-volume classification, a frontier model for reasoning-heavy cases, with a router and human escalation on low confidence. Public leaderboards only shortlist.”
    “Why not just use the Arabic model? It is built here, for us.”

    It may well be the right choice, especially for in-country hosting and high-volume tasks. But being local does not mean better on our task. I'd compare it with a frontier model on our own labelled messages, by slice, and also check reasoning and instruction following if it will power an agent. If it wins or ties at lower cost with data staying in-country, we use it.

    “Why not just use a frontier model? It speaks Arabic fine.”

    On formal Arabic and many chat messages it does, and for reasoning-heavy work it is often the best. The open questions are dialect and Arabizi accuracy, token cost for Arabic text, and whether customer data may go to an external API. Those are testable. I'd keep it as a baseline and as the escalation tier even if a regional model handles the bulk.

    “A vendor says their model scores 90% on Arabic. Is that good?”

    I can't tell yet. I'd ask which benchmark, whether it is native Arabic or translated, whether it covers Gulf dialect and Arabizi, how many items, and whether the model could have seen it in training. Then I'd run a blind pilot on our own sample.

    “Our customers write in Arabizi. Do we need a special model?”

    Not necessarily. Capable general models read common Arabizi reasonably, and a pipeline can detect it and instruct the model accordingly. But accuracy on names, merchants and slang is lower and under-measured, so I'd test it as its own slice and send low-confidence cases to a human. If it is a large share of traffic, a small fine-tuned model on labelled Arabizi examples is worth a pilot.

    “How many labelled messages do we need to compare two models?”

    To see a five-point gap near 80% accuracy, you need roughly 250 to 300 items per slice that you want to read separately, since the interval at 250 is about ±5 points. For smaller differences, many more. Start with 200 to 300 per slice, report intervals, and say "not distinguishable" when it is true.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can a model that tops an Arabic leaderboard still perform badly on your WhatsApp channel?

    The leaderboard is mostly MSA knowledge questions, sometimes translated, while the channel is Gulf dialect, Arabizi and code-switched text with non-standard spelling. Different distribution, different task. Only a test on your own messages answers it.

    2. Name two costs of a tokeniser that has seen little Arabic.

    More tokens per word, so higher API cost and fewer words per context window; and worse modelling, since pieces carry no meaning. Any of: slower generation, weaker handling of word structure.

    3. Name three situations that favour a regional open model over a frontier API.

    Data that must stay on the bank's own or an in-country platform; high-volume, narrow tasks such as classification where a small fine-tuned model is cheaper; and the need to freeze a version for model-risk reasons. Cultural or religious content can be a fourth.

    4. The word "7awel" has been through a dictionary and two Arabic words remain. What does this tell you about a rule-based transliterator?

    The ambiguity is real, not a flaw in the rules: "try" and "transfer" share one Latin spelling. Only the surrounding sentence resolves it, so you need a model that reads context, or a human for low-confidence cases.

    5. An Arabizi word has 3 letters with two options each and 2 letters with three options each. How many spellings?

    2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 72, and the dictionary then prunes them. The count multiplies, so long words explode quickly.

    6. Your classifier gets 92% overall. Arabizi is 12% of traffic. What else do you need before trusting it?

    Accuracy per slice with counts and intervals. If Arabizi accuracy is, say, 60%, it contributes about 4.8 points of error out of 8, which is the largest single source, and is invisible in the average.

    7. Why keep the raw text after normalising Arabic script?

    Normalisation is lossy: alef and ya variants, diacritics and tatweel disappear. Investigators, auditors and later better models need the original. It is also evidence of what the customer wrote.

    8. With 100 test items, model A scores 82% and model B 84%. Can you say B is better?

    No. The interval at 100 items is about ±7.5 points for each, so the difference of 2 points is well inside the noise. You would need hundreds or thousands of items, or a paired test on the same items.
