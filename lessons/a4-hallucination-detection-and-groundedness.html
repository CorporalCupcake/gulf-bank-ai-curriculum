    Catching Hallucinations

    Part 1

    What a hallucination is

    In one sentenceA hallucination is a fluent, confident answer that is wrong or unsupported, and nothing in the tone gives it away.

    Ask the bank's assistant for the Gold card late fee. It says "AED 230 per late payment", calmly and clearly. The product sheet says AED 200. A model writes plausible text, and a wrong figure reads as plausibly as a right one.

    Two different failures hide under the one word.

    • Unfaithful to the context: the model was given a document, such as a retrieved policy, and says something it doesn't support or that contradicts it. You can check this against the document.
    • Unfactual: the model answers from memory and is wrong. You need an outside source of truth, which is much harder.

    The first matters most at a bank, where assistants must stay inside the supplied text. A fabricated citation is a common form.

    Why it happens: a model predicts the next word. A fact it has seen many times gets high probability. A fact seen once or never, like one bank's fee schedule, has no pattern, yet the model must still output something, like a student guessing rather than leaving the exam blank.

    Part 2

    Ask again: do the answers agree?

    In one sentenceSemantic entropy asks the model the same question several times, groups the answers by meaning, and measures how split the groups are.

    Raw word probabilities mislead, because one meaning has many wordings. A model certain the fee is AED 200 might phrase it six ways and look unsure. Wording variation is noise, not doubt. It is like polling a room: count the distinct opinions, not the ways people phrased them.

    1. Sample 5 to 10 answers at a non-zero temperature.
    2. Group them by meaning, usually with a second model that checks whether two answers imply each other.
    3. Measure how spread out the groups are.

    How to read it

    Read the number as bits of doubt. 0 means every answer means the same. 1 is a coin flip between two meanings. A move from 0.5 to 1.3 means a real rival answer has appeared.

    • Strong at: catching confabulation, where the model is making it up.
    • Weak at: stable mistakes. A model can be consistently wrong, like a student who confidently repeats a textbook error.
    • Cost: about ten model calls per answer, so use it for audits and high-stakes answers.
    Part 3

    Check against the source

    In one sentenceGroundedness checking breaks an answer into small claims and marks each one against the document, like a marker with a mark scheme.
    1. Decompose the answer into atomic claims, one checkable statement each.
    2. Retrieve the relevant source passage for each claim.
    3. Verify each claim as supported, contradicted, or not enough information. A small model or an LLM judge does this.
    4. Aggregate: faithfulness is the share of claims supported, or flag the whole answer if any claim fails.

    Citation checks are the cheap layer. First confirm the cited passage exists (fabricated references fail here), then that it supports the claim.

    False alarms pile up with length. A verifier that wrongly flags 3% of good claims flags about 22% of good eight-claim answers. Long answers swamp reviewers.

    Part 4

    Abstaining with a guarantee

    In one sentenceConformal prediction turns any detector's score into a cut-off with a promised error rate, set from labelled examples.

    Every detector gives a score. Below a line, the assistant says "not sure, ask the product team". The cut-off is a business decision: wrong claims let through, against reviewer hours spent. Conformal prediction picks the line so that the error rate among released answers stays under a budget you choose, say 5%.

    It is like setting the pass mark from last year's marked papers and trusting it this year if the cohort is similar. The one assumption is exchangeability: calibration and future examples come from the same process.

    1. Hold out labelled examples the model never trained on (the calibration set).
    2. Score each one for how wrong or surprising the model was.
    3. Set the threshold at the score that only your error budget (called alpha) of examples exceed.
    4. Apply that threshold to new examples.

    How to read the guarantee

    • The promise: a new example lands on the safe side at least 1 minus alpha of the time. Alpha of 5% means at least 95%.
    • Tighter budget costs answers. More answers or claims get deleted or abstained.
    • It is an average. 99% on easy questions and 80% on hard ones still averages 95%.
    • Small calibration sets are noisy. Your particular threshold can be unlucky, so use hundreds of examples, not tens.

    For free text there are two approaches. Conformal factuality scores each claim, calibrates on answers whose false claims are known, then deletes every claim below the threshold. With probability at least 1 minus alpha, all remaining claims are true. Too strict a budget leaves "the card has a fee". Abstention by threshold answers only if a confidence score (semantic entropy, verifier score or a mix) is on the safe side of the calibrated line; otherwise it hands over to a human.

    Part 5

    Which to use

    MethodNeedsBest forCost
    Semantic entropyJust the modelAnswers from memoryAbout 10 calls per answer
    Claim verificationA source documentRAG and policy answersOne check per claim
    Citation checkThe cited passagesCheap first layerVery low
    Conformal cut-offLabelled calibration setPromising an error rateLabelling effort

    Detection is not truth. A detector has false alarms and misses like any classifier, so it needs a threshold chosen from costs and an honest test set.

    Show the formula

    Chance a good answer gets flagged = 1 − (1 − f)n, where f is the false-alarm rate per claim and n the number of claims. With f = 3% and 8 claims: 1 − 0.978 ≈ 22%.

    In banking, a fee or rate quoted wrongly to a customer is a complaint or a regulatory problem, so high-stakes answers should be checked against the product sheet and abstain when unsure.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting raw word probabilities

      Different wordings of the same meaning look like doubt. Group by meaning first.

    • Treating agreement as correctness

      A model can be consistently wrong. Combine consistency with a source check.

    • Flagging long answers as a whole

      Small per-claim false alarms add up, and reviewers drown. Choose the cut-off from the reviewer's cost.

    • Calibrating on too little data or the wrong data

      The guarantee needs calibration examples that look like live traffic, and enough of them.

    1. What is the difference between unfaithful and unfactual?

    Unfaithful means the answer disagrees with a document the model was given. Unfactual means it is wrong about the world, with no document to check against.

    2. Ten samples give four different fee amounts. What does that suggest?

    The model is guessing. Semantic entropy would be high.

    3. Which hallucination does semantic entropy miss?

    Stable ones, where the model is consistently wrong.

    4. What does a conformal threshold with a 5% budget promise?

    On average, at least 95% of new cases land on the safe side, if calibration and live data come from the same process.

    5. First step of a citation check?

    Confirm the cited passage exists. Fabricated references fail there.
