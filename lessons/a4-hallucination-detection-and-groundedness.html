    Catching Hallucinations, and Promising When to Abstain

    How to tell when a language model is making things up: disagreement between its own samples, claim-by-claim checks against the source, and conformal prediction, which turns a rough confidence score into a statistical promise about how often the answers you let through are wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA hallucination detector is a noisy score, not a verdict; the useful questions are whether the answer is supported by the source you gave it, whether the model would say the same thing twice, and how to choose a cut-off on the score so that the error rate among answers you release is provably below a number you picked, provided tomorrow looks like your calibration data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks the bank's internal assistant: "What is the late-payment fee on the Gold card?" The assistant answers in a calm, fluent sentence: "AED 230 per late payment." The real figure in the product sheet is AED 200. Nothing in the tone gives it away. A language model is trained to produce plausible text, and a wrong figure is exactly as plausible as a right one.

    Three ideas make this manageable, and the lesson is built on them.

    • Ask again. If you ask the same question ten times and get "AED 200" nine times and something different once, the model is fairly sure. If you get four different amounts, it is guessing. Disagreement between a model's own answers is a signal of ignorance. The catch: the answers must be compared by meaning, not by wording. "AED 200" and "a fee of 200 dirhams" agree.
    • Check against the source. If the assistant was given the product sheet, every claim in its answer should be traceable to a sentence in it. That is a smaller, more checkable problem than "is this true of the world".
    • Decide when to stay quiet, with a guarantee. Every detector produces a score. Somewhere you must draw a line: below it, the assistant says "I'm not sure, please check with the product team". Conformal prediction picks that line using held-out examples so that the error rate among answers released is at most, say, 5%, with a statement you can defend to a validator.

    None of these makes a model truthful. They make its unreliability measurable, and a measurable error rate is something a bank can govern.

    Hold onto thisDetection is not truth. A detector has its own false alarms and misses, so it needs the same treatment as any other classifier: a threshold chosen from costs, an honest test set, and an interval. Conformal prediction is the tool that makes the threshold choice carry a guarantee.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What "hallucination" means, and why models do it

    Two different failures hide under one word, and the tests differ.

    • Unfaithful to the context (a groundedness failure). The model was given a document, such as a retrieved policy or a loan agreement, and says something the document does not support or contradicts. You can check this against the document.
    • Unfactual (a world-knowledge failure). The model answers from memory and is wrong. To check, you need an external truth, which is much harder to get.

    For a bank, the first matters most day to day: retrieval-augmented assistants, summarisers of credit memos, and contact-centre copilots are all supposed to stay inside supplied text. A separate failure is the silent omission or a fabricated citation: the answer names "clause 7.2" that does not exist.

    Why does it happen? A model is trained to predict the next token. When the training data contains a fact many times, the right continuation gets high probability. For a fact seen once or never, such as one bank's fee schedule, there is no pattern to learn, but the model still must produce something. A 2025 OpenAI analysis (Kalai and colleagues) makes the point sharply: much of this is a statistical inevitability for rarely seen facts, and common benchmark scoring, which gives zero for "I don't know" and a chance of credit for a guess, trains models to guess rather than abstain. That is the formal reason abstention, the second half of this lesson, matters.

    Disagreement as a signal: sampling consistency and semantic entropy

    A model can give you a probability for every token. The obvious uncertainty measure, the entropy of the sequence, fails because one meaning has many wordings. If the model is certain the fee is AED 200 but could phrase that six ways, the probability is split across six strings and looks uncertain. Wording variation is noise, not doubt.

    Semantic entropy (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023; developed in Farquhar and colleagues, Nature, 2024) fixes this in three steps:

    1. Sample several answers (typically 5 to 10) at a non-zero temperature.
    2. Group them by meaning. In the published method a second model checks bidirectional entailment: A and B go in the same group if each implies the other.
    3. Compute the entropy of the group sizes. One big group means low entropy and confidence. Many small groups means high entropy and a likely confabulation.
    Hsem=−∑cpclog2pc

    pc is the share of sampled answers falling in meaning-group c (in the full method, the share of probability mass). Zero bits: every sample means the same thing. One bit: a coin flip between two meanings. Ten samples split 9 to 1 give 0.47 bits. Ten samples in ten different groups give 3.3 bits.

    Three practical points. First, samples cost money: ten answers plus pairwise entailment checks is an order of magnitude more than one call, so teams use it for sampling audits and high-stakes answers rather than every request. Second, semantic entropy probes (2024) train a small classifier on the model's hidden states to predict the semantic entropy from a single pass, getting most of the signal at near-zero cost, but they need white-box access, which excludes hosted API models. Third, SelfCheckGPT (Manakul and colleagues, 2023) is the black-box cousin: sample extra answers and check each sentence of the main answer against them. A sentence that the other samples do not support is likely invented.

    The limit: consistency is not correctness. A model can be consistently wrong, repeating a mistake from its training data every time. Semantic entropy catches confabulation, the arbitrary, different-each-time kind of error, and misses stable misconceptions. This is why it is one signal among several.

    Groundedness: claim verification and citation checks

    When the model answers from supplied documents, you can check it directly. The standard pipeline has four stages:

    1. Decompose the answer into atomic claims, each a single checkable statement. ("The Gold card has a late fee of AED 200 and it is waived in the first year" is two claims.) An LLM usually does this. FActScore (Min and colleagues, 2023) popularised the method for long-form text.
    2. Retrieve the relevant passage for each claim from the source.
    3. Verify each claim against its passage: supported, contradicted, or not enough information. This is natural language inference (NLI), done by a specialised small model or an LLM judge (A4.2 explains judge biases).
    4. Aggregate: a faithfulness score is the share of claims supported; or you flag the answer if any claim fails.

    Citation checks are the cheap, mechanical layer. If the assistant cites "[Policy 4.2, p.7]", confirm that the document and the passage exist (a plain lookup), then confirm the cited passage supports the claim (an NLI call). Fabricated references fail at the first step. A subtler failure is a real citation attached to a claim it does not support; only the second step catches that.

    One quantity decides how hard this is: the answer-level error compounds claim-level error. A verifier with a small false alarm rate per claim produces many false alarms per answer, because a long answer has many chances to trip it. The simulation shows how quickly.

    The simulation holds a lesson that applies to any detector: you cannot pick the cut-off from the score alone. How many wrong claims you are willing to let through, against how many reviewer hours you can spend, is a business decision. The next step is to make that decision with a guarantee.

    Conformal prediction: a promise from a calibration set

    Conformal prediction (Vovk and colleagues; a gentle modern introduction is Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021) wraps any model and turns its scores into guaranteed statements. It needs no assumption about the model or the shape of the score. It needs one thing: exchangeability, meaning the calibration examples and future examples are drawn from the same process, in any order. The recipe is short:

    1. Hold out n labelled examples that the model never trained on. These are the calibration set.
    2. For each, compute a nonconformity score: a number that is large when the model is wrong or surprised.
    3. Take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest of these scores as the threshold τ. The "+1" accounts for the new example being one more draw from the same pool.
    4. On a new example, use τ to decide what to output.

    The guarantee, marginal over the random draw of calibration set and test example, is that the new example's score falls below τ with probability at least 1−α. The proof is a counting argument: if the new example is exchangeable with the n others, its rank among all n+1 is equally likely to be anywhere.

    P(snew≤τ)≥1−α

    The probability that a fresh example is "covered" is at least 1−α. It is also at most 1−α+1/(n+1), so it is tight: the promise is not conservative by more than the size of one calibration example. With α = 5% and n = 199, coverage lies between 95% and 95.5%.

    Notice what the guarantee is not. It is an average over examples, not a promise for each question type: coverage can be 99% on easy questions and 80% on hard ones and still average 95%. And it holds for a random calibration set: with a small one, your particular τ can be unlucky, so realised coverage varies from calibration set to calibration set. Simulation 3 shows that spread.

    Conformal prediction for LLMs: filtering claims and abstaining

    Applying this to free text needs a good definition of "correct" and of the score. Three published approaches show the range:

    • Conformal factuality (Mohri and Hashimoto, 2024). Decompose each calibration answer into claims, score each claim with any confidence measure, and let each answer's nonconformity be the highest score among its false claims. Set τ at the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest. At test time, delete every claim scoring at or below τ. The promise: with probability at least 1−α, all remaining claims are true. You trade completeness for factuality, and the guarantee tells you exactly how.
    • Conformal language modelling (Quach and colleagues, 2024). Sample answers until a stopping rule says that, with calibrated probability, at least one in the set is acceptable, with a rejection rule to drop redundant or low-quality samples. The output is a set of answers.
    • Abstention by threshold. The simplest design: answer only if a confidence score (semantic entropy, verifier score, or a combination) is on the safe side of a calibrated line; otherwise hand over to a human. Controlling the error rate among answered questions, rather than coverage over all questions, is the subject of conformal risk control (Angelopoulos and colleagues) and related "learn then test" methods. Naive versions need care, because you choose τ by looking at the same errors you then claim to control.

    Simulation 3 uses the conformal-factuality recipe on invented data, with the same verifier model as before.

    Two honest details. A calibration answer with no false claims gets nonconformity minus infinity, so it never raises τ. And the whole guarantee depends on how the calibration labels were produced: if "false claim" was marked by an LLM judge rather than a person, the promise holds for the judge's idea of truth. Calibrate on human-verified claims, and re-verify a sample regularly.

    How it connects

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Policy and product assistants. Staff and customers ask about fees, eligibility, cut-off times. Wrong figures create complaints and conduct risk. Groundedness against the current policy store, plus a "not sure, ask the product team" route, is the standard defence.
    • Credit memo and covenant summaries. A summary that invents a covenant or misstates a ratio is dangerous. Claim-level checks against the source filings give the reviewer a list of unsupported lines instead of a vague warning (see A10.3).
    • AML investigation copilots. A narrative for a suspicious activity report must not contain facts absent from the case file. Every sentence should link to a transaction or document.
    • Contact-centre agent assist. Real-time, so latency limits you to a cheap verifier; sampling-heavy methods are for after-the-fact audits.
    • Model risk and validation. A statement such as "no more than 5% of released answers contain an unsupported claim, measured on 400 human-labelled answers" is the kind of claim a validator can examine and challenge.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The picture has settled into a pattern: no single detector is reliable, strong systems combine several signals, and conformal calibration is increasingly the last layer that sets the cut-off. The argument is about cost, about language coverage, and about how to get the guarantee to survive long answers and shifting data.

    1. 2022
      Models partly know what they know

      Kadavath and colleagues showed that large models' own probability that an answer is correct is reasonably calibrated on multiple-choice tasks, and that they can be trained to predict whether they know the answer. The root of self-assessment methods.

      Kadavath et al., arXiv 2207.05221
    2. 2023
      Semantic uncertainty, SelfCheckGPT, FActScore

      Meaning-level entropy, black-box sentence checking against extra samples, and decomposition into atomic facts all arrived within a year.

      Kuhn et al., ICLR 2023; Manakul et al., EMNLP 2023; Min et al., EMNLP 2023
    3. 2024
      Semantic entropy in Nature; conformal factuality and conformal language modelling

      Farquhar and colleagues showed semantic entropy detects confabulations across several tasks. In the same year conformal methods were extended to claim-level guarantees and to sampled answer sets. Cheap single-pass probes that mimic semantic entropy appeared.

      Farquhar et al., Nature 2024; Mohri & Hashimoto, ICML 2024; Quach et al., ICLR 2024
    4. 2025
      Why models hallucinate: the incentive problem

      Kalai and colleagues argued that hallucination arises from statistical limits on rare facts and is then reinforced because common evaluations reward confident guesses over abstention. The suggested fix is to change the scoring to penalise confident errors.

      Kalai et al., arXiv 2509.04664
    5. 2025–26
      Detection as hypothesis testing, and conformal wrappers on detectors

      Reported in search results: work that combines several evaluation scores through conformal p-values to control the false alarm rate (arXiv August 2025, later in ACL 2026 Findings), and a December 2025 paper arguing that embedding-similarity detection alone is not enough for production RAG, with a conformal approach added.

      arXiv 2508.18473; ACL 2026 Findings; arXiv 2512.15068
    6. 2026
      Entropy alone is not enough; adaptive sampling; calibrated abstention

      As reported: a March 2026 preprint finds that entropy signals by themselves are insufficient for safe selective prediction and that adding a correctness probe helps; other 2026 preprints reduce the sampling bill by adapting the number of samples and report conformal calibration on semantic entropy to give an error guarantee among accepted answers. Treat the headline benchmark numbers in these as unverified by me.

      arXiv 2603.21172; arXiv 2603.22812

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are about the detector or the guarantee, not the model. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Consistent but wrong

      The model repeats the same mistake in every sample, so semantic entropy is zero and the answer passes.

      Spot itTest on known stable misconceptions and on bank-specific facts that older versions of a policy used to say. Pair sampling with a source check.
    • Grounded in the wrong document

      The answer is faithful to the retrieved passage, but that passage was an outdated or irrelevant version.

      Spot itEvaluate retrieval separately: for a set of questions with known right passages, is the right one in the top results, and is it the current version?
    • Verifier with the model's own blind spots

      The same family of model writes the answer and judges it, so both make correlated errors, and the judge favours fluent text.

      Spot itMeasure the verifier against human labels on a hard set, with an interval, and include answers that are fluent but wrong.
    • Answer-level alarms drown the reviewers

      A claim-level false alarm rate that looks low produces a high answer-level one on long outputs.

      Spot itCompute 1 − (1 − f)k for k claims per answer, and watch the queue volume in a pilot.
    • Calibration set that does not match the traffic

      Calibrated on easy FAQ questions, deployed on complex corporate-lending queries. Exchangeability fails and the guarantee is void.

      Spot itCompare the distribution of question types, languages and lengths in calibration against live traffic. Recalibrate when they diverge; monitor the realised error rate on a labelled live sample.
    • Tuning the model or prompt on the calibration set

      The set was reused to pick prompts, so errors seen in it are no longer a fair draw.

      Spot itAsk when the calibration set was last touched and whether anything was selected using it. Keep a sealed set for the guarantee.
    • Quoting the average as a per-case promise

      "95% of answers are right" gets heard as "this answer is 95% likely to be right", or as holding in every customer segment.

      Spot itReport coverage by subgroup: language, product, question type. Averages can hide a segment at 80%.
    • A tiny calibration set

      With 30 examples, a single unlucky draw gives a threshold that misses the target by a wide margin.

      Spot itAsk for the calibration size. The distribution of realised coverage narrows only slowly, roughly with the square root of n (Simulation 3).
    • Filtering that deletes the answer

      A strict error budget removes so many claims that what remains is useless, like "the card has a fee".

      Spot itReport completeness (share of true claims kept) next to the guarantee, and show users a sample of filtered outputs.
    • Arabic and mixed-language text

      Clustering and entailment models treat "٢٣٠ درهم" and "AED 230" as different, or miss negation in Arabic.

      Spot itBuild a small test set of paraphrase pairs, digit variants and negations in each language and measure the clustering step on it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We can't make the assistant never wrong, but we can measure how often it is wrong and make it say 'I'm not sure' when it should. We check every statement against the policy documents it was given, and we pick the point at which it steps back by testing on a few hundred answers that our own staff have marked. That gives us a sentence like 'at most one answer in twenty released contains an unsupported statement', and we can monitor that number every month.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For grounded tasks I'd decompose answers into atomic claims, verify each against retrieved evidence with an NLI model or a judge validated on human labels, and report claim-level and answer-level precision and recall with intervals. For the abstention rule I'd use split-conformal calibration on human-verified claims, in the Mohri and Hashimoto style, with a held-out set never used for prompt selection, and report realised coverage by language and product. The guarantee is marginal and needs exchangeability, so I'd add drift monitoring on the input distribution and a rolling labelled sample.”
    “Can't we just ask the model how confident it is?”

    You can, and for some models and tasks the stated confidence is informative, but it is often overconfident and shifts with phrasing. Use it as one feature, check it against labelled outcomes, and let calibration decide the cut-off. Sampling-based and source-based checks add information that a single stated number does not.

    “Why not use temperature zero so it gives the same answer every time?”

    It makes the output repeatable, not correct. A deterministic model that is wrong is wrong every time and hides its uncertainty. Semantic entropy needs variation to measure; you can sample for the audit and serve the greedy answer if you want.

    “What exactly does 'guaranteed' mean in conformal prediction?”

    If calibration and future data are exchangeable, then averaged over calibration sets and test cases, the share of released answers meeting the criterion is at least 1−α. It is not a guarantee for each case, each customer segment, or a shifted population, and with a small calibration set any single threshold may be off. It is a precise statement under a stated assumption, which is why it is auditable.

    “The verifier flags 3% of claims wrongly. Is that good enough?”

    Depends on answer length. With five claims, about 14% of correct answers get flagged; with ten, about 26%. Ask about volume per day times review minutes, not the per-claim rate. And ask what share of real errors it catches.

    “Does RAG fix hallucination?”

    It reduces it for questions the documents answer, by giving the model something to quote. It does not remove it: the model can ignore, misread or embellish the passage, and retrieval can fetch the wrong one. That is why groundedness checks stay in place after RAG.

    “How many labelled examples do we need to calibrate?”

    Enough that the spread in realised coverage is acceptable. With α = 10%, a few hundred answers give a tight result; below about 50 the outcome varies a lot between calibration sets. I'd start with a few hundred human-labelled answers per major language and product area, and treat it as a test-set cost alongside the evaluation budget.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You sample 10 answers and cluster them by meaning: 6, 3 and 1. Compute the semantic entropy and say whether it indicates doubt.

    H = −(0.6 log₂0.6 + 0.3 log₂0.3 + 0.1 log₂0.1) = 0.442 + 0.521 + 0.332 ≈ 1.30 bits. Three competing meanings with the top one holding only 60% is clear doubt; a confident answer would be near 0.5 bits or less.

    2. Why can plain string entropy mislead, and how does semantic entropy avoid it?

    Different wordings of the same answer are counted as different outcomes, so a model that is sure but varied in phrasing looks uncertain. Semantic entropy first merges answers that mean the same (mutual entailment), so only disagreement of meaning adds entropy.

    3. A claim verifier flags 3% of correct claims. Answers have 8 claims. What share of fully correct answers get flagged?

    1 − 0.97⁸ = 1 − 0.784 ≈ 22%. False alarms compound with answer length.

    4. With n = 99 calibration scores and α = 0.1, which ranked score is the threshold? What coverage range is guaranteed?

    ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉ = ⌈100 × 0.9⌉ = the 90th smallest. Coverage is at least 90% and at most 90% + 1/100 = 91%.

    5. Two teams have the same average coverage of 95%. One has 100 calibration examples and one has 2,000. Which would you trust for a single deployment, and why?

    The 2,000. The average over calibration sets is the same, but realised coverage for one particular set varies much more with 100, so your deployed threshold may fall well short of 95%. Spread shrinks roughly with the square root of n.

    6. In conformal factuality, what is the nonconformity score of a calibration answer, and what does the final guarantee say?

    The highest confidence score among that answer's false claims (or minus infinity if it has none). After removing all test claims scoring at or below τ, the probability that every remaining claim is true is at least 1−α, under exchangeability.

    7. The assistant was calibrated on retail FAQs and is now rolled out to corporate-trade-finance questions. What breaks, and what do you do?

    Exchangeability: the new questions are harder and the score-to-error relationship changes, so the stated coverage no longer holds. Build a labelled calibration set from the new domain, recalibrate (or calibrate per domain), and monitor realised error on a labelled live sample.

    8. Name a hallucination that semantic entropy will miss, and what catches it.

    A stable falsehood: the model gives the same wrong answer every time, such as an out-of-date fee. Checking claims against the current source document catches it, which is why groundedness checks and sampling checks are combined.
