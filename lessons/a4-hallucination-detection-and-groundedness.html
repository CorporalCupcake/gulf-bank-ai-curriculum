    Catching Hallucinations, and Promising When to Abstain

    How to tell when a language model is making things up: disagreement between its own samples, claim-by-claim checks against the source, and conformal prediction, which turns a rough confidence score into a statistical promise about how often the answers you let through are wrong.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA hallucination detector is a noisy score, not a verdict; the useful questions are whether the answer is supported by the source you gave it, whether the model would say the same thing twice, and how to choose a cut-off on the score so that the error rate among answers you release is provably below a number you picked, provided tomorrow looks like your calibration data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A language model writes plausible text, and a wrong figure is as plausible as a right one. Ask the bank's assistant for the Gold card late fee and it says "AED 230 per late payment" in a calm, fluent sentence. The product sheet says AED 200. Nothing in the tone gives it away.

    Three ideas make this manageable.

    • Ask again. Ten samples that agree in meaning mean the model is fairly sure; four different amounts means it is guessing.
    • Check against the source. Every claim should trace to a sentence in the product sheet the assistant was given.
    • Abstain, with a guarantee. Every detector gives a score. Below a line, the assistant says "not sure, ask the product team". Conformal prediction picks the line from held-out examples so that the error rate among released answers stays under a budget you chose, say 5%.
    Hold onto thisDetection is not truth. A detector has false alarms and misses like any classifier, so it needs a threshold chosen from costs and an honest test set. Conformal prediction makes the threshold carry a guarantee.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What "hallucination" means, and why models do it

    Two failures hide under one word.

    • Unfaithful to the context (groundedness failure): the model was given a document, such as a retrieved policy, and says something it does not support or contradicts. Check it against the document.
    • Unfactual (world-knowledge failure): the model answers from memory and is wrong. You need an outside truth, which is much harder.

    The first matters most at a bank, where assistants must stay inside supplied text. A fabricated citation is a common form.

    Why it happens: a model predicts the next word. A fact seen many times gets high probability. A fact seen once or never, like one bank's fee schedule, has no pattern, yet the model must still output something, like a student guessing rather than leaving the exam blank. That is why abstention matters.

    Disagreement as a signal: sampling consistency and semantic entropy

    Raw token probabilities mislead, because one meaning has many wordings. A model certain the fee is AED 200 might phrase it six ways and look unsure. Wording variation is noise, not doubt.

    Semantic entropy fixes this. It is like polling a room: count the distinct opinions, not the ways people phrased them.

    1. Sample 5 to 10 answers at non-zero temperature.
    2. Group them by meaning, usually with a second model that checks whether two answers imply each other.
    3. Measure how spread out the groups are. One big group means confidence. Many small groups mean the model is likely confabulating.

    Read the number as bits of doubt: 0 means every sample means the same, 1 is a coin flip between two meanings. A move from 0.5 to 1.3 means a real rival answer has appeared.

    It costs roughly ten model calls per answer, so use it for audits and high-stakes answers. The limit: consistency is not correctness. A model can be consistently wrong, like a student who confidently repeats a textbook error. Semantic entropy catches confabulation and misses stable misconceptions, so use it as one signal among several.

    Groundedness: claim verification and citation checks

    When the model answers from supplied documents, check it like a marker with a mark scheme.

    1. Decompose the answer into atomic claims, one checkable statement each.
    2. Retrieve the relevant source passage for each claim.
    3. Verify each claim: supported, contradicted, or not enough information. A small model or an LLM judge does this.
    4. Aggregate: faithfulness is the share of claims supported, or flag the answer if any claim fails.

    Citation checks are the cheap layer. First confirm the cited passage exists (fabricated references fail here), then that it supports the claim.

    False alarms pile up with length. A verifier that wrongly flags 3% of good claims flags about 22% of good eight-claim answers. Long answers swamp reviewers.

    The cut-off is a business decision: wrong claims let through, against reviewer hours spent. Conformal prediction makes that decision carry a guarantee.

    Conformal prediction: a promise from a calibration set

    Conformal prediction wraps any model and turns its scores into a guaranteed error rate. Analogy: set the pass mark from last year's marked papers, and trust it this year if the cohort is similar. The only assumption is exchangeability: calibration and future examples come from the same process.

    1. Hold out labelled examples the model never trained on (the calibration set).
    2. Score each one for how wrong or surprising the model was.
    3. Set the threshold at the score that only a chosen share (your error budget α) of calibration examples exceed.
    4. Apply that threshold to new examples.

    The guarantee: a new example lands on the safe side of the threshold at least 1−α of the time. Tightening α buys safety and costs more deleted or abstained answers.

    It is an average, not a per-case promise: 99% on easy questions and 80% on hard ones still averages 95%. With a small calibration set, your particular threshold can also be unlucky (Simulation 3).

    Conformal prediction for LLMs: filtering claims and abstaining

    Free text needs a definition of "correct" and a score. Two approaches:

    • Conformal factuality. Score each claim, calibrate on answers whose false claims are known, then delete every claim below the threshold. With probability at least 1−α, all remaining claims are true. Like redacting every line you cannot vouch for, it trades completeness for factuality; too strict a budget leaves "the card has a fee".
    • Abstention by threshold. Answer only if a confidence score (semantic entropy, verifier score or a mix) is on the safe side of a calibrated line; otherwise hand over to a human.

    Simulation 3 uses conformal factuality on invented data, with the same verifier as before.

    The promise holds for whoever labelled "false claim". Calibrate on human-verified claims, not an LLM judge's opinion.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are in the detector or the guarantee, not the model.

    • Consistent but wrong

      The model repeats the same mistake every time, so semantic entropy is zero and the answer passes.

      Spot itPair sampling with a source check.
    • Grounded in the wrong document

      The answer is faithful to the retrieved passage, but the passage was outdated or irrelevant.

      Spot itEvaluate retrieval separately.
    • Verifier with the model's blind spots

      The same model family writes and judges, so errors correlate and the judge favours fluent text.

      Spot itMeasure it against human labels on a hard set.
    • Calibration set that does not match the traffic

      Calibrated on easy FAQs, deployed on corporate-lending queries. Exchangeability fails and the guarantee is void. Reusing the set to tune prompts has the same effect.

      Spot itCompare question types and languages against live traffic, and monitor realised error on a labelled sample.
    • Quoting the average as a per-case promise

      "95% of answers are right" gets heard as "this answer is 95% likely right", or as true in every segment.

      Spot itReport coverage by language and product.
    • Arabic and mixed-language text

      Clustering and entailment models treat "٢٣٠ درهم" and "AED 230" as different, or miss Arabic negation.

      Spot itTest paraphrases, digit variants and negations per language.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You sample 10 answers and cluster them by meaning: 6, 3 and 1. Does semantic entropy indicate doubt?

    Yes. Three rival meanings, with the top one at only 60%, is clear doubt (about 1.3 bits).

    2. Why can plain string entropy mislead, and how does semantic entropy avoid it?

    String entropy counts different wordings of one answer as different outcomes, so a sure but varied model looks uncertain. Semantic entropy merges same-meaning answers first.

    3. A claim verifier wrongly flags only 3% of correct claims. Why can it still bury reviewers?

    False alarms pile up with length. With 8 claims per answer, about 22% of fully correct answers get flagged, because any one false alarm flags the whole answer.

    4. You move the error budget α from 10% to 5%. What do you gain and what does it cost?

    You gain safety: the promised share of covered answers rises from 90% to 95%. It costs more deleted claims or abstentions, so the assistant is less useful.

    5. Two teams both average 95% coverage. One calibrated on 100 examples, one on 2,000. Which do you trust for a single deployment, and why?

    The 2,000. The average is the same, but realised coverage for one particular set varies far more at 100, so your deployed threshold may fall well short of 95%.

    6. In conformal factuality, what does the final guarantee say?

    After removing claims scoring below the threshold, the probability that every remaining claim is true is at least 1−α, provided calibration and live data are exchangeable.

    7. The assistant was calibrated on retail FAQs and is rolled out to corporate trade finance. What breaks, and what do you do?

    Exchangeability. The questions are harder, so the stated coverage no longer holds. Recalibrate on a labelled set for the new domain and monitor realised error.

    8. Name a hallucination that semantic entropy will miss, and what catches it.

    A stable falsehood, such as an out-of-date fee given the same way every time. Checking claims against the current source catches it, so combine groundedness and sampling checks.
