    Recommenders and Document AI: Next-Best-Product, Ranking, and Reading Cheques and Scans

    How a bank decides what to offer a customer who has told it almost nothing, why ranking metrics differ from classification metrics, and how a scanned cheque becomes trustworthy data.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth turn a sparse, noisy input into a short ranked list or a few fields, and the value comes less from the model than from the constraints, the evaluation and routing uncertain cases to people.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Recommending: guess the empty cells

    Picture a table: one row per customer, one column per product. A cell is 1 if held, blank otherwise. Two million customers and thirty products is sixty million cells, mostly blank. A blank rarely means "does not want it". It means "never offered".

    A recommender guesses how well each blank would fit. The idea is a good shop assistant's: people who look alike want similar things. If customers with a salary account, credit card and travel insurance often go on to take a gold card, the next customer with that trio is a good candidate. Nobody wrote that rule. It fell out of the table.

    Two things make it harder. The answer is a ranking: an app screen has room for three offers, so which three, in which order, matters. And a bank cannot offer just anything, so a bank recommender is a model inside a set of hard rules.

    Document AI: from pixels to a field you can trust

    A corporate customer submits a letter of credit, bill of lading and invoice: phone photos, stamps over text, Arabic and English lines. A clerk keys 40 fields. We want a model to do the reading.

    The model will be right most of the time and will not know when it is wrong. So the question is never "how accurate is the OCR?" but can the system tell which fields it is sure of? If yes, it posts the sure ones and sends the doubtful ones to a person. If no, you have a fast way to put wrong amounts in the ledger.

    Hold onto thisA recommender is judged on the top of a ranked list and constrained by eligibility, suitability and consent. A document reader is judged on whether its confidence is honest. In both, the model is the smaller part.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F9.5 · The shape of a recommender: retrieve, then rank

    Think of hiring: skim thousands of CVs cheaply, then interview a shortlist properly. Big recommenders work in stages for the same reason.

    1. Candidate generation. A cheap model cuts the catalogue to a few hundred items.
    2. Filtering. Remove anything not allowed: ineligible, already held, opted out, not Sharia-compliant for this customer. These are rules, not predictions.
    3. Ranking. A richer model scores the survivors using customer history and context, and orders them.
    4. Re-ranking and policy. Business constraints: no three credit products in a row, contact limits, a little exploration.

    A bank with thirty products can skip retrieval: one propensity model per product ("probability this customer takes it if offered"), ranked by expected value. That is plain classification (F4, F7) plus a ranking step. Recommender machinery earns its place with large catalogues, thin history, or a wish for one shared representation.

    Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation

    Neighbourhood methods are what Simulation 1 does. Two products are similar if the same customers hold them (cosine similarity, F1.1: it compares direction only, ignoring size). Score a candidate by its similarity to what the customer already holds. Simple, explainable ("customers with your products often take this") and a hard baseline to beat.

    Matrix factorisation compresses the table, like describing each film by a few taste dimensions rather than listing every viewer. Every customer and every product gets a short list of numbers, trained so that when a customer's tastes and a product's profile point the same way, the score is high. Because those short lists are learned from all the cells you did observe, a blank cell gets a sensible guess.

    Where the blanks come from matters. In banking nearly every signal is implicit (holds the product, opened the offer). The standard treatment treats each blank as a weak negative and each observed cell as a positive that grows more confident with evidence. Same quiet assumption as F3.5: "no label" is not "no".

    • Factors are not interpretable. You can see which products sit near each other, but "factor 7" has no name. To tell a compliance reviewer why an offer was made, use a neighbourhood explanation or a separate explainer (F8).
    • A tuned simple model is hard to beat. A well-known neural approach lost to a properly tuned dot product when re-tested (Rendle et al., 2020). Untuned baselines make fancy models look good.
    • Cold start is structural. A new customer or product has no vector. Fix it with features (age band, segment, category) and with popularity or rules until behaviour accumulates.

    Two-tower models and sequences

    The modern retrieval workhorse is the two-tower model: one network turns everything known about the customer into a vector, another does the same for the product, and training pulls matching pairs together and pushes random pairs apart (the contrastive idea from F5.4). Because vectors come from features, new users and items get one at once. At serving time the customer's vector fetches the closest few hundred products in milliseconds by fast approximate search, which trades a little recall for speed.

    A newer thread treats history as a sequence and predicts the next event like a language model predicts the next word (F5.3). For a bank the natural sequence is the stream of transactions and product events. Treat these as later steps after a tuned baseline.

    Ranking metrics: judging a list, not a label

    A classifier is scored on every row. A recommender is scored on the top of a list, because nobody scrolls to position 40. A right answer on page 3 is a miss.

    • Precision@k: of the k shown, the share taken. 0.33 at k=3 means one offer in three landed. It ignores order within the top k.
    • Recall@k: of everything they took, the share that appeared in the top k. 0.8 means you surfaced four of five.
    • NDCG@k: a score from 0 to 1 that rewards hits near the top, with a smooth discount for each position further down. Going from 0.5 to 0.7 means your hits sit noticeably higher; 1.0 is perfect order. Average precision (AP) is a similar early-hits-count-more measure.

    Two cautions. Match the metric to the product: if the app shows three offers, report @3, not @20. And offline numbers reward predicting what customers would have done anyway, as the next section shows.

    Next-best-action in a bank: the extra layers

    A bank recommender differs from a streaming service's in four ways.

    • Eligibility and suitability are hard constraints. A product the customer cannot or should not be sold is never a candidate, however high it scores. Check specifics with compliance. A customer who asked for Sharia-compliant products gets only those.
    • The objective is value, not clicks. Rank by propensity × margin × (1 − risk). A personal-finance offer accepted by customers who then default is a loss.
    • Contact is scarce. SMS and push have limits and customers opt out. A next-best-action engine chooses among offers, service messages and doing nothing, within a contact budget.
    • Prediction is not incrementality. Like giving a free umbrella to people already carrying one: the top-ranked customer is the one most likely to buy anyway. The target is the customer whose behaviour changes because of the offer (uplift). Measure with a controlled holdout (F3.6) or uplift methods, and learn online with bandits.

    The feedback loop is the other danger. The model learns only about products it showed, so popular items get shown, clicked and more popular. A little exploration (say a random 5% of impressions) keeps the picture whole. It is the same selective-labels problem as F2.6.

    F9.6 · From pixels to fields: how document AI works

    Meaning in a document comes from where text sits as much as what it says. So the pipeline has stages, and each can fail:

    1. Capture and clean-up. Deskew, fix contrast, split pages.
    2. Classify the document. Cheque, invoice, ID, bill of lading? Layout is a strong signal.
    3. Layout analysis. Find header, table, signature, stamp, the MICR line on a cheque.
    4. Text recognition (OCR). Pixels to characters, with a confidence per word.
    5. Field extraction. Which text is the payee, the amount, the date, the IBAN.
    6. Validation. Check fields against each other and the world: figures match words, IBAN checksum passes, totals add up.
    7. Routing. Post automatically, or send to a human with doubtful fields highlighted.

    Stage 6 gives most of the real accuracy. A cheque states the amount twice, in figures and words, precisely so a misread can be caught.

    Reading models: OCR, layout-aware models and vision-language models

    Three generations of reader exist, and a bank may run all three.

    ApproachHow it readsStrengthWeakness
    Classic OCRDetects text lines, recognises characters, outputs text and boxes.Fast, cheap, predictable on clean print.No sense of meaning. Struggles with handwriting, stamps over text and poor scans.
    Layout-aware transformers (LayoutLM family)Take OCR text plus each word's position and label each word: payee, amount, date.Learn from a few hundred labelled documents. Strong on forms and invoices.Inherit OCR mistakes. Need labels per document family.
    Vision-language modelsLook at the whole page image and generate the fields directly.Handle messy layouts with little template work.Can hallucinate text not on the page, give no per-character confidence, cost more per page.

    Hallucination is the key difference. A classic engine that cannot read a digit outputs the wrong digit or nothing. A generative reader can output a fluent, well-formed, wrong number with no sign of doubt. So add a second check: re-read another way, compare with a redundant field, or tie every value to a bounding box a human can see.

    Regional traps: Arabic runs right to left but numbers inside it run left to right; one page may mix Western and Arabic-Indic digits, and Hijri and Gregorian dates; names transliterate several ways (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed), which matters when matching a customer file or sanctions list (see human approval gates and document AI for trade finance).

    Confidence, thresholds and straight-through processing

    The business goal is straight-through processing (STP): the share of documents going from scan to ledger with no human touch. The lever is a confidence threshold. Fields above it are accepted, and a document is automatic only if every field passes.

    "Every" is why document AI is harder than accuracy numbers suggest. Think of a chain of 12 links, each 98% reliable: the whole chain holds only about 78% of the time. Diagnose at field level; decide at document level.

    A confidence score is useful only if calibrated (F7.4): among fields scored 0.95, about 95% should be right. Neural readers are usually overconfident, so check on held-out documents, per field type. The trade-off is the fraud-threshold logic of F7.3: a higher threshold sends more to people (cost) and lets fewer errors through (loss).

    • Field count drives everything. A cheque has a handful of fields and can reach high STP. A letter of credit with dozens almost never passes untouched, so trade finance aims for "assisted" processing: the system pre-fills and highlights, a person confirms.
    • Review the field, not the document. Route only doubtful fields to a person, with the image crop beside the value.
    • Error costs differ. A wrong cheque amount is a loss event; a wrong postcode is a nuisance. Set thresholds per field type.

    Document AI is also a fraud surface: cheques and IDs can be altered or forged, and deepfakes affect KYC face-matching. Pair the reader with tamper checks. A correctly read document may still be fake.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Popularity wins and feeds itself

      Offline score is excellent because the model recommends the most-held products: correct and useless. It also learns only from what it showed, so the head of the catalogue gets richer, and new customers and products get nothing sensible.

      Spot itCompare with a "top-N most popular" baseline. Report coverage (distinct products ever recommended), impression share over months, and metrics for new customers and new products separately. Keep an exploration slice.
    • Random split for a time-ordered problem

      Training on later purchases to predict earlier ones inflates hit rates.

      Spot itAsk how the split was made. It should be by time: features up to date T, outcomes after T.
    • Rewarding predictions that would have happened anyway

      Targeting people most likely to take a card, who would have taken it without an offer. The campaign "works" and adds nothing.

      Spot itAsk for a randomised holdout and the lift against it in AED, not offline AUC or NDCG.
    • Recommending what the customer cannot or should not have

      An ineligible product, a non-Sharia-compliant product to an Islamic-only customer, a high-risk fund to an unsuited customer.

      Spot itCheck that eligibility, suitability and consent filters run after the model on every request, and that every recommendation shown is logged with its reason.
    • Field accuracy and confidence taken at face value

      "98% accurate" on documents with 20 fields means about one document in three has an error. And a confidence of 0.99 on a misread digit gives false comfort.

      Spot itAsk for document-level exact match and the error rate among automatically processed documents. Check accuracy against confidence on held-out documents, per field type.
    • Reader that invents text, or was only tested on clean samples

      A vision-language model fills in a plausible beneficiary or amount that is not on the page. Or the vendor demo used crisp printed English invoices, while yours are phone photos of Arabic and English documents with stamps over text.

      Spot itRequire each value tied to an image region and test on blurred samples where the right answer is "unreadable". Build a test set from your own recent documents, reported by scan quality, language and type.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a recommender that wins on offline hit rate still add no value to the bank?

    Hit rate shows customers took a product, not that the offer caused it. The model may target people who would have bought anyway, or just push popular products. You need a randomised holdout and lift in AED.

    2. A customer took exactly one product and your list ranked it third. Which ranking metric notices that it was third rather than first, and which does not?

    NDCG (and average precision) notice: a hit at position 3 scores lower than one at position 1. Precision@3 and recall@3 do not, because they only ask whether the hit is somewhere in the top 3.

    3. What does it mean that matrix-factorisation factors are not interpretable, and how do you still explain a recommendation?

    The learned dimensions have no assigned meaning; only which things sit close together matters. Explain with neighbours (similar customers or products), the strongest features, and logged eligibility rules. Do not invent names for factors.

    4. A new product launches next month. How does a pure collaborative filter treat it, and what would you add?

    It has no interactions, so it is never recommended. Add content features (type, price band, target segment) so a feature-based model can place it at once, plus an exploration slice to gather early data.

    5. A reader is 98.5% accurate per field and a trade document has 25 fields. Why is it wrong to expect 98.5% of documents to be fully correct?

    A document is only right if every field is right, so small errors compound: roughly one document in three would contain a mistake. Hence document-level evaluation and field-level routing. Real errors are correlated, so the exact figure differs.

    6. What does it mean for a confidence score to be calibrated, and how would you test it for a cheque amount field?

    Among amounts scored 0.9, about 90% should be right. Group held-out cheques by confidence and check actual accuracy in each group. If the 0.99 group is only 95% accurate, the score is overconfident and needs recalibration (F7.4).

    7. Name three cross-checks that catch misread fields without a human.

    Figures against words; IBAN or account checksum; date plausibility; invoice totals against line items; beneficiary against the counterparty file.

    8. Why is a generative document reader riskier than classic OCR for amounts, and what mitigates it?

    It can produce a fluent, plausible number that is not on the page, with no sign of doubt. Tie each value to an image region, re-read with a second method, cross-check redundant fields, and route disagreement to a person.
