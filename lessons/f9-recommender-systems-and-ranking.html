    Recommenders and Document AI: Next-Best-Product, Ranking, and Reading Cheques and Scans

    How a bank decides what to offer a customer when most customers have told it almost nothing, why ranking metrics differ from classification metrics, and how a scanned cheque or trade document becomes trustworthy data, with confidence scores that decide what a human must check.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth topics are about turning an enormous, sparse, noisy input (a customer's thin history; a scan of a stamped, folded page) into a short ranked list or a few fields, and in both the value is decided less by the model than by the constraints, the evaluation and the routing of uncertain cases to people.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Recommending: guess the empty cells

    Picture a table with one row per customer and one column per product: salary account, savings, credit card, home finance, family takaful, an investment fund. A cell is 1 if the customer holds it and blank otherwise. For a bank with two million customers and thirty products, that is sixty million cells, and most are blank. A blank does not mean "does not want it". It mostly means "was never offered it, or has not got round to it".

    A recommender fills in the blanks with a guess of how well each one would fit. The oldest idea in the field is also the best: people who look alike want similar things. If customers who hold a salary account, a credit card and travel insurance often go on to take a gold card, then the next customer with that trio is a good candidate for one. Nobody wrote that rule. It fell out of the table.

    Two things make this harder than it sounds. First, the answer is a ranking, not a yes or no: you have room for three offers on an app screen, so what matters is which three, in which order. Second, a bank cannot offer just anything. Some customers are not eligible, some products are unsuitable for them, some have opted out of marketing. So a recommender at a bank is a model inside a set of hard rules.

    Document AI: from pixels to a field you can trust

    Now the second problem. A corporate customer presents a letter of credit, a bill of lading and a commercial invoice, as scans, some photographed on a phone, with stamps across the text, a few lines in Arabic and a few in English, dates in two calendars. A clerk reads them and keys 40 fields into a system. The ambition is to have a model do the reading.

    The model's reading will be right most of the time and wrong some of the time, and it will not know which. So the useful question is never "how accurate is the OCR?" It is: can the system tell which fields it is sure of? If it can, it posts the sure ones automatically and sends the doubtful ones to a person. If it cannot, you have either an expensive human process with extra steps or a fast way to put wrong amounts into the ledger.

    Hold onto thisA recommender is judged on the top of a ranked list and constrained by eligibility, suitability and consent. A document reader is judged on whether its confidence is honest. In both, the model is the smaller part of the job. The larger part is what you measure, what you forbid, and who looks at the doubtful cases.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F9.5 · The shape of a recommender: retrieve, then rank

    Big recommenders have two stages because scoring every item for every user with a heavy model is too slow. A retail bank with thirty products could in principle score all of them. A video or e-commerce platform with millions of items cannot, and its design has become the vocabulary of the field.

    1. Candidate generation (retrieval). A cheap model narrows the full catalogue to a few hundred candidates: items similar to what the user held, items popular with similar users, items from a business rule such as "all products this segment is eligible for".
    2. Filtering. Remove anything not allowed: ineligible, already held, customer opted out, product withdrawn, not Sharia-compliant for this customer's declared preference. These are rules, not predictions, and they run on every request.
    3. Ranking. A richer model scores the survivors using many features (the customer's history, the product, the context such as time of day or channel) and orders them.
    4. Re-ranking and policy. Apply business constraints: do not show three credit products in a row, cap how often the same offer repeats, enforce contact limits, add a little exploration.

    For next-best-product at a bank the catalogue is small, so stages 1 and 3 often merge into one propensity model per product: "probability this customer takes product j if offered in the next 30 days", ranked by expected value. That is a plain classification problem (F4, F7) with a ranking step bolted on. The recommender machinery earns its place when the catalogue is large (merchant offers, content, funds), when history is thin, or when you want one shared representation rather than thirty separate models.

    Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation

    Neighbourhood methods do what Simulation 1 does. Measure how similar two products are by who holds them (cosine similarity from F1.1: the angle between their columns), then score a candidate by its similarity to what the customer already has. Swap rows and columns and you get user-based filtering: find customers like you, recommend what they have. Simple, explainable ("customers with your products often take this") and a surprisingly hard baseline to beat.

    Matrix factorisation compresses the table. Give every customer a short vector u and every product a short vector v, each of say 32 numbers, and train them so the dot product predicts the cell:

    r^(i,j)=bj+ui·vj

    bj is a baseline popularity term for product j; ui and vj are the learned vectors for customer i and product j. A large dot product means the two vectors point the same way, which is the same geometry as the embeddings in F1.1. Training minimises the error on the known cells plus a penalty that keeps the vectors small (regularisation, F4.1). Two million customers and thirty products is 60 million cells, but with 32 factors it is only about 64 million numbers to learn and they are shared across all the cells you did not observe.

    Where the "gaps" come from matters. If the cells are star ratings, a blank is truly unknown. In banking nearly all signals are implicit: holds the product, made a purchase, opened the offer. The standard treatment (Hu, Koren and Volinsky, 2008) treats every blank as a weak negative and every observed cell as a positive with a confidence that grows with how much evidence there is. It is the same quiet assumption as in F3.5: "no label" is not "no".

    Three honest points about factorisation.

    • The factors are not interpretable by design. You can inspect which products sit near each other, but "factor 7" has no name. If a compliance reviewer wants to know why a customer was offered a product, you need either a neighbourhood explanation or a separate explainer (F8).
    • A dot product is hard to beat. Rendle and colleagues (2020) re-ran a well-known neural approach against a properly tuned dot-product model and found the dot product won. The lesson is about tuning: untuned baselines make fancy models look good.
    • Cold start is structural. A new customer has no vector; a new product has none either. The practical fixes are features (age band, segment, channel, product category) so that a new row gets a vector from what is known, and popularity or rules until behaviour accumulates.

    Two-tower models and sequences

    The modern workhorse for retrieval at scale is the two-tower model. One neural network turns everything known about the customer into a vector; another turns the product into a vector; training pushes the dot product up for pairs that really happened (a purchase, a click) and down for pairs that did not. It is matrix factorisation with the free vectors replaced by functions of features, so new items and new users can be embedded immediately, and the training uses the contrastive idea from F5.4: pull matching pairs together, push random pairs apart. At serving time all product vectors sit in a nearest-neighbour index and a customer's vector fetches the closest few hundred in milliseconds (Covington et al., 2016; Yi et al., 2019).

    A newer thread treats a customer's history as a sequence and predicts the next event the way a language model predicts the next word, using attention (F5.3). Two examples: sequential models that read the last N interactions, and "generative" recommenders that give each item a short token code and generate the next code directly. Part 4 says where that stands. For a bank, the natural sequence is the stream of transactions and product events, which is also the idea behind foundation models for transaction data.

    Ranking metrics: judging a list, not a label

    A classifier is scored on every row. A recommender is scored on the top of a list, because nobody scrolls to position 40. The metrics all start from the same grid: for each customer, a ranked list and a set of items they actually went on to take.

    • Precision@k: of the top k shown, what share were taken.
    • Recall@k: of everything they took, what share appeared in the top k.
    • Average precision (AP): reward hits more the higher they sit, by averaging precision at each hit.
    • NDCG@k: a hit at position p is worth 1/log2(p+1), so position 1 is worth 1, position 3 is worth 0.5, position 7 is worth 0.33. Normalise by the best possible ordering so the score lies between 0 and 1.
    DCG@k=∑p=1krel(p)log2(p+1),NDCG@k=DCG@kideal DCG@k

    rel(p) is 1 if the item at position p was taken, else 0. The log in the denominator is a discount: later positions count less. Ideal DCG puts all the taken items first. If a customer takes exactly one product and you ranked it third, NDCG@3 is 0.5 ÷ 1 = 0.5.

    Two cautions on metrics. They must match the product: if the app shows three offers, report @3, not @20. And the offline numbers reward the model for predicting what customers would have done anyway. Someone who was going to take a credit card tomorrow counts as a hit whether or not your offer mattered. That gap, between predicting an action and causing it, is the subject of the next section.

    Next-best-action in a bank: the extra layers

    A bank's recommender differs from a streaming service's in four ways that change the design.

    • Eligibility and suitability are hard constraints. A product the customer cannot be sold is never a candidate, however high the model scores it. Suitability (is this appropriate for this customer's circumstances and risk profile?) belongs to compliance. Rules about this exist in both UAE and Saudi frameworks, but I will not quote specifics: check with your compliance and conduct-of-business teams. Islamic products add another eligibility dimension, since a customer who asked for Sharia-compliant products must be offered only those.
    • The objective is value, not clicks. The model ranks by expected profit net of risk and cost, or by customer need, not by likelihood of a click. A personal finance offer that is accepted by customers who then default is a loss. Rank by propensity × margin × (1 − risk), with the risk piece from the credit team's models.
    • Contact is a scarce resource. SMS and push have limits, customers opt out, and fatigue is real. A next-best-action engine chooses among offers, service messages and doing nothing, subject to a contact budget.
    • Prediction is not incrementality. The customer you rank highest is the one most likely to buy anyway. The right target is the customer whose behaviour changes because of the offer (uplift). Model that with a controlled holdout (F3.6) or uplift methods (F9.4), and learn what works online with bandits.

    One more danger is the feedback loop. The model only learns about products it showed. If it never shows the investment fund to customers it thinks are uninterested, it never learns it was wrong. Popular items get shown, get clicked, and become more popular. A small amount of deliberate exploration (a random 5% of impressions, or a bandit) is how you keep seeing the whole picture, and it is the same selective-labels problem as F2.6.

    F9.6 · From pixels to fields: how document AI works

    An image is a grid of numbers (F1.1), but meaning in a document comes from where things are as much as what they say. "AED 250,000" next to "Amount" and the same figure in a footnote are different facts. A classic document pipeline therefore has several stages, and each can fail:

    1. Capture and clean-up. Deskew a rotated page, remove shadows, improve contrast, split multi-page scans, and drop blank sheets.
    2. Classify the document. Is this a cheque, an invoice, an ID card, a bank statement, a bill of lading? A convolutional or vision-transformer classifier (F5.2) does this well because layout is a strong signal.
    3. Layout analysis. Find regions: header, table, signature, stamp, MICR line on a cheque, the machine-readable zone on a passport.
    4. Text recognition (OCR). Turn pixels into characters, with a position and confidence for each word or line.
    5. Field extraction. Decide which text is the payee, the amount in figures, the amount in words, the date, the beneficiary, the IBAN. Early systems used templates; modern ones learn from layout and text together.
    6. Validation. Check the fields against each other and against the world: figures match words, the IBAN checksum passes, the date is not in the future, the beneficiary matches a customer file, totals add up.
    7. Routing. Post automatically, or send to a human with the doubtful fields highlighted.

    Stage 6 is where most of the real accuracy comes from. The cheque's amount in figures and in words is redundancy that exists precisely so that a reader can catch a misread. A system that reads both and compares them gets error detection almost for free.

    Reading models: OCR, layout-aware models and vision-language models

    There are three generations of reader, and a bank may run all three.

    The hallucination point deserves emphasis. A classic OCR engine that cannot read a digit tends to output the wrong digit or nothing. A generative reader that cannot read a digit can output a fluent, well-formed, wrong number with no sign of doubt. That is why serious deployments of generative readers add a second check: re-read the same field with a different method, compare with a redundant field (words against figures), or ground every extracted value back to a bounding box on the page so a human can see the evidence.

    Some features worth knowing for this region. Arabic is written right to left with letters that change shape by position, but numbers inside Arabic text run left to right, so mixed lines confuse naive readers. Documents may use Western digits (0 1 2 3) or Arabic-Indic digits (٠ ١ ٢ ٣) on the same page, and Hijri as well as Gregorian dates. Names are transliterated several ways (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed), which matters when the extracted name is matched against a customer file or a sanctions list (human approval gates and document AI for trade finance go deeper).

    Confidence, thresholds and straight-through processing

    The business outcome of document AI is straight-through processing (STP): the share of documents that go from scan to ledger with no human touch. The lever is a confidence threshold. Each extracted field has a score; fields above the threshold are accepted, and a document is automatic only if every field passes.

    That "every" is what makes document AI harder than the accuracy numbers suggest. Fields multiply. If each field is independently right 98% of the time, a document with 12 fields is entirely right only 0.9812 ≈ 78% of the time. A model with "98% field accuracy" produces a wrong document more than one time in five. Hence evaluation at the field level for diagnosis, and at the document level for the business decision.

    A confidence score is only useful if it is calibrated (F7.4): among fields scored 0.95, about 95% should be right. Neural readers tend to be overconfident, so calibrate on held-out documents, field by field, because dates and amounts fail at different rates. The trade-off is then the same threshold logic as in fraud (F7.3): a higher threshold sends more to people (cost) and lets fewer errors through (loss).

    Three practical lessons from the simulation.

    • Field count drives everything. A cheque has a handful of fields and can reach high STP. A letter of credit with dozens of fields almost never goes through untouched, which is why trade finance projects aim for "assisted" processing: the system pre-fills and highlights, the person confirms.
    • Review the field, not the document. The cheaper design routes only the doubtful fields to a person, with the crop of the image beside the value. Review time per field is seconds, not minutes.
    • The cost of an error is not uniform. A wrong cheque amount is a loss event. A wrong postcode is a nuisance. Set thresholds per field type from what the error costs.

    Last, document AI is also a fraud surface. Cheques and IDs can be altered, forged or regenerated. A reader must be paired with tamper checks (inconsistent fonts or compression patterns, signs of editing, signature comparison to a specimen, MRZ and chip data against the printed face on an ID), and deepfakes now affect face-matching in KYC. Reading a document correctly is not the same as the document being genuine.

    How it connects

    Recommenders sit next to personalisation, experimentation and customer value. Document AI sits next to vision, language and control. Here are the links.

    F1.1 · Vectors and similarityDot products and cosines are the engine

    Customer and product vectors, nearest-neighbour retrieval and item similarity are all F1.1 geometry. If it feels abstract, return to it.

    F5.4 · Contrastive learningHow two-tower models train

    Pull matching customer-product pairs together and push random pairs apart. The same loss trains image-text models and text embeddings.

    F5.2 · ConvolutionsWhere image models come from

    The local-pattern idea behind OCR, signature checks and document classifiers. Vision transformers now compete, using attention over image patches.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Metrics and calibrationThresholds and honest scores

    A recommender's top-k precision and a document reader's confidence threshold are the same decision as a fraud alert threshold. Calibration decides whether the score can be trusted.

    F9.4 · F3.6 · Causal inference and experimentsDid the offer change anything?

    Offline ranking metrics measure prediction. Whether a campaign created value needs a holdout group or an uplift design.

    F9.7 · BanditsExploring while recommending

    A bandit is the principled version of "show something other than the top pick sometimes" and fixes the feedback loop.

    F3.1 · LeakageFeatures as of the offer date

    A propensity model that uses a product held after the offer date to predict the offer's success looks perfect and is useless. Split by time, group by customer.

    B6 · Churn and segmentationValue and segments feed the ranking

    Lifetime value tells you what a product relationship is worth; segments define the cold-start fallbacks. See also churn, since the best next offer is sometimes a retention one.

    B7 · M3 · A14 · Document AI at scaleTrade finance, parsing, Arabic OCR

    Next stops: parsing messy PDFs for retrieval, Arabic OCR, and searching document images directly.

    M3 · Hybrid retrievalMerging ranked lists

    Rank fusion combines two ranked lists, such as a keyword list and a vector list. It is the recommender's "blend retrievers" step with a different name, and it is judged by the same NDCG.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Next-best-product in the app and at the branch. Which three offers to show, or what the relationship manager should raise, under eligibility and consent rules.
    • Merchant and card offers. A large catalogue and a thin history per customer, which is the true recommender setting.
    • Funds and wealth. Suitability rules matter most. The recommender ranks within the allowed set; it does not decide what is allowed.
    • Cheque clearing and deposit. Read amount, payee, date and signature from images, with validation and routing.
    • KYC and onboarding. Read an Emirates ID or national ID, check the machine-readable zone, match to a selfie, and send mismatches to a human.
    • Trade finance and statements. Extract terms from letters of credit and invoices; read salary and cash-flow patterns from uploaded statements for credit assessment.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    In recommenders, the live argument is whether sequence and generative models will replace the retrieve-then-rank stack, and whether the headline gains survive honest baselines. In document AI, general vision-language models have largely absorbed what used to be separate OCR, layout and extraction products, and the argument has moved to reliability, cost and languages other than English.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with two quick searches. The older papers I cite from memory; the 2026 items are only ones I found indexed, and I did not read the papers themselves. Treat the numbers as indications, not facts to quote.

    1. 2008–09
      Implicit feedback and matrix factorisation become standard

      Hu, Koren and Volinsky formalised factorisation for implicit signals. Koren, Bell and Volinsky's 2009 paper, after the Netflix Prize, made latent factors the default mental model.

      Hu et al., ICDM 2008; Koren et al., IEEE Computer, 2009
    2. 2016–19
      Deep two-stage systems, and sampling-bias-corrected two-tower retrieval

      YouTube described the retrieve-then-rank design with neural networks. Later work showed how to train two-tower retrievers properly when negatives are sampled from the batch, which would otherwise over-penalise popular items.

      Covington et al., RecSys 2016; Yi et al., RecSys 2019
    3. 2019–20
      A reproducibility warning

      Dacrema et al. tried to reproduce 18 neural recommendation methods from top conferences: only 7 could be reproduced with reasonable effort, and 6 of those were beaten by simple, well-tuned nearest-neighbour or graph baselines. Rendle et al. found a tuned dot product beats a neural-network similarity. Always ask for the tuned simple baseline.

      Dacrema et al., RecSys 2019; Rendle et al., RecSys 2020
    4. 2023
      Generative retrieval with semantic IDs

      TIGER gives each item a short sequence of discrete codes learned from its content, then trains a sequence model to generate the codes of the next item. It handles new items better because content, not an arbitrary ID, defines the code.

      Rajput et al., NeurIPS 2023
    5. 2024
      Sequential transducers at industrial scale

      Meta's HSTU reformulated ranking and retrieval as sequence transduction over very long user histories, reporting that quality scales with compute in a way earlier recommenders did not.

      Zhai et al., ICML 2024
    6. 2025–26
      End-to-end generative recommenders in production

      Kuaishou's OneRec replaces the cascaded retrieve-and-rank pipeline with one generative model plus preference alignment. A 2026 survey I found reports gains of up to about 1.8% in session watch time and 3.4% in like-through rate over strong baselines on its own platform. 2026 preprints focus on how to design semantic IDs and on whether they beat plain item IDs. These are large consumer platforms with billions of interactions; I would not assume the same gains for a bank with thirty products.

      Deng et al., OneRec, 2025; Generative Recommendation survey (preprint, 2026); check the original papers
    7. 2021–22
      Pre-trained document models

      TrOCR used transformers for text recognition. LayoutLMv3 trained jointly on text, layout and image patches. Donut read a page image and generated the structured output with no separate OCR stage.

      Li et al., TrOCR, 2021; Huang et al., LayoutLMv3, ACM MM 2022; Kim et al., Donut, ECCV 2022
    8. 2025–26
      A wave of open vision-language OCR models

      Models such as olmOCR, DeepSeek-OCR, PaddleOCR-VL and dots.ocr convert page images to markdown or structured text, and compete on benchmarks such as olmOCR-Bench and OmniDocBench. A 2026 roundup I found put leading open models at about 82 to 86 on olmOCR-Bench, and sources disagreed on some scores, which tells you to treat leaderboards with care. I found no Arabic-specific benchmark in my searches; test on your own documents.

      Docsumo OCR roundup, 2026 (secondary source); Koncile open-source OCR list, 2026; check the primary papers

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Popularity wins, and nobody notices

      The model's offline score is excellent because it recommends the most held products. It is correct and useless; the customer already knows about the salary account.

      Spot itCompare against a "top-N most popular" baseline and report coverage (how many distinct products are ever recommended) and results on less common products.
    • Random split for a time-ordered problem

      Training on customers' later purchases to predict earlier ones. Hit rates are inflated.

      Spot itAsk how the split was made. It should be by time: features up to date T, outcomes after T, and no overlap in customers where possible.
    • Rewarding predictions that would have happened anyway

      Targeting the people most likely to take a card, who would have taken it without an offer. The campaign "works" and adds nothing.

      Spot itAsk for a randomised holdout and the lift against it, in AED, not an offline AUC or NDCG.
    • Recommending what the customer cannot or should not have

      An ineligible product, a non-Sharia-compliant product to a customer who asked for Islamic only, or a high-risk fund to an unsuited customer.

      Spot itCheck that eligibility, suitability and consent filters run after the model on every request, and log every recommendation shown with its reason.
    • Feedback loop and popularity bias

      The model only learns from what it showed, so the head of the catalogue gets richer.

      Spot itPlot share of impressions by product over months. Keep an exploration slice and evaluate on it.
    • Cold-start ignored

      Good numbers on existing customers; new customers and new products get nothing sensible.

      Spot itReport metrics separately for customers with fewer than N events, and for products launched in the test window.
    • Field accuracy quoted as document accuracy

      "The reader is 98% accurate" on documents with 20 fields means about one document in three has an error.

      Spot itAsk for document-level exact match and for error rate among documents processed automatically, not field-level accuracy alone.
    • Overconfident reader

      Confidence of 0.99 on a misread digit. The threshold gives a false comfort.

      Spot itPlot accuracy against confidence on held-out documents, per field type. If 0.99-confidence fields are wrong 3% of the time, the score is not calibrated.
    • Generative reader invents text

      A vision-language model fills in a plausible beneficiary name or amount that is not on the page.

      Spot itRequire each value to be tied to a region of the image, re-read it with a second method, and test on blurred or partly obscured samples where the right answer is "unreadable".
    • Tested on clean, English, one-format samples

      The vendor demo used crisp printed English invoices. Your files are phone photos of Arabic and English documents with stamps over text.

      Spot itBuild a test set from your own recent documents, stratified by scan quality, language, document type and branch, and report results per stratum.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “For offers, the model's job is to rank a short list within the products each customer is actually allowed and suited to. We'll prove value with a control group who get no offer, because the people most likely to buy are the ones who would have bought anyway. For documents, we'll let the system read cheques and trade papers, check them against each other, and send only the doubtful fields to staff. The number to watch is not how accurate the reader is, but what share goes straight through and how many of those still contain a mistake.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For next-best-product I'd start with per-product propensity models and a tuned item-based or factorisation baseline, rank by expected margin net of risk, apply eligibility, suitability and consent filters after the model, evaluate NDCG and precision at the screen size on a time split, and measure incrementality with a randomised holdout. For documents I'd build a pipeline with classification, layout, recognition, extraction and cross-field validation, calibrate confidence per field type, and set thresholds from error costs, reporting document-level exact match and the error rate among auto-processed documents, on our own Arabic and English samples.”
    “Why not just recommend the most popular products?”

    Popularity is a strong baseline and we should beat it before claiming anything. But it recommends the same things to everyone, mostly products they already know about or hold. Personalisation matters when the catalogue is wide or customers differ, and it is judged by lift against a control group, not by offline hit rate.

    “Should we use a transformer or generative recommender?”

    Maybe later. The published gains come from platforms with billions of interactions and large catalogues. We have thirty products and thin histories, so I'd get a tuned propensity or factorisation baseline and a proper holdout first, then test a sequence model on transaction history if the baseline plateaus. Evidence from replication studies is that untuned baselines make new models look better than they are.

    “How do we handle a customer who has no history?”

    With features instead of history: segment, age band, channel, salary band if known, and fallback rules for the segment. Popularity within the segment is the honest cold-start default. Then an exploration slice so we learn quickly, and a switch to personalised ranking as events accumulate.

    “The vendor says their reader is 99% accurate on our cheques.”

    I'd ask: 99% of what, on what data? Characters, fields or documents? A cheque has several fields, so field accuracy of 99% is document accuracy of roughly 95%. I'd want results on a sample of our own cheques, including poor scans and handwritten amounts, plus the error rate among automatically cleared items and a calibration plot of their confidence.

    “Can a vision-language model replace the OCR and the checkers?”

    It can replace a lot of the template and layout work, and it is strong on messy pages. But it can produce confident, plausible text that is not on the page, and gives no per-character confidence. I'd use it as one reader among several, tie each value to a region of the image, cross-check against redundant fields and the customer file, and keep a person on anything doubtful.

    “Is the recommendation explainable to a regulator or customer?”

    Neighbourhood methods are easy to explain: customers with products like yours often take this. Factorisation and neural models are not, by themselves, so we log which rule or feature drove each recommendation and keep the eligibility and suitability decisions in transparent rules. What is explainable matters most where the recommendation is closest to advice. I'd confirm with compliance what the local rules require.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can a recommender that wins on offline hit rate still add no value to the bank?

    Offline hit rate measures whether customers took a product, not whether the offer caused it. The model may target people who would have bought anyway, or recommend only popular products. You need a randomised holdout and lift in AED.

    2. A customer has taken exactly one product and your list ranked it third. What are precision@3, recall@3 and NDCG@3?

    Precision@3 = 1/3. Recall@3 = 1/1 = 1. NDCG@3: DCG = 1/log2(4) = 0.5; ideal DCG = 1/log2(2) = 1; so NDCG = 0.5.

    3. What does it mean that the factors in matrix factorisation are not interpretable, and how do you still explain a recommendation?

    The learned dimensions have no assigned meaning; only distances and directions matter. Explain with neighbours (similar customers or products), with the strongest contributing features if you use a feature-based model, and with logged rules for eligibility. Do not invent names for factors.

    4. A new product launches next month. How does a pure collaborative filter treat it, and what would you add?

    It has no interaction history so no vector, and it will never be recommended. Add content features (product type, price band, target segment) so a two-tower or feature-based model can embed it immediately, plus an exploration slice to gather early data.

    5. A reader has 98.5% accuracy per field. A trade document has 25 fields. Roughly what share of documents are completely correct if errors are independent?

    0.98525 ≈ e25 × ln 0.985 = e−0.378 ≈ 0.685. About 68%. This is why document-level evaluation and field-level routing are needed. In reality errors are correlated, so the real figure is somewhat different, but the order of magnitude is the point.

    6. What does it mean for a confidence score to be calibrated, and how would you test it for a cheque amount field?

    Among amounts scored 0.9, about 90% should be right. Take held-out cheques, bin the amount confidence, and plot the actual accuracy per bin. If the 0.99 bin is only 95% accurate, the score is overconfident and the threshold needs recalibration (F7.4).

    7. Name three cross-checks that catch misread fields without a human.

    Amount in figures against amount in words; IBAN or account checksum; date plausibility (not in the future, within validity); totals on an invoice against the sum of line items; beneficiary against the customer or counterparty file; MRZ against the printed face of an ID.

    8. Why is a generative document reader riskier than classic OCR for amounts, and what mitigates it?

    It can produce a fluent, plausible number that is not on the page, with no sign of doubt. Mitigate by grounding each value to a bounding box, re-reading with a second method, cross-checking redundant fields, testing with unreadable samples where the right answer is "cannot read", and routing disagreement to a person.
