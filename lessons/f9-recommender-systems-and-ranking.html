    Recommender Systems and Ranking

    Part 1

    What a recommender is

    In one sentenceA recommender guesses what each person will want next, based on what similar people liked, and shows a short ranked list.

    Picture a table of viewers by films. A tick means "watched". Most cells are blank, and a blank means "not watched yet", not "would dislike". The recommender's job is to fill in the blanks.

    How it differs from other models

    • Classification predicts a label for one row ("will this customer leave?"). A recommender produces an ordered list for each person, and only the top matters.
    • Plain popularity offers everyone the same most-watched items. A recommender personalises the list.
    • Clustering puts people into groups. A recommender uses similarity too, but scores individual items for each person.
    Part 2

    How a recommender works

    Big systems work in stages, like hiring: skim thousands of CVs cheaply, then interview a shortlist properly.

    1. Candidates. A cheap method cuts the catalogue to a few hundred items.
    2. Rules. Remove anything not allowed: already owned, ineligible, opted out. These are rules, not predictions.
    3. Rank. A richer model scores what is left, using the person's history and context, and sorts it.
    4. Adjust. Apply business limits, such as no three loans in a row, contact limits and a little exploration.

    Where the scores come from

    • Neighbourhood method. Call two films similar if the same viewers watched both, then score each unseen film by its similarity to what you watched. It is simple, explainable ("people with your history also took this") and a hard baseline to beat.
    • Matrix factorisation. Give every viewer and every film a short list of learned numbers (its "taste profile"). When a viewer's profile and a film's profile point the same way, the score is high. Because the numbers are learned from all the ticks, every blank gets a sensible guess.
    • Two-tower model. One neural network turns what you know about a person into a list of numbers, another does the same for each item. Matching pairs are pulled together in training. It copes well with new people and items, and is the usual way to pick candidates at scale.

    The cold-start problem: a new customer or product has no ticks, so nothing to learn from. Use their features (age band, category) and popular items until behaviour builds up.

    Part 3

    How to judge a ranked list

    A recommender is scored on the top of its list, because nobody scrolls to position 40. Say the screen shows 3 films and the viewer actually likes 5 films overall.

    • Precision at k. Of the k films shown, what share did they like? 1 hit in 3 shown is 0.33. It ignores order inside the top k.
    • Recall at k. Of the films they like, what share appeared in the top k? Two of their five in the top 3 is 0.4.
    • NDCG at k. A score from 0 to 1 that rewards hits near the top and discounts hits further down. Same hits in a different order give a different score.

    Match k to the screen. If the app shows three offers, report the metrics at 3, not at 20.

    Be wary of offline scores. They reward predicting what customers would have done anyway. The top-ranked customer may buy regardless, like a free umbrella for someone already carrying one. The real goal is customers whose behaviour changes because of the offer, which you measure with a controlled holdout (see the causal inference lesson).

    Feedback loops. The model learns only from what it showed, so popular items get more popular. Showing a random 5% of impressions keeps the picture honest.

    Part 4

    Which approach to use

    ApproachHow it worksStrong atWeak at
    PopularityShow what everyone takesTrivial; handles new customersNo personalisation
    NeighbourhoodItems held by the same peopleSimple, explainableStruggles with thin, sparse data
    Matrix factorisationLearned taste profilesGood guesses for blanks, scales wellProfiles have no names; cold start
    Two-tower / sequenceNeural nets on rich featuresNew items, order of eventsNeeds far more data and engineering
    One propensity model per productClassify, then rank by expected valueFine for around thirty productsDoesn't scale to huge catalogues

    Recommenders are strong at:

    • Large catalogues where nobody can browse everything.
    • Finding items you didn't know to ask for.

    They are weak at:

    • New customers and products, where there is no history.
    • Explanations. A compliance reviewer needs a reason for an offer, and "factor 7" is not one. Use a neighbourhood explanation.

    In practice: start with popularity and a tuned simple model. Try fancier ones only if they beat it. A well-known neural approach lost to a properly tuned dot product when re-tested (Rendle et al., 2020).

    Show the formula

    Score = viewer's taste numbers multiplied by the film's taste numbers, then added up. If the two profiles point the same way the total is large. If they point in different directions it is small or negative.

    In banking, this is next-best-product: eligibility and suitability rules come first, the ranking only chooses among allowed products, and the engine may choose "do nothing". Document AI, which reads cheques and scans, is a different kind of problem and is covered in the banking track.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating a blank as a "no"

      Most customers simply haven't been shown or offered the product. Not holding it is weak evidence, not a rejection.

    • Judging on accuracy over the whole list

      Only the first few positions are seen. Measure at the k that the screen shows.

    • Recommending what they'd buy anyway

      High propensity is not the same as being persuaded. Test with a holdout group.

    • Letting the model override the rules

      An unsuitable or ineligible product is never a candidate, however high it scores.

    1. What does a blank cell in the viewer-by-film table mean?

    "Not watched yet". It does not mean the viewer would dislike the film.

    2. Why work in stages (candidates, rules, rank)?

    A cheap method cuts thousands of items to hundreds, so the rich model only scores a shortlist. Rules sit in between so banned items never appear.

    3. What is cold start, and what can you do?

    A new person or item has no history. Use features and popular items until behaviour builds up.

    4. The screen shows 3 offers. Which metric version should you report?

    The ones at 3, such as precision at 3 and NDCG at 3, because anything lower is never seen.

    5. A bank has 30 products. Do you need a recommender system?

    Often not. Train one propensity model per product and rank by expected value.
