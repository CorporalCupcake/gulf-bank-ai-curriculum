    Recommenders and Document AI: Next-Best-Product, Ranking, and Reading Cheques and Scans

    How a bank decides what to offer a customer when most customers have told it almost nothing, why ranking metrics differ from classification metrics, and how a scanned cheque or trade document becomes trustworthy data, with confidence scores that decide what a human must check.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth topics turn a huge, sparse, noisy input (a thin customer history; a stamped, folded scan) into a short ranked list or a few fields, and the value comes less from the model than from the constraints, the evaluation and the routing of uncertain cases to people.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Recommending: guess the empty cells

    Picture a table: one row per customer, one column per product (salary account, savings, credit card, home finance, takaful, fund). A cell is 1 if held, blank otherwise. Two million customers and thirty products is sixty million cells, mostly blank. A blank rarely means "does not want it". It means "never offered, or not got round to it".

    A recommender guesses how well each blank would fit. The core idea is the same as a good shop assistant's: people who look alike want similar things. If customers with a salary account, credit card and travel insurance often go on to take a gold card, the next customer with that trio is a good candidate. Nobody wrote that rule. It fell out of the table.

    Two things make it harder than it sounds. The answer is a ranking, not yes or no: an app screen has room for three offers, so which three, in which order, is what matters. And a bank cannot offer just anything: some customers are ineligible, some products unsuitable, some customers have opted out. A bank recommender is a model inside a set of hard rules.

    Document AI: from pixels to a field you can trust

    A corporate customer submits a letter of credit, bill of lading and invoice: phone photos, stamps over text, Arabic and English lines, dates in two calendars. A clerk keys 40 fields. We want a model to do the reading.

    The model will be right most of the time and will not know when it is wrong. So the question is never "how accurate is the OCR?" but can the system tell which fields it is sure of? If yes, it posts the sure ones and sends the doubtful ones to a person. If no, you have an expensive human process with extra steps, or a fast way to put wrong amounts in the ledger.

    Hold onto thisA recommender is judged on the top of a ranked list and constrained by eligibility, suitability and consent. A document reader is judged on whether its confidence is honest. In both, the model is the smaller part: the larger part is what you measure, what you forbid, and who sees the doubtful cases.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F9.5 · The shape of a recommender: retrieve, then rank

    Think of hiring: skim thousands of CVs cheaply, then interview a shortlist properly. Scoring every item with a heavy model is too slow for millions of items, so big recommenders work in stages.

    1. Candidate generation. A cheap model cuts the catalogue to a few hundred: items similar to what the user held, items popular with similar users, or a rule such as "all products this segment is eligible for".
    2. Filtering. Remove anything not allowed: ineligible, already held, opted out, withdrawn, not Sharia-compliant for this customer. These are rules, not predictions, and run on every request.
    3. Ranking. A richer model scores survivors using customer history, product and context (time, channel) and orders them.
    4. Re-ranking and policy. Business constraints: no three credit products in a row, cap repeats, enforce contact limits, add a little exploration.

    A bank with thirty products can skip retrieval: stages 1 and 3 merge into one propensity model per product ("probability this customer takes product j if offered in the next 30 days"), ranked by expected value. That is plain classification (F4, F7) plus a ranking step. Recommender machinery earns its place with large catalogues (merchant offers, content, funds), thin history, or a wish for one shared representation instead of thirty models.

    Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation

    Neighbourhood methods are what Simulation 1 does. Two products are similar if the same customers hold them (cosine similarity from F1.1, the angle between their columns). Score a candidate by its similarity to what the customer already holds. Swap rows and columns for user-based filtering: find customers like you, recommend what they hold. Simple, explainable ("customers with your products often take this") and a hard baseline to beat.

    Matrix factorisation compresses the table, like describing each film by a few taste dimensions rather than listing every viewer. Give every customer a short vector u and every product a short vector v (say 32 numbers each), trained so their dot product predicts the cell:

    r^(i,j)=bj+ui·vj

    bj is a baseline popularity term for product j; ui and vj are the learned vectors for customer i and product j. Training minimises the error on the known cells plus a penalty that keeps the vectors small (regularisation, F4.1). Read it as: a big dot product means the customer's tastes and the product's profile point the same way, so the score is high. The vectors are shared across all the cells you did not observe, which is how a blank gets a guess.

    Where the blanks come from matters. With star ratings, a blank is truly unknown. In banking nearly every signal is implicit (holds the product, opened the offer). The standard treatment (Hu, Koren and Volinsky, 2008) treats each blank as a weak negative and each observed cell as a positive whose confidence grows with evidence. Same quiet assumption as F3.5: "no label" is not "no".

    Three points about factorisation.

    • Factors are not interpretable. You can see which products sit near each other, but "factor 7" has no name. To tell a compliance reviewer why an offer was made, use a neighbourhood explanation or a separate explainer (F8).
    • A tuned dot product is hard to beat. Rendle and colleagues (2020) re-ran a well-known neural approach against a properly tuned dot product and the dot product won. Untuned baselines make fancy models look good.
    • Cold start is structural. A new customer or product has no vector. Fix it with features (age band, segment, channel, category) so a new row gets a vector from what is known, and with popularity or rules until behaviour accumulates.

    Two-tower models and sequences

    The modern retrieval workhorse is the two-tower model: one network turns everything known about the customer into a vector, another does the same for the product, and training pushes the dot product up for pairs that happened (purchase, click) and down for pairs that did not. It is matrix factorisation with free vectors replaced by functions of features, so new users and items can be embedded at once. Training uses the contrastive idea from F5.4: pull matching pairs together, push random pairs apart. At serving time product vectors sit in a nearest-neighbour index, and a customer's vector fetches the closest few hundred in milliseconds (Covington et al., 2016; Yi et al., 2019).

    A newer thread treats history as a sequence and predicts the next event like a language model predicts the next word (attention, F5.3): models that read the last N interactions, and "generative" recommenders that give each item a short token code and generate the next code. For a bank the natural sequence is the stream of transactions and product events, the idea behind foundation models for transaction data. Treat them as later steps after a tuned baseline.

    Ranking metrics: judging a list, not a label

    A classifier is scored on every row. A recommender is scored on the top of a list, because nobody scrolls to position 40. Like a search engine: a right answer on page 3 is a miss. Each metric starts from a ranked list and the set of items the customer actually took.

    • Precision@k: of the k shown, the share taken. 0.33 at k=3 means one offer in three landed. Beware: it ignores order within the top k.
    • Recall@k: of everything they took, the share that appeared in the top k. 0.8 means you surfaced four of five.
    • Average precision (AP): average of the precision at each hit, so early hits count more.
    • NDCG@k: a hit at position p earns 1/log2(p+1): 1 at position 1, 0.5 at 3, 0.33 at 7. Divide by the best possible ordering to get 0 to 1. Going from 0.5 to 0.7 means your hits sit noticeably higher; 1.0 means perfect order.
    DCG@k=∑p=1krel(p)log2(p+1),NDCG@k=DCG@kideal DCG@k

    rel(p) is 1 if the item at position p was taken, else 0. Ideal DCG puts all taken items first. If a customer takes exactly one product and you ranked it third, NDCG@3 is 0.5 ÷ 1 = 0.5.

    Two cautions. Match the metric to the product: if the app shows three offers, report @3, not @20. And offline numbers reward predicting what customers would have done anyway: someone about to take a credit card counts as a hit whether or not your offer mattered. Predicting an action is not causing it, as the next section shows.

    Next-best-action in a bank: the extra layers

    A bank recommender differs from a streaming service's in four ways.

    • Eligibility and suitability are hard constraints. A product the customer cannot be sold is never a candidate, however high it scores. Suitability (appropriate for this customer's circumstances and risk profile) belongs to compliance. Both UAE and Saudi frameworks have rules; check specifics with compliance and conduct-of-business teams. A customer who asked for Sharia-compliant products gets only those.
    • The objective is value, not clicks. Rank by propensity × margin × (1 − risk), with risk from the credit team's models. A personal-finance offer accepted by customers who then default is a loss.
    • Contact is scarce. SMS and push have limits, customers opt out, fatigue is real. A next-best-action engine chooses among offers, service messages and doing nothing, within a contact budget.
    • Prediction is not incrementality. Like giving a free umbrella to people already carrying one: the top-ranked customer is the one most likely to buy anyway. The target is the customer whose behaviour changes because of the offer (uplift). Measure with a controlled holdout (F3.6) or uplift methods (F9.4), and learn online with bandits.

    The feedback loop is the other danger. The model learns only about products it showed. If it never shows the fund to customers it thinks are uninterested, it never learns it was wrong, and popular items get shown, clicked and more popular. A little exploration (a random 5% of impressions, or a bandit) keeps the picture whole. It is the same selective-labels problem as F2.6.

    F9.6 · From pixels to fields: how document AI works

    Meaning in a document comes from where text sits as much as what it says: "AED 250,000" beside "Amount" and the same figure in a footnote are different facts. So the pipeline has stages, and each can fail:

    1. Capture and clean-up. Deskew, remove shadows, fix contrast, split multi-page scans, drop blanks.
    2. Classify the document. Cheque, invoice, ID, statement, bill of lading? An image classifier (F5.2) does this well because layout is a strong signal.
    3. Layout analysis. Find header, table, signature, stamp, the MICR line on a cheque, the machine-readable zone on a passport.
    4. Text recognition (OCR). Pixels to characters, with a position and confidence per word or line.
    5. Field extraction. Which text is the payee, amount in figures, amount in words, date, beneficiary, IBAN. Early systems used templates; modern ones learn from layout and text together.
    6. Validation. Check fields against each other and the world: figures match words, IBAN checksum passes, date not in the future, beneficiary on the customer file, totals add up.
    7. Routing. Post automatically, or send to a human with doubtful fields highlighted.

    Stage 6 gives most of the real accuracy. A cheque states the amount twice, in figures and words, precisely so a reader can catch a misread. Comparing the two gives error detection almost free.

    Reading models: OCR, layout-aware models and vision-language models

    Three generations of reader exist, and a bank may run all three.

    ApproachHow it readsStrengthWeakness
    Classic OCR (Tesseract, commercial engines)Detects text lines, recognises characters, outputs text and boxes.Fast, cheap, predictable on clean print.No sense of meaning. Struggles with handwriting, stamps over text, poor scans, and Arabic joined script unless trained for it.
    Layout-aware transformers (LayoutLM family)Take OCR text plus each word's position (often the image) and label each word: payee, amount, date.Learn extraction from a few hundred labelled documents. Strong on forms and invoices.Inherit OCR mistakes. Need labels per document family.
    End-to-end vision-language models (Donut-style, general VLMs, newer open OCR models)Look at the whole page image and generate the text or fields directly.Handle messy layouts and mixed content with little template work; can answer "who is the beneficiary?".Can hallucinate plausible text not on the page, give no per-character confidence, cost more per page.

    Hallucination is the key difference. A classic engine that cannot read a digit outputs the wrong digit or nothing. A generative reader can output a fluent, well-formed, wrong number with no sign of doubt. Serious deployments add a second check: re-read the field another way, compare with a redundant field (words against figures), or tie every value to a bounding box so a human can see the evidence.

    Regional traps. Arabic runs right to left with letters that change shape by position, but numbers inside it run left to right, so mixed lines confuse naive readers. One page may mix Western (0 1 2 3) and Arabic-Indic (٠ ١ ٢ ٣) digits, and Hijri and Gregorian dates. Names transliterate several ways (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed), which matters when matching against a customer file or sanctions list (see human approval gates and document AI for trade finance).

    Confidence, thresholds and straight-through processing

    The business goal is straight-through processing (STP): the share of documents going from scan to ledger with no human touch. The lever is a confidence threshold. Fields scoring above it are accepted; a document is automatic only if every field passes.

    "Every" is why document AI is harder than accuracy numbers suggest. It is like a chain of 12 links: each 98% reliable, the whole chain holds only 0.9812 ≈ 78% of the time. "98% field accuracy" means a wrong document more than one time in five. Diagnose at field level; decide at document level.

    A confidence score is useful only if calibrated (F7.4): among fields scored 0.95, about 95% should be right. 0.95 is not a probability until you have checked. Neural readers are usually overconfident, so calibrate on held-out documents, per field type (dates and amounts fail at different rates). The trade-off is the fraud-threshold logic of F7.3: a higher threshold sends more to people (cost) and lets fewer errors through (loss).

    Three lessons from the simulation.

    • Field count drives everything. A cheque has a handful of fields and can reach high STP. A letter of credit with dozens almost never passes untouched, so trade finance aims for "assisted" processing: the system pre-fills and highlights, a person confirms.
    • Review the field, not the document. Route only doubtful fields to a person, with the image crop beside the value. Seconds per field, not minutes.
    • Error costs differ. A wrong cheque amount is a loss event; a wrong postcode is a nuisance. Set thresholds per field type from what the error costs.

    Document AI is also a fraud surface. Cheques and IDs can be altered, forged or regenerated. Pair the reader with tamper checks (inconsistent fonts or compression, signs of editing, signature against specimen, MRZ and chip data against the printed face), and note that deepfakes now affect KYC face-matching. A correctly read document may still be fake.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Popularity wins, and nobody notices

      Offline score is excellent because the model recommends the most-held products. Correct and useless: the customer knows about the salary account.

      Spot itCompare with a "top-N most popular" baseline; report coverage (distinct products ever recommended) and results on less common products.
    • Random split for a time-ordered problem

      Training on later purchases to predict earlier ones inflates hit rates.

      Spot itAsk how the split was made. It should be by time: features up to date T, outcomes after T, customers not overlapping where possible.
    • Rewarding predictions that would have happened anyway

      Targeting people most likely to take a card, who would have taken it without an offer. The campaign "works" and adds nothing.

      Spot itAsk for a randomised holdout and the lift against it in AED, not offline AUC or NDCG.
    • Recommending what the customer cannot or should not have

      An ineligible product, a non-Sharia-compliant product to an Islamic-only customer, a high-risk fund to an unsuited customer.

      Spot itCheck that eligibility, suitability and consent filters run after the model on every request, and that every recommendation shown is logged with its reason.
    • Feedback loop and popularity bias

      The model learns only from what it showed, so the head of the catalogue gets richer.

      Spot itPlot impression share by product over months. Keep an exploration slice and evaluate on it.
    • Cold-start ignored

      Good numbers on existing customers; new customers and products get nothing sensible.

      Spot itReport metrics separately for customers with fewer than N events and for products launched in the test window.
    • Field accuracy quoted as document accuracy

      "98% accurate" on documents with 20 fields means about one document in three has an error.

      Spot itAsk for document-level exact match and the error rate among automatically processed documents.
    • Overconfident reader

      Confidence 0.99 on a misread digit gives false comfort.

      Spot itPlot accuracy against confidence on held-out documents, per field type. If 0.99-confidence fields are wrong 3% of the time, the score is not calibrated.
    • Generative reader invents text

      A vision-language model fills in a plausible beneficiary or amount that is not on the page.

      Spot itRequire each value tied to an image region, re-read with a second method, and test on blurred or obscured samples where the right answer is "unreadable".
    • Tested on clean, English, one-format samples

      The vendor demo used crisp printed English invoices. Yours are phone photos of Arabic and English documents with stamps over text.

      Spot itBuild a test set from your own recent documents, stratified by scan quality, language, type and branch; report per stratum.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a recommender that wins on offline hit rate still add no value to the bank?

    Hit rate measures whether customers took a product, not whether the offer caused it. The model may target people who would have bought anyway, or only popular products. You need a randomised holdout and lift in AED.

    2. A customer took exactly one product and your list ranked it third. What are precision@3, recall@3 and NDCG@3?

    Precision@3 = 1/3. Recall@3 = 1/1 = 1. NDCG@3: DCG = 1/log2(4) = 0.5; ideal DCG = 1/log2(2) = 1; NDCG = 0.5.

    3. What does it mean that matrix-factorisation factors are not interpretable, and how do you still explain a recommendation?

    The learned dimensions have no assigned meaning; only distances and directions matter. Explain with neighbours (similar customers or products), the strongest features if the model is feature-based, and logged eligibility rules. Do not invent names for factors.

    4. A new product launches next month. How does a pure collaborative filter treat it, and what would you add?

    It has no interactions, so no vector, and is never recommended. Add content features (type, price band, target segment) so a two-tower or feature-based model can embed it at once, plus an exploration slice to gather early data.

    5. A reader has 98.5% accuracy per field. A trade document has 25 fields. What share of documents are completely correct if errors are independent?

    0.98525 ≈ e25 × ln 0.985 = e−0.378 ≈ 0.685, about 68%. Hence document-level evaluation and field-level routing. Real errors are correlated, so the true figure differs, but the order of magnitude is the point.

    6. What does it mean for a confidence score to be calibrated, and how would you test it for a cheque amount field?

    Among amounts scored 0.9, about 90% should be right. Bin the amount confidence on held-out cheques and plot actual accuracy per bin. If the 0.99 bin is only 95% accurate, the score is overconfident and needs recalibration (F7.4).

    7. Name three cross-checks that catch misread fields without a human.

    Figures against words; IBAN or account checksum; date plausibility; invoice totals against line items; beneficiary against the counterparty file; MRZ against the printed face of an ID.

    8. Why is a generative document reader riskier than classic OCR for amounts, and what mitigates it?

    It can produce a fluent, plausible number that is not on the page, with no sign of doubt. Ground each value to a bounding box, re-read with a second method, cross-check redundant fields, test with unreadable samples where the right answer is "cannot read", and route disagreement to a person.
