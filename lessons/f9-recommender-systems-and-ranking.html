    Recommenders and Document AI: Next-Best-Product, Ranking, and Reading Cheques and Scans

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Recommending: guess the empty cells

    Picture a table of viewers by films, with a tick where someone has watched a film and most cells blank. A blank means "not watched yet", not "would not enjoy it". A recommender guesses each blank on the idea that people who look alike want similar things. If you and a stranger have watched the same five films, the stranger's sixth is a good guess for you. In banking the viewers are customers and the films are products.

    The answer is a short ranking, and a bank recommender is a model inside a set of hard rules.

    Document AI: from pixels to a field you can trust

    A model reads trade documents from phone photos. It is usually right and does not know when it is wrong. So ask can it tell which fields it is sure of? If so, sure fields post and doubtful ones go to a person.

    Hold onto thisJudge a recommender on the top of its list, within eligibility rules. Judge a reader on whether its confidence is honest.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F9.5 · The shape of a recommender: retrieve, then rank

    Like hiring: skim thousands of CVs cheaply, then interview a shortlist properly. Big recommenders work in stages for the same reason.

    1. Candidate generation. A cheap model cuts the catalogue to a few hundred items.
    2. Filtering. Remove anything not allowed: ineligible, already held, opted out, not Sharia-compliant for this customer. These are rules, not predictions.
    3. Ranking. A richer model scores the survivors using customer history and context, and orders them.
    4. Re-ranking and policy. Business constraints: no three credit products in a row, contact limits, a little exploration.

    A bank with thirty products can skip retrieval. Train one propensity model per product ("probability this customer takes it if offered") and rank by expected value. That is plain classification plus a ranking step. Recommender machinery earns its place with large catalogues or thin history.

    Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation

    Neighbourhood methods (Simulation 1) call two products similar if the same customers hold them, and score candidates by similarity to what the customer holds. Simple, explainable ("customers with your products often take this") and a hard baseline to beat.

    Matrix factorisation compresses the table, like describing each film by a few taste dimensions rather than listing every viewer. Every customer and product gets a short list of numbers (the factors), trained so that when a customer's tastes and a product's profile point the same way, the score is high. Because those lists are learned from all the cells you did observe, a blank gets a sensible guess. Too few factors blur distinct tastes; too many memorise noise, so add regularisation (a penalty that keeps numbers small).

    Banking signals are implicit (holds the product, opened the offer). The standard treatment reads each blank as a weak negative and each observed cell as a positive that grows more confident with evidence. "No label" is not "no".

    • Cold start is structural. A new customer or product has no vector. Use features (age band, segment, category) and popularity or rules until behaviour accumulates.
    • Factors are not interpretable. "Factor 7" has no name. To tell a compliance reviewer why an offer was made, use a neighbourhood explanation.
    • A tuned simple model is hard to beat. A well-known neural approach lost to a properly tuned dot product when re-tested (Rendle et al., 2020).

    Two-tower models and sequences

    The two-tower model is the modern retrieval workhorse. One network turns everything known about the customer into a vector, another does the same for the product. Training pulls matching pairs together and pushes random pairs apart. New users and items get a vector at once, easing cold start.

    At serving time the customer's vector fetches the closest few hundred products in milliseconds using approximate nearest-neighbour search, which trades a little recall for speed. Sequence models predict the next event from a customer's history, like a language model predicts the next word. Treat both as later steps after a tuned baseline: they need far more data and engineering.

    Ranking metrics: judging a list, not a label

    A recommender is scored on the top of a list, because nobody scrolls to position 40.

    • Precision@k: of the k shown, the share taken. 0.33 at k=3 means one offer in three landed. It ignores order within the top k.
    • Recall@k: of everything they took, the share in the top k. 0.8 means you surfaced four of five.
    • NDCG@k: 0 to 1, rewarding hits near the top with a smooth discount for each position further down. Average precision (AP) is a similar early-hits-count-more measure.

    Match the metric to the screen: three offers means report @3, not @20. And remember that offline numbers reward predicting what customers would have done anyway, as the next section shows.

    Next-best-action in a bank: the extra layers

    • Eligibility and suitability are hard constraints. An unsuitable product is never a candidate, however high it scores.
    • Optimise value, not clicks. Weigh propensity, margin and risk.
    • Contact is scarce. A next-best-action engine chooses among offers, service messages and doing nothing, within a contact budget.
    • Prediction is not incrementality. The top-ranked customer would buy anyway, like a free umbrella for someone already carrying one. The target is the customer whose behaviour changes because of the offer. Measure with a controlled holdout, uplift methods, or learn online with bandits.

    Beware the feedback loop: the model learns only from what it showed, so popular items get more popular. A small exploration slice (say a random 5% of impressions) keeps the picture whole. See F2.6.

    F9.6 · From pixels to fields: how document AI works

    Meaning in a document comes from where text sits as much as what it says. So the pipeline has stages, and each can fail:

    1. Capture, classify, layout. Straighten the scan, decide if it is a cheque, invoice or ID, and find header, table, stamp and the MICR line.
    2. Text recognition (OCR). Pixels to characters, with a confidence per word.
    3. Field extraction. Which text is the payee, the amount, the date, the IBAN.
    4. Validation. Check fields against each other and the world: figures match words, IBAN checksum passes, totals add up.
    5. Routing. Post automatically, or send to a human with doubtful fields highlighted.

    Validation gives most of the real accuracy. A cheque states the amount twice, in figures and words, precisely so a misread can be caught.

    Reading models: OCR, layout-aware models and vision-language models

    ApproachStrengthWeakness
    Classic OCRFast, cheap, predictable on clean print.No sense of meaning. Struggles with handwriting, stamps over text and poor scans.
    Layout-aware transformersTake OCR text plus each word's position and label each word: payee, amount, date. Learn from a few hundred labelled documents.Inherit OCR mistakes. Need labels per document family.
    Vision-language modelsHandle messy layouts with little setup.Can hallucinate text, give no confidence, cost more.

    Hallucination is the key risk: a generative reader can output a fluent, wrong number with no sign of doubt. Defend by re-reading another way, comparing redundant fields, and tying every value to a visible image region.

    Regional traps: Arabic runs right to left but its numbers run left to right. One page may mix Western and Arabic-Indic digits, and Hijri and Gregorian dates. Names transliterate several ways (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed), which matters for sanctions matching (see human approval gates and document AI for trade finance).

    Confidence, thresholds and straight-through processing

    The goal is straight-through processing (STP): the share of documents going from scan to ledger with no human touch. The lever is a confidence threshold. Fields above it are accepted, and a document is automatic only if every field passes.

    "Every" is why it is harder than accuracy suggests. A chain of 12 links, each 98% reliable, holds only about 78% of the time.

    A confidence score is useful only if calibrated (F7.4): among fields scored 0.95, about 95% should be right. Neural readers are usually overconfident, so check on held-out documents, per field type. The trade-off is the fraud-threshold logic of F7.3: a higher threshold sends more to people (cost) and lets fewer errors through (loss).

    • Field count drives everything. A cheque has a handful of fields and can reach high STP. A letter of credit with dozens almost never passes untouched, so trade finance aims for "assisted" processing: the system pre-fills and highlights, a person confirms.
    • Error costs differ. A wrong cheque amount is a loss event; a wrong postcode is a nuisance. Set thresholds per field type.

    A correctly read document may still be forged, so pair the reader with tamper checks (see deepfakes).

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Recommenders are strong at:

    • Large catalogues and many customers. Shared representations let one model serve millions of customers without hand-built rules per segment.
    • Finding patterns nobody wrote down. Co-holding structure in the table (salary plus card plus travel cover leads to a gold card) falls out of the data.

    Recommenders are weak at, or wrong when:

    • Thin history and new items. Collaborative methods have nothing to learn from for a new customer or product.
    • Anything causal. They predict who will buy, not who buys because of the offer. Offline hit rate says little about lift.
    • Regulated suitability. A score cannot decide what may be sold. Eligibility and suitability rules must sit outside the model.

    Document AI is strong at removing keying work on high-volume, repetitive documents, because validation rules and per-field confidence let it post the easy fields and flag the rest. It is weak when scans are poor, layouts change constantly, documents carry dozens of fields, or a single wrong field is costly and nothing downstream can catch it.

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Per-product propensity modelsFew products, tabular features, a need for explainable scores.Thousands of items or very thin per-product data.
    Neighbourhood or matrix factorisationPlenty of interaction history, a tuned baseline is wanted.Mostly new customers or items.
    Two-tower or sequence modelsBig catalogue, rich features, engineers to run it.Small data. A tuned dot product usually matches them there.
    Classic OCR plus rulesClean print, fixed layouts such as cheques.Handwriting, stamps, varied layouts.
    Vision-language modelMessy, varied pages where speed to launch matters, with checks on top.The only control is trusting its output, as in amounts.

    In practice. For recommendations, start with a popularity baseline, then per-product propensity or item-based similarity, evaluated on a time-based split at the k your screen shows. Add factorisation or two-tower models only if they beat it on a holdout. Key knobs: number of factors, regularisation and the exploration share. In banking this is next-best-action in the app, behind eligibility rules and a contact budget.

    For documents, start with a classic or layout-aware reader and make the validation rules strong, then add a vision-language model for the hard pages. Tune thresholds per field type on your own scans. Cheques, KYC forms and trade packs are the usual uses.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Popularity wins and feeds itself

      A high offline score may just mean it recommends what is already popular: correct and useless. It also learns only from what it showed, so the head of the catalogue gets richer.

      Spot itCompare with a "most popular" baseline. Report coverage (distinct products ever recommended) and impression share over months.
    • Random split for a time-ordered problem

      Training on later purchases to predict earlier ones inflates hit rates.

      Spot itThe split should be by time.
    • Rewarding what would have happened anyway

      It targets people who would have bought regardless.

      Spot itAsk for a randomised holdout and the lift in AED.
    • Recommending what the customer cannot or should not have

      An ineligible, unsuitable or non-Sharia-compliant product.

      Spot itCheck that filters run after the model, with logging.
    • Field accuracy and confidence taken at face value

      "98% accurate" on 20 fields leaves one document in three with an error.

      Spot itAsk for document-level exact match and the error rate among automatic documents.
    • Reader that invents text, or was only tested on clean samples

      A vision-language model fills in a plausible amount that is not on the page. Or the vendor demo used crisp English invoices, while yours are phone photos with stamps.

      Spot itRequire each value tied to an image region and test on blurred samples where the right answer is "unreadable". Test on your own documents.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a recommender that wins on offline hit rate still add no value?

    Hit rate shows a product was taken, not caused. You need a randomised holdout.

    2. Which ranking metric notices that a hit came third rather than first?

    NDCG and average precision do. Precision@3 and recall@3 only ask whether the hit is somewhere in the top 3.

    3. How do you explain a recommendation when factors have no meaning?

    Use neighbours, strong features and logged eligibility rules. Do not invent names for factors.

    4. Why is 98.5% accuracy per field not 98.5% of documents correct?

    Every field must be right, so errors compound: with 25 fields, about one document in three has a mistake.

    5. What does a calibrated confidence score mean, and why does it matter?

    Among fields scored 0.9, about 90% are right. Without that, thresholds cannot separate safe fields from doubtful ones.

    6. You have 30 products, 200,000 customers and two analysts. Would you build a two-tower model or per-product propensity models, and why?

    Per-product propensity models. The catalogue is small, so retrieval is unnecessary, and tabular models are quicker to build, explain and monitor.

    7. A vendor offers a vision-language model for trade documents and a layout-aware model with a validation layer. Which would you choose for amounts, and why?

    The layout-aware model with validation for amounts: its values are tied to the page and it gives confidence you can threshold. Use the vision-language model for hard pages, with a second check, as it can give a fluent wrong number.
