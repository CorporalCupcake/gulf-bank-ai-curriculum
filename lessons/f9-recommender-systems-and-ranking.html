    Recommenders and Document AI: Next-Best-Product, Ranking, and Reading Cheques and Scans

    What to offer a customer who has told the bank little, how to judge a ranked list, and how a scanned cheque becomes trusted data.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth turn noisy input into a short list or a few fields, and the value lies in constraints, evaluation and routing doubt to people.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Recommending: guess the empty cells

    Picture a table of customers by products, mostly blank. A blank means "never offered", not "does not want it". A recommender guesses each blank on the idea that people who look alike want similar things.

    The answer is a short ranking, and a bank recommender is a model inside a set of hard rules.

    Document AI: from pixels to a field you can trust

    A model reads trade documents from phone photos. It is usually right and does not know when it is wrong. So ask can it tell which fields it is sure of? If so, sure fields post and doubtful ones go to a person.

    Hold onto thisJudge a recommender on the top of its list, within eligibility rules. Judge a reader on whether its confidence is honest.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F9.5 · The shape of a recommender: retrieve, then rank

    Like hiring: skim many CVs cheaply, then interview a shortlist. Recommenders cut the catalogue to candidates, filter out anything not allowed (rules, not predictions), rank the rest, then apply business policy. A bank with thirty products can skip the first step and run one propensity model per product.

    Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation

    Neighbourhood methods (Simulation 1) call two products similar if the same customers hold them, and score candidates by similarity to what the customer holds. Simple and a hard baseline to beat.

    Matrix factorisation gives every customer and product a short list of learned numbers, so a blank gets a sensible guess. Banking signals are implicit: "no label" is not "no".

    Cold start is structural. A new customer has no vector, so use features and popularity at first. Factors have no names, so explain offers by neighbours.

    Two-tower models and sequences

    The two-tower model turns customer and product into vectors from their features, pulling matching pairs together. New users and items get a vector at once. Try it after a tuned baseline.

    Ranking metrics: judging a list, not a label

    A recommender is scored on the top of a list, because nobody scrolls to position 40.

    • Precision@k: of the k shown, the share taken.
    • Recall@k: of everything they took, the share in the top k.
    • NDCG@k: 0 to 1, rewarding hits near the top. Higher means your hits sit higher.

    Match the metric to the screen: three offers means report @3.

    Next-best-action in a bank: the extra layers

    • Eligibility and suitability are hard constraints. An unsuitable product is never a candidate, however high it scores.
    • Optimise value, not clicks. Weigh propensity, margin and risk.
    • Prediction is not incrementality. The top-ranked customer would buy anyway, like a free umbrella for someone already carrying one. Measure with a holdout, uplift or bandits.

    Beware the feedback loop: the model learns only from what it showed, so popular items get more popular. A small exploration slice helps (F2.6).

    F9.6 · From pixels to fields: how document AI works

    It is a chain of stages: clean-up, classify, layout, read the text (OCR), extract fields, validate, route. Validation does most of the work: a cheque states its amount in figures and words, and an IBAN has a checksum. Routing sends doubtful fields to a person.

    Reading models: OCR, layout-aware models and vision-language models

    ApproachStrengthWeakness
    Classic OCRFast, cheap, predictable on clean print.No sense of meaning. Poor on handwriting and stamps.
    Layout-aware transformersLabel each word by position: payee, amount, date.Inherit OCR mistakes. Need labels.
    Vision-language modelsHandle messy layouts with little setup.Can hallucinate text, give no confidence, cost more.

    Hallucination is the key risk: a generative reader can output a fluent, wrong number with no sign of doubt. Tie every value to a visible image region. Mixed Arabic and English digits and name spellings also matter (see human approval gates and document AI for trade finance).

    Confidence, thresholds and straight-through processing

    The goal is straight-through processing (STP): no human touch from scan to ledger. A document is automatic only if every field passes the threshold, so it is harder than per-field accuracy suggests. Confidence must be calibrated (F7.4), and neural readers are usually overconfident.

    More fields means less STP, so trade documents are "assisted": the system pre-fills, a person confirms. Set thresholds per field type, since a wrong amount costs more than a wrong postcode. A well-read document may still be forged.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Popularity wins and feeds itself

      A high offline score may just mean it recommends what is already popular.

      Spot itCompare with a "most popular" baseline.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Training on later purchases to predict earlier ones inflates hit rates.

      Spot itThe split should be by time.
    • Rewarding what would have happened anyway

      It targets people who would have bought regardless.

      Spot itAsk for a randomised holdout and the lift in AED.
    • Recommending what the customer cannot or should not have

      An ineligible, unsuitable or non-Sharia-compliant product.

      Spot itCheck that filters run after the model, with logging.
    • Trusting field accuracy and confidence, or clean demos

      "98% accurate" on 20 fields leaves one document in three with an error, and a generative reader can invent text.

      Spot itAsk for document-level error rates on your own scans.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a recommender that wins on offline hit rate still add no value?

    Hit rate shows a product was taken, not caused. You need a randomised holdout.

    2. Which ranking metric notices that a hit came third rather than first?

    NDCG and average precision do. Precision@3 and recall@3 only ask whether the hit is somewhere in the top 3.

    3. How do you explain a recommendation when factors have no meaning?

    Use neighbours, strong features and logged eligibility rules.

    4. Why is 98.5% accuracy per field not 98.5% of documents correct?

    Every field must be right, so errors compound: with 25 fields, about one document in three has a mistake.

    5. What does a calibrated confidence score mean, and why does it matter?

    Among fields scored 0.9, about 90% are right. Without that, thresholds cannot separate safe fields from doubtful ones.
