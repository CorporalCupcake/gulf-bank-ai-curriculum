    Tuning Models Without Fooling Yourself: Search Strategies and the Classic Algorithms

    Why random search beats a grid, what Bayesian search actually does, how tuning quietly overfits your validation data, and what k-NN, SVMs and naive Bayes assume, so you know when each one still wins.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceSpread your tuning trials instead of gridding them, spend them adaptively if each is costly, and remember that every trial scored on the same data makes the winning score more flattering.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A gradient-boosted model has a dozen settings that data cannot teach it: number of trees, depth, learning rate. These are hyperparameters. You set them by trying values and watching a validation score, and each try costs a full training run.

    Idea 1: spread your trials. A grid of three values per knob feels systematic, but a couple of knobs decide most of the result. A grid tries three values of the knob that matters; thirty random draws try thirty.

    Idea 2: the winner's score is flattering. Each time you keep the best of many scored settings, you select on luck as well as quality. Tune on the test set and your final number is optimistic by construction.

    The second half covers k-NN, SVMs and naive Bayes. Knowing what each assumes tells you where it works and where it fails.

    Hold onto thisDecide the budget first, explore the knobs that matter, keep a test set nothing touched, and report a score you could defend.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Grid versus random search: low effective dimension

    Think of tuning a radio: one dial changes the station, the others barely matter. Validation error usually moves a lot along one or two knobs and hardly at all along the rest, and which knobs matter changes by dataset (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). A learning rate that is too high ruins a boosted model; minimum leaf size shifts the score by a fraction of a point.

    A grid with several knobs explodes in size yet tests only a few distinct values of the knob that matters. Random search draws every knob independently, so every trial is a new value of the important one. You can also stop anytime or add trials later. A grid is fine for one or two knobs; beyond that it is habit, not method.

    Sample scale-type knobs (learning rate, regularisation strength) on a log scale, and set ranges from knowledge so no trials go to absurd settings.

    Where the important knob's landscape is flat, grid and random tie. Where it is sharp (common for learning rates), random wins clearly at small budgets. If a trial takes an hour, though, neither is how to spend 50 hours.

    Bayesian search and early stopping

    When trials are costly, learn from earlier ones. Like a treasure hunter who uses each failed dig to decide where to dig next, Bayesian optimisation fits a cheap surrogate model to the (setting, score) pairs so far. For any untried setting it predicts a score and an uncertainty. It then picks the next trial where the predicted error is low (exploit) or the surrogate is unsure (explore). That balance is called expected improvement.

    In practice you use TPE (default in Hyperopt and Optuna) or SMAC, which cope with mixed and conditional knobs. For boosted trees on tabular data they do well and are easy to run.

    Bayesian methods are not magic. They need a sensible space, and on noisy scores they can chase noise. They pay off when each trial is expensive and knobs are few. For a model that trains in a minute, 200 random trials may beat a careful 30.

    The second lever is not finishing bad trials. Like auditioning many singers and hearing only the best in full, successive halving gives many settings a small budget, keeps the best half and repeats with more budget. Boosting libraries also build in early stopping: stop adding trees once validation error stops improving, so "number of trees" becomes an outcome, not a knob.

    What you write is short:

    from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV, TimeSeriesSplit
from scipy.stats import loguniform, randint

search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    model,                                   # a pipeline: scaling lives inside it
    {"learning_rate": loguniform(1e-3, 0.3),
     "max_depth": randint(2, 8),
     "min_child_weight": randint(1, 50)},
    n_iter=60, scoring="average_precision",
    cv=TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4),          # forward in time, see F4.7
    random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)
search.fit(X_train, y_train)                 # X_test is not seen here

    Tuning on the test set, and how to stop doing it

    Twenty students of identical ability sit a 100-question quiz: whoever tops the class got lucky. Likewise, suppose twenty models all truly have 80% recall. Scored on 100 frauds, they scatter by about 4 points through sampling noise, and the best of twenty reads around 87%. That is the number you would report, and it is wrong. This selection effect is the winner's curse (F2.4).

    Use three sets, each for one job:

    • Training data fits the parameters.
    • Validation data (or CV folds) chooses hyperparameters. Its best score is optimistic.
    • Test data is touched once, at the end. If you look, change something and look again, it has become validation data.

    With little data, use nested cross-validation: an inner loop searches, an outer loop scores the whole procedure on folds the search never saw. It is costly, so it suits small data, not nightly jobs. For anything in time, the outer split runs forward in time (F4.7).

    What to carry away. Optimism depends on how big the evaluation noise is compared with real differences between candidates. If candidates differ by a point and noise is four points, tuning 200 settings finds noise. Stop early, pick a simple setting (the one-standard-error rule: the simplest model within one standard error of the best), cap trials in advance, and log every trial so the search can be audited.

    k-NN, SVMs and naive Bayes: what each assumes

    Three older algorithms still turn up in interviews and in production, as baselines or components. Each is a clear assumption about the world.

    AlgorithmCore ideaWhat it assumesWhere it still wins
    k-nearest neighboursFind the k most similar training cases and take a vote. No training step.Nearby points share labels, and every feature is on a scale where distance means something.Small, low-dimensional data; look-alike lookups; retrieval (vector search).
    Support vector machineFind the widest-margin boundary between classes; only points near it matter. A kernel curves the boundary.A wide margin generalises; features are scaled.Small to medium data with many features, such as text.
    Naive BayesCount how much each feature points to each class, treating features as independent.Independence given the class. Almost never true, but the ranking often survives.Text baselines, tiny training sets, fast sanity checks. Probabilities are poorly calibrated.

    k-NN: scale first. A feature in AED (tens of thousands) swamps one in years (tens), so the model quietly becomes "nearest by income". Standardise. Then the curse of dimensionality: with hundreds of raw features, nearest and farthest neighbours end up almost equally far, like finding a "close" restaurant that must match 300 criteria. k-NN on a learned embedding works well, which is what vector search does (F1.1, M3).

    SVM: the widest road. Picture a road drawn between two groups of houses: the SVM picks the widest. C sets how many points may sit on the wrong side, and the kernel width γ sets how wiggly the boundary is (large γ overfits). Tune them together on a log scale. Cost is the limit: beyond about a hundred thousand rows, use boosted trees or a linear model.

    Naive Bayes: count the evidence. For a message, it tallies how typical each word is for each class and picks the class with the most evidence. Words like "transfer" and "urgent" co-occur, so it counts the same evidence twice and its probabilities come out too extreme, though the ranking is often still good. Use it as a first baseline for classifying complaints, not as a source of probabilities. Logistic regression wins with enough data, naive Bayes with very little.

    This is the bias-variance trade-off of F4.1 with k as the dial: k = 1 is high variance, huge k is high bias. Do scaling inside a pipeline fitted on training folds only, or validation data leaks (F3.1).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Tuning on the test set

      "I tried a few settings and kept the best test score." The test number is now a validation number and optimistic.

      Spot itAsk how often the test set was scored. More than once, or before the model was frozen, is a problem.
    • Quoting the best validation score as performance

      The search's best CV score is reported as the expected live result.

      Spot itAsk for the score on a separate, later period. A big gap means selection effects.
    • Grid search over many knobs

      Five values on six knobs is 15,625 runs, and still only five learning rates.

      Spot itCount the grid. Hundreds of trials with a handful of values per knob: switch to random.
    • Leaky preprocessing or random folds on temporal data

      Scaler or encoder fitted before cross-validation, or shuffled folds on time-ordered data. Tuning rewards the leak, and the model underperforms live.

      Spot itLook for a pipeline object and a forward-in-time splitter. Fit calls outside the CV loop give it away.
    • Chasing differences smaller than the noise

      Two hundred trials to move validation AUC from 0.912 to 0.914.

      Spot itCompare the gain with the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Inside it, stop and pick the simpler model.
    • Unscaled features, or trusting scores as probabilities

      Income in AED drowns out age in k-NN or an SVM. A naive Bayes 0.99 is not a 99% chance, and SVM margins are not probabilities at all.

      Spot itPrint feature ranges for scaling. Plot a calibration curve on held-out data (F7) for probabilities.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. You have 25 trials and 3 hyperparameters. Why does random search explore the important one better than a grid?

    A grid spends its budget on combinations, so 25 trials gives only about 2 distinct values per knob. Random search tests 25 distinct values of every knob, including the one that matters.

    2. Why sample the learning rate on a log scale?

    Its effect depends on order of magnitude: 0.001 to 0.01 matters as much as 0.01 to 0.1. A uniform draw over 0.001–0.3 puts most trials above 0.03.

    3. Twenty identical models are each scored on 100 frauds. Why does the best one look better than the rest, and is it?

    Sampling noise scatters the scores by a few points, and the maximum of twenty noisy scores sits well above the true value (around 87% against a true 80%). It is no better; you selected on luck.

    4. What does expected improvement do that "pick the best predicted score" does not?

    It rewards uncertainty as well. A mediocre prediction with a very uncertain surrogate might hide a big gain. Picking the best prediction only keeps sampling near known points and never explores.

    5. What is the difference between validation and test data in tuning?

    Validation data is used repeatedly to choose settings, so its best score is optimistic. Test data is used once, after the choices are frozen. If test results change anything, it has become validation data.

    6. A k-NN model scores 100% on training data and 71% on new data at k = 1. What does raising k do?

    Training accuracy drops; new-data accuracy typically rises at first. At k = 1 each point is its own neighbour, so the model memorises noise. More neighbours smooth the boundary until it oversmooths.

    7. You feed age (years) and income (AED) unscaled into an RBF SVM. What goes wrong?

    Income dominates the distance, so age is effectively ignored. Standardise both inside a pipeline fitted on training folds only.

    8. When would you pick naive Bayes over logistic regression?

    With very little labelled data, a need for a fast baseline, and text counts as features. With more data, logistic regression usually wins and gives better probabilities.
