    F4.9 · F4.10 Day one About 40 minutes

    Tuning Models Without Fooling Yourself: Search Strategies and the Classic Algorithms

    Why random search beats a grid, what Bayesian search actually does, how tuning quietly overfits your validation data, and what k-NN, SVMs and naive Bayes assume, so you know when each one still wins.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTuning is a search under a fixed compute budget where a few knobs matter and most do not, so spread your trials rather than lay them on a grid, spend them adaptively if each trial is costly, and remember that every trial you score on the same data makes the winning score a little more flattering.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A gradient-boosted model has a dozen settings you do not learn from data: how many trees, how deep, how fast to learn, how much to subsample. These are hyperparameters. The training algorithm cannot set them, so you do, by trying values and watching a validation score.

    Each try costs a full training run. Say you can afford 30. How do you choose the 30 settings? The obvious answer is a grid: three values of each knob, laid out evenly. It feels systematic. It is also wasteful, because of one fact about nearly every model: a couple of knobs decide most of the result, and you do not know in advance which ones. A grid tries only three distinct values of the knob that matters. Thirty random draws try thirty.

    The second idea is more uncomfortable. Every time you score a setting on your validation data and keep the best, you are selecting on noise as well as on quality. Try enough settings and the winner's score is partly luck. Tune on the test set and your final number is optimistic by construction; the test set was supposed to be the one thing you did not touch.

    The second half of the lesson steps back from tuning to the three classic algorithms that interviewers still ask about. They are small, fast, easy to explain and, in the right place, hard to beat. Knowing what each one assumes tells you where it works and where it falls apart.

    Hold onto thisTuning is cheap to do badly and easy to over-trust. Decide the budget first, search in a way that explores the knobs that matter, keep a final test set that nothing touched, and report a score you could defend.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Grid versus random search: low effective dimension

    Write the validation error as a function of the hyperparameters. For most models this function has low effective dimension: it moves a lot along one or two directions and barely at all along the rest. Which directions matter changes from dataset to dataset (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). A learning rate that is too high ruins a boosted model; the minimum leaf size shifts the score by a fraction of a point.

    A grid with k values per knob and d knobs needs kd runs, and projected onto the one knob that matters it explores only k distinct values. Random search draws each knob independently, so with n trials it explores n distinct values of every knob, including the important one. It also lets you stop at any point, add more trials later, and lose a few crashed jobs without leaving a hole in the design. Grid search is fine for one or two knobs where you care about a clean picture. Beyond that it is a habit, not a method.

    Two practical rules go with random search. Sample scale-type knobs on a log scale (learning rate from 0.001 to 0.3, regularisation strength over several orders of magnitude), and sample integer or share knobs uniformly. And set the ranges from knowledge: if a depth of 40 is absurd for your data, do not spend trials there.

    The size of the effect depends on how peaked the important knob is. When the landscape is flat, grid and random are equal. When it is sharp, which is common for learning rates and regularisation, random wins clearly at small budgets. Both are only a floor. If a trial takes an hour, neither is how you want to spend 50 hours.

    Bayesian search and early stopping

    If trials are costly, use what the earlier ones told you. Bayesian optimisation fits a cheap surrogate model to the (setting, score) pairs seen so far, then picks the next setting where an acquisition function says the payoff is best. The classic surrogate is a Gaussian process (GP): for any untried setting it gives a predicted score and an uncertainty that grows with distance from tried points. The classic acquisition is expected improvement (EI):

    EI(x)=(y*−μ)Φ(z)+σφ(z),z=y*−μσ

    For minimising error. y* is the best error so far, μ and σ the surrogate's prediction and uncertainty at x, Φ and φ the normal distribution and density. In words: EI is large where the predicted error is below the best (exploit) or where the surrogate is unsure (explore). Near a tried point σ is small, so EI is small there, which is why it does not keep resampling the same spot.

    Gaussian processes get slow and awkward with many knobs or with categorical ones. The popular alternative, TPE (tree-structured Parzen estimator, the default in Hyperopt and Optuna), models the density of good settings against bad ones instead and handles mixed and conditional spaces naturally. SMAC uses random forests as the surrogate. Which is better is empirical; for tree boosting on tabular data, TPE and SMAC-style methods tend to do well and are easy to run.

    Bayesian methods are not magic. They need a sensible search space, they spend time proposing trials, and with noisy evaluations (cross-validation scores wobble) the surrogate can chase noise. Their edge is largest when each trial is expensive and the number of knobs is small. For a model that trains in a minute, 200 random trials may beat a careful 30.

    The second lever is not finishing bad trials. Successive halving gives many settings a small budget (a few trees, one epoch, a data sample), keeps the best half, doubles the budget, and repeats. Hyperband runs several such schedules to hedge against slow starters (Li et al., 2017). Boosting libraries have the same idea built in as early stopping: stop adding trees once validation error stops improving, which turns "number of trees" from a knob into an outcome. Combining the two (BOHB, and the pruners in Optuna) is the modern default for anything expensive.

    What you actually write is short:

    from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV, TimeSeriesSplit
from scipy.stats import loguniform, randint

search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    model,                                   # a pipeline: scaling lives inside it
    {"learning_rate": loguniform(1e-3, 0.3),
     "max_depth": randint(2, 8),
     "min_child_weight": randint(1, 50)},
    n_iter=60, scoring="average_precision",
    cv=TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4),          # forward in time, see F4.7
    random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)
search.fit(X_train, y_train)                 # X_test is not seen here

    Tuning on the test set, and how to stop doing it

    Suppose twenty candidate models are all truly equal at 80% recall. Score each on 100 frauds and the scores scatter by about ±4 points through plain sampling noise. The best of twenty will show around 86 to 87%. That is the number you report, and it is wrong: the "winner" is the one with the luckiest sample. This is a selection effect, the winner's curse. It is the same arithmetic as the multiple-comparisons problem in F2.4.

    The honest ingredients are three sets, used once each. Training data fits the parameters. Validation data (or cross-validation folds) is used as often as you like to choose hyperparameters, and its best score is understood to be optimistic. Test data is touched once, at the end, to report a number. If you look at the test score, change something, and look again, it has become validation data and you have no test set left.

    If you also want to report the tuning procedure's expected performance with little data, use nested cross-validation: an inner loop does the search, an outer loop scores the whole procedure including the search on folds the search never saw (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). It costs outer folds × inner folds × trials fits, so it is for small data and for comparing methods, not for the nightly production job. For anything that lives in time, the outer split must run forward in time (F4.7), with an embargo for label delay if labels mature late (F3.5).

    The curve says something practical. The optimism comes from the ratio of noise to real differences. If your candidates differ by a point and the evaluation noise is four points, tuning 200 settings finds noise. In that regime stop early, pick a simple setting, and prefer the one-standard-error rule: choose the simplest model whose score is within one standard error of the best. Other habits that keep you honest: fix seeds and report the spread over several; tune with a metric that matches the decision (precision at review capacity, not accuracy, F7); cap the number of trials in advance; and log every trial, because an unlogged search cannot be audited (P2).

    k-NN, SVMs and naive Bayes: what each assumes

    Three older algorithms are still standard interview material and still sit in production, usually as baselines or components. Treat each as a clear assumption about the world.

    k-NN is where the scaling lesson is clearest. It measures distance, so a feature in AED (tens of thousands) swamps a feature in years (tens), and the model quietly becomes "nearest by income". Standardise first. The second lesson is the curse of dimensionality: as features multiply, points spread out and the nearest and farthest neighbours become almost equally far (Beyer et al., 1999), so "nearest" stops carrying information. This is why plain k-NN on 300 raw features misleads, while k-NN on a learned embedding of a few hundred dimensions works well. That is exactly what vector search does with approximate indexes such as HNSW (see F1.1 on the geometry and M3 on retrieval).

    SVMs reframe classification as geometry. With a linear kernel the boundary is a hyperplane; the margin is the gap to the nearest points on each side, and a larger C allows fewer violations at the cost of a narrower margin. The RBF kernel scores similarity as e−γ‖x−x′‖², so a large γ makes each point's influence tiny and the boundary wiggly (overfitting) and a small γ makes it smooth. C and γ interact, so they are tuned together, on a log grid or random search, which is Part 2's first topic in action. Cost is the limit: a kernel SVM needs a matrix of pairwise similarities, so beyond about a hundred thousand rows gradient-boosted trees or a linear model are the practical choice.

    Naive Bayes for a message with words w1…wm multiplies the class prior by each word's probability within the class, usually computed in logs so tiny numbers add instead of vanishing. Words never seen in a class would give zero, so you add a small count (Laplace smoothing). It is a generative model: it learns how each class writes. Its independence assumption is false, because "transfer" and "urgent" co-occur, so it counts the same evidence twice and its probabilities come out too extreme. The ranking is often still good (Domingos and Pazzani, 1997), which makes it a strong first baseline for classifying complaints in Arabic or English and a poor source of calibrated probabilities. In logistic-regression terms it is a restricted cousin of the model in F2.5; logistic regression usually wins with enough data, naive Bayes with very little.

    The same chart is the bias-variance trade-off of F4.1 with k as the dial: k = 1 has high variance, huge k has high bias. Notice what else it shows: scaling is a modelling choice with a larger effect than most hyperparameters. Do it inside a pipeline, fitted on training folds only, or the scaler's mean and spread leak validation data (F3.1).

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Tuning is where several earlier ideas meet: noise, splits and selection.

    F4.1 · Bias, variance, generalisationWhat the knobs trade off

    Most hyperparameters are dials between bias and variance. Tuning is choosing the dial setting that minimises held-out error.

    F4.2 · F4.3 · Trees and boostingWhere the knobs live

    Depth, learning rate, number of trees, leaf size and subsampling are the usual targets. Early stopping removes one.

    F4.7 · Cross-validationThe loop inside the search

    Every tuning trial is scored by a cross-validation scheme. Pick one that respects time, groups and label delay.

    F3.1 · LeakagePreprocessing belongs inside the loop

    Scalers, encoders and imputers must be refitted on each training fold, or tuning rewards leakage.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataFew positives, wide noise

    With 200 frauds, evaluation noise is large and tuning overfits quickly. Class weights and thresholds are themselves hyperparameters.

    F2.4 · F2.1 · Multiple comparisons, intervalsThe winner's curse is a testing problem

    Picking the best of many scores without adjustment is the same mistake as picking the best of many p-values.

    F7 · Metrics and thresholdsTune to the decision

    Optimising accuracy gives a model that is great at the wrong thing. Use a metric that reflects review capacity and costs.

    F1.1 · M3 · Embeddings and retrievalk-NN grew up into vector search

    Finding nearest neighbours of an embedding is the heart of retrieval. The scale and dimensionality lessons carry over.

    P2 · Model lifecycleExperiment tracking

    Validators will ask what you tried and why you chose this. A logged search with its space, seeds and budget is the answer.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Model validation. An independent validator will ask whether the test set was touched during tuning and how many settings were tried. Be ready with the count and the log.
    • Fraud and credit scorecards. Boosted trees tuned with random or TPE search under a forward-in-time split; a regularised logistic model as the transparent challenger.
    • Complaint and KYC text. Naive Bayes or a linear SVM over word counts as a baseline against which any language-model approach must justify its cost, in Arabic and English alike.
    • Matching and look-alikes. Nearest-neighbour logic underlies customer deduplication, name matching in sanctions screening (B2) and similar-case retrieval for investigators.
    • Cost. A tuning run on a bank's cloud or on-premises cluster uses real compute. A budget set in advance is both governance and economy.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The fundamentals have been stable for over a decade. What changed is tooling, and the shift of effort from tuning small knobs towards choosing data, features and the model family.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in human and AI-generated notebooks alike. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Tuning on the test set

      "I tried a few settings and kept the best test score." The test number is now a validation number and optimistic.

      Spot itAsk how many times the test set was scored. More than once, or ever before the model was frozen, is a problem.
    • Reporting the best validation score as performance

      The search's best cross-validation score is quoted as the expected live result.

      Spot itAsk for the score on a separate, later test period. A gap larger than the interval means selection effects.
    • Grid search over many knobs

      Five values on six knobs is 15,625 runs, and still only five learning rates.

      Spot itCount the grid. If trials exceed a few hundred and only a handful of distinct values per knob, switch to random.
    • Preprocessing fitted on all the data

      Scaler, encoder or feature selection fitted before cross-validation. Tuning rewards the leak.

      Spot itLook for a pipeline object. Fit calls outside the cross-validation loop are the giveaway.
    • Random folds on temporal data

      The tuned model looks great, then underperforms in its first live quarter.

      Spot itCheck the CV splitter. For time-dependent problems it should be forward-in-time or grouped.
    • Chasing differences smaller than the noise

      Two hundred trials to move validation AUC from 0.912 to 0.914.

      Spot itCompare the gain with the fold-to-fold standard deviation. Inside it, stop and pick the simpler model.
    • Unscaled features in k-NN or an SVM

      Income in AED drowns out age, tenure and counts.

      Spot itPrint feature ranges. If they differ by orders of magnitude and no scaler is in the pipeline, the distance is meaningless.
    • Trusting naive Bayes or SVM scores as probabilities

      A score of 0.99 from naive Bayes is not a 99% chance. SVM margins are not probabilities at all.

      Spot itPlot a calibration curve on held-out data (F7). If the curve is far from diagonal, recalibrate before using it as a number.
    • An unlogged, unrepeatable search

      "We tuned it" with no record of the space, the seed or the trial count.

      Spot itAsk for the search space, the budget, the seed and the trial log. If they cannot be produced, the result cannot be reviewed.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We fix the amount of tuning in advance, say sixty trials, because beyond that we mostly find noise. The number we show you comes from a later period of data that the tuning never saw, so it is what you should expect in production, not the best of sixty tries. If a simpler setting is within the margin of error of the best one, we take the simpler one.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Random or TPE search with a fixed budget and pruning, log-scaled ranges for rates and regularisation, scoring on precision at review capacity with a forward-in-time splitter, with all preprocessing inside the pipeline. The final test period is touched once, after freezing. Because the best validation score is biased upward, I report the test score, and where data is small I use nested cross-validation to estimate the whole procedure. All trials, seeds and the search space go into the experiment log.”
    “Why not just do a grid search? It's exhaustive.”

    It is exhaustive over a design that wastes trials. Usually one or two knobs drive the score, and a grid tests few distinct values of them. With the same number of runs, random search tests every value anew, so it is more likely to land near the best of the important knob (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). A grid is fine for one or two knobs where you want a clean picture.

    “When is Bayesian optimisation worth the trouble?”

    When trials are expensive, such as hours per run, the space has a few continuous knobs, and the budget is tens of trials rather than hundreds. When training takes a minute, plenty of random trials with early stopping is simpler and often as good. With noisy scores it can chase noise, so I still check the gain against the fold spread.

    “Our tuned model scored 0.93 AUC in validation and 0.89 in the final test. Which is right?”

    The test figure. The validation score was the maximum over many tries, so it carries selection bias, and the gap is typical of it. If the test period is also later in time, some of the drop may be drift. I would report 0.89 with an interval and use the gap as a measure of how much we over-tuned.

    “Does anybody still use k-NN, SVMs or naive Bayes?”

    Yes, in specific places. k-NN is the idea behind retrieval and look-alike search, with approximate indexes. SVMs suit small to medium, high-dimensional data. Naive Bayes is a quick, strong text baseline when labels are scarce. For large tabular banking data, gradient-boosted trees are usually the better starting point, but these three show what each assumption buys.

    “Why is naive Bayes called naive, and why does it work?”

    It assumes features are independent given the class, which is rarely true. The assumption makes the model simple, fast and robust with little data. It often ranks cases correctly even when its probabilities are overconfident, because the errors from double counting tend to push scores the same way. So use it to rank, and recalibrate if you need real probabilities.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. You have a budget of 25 trials and 3 hyperparameters. How many distinct values of the most important one does a grid test, and how many does random search?

    A grid with an equal number per knob gets about the cube root of 25, so 2 values per knob (8 runs) or 3 (27 runs, over budget); say 2. Random search tests 25 distinct values.

    2. Why sample the learning rate on a log scale?

    The effect of the rate depends on its order of magnitude: the difference between 0.001 and 0.01 matters as much as between 0.01 and 0.1. A uniform draw over 0.001–0.3 would put 90% of trials above 0.03 and almost none in the low range.

    3. Twenty identical models, each scored on 100 frauds with a true recall of 80%. About how high will the best score read?

    Standard error is √(0.8×0.2/100) = 4 points. The expected maximum of 20 normal draws is about 1.87 standard errors, so the best reads about 87.5%, though every model is really at 80%.

    4. What does the expected-improvement acquisition do that "pick the point with the best predicted score" would not?

    It also rewards uncertainty. A point with a mediocre prediction but a very uncertain surrogate might hide a big improvement; EI values that chance. Picking the best prediction only would keep sampling near known points and never explore.

    5. What is the difference between validation data and test data in a tuning workflow?

    Validation data is used repeatedly to choose settings, so its best score is optimistic. Test data is used once, after the choices are frozen, to report an unbiased estimate. If you use test results to change anything, it becomes validation data.

    6. A k-NN model has 100% accuracy on its training data and 71% on new data at k = 1. What would raising k do, and why?

    Training accuracy would drop, and new-data accuracy would typically rise at first. At k = 1 each training point is its own nearest neighbour, so the model memorises noise. Averaging over more neighbours smooths the boundary (less variance, more bias) until it oversmooths.

    7. You feed age (years) and income (AED) unscaled into an SVM with an RBF kernel. What goes wrong?

    The kernel uses distance, which income dominates, so age is effectively ignored and γ is effectively set by the income scale. Standardise both inside a pipeline fitted on training folds only.

    8. When would you pick naive Bayes over logistic regression?

    With very little labelled data, a need for a fast baseline or incremental updates, and text counts as features. With more data, logistic regression usually wins and gives better probabilities.
