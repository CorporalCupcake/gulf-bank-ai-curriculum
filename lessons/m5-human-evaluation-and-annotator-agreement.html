    Human Evaluation and Building Test Sets

    Part 1

    Why human labels, and where test questions come from

    In one sentenceA test set is a collection of real questions with a clear way to mark each answer, and human labels are the ruler you check every automatic score against. If the ruler is wobbly, the measurements mean nothing.

    Running example: two compliance reviewers each mark 200 answers from a bank assistant as "acceptable" or "problem". They agree on 93%. The project lead calls that excellent. It is not, and the first half of this lesson explains why.

    The second half is about where to get good questions: from your own system's failures, not from a public benchmark or the team's imagination.

    Part 2

    Measuring agreement properly

    In one sentenceCohen's kappa is agreement after removing the part that luck would give you: 0 means no better than guessing, 1 means perfect.

    Only 5% of the 200 answers are real problems. Most of the time both reviewers simply write "acceptable", so they agree constantly. It is like two people who always guess "dry" for tomorrow's desert weather: they agree and prove nothing.

    How to read kappa

    Here kappa is (93 − 88) ÷ (100 − 88), about 0.42: only moderate. Rough bands: 0.4 to 0.6 moderate, 0.6 to 0.8 substantial, above 0.8 near-perfect. Judge by what a disagreement costs, not by the band alone.

    • High raw agreement, low kappa usually means a rare class, not bad raters. With 95%-accurate reviewers and 5% problems, raw agreement is about 90% and kappa about 0.45.
    • Do not compare kappa across datasets with different class balance. Show the label counts beside it.
    • Krippendorff's alpha reads like kappa but handles many raters, gaps and 1 to 5 scales. It is the usual default in evaluation work.
    • Always add an interval. Kappa is unstable on rare classes.

    Why people disagree

    A low kappa says something is inconsistent, not what. Sort each cluster of disagreements into one of four causes:

    CauseLooks likeFix
    Guideline gapThe rubric is silent: does leaving out a fee clause break "faithful"?Write the rule, add examples, relabel. Commonest and cheapest.
    Rater error or driftOne outlier rater, or labels worsen late in a sessionCalibration session, shorter batches, adjudicate.
    Ambiguous item"Can I withdraw it?" with no account namedAdd context, or remove the item.
    Real difference of viewTone, formality, dialectKeep the spread as a soft label, or name the target audience.

    What is the "right" label?

    • Adjudicate when there is a fact of the matter: a senior reviewer resolves each split using the guideline.
    • Majority vote is cheap and robust if raters err independently. It fails if the majority shares a bias.
    • Keep the spread for questions of taste. If 3 of 5 experts say "too blunt", the label is 60% blunt.

    Always keep the raw ratings. And never demand more of an LLM judge than humans achieve: if reviewers reach kappa 0.45 between themselves, that is the ceiling.

    Noisy labels hide real differences

    Wrong labels pull every score towards 50%, like measuring with a stretchy tape. If each reviewer is right 85% of the time, a true 4-point gap between two models shows as only about 2.8. A majority of three reviewers cuts label error from 15% to about 6%. Beyond about seven the gain is small.

    Run the evaluation sensibly: pilot the guideline on 30 to 50 items with 2 or 3 reviewers, read every disagreement, rewrite and repeat. Ask narrow questions ("does it state the correct fee?") instead of "rate quality 1 to 5". For an 80% pass rate, 100 items give roughly ±8 points and 400 give ±4.

    Part 3

    Building a test set from real failures

    In one sentenceRead your system's real mistakes, group them into a few failure modes, and turn each mode into test cases with a clear pass condition.

    Your system fails in specific, repeated ways: it mixes up two similar products, ignores a circular's date, or answers an Arabic question in English. A test set built from those measures what you actually need to fix.

    Where questions come from

    SourceStrong atWeak at
    Public benchmarkFree, comparable across modelsNot your documents, languages or users
    Invented by the teamFast, good for week oneToo clean. Reflects what the team imagines.
    Real traffic and failuresReal phrasing, typos, mixed Arabic and EnglishNeeds logging, privacy care, labelling effort

    The error-analysis loop

    1. Collect about 100 traces: question, retrieved context and answer.
    2. Write free notes in plain words. Preset labels like "hallucination" make you see only those.
    3. Group and count. Five to ten failure modes usually explain most failures.
    4. Turn each mode into cases and stop when new traces reveal no new modes. Repeat after any major change.

    Random reading finds mostly successes, so also read from thumbs-down, complaints and escalations. The price: you stop measuring how often things fail. At a 4% failure rate, 100 random traces show only about 4 failures.

    What a good case contains

    • The input as it arrived, with the context the system saw. Untidied.
    • What good looks like: a reference answer for facts, or a checklist ("states the 90-day notice; promises no rate") for open answers. Checklists are easier to agree on.
    • A failure-mode tag, so you can see which fix moved which number.

    Keeping it honest

    • Split it. Tune on one part, keep a held-out part you rarely look at.
    • Add a random sample. The failure set says what to fix. The random one says how often things fail.
    • Protect the data. Real chats contain names and account numbers. Mask them, restrict access, and ask privacy and compliance before using a hosted tool.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    NumberAnswersUse it when
    Raw agreementHow often do labels match?Only alongside kappa and class balance
    Cohen's kappaAgreement beyond luck, two ratersBinary labels, rare or common classes
    Krippendorff's alphaSame, for many raters or scalesThe default for evaluation work
    Kendall's tauDo raters rank systems the same way?Comparing rankings (1 same, 0 unrelated, −1 reversed)
    Show the formula

    Kappa = (observed agreement − chance agreement) ÷ (1 − chance agreement). Chance agreement for two raters who each say "problem" a share p of the time is p² + (1 − p)².

    In banking, a regulator or auditor may ask how you know an assistant is safe. Reviewed labels with documented agreement, plus a test set drawn from real failures, are the evidence.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting raw agreement alone

      "Reviewers agree 93%" on a set that is 95% acceptable is barely better than chance. Ask for kappa or alpha, class balance, item count and an interval.

    • Averaging away the splits

      A 3 to 2 vote turned into "problem" hides the ambiguity, and the lesson about your guideline with it.

    • A test set of only successes or only failures

      Random reading finds few failures. A failure-only set says nothing about the overall rate and flatters a model that fails differently.

    • Tuning until every case passes

      You have fitted the set, not improved the system. Keep a held-out portion, and watch for test cases leaking into prompts.

    1. Reviewers are 95% accurate and 5% of items are problems. Why is raw agreement about 90% but kappa only moderate?

    Most items are easy "acceptable" ones, so much of the agreement happens by chance. Kappa strips that out and leaves the rare class, where the difficulty sits.

    2. Two reviewers agree on 93 of 100 items. What must you know before calling that good?

    The class balance. If 94% are acceptable, chance alone gives about 88%, and kappa is only about 0.42.

    3. Why do three reviewers give better labels than one, and when does it help less?

    One slip is outvoted, provided errors are independent. If reviewers share a bias, the vote repeats it.

    4. Name the four causes of disagreement.

    Guideline gap, rater error or drift, ambiguous item, and genuine difference of view. Guideline gaps are the commonest and cheapest to fix.

    5. Why pair a failure-mined test set with a random sample?

    The failure set shows what to fix but not how often the system fails. The random sample estimates the overall rate and catches new problems.
