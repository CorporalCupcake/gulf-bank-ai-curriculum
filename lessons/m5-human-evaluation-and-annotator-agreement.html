    M5.3 · M5.4 Day one ★ About 42 minutes

    When Humans Disagree, and Test Sets Built from Real Failures

    Why raw agreement flatters you, what a human label can and cannot be worth, what to do when two expert reviewers split, and why a few hundred cases mined from real failures will improve a GenAI system faster than any public benchmark.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceHuman judgement is the reference every other GenAI metric is checked against, so measure how consistent it is (chance-corrected, with the failure mode of each disagreement diagnosed), then spend it on a test set made of your own system's real failures, because that set tells you what to fix and whether you fixed it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank builds an assistant that answers staff questions about product terms. To judge it, two compliance reviewers each read 200 answers and mark each "acceptable" or "problem". They agree on 93% of them. The project lead calls that excellent and moves on.

    Look closer. Only 5% of answers are problems, so most of that agreement is two people writing "acceptable" over and over. Two reviewers who flipped coins weighted 95/5 would agree about 90% of the time. The 93% hides how little they agree about the answers that matter: the problems.

    Two ideas follow, and they are the two halves of this lesson.

    • Human judgement is the ruler, and rulers have error. Every automatic metric in this module, and every LLM judge, is ultimately validated against human labels. If the humans are inconsistent, you cannot tell a good judge from a bad one, and you cannot tell a good model from a slightly worse one. So measure the ruler first. This is M5.3.
    • The best questions come from your own failures. Public benchmarks measure what someone else cared about. Your system fails in specific, repeated, often boring ways: it mixes up two similar products, ignores the date on a circular, answers in English to an Arabic question. Collect those failures, label them carefully, and you have a test set that measures what you actually need to improve. This is M5.4.
    Hold onto thisNever quote raw agreement alone. Give a chance-corrected number, the number of items, and the class balance, and then ask what each disagreement was about. A disagreement is information about your task, not just noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.3 · Measuring agreement properly

    Start with what people can disagree about. In a GenAI review there are usually three layers: is the answer factually right, is it faithful to the source document, and is it good (clear, appropriately toned, complete, safe). Agreement is high on the first, lower on the second, lowest on the third. The more your rubric leans on taste, the more you should expect disagreement and design for it.

    Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement two raters would reach by chance, given how often each uses each label:

    κ=po−pe1−pe

    po is the observed share of items on which the raters agree. pe is the agreement you would expect if each rater labelled at random with their own label frequencies. Kappa is the fraction of the possible improvement over chance that the raters achieved: 0 means chance, 1 means perfect. Worked example: 100 answers, both say "acceptable" on 90, both say "problem" on 3, and they split on 7 (4 and 3). po = 0.93. Rater A says acceptable on 94 items and B on 93, so pe = 0.94 × 0.93 + 0.06 × 0.07 = 0.878. Kappa = (0.93 − 0.878) ÷ (1 − 0.878) ≈ 0.42. A 93% agreement turns out to be moderate.

    Which statistic to use depends on the setup.

    SituationUseNote
    Two raters, categoriesCohen's kappaSensitive to class balance, as Simulation 1 shows. Report the label frequencies beside it.
    Many raters, each item seen by a fewKrippendorff's alphaHandles missing ratings, any number of raters, and ordinal or interval scales. The usual default for evaluation work.
    Fixed number of raters on every itemFleiss' kappaGeneralises Cohen's idea to a panel.
    1–5 or 1–7 ratingsWeighted kappa or ordinal alphaA 4 versus a 5 is a smaller disagreement than a 1 versus a 5, and the statistic should know.
    Rankings and pairwise preferencesKendall's tau, or agreement on pairsChecks whether raters order systems the same way, which is what a leaderboard needs.

    Three honest caveats about the numbers. First, the verbal bands often quoted ("0.6 to 0.8 is substantial", from Landis and Koch, 1977) are conventions, not laws. Krippendorff himself says to look at the consequences of the disagreement for the decision, and the common 0.8 cut-off is a rule of thumb. Second, kappa and alpha are unstable on rare classes: with 12 problems in 400 items, a handful of changed labels moves them a lot, so put an interval on them (bootstrap the items, F2.2). Third, a low value says "something is inconsistent", not what. That needs the diagnosis below.

    Why people disagree, and what to do about each kind

    The question in the dek, when humans disagree, what is the right answer?, has no single answer, because disagreement has several causes with different cures. Sort every cluster of disagreements into one of four.

    CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
    Guideline gapThe rubric is silent or vague. "Faithful" does not say whether leaving out a fee clause counts. Reviewers fill the gap differently.Write the rule, add worked examples, relabel. This is the commonest and cheapest fix.
    Rater error or driftOne rater is an outlier, or labels worsen late in a session, or a rater has a private rule. Disagreement correlates with the rater, or with time spent.Calibration session, rater feedback, shorter batches, and adjudicate the affected items.
    Ambiguous itemThe question itself is underspecified ("Can I withdraw it?" with no account named). Reasonable raters assume different contexts.Fix or remove the item, or specify the missing context. A system that answers without asking is arguably the real failure.
    Legitimate difference of viewTone, formality, dialect, cultural fit. Informed raters stably differ, and a second adjudicator would not settle it.Keep the spread. Record it as a soft label, decide the target audience explicitly, or sample raters to match your customers.

    So what is the "right" label?

    There are four defensible answers, and which you choose depends on the task.

    • Adjudicate to a single gold label. A senior reviewer or panel resolves each split with the guideline in hand. Best for factual and policy questions where one correct answer exists. Cost: it hides any residual ambiguity.
    • Majority vote. Cheap and robust when raters err independently. Simulation 3 shows how quickly it helps. It is wrong when the majority shares a bias (all raters are English-first and judge Arabic fluency by reading speed).
    • Model the raters. Methods in the Dawid-Skene tradition (1979) estimate each rater's reliability and the true label together, so a careful rater counts for more than a careless one. Worth it when you have many raters and sparse overlap, as with crowd work.
    • Keep the distribution. If 3 of 5 experts say "too blunt", the honest label is 60% blunt. Training and evaluation on such soft labels is the focus of the perspectivist line of research (Plank, 2022). Scoring a model that confidently says "100% fine" on an item where experts are split should cost it, which is what a proper scoring rule (Brier score) does.

    The practical rule: adjudicate when the task has a fact of the matter, keep the spread when it has a taste component, and always keep the raw ratings so the choice can be revisited.

    Noisy labels set a ceiling on what you can measure

    Suppose each rater is right 85% of the time and errors are independent. A single label is wrong 15% of the time. A model that is truly correct 82% of the time will be scored against those labels as correct only when model and label are both right or both wrong in the same way, which for a binary judgement gives a measured score of 0.82 × 0.85 + 0.18 × 0.15 = 72.4%. A better model, truly 86%, measures 75.2%. The true gap of 4.0 points shrinks to 2.8. Label noise compresses differences towards the middle, so real improvements look smaller and you need more items to see them.

    The cure is partly more raters per item and partly better guidelines. Simulation 3 shows how fast majority voting repairs independent noise, and its limits.

    Human agreement as the yardstick for an LLM judge

    The standard test for an automatic judge (M5.2) is whether it agrees with humans as well as humans agree with each other. That needs the human-to-human number as a ceiling. If your reviewers reach alpha 0.45, a judge with alpha 0.40 against them is performing about as well as a reviewer, and demanding 0.9 of it is asking for more than the task allows. Compare human-human and judge-human on the same items with the same statistic. Also report the judge's true-positive and true-negative rates separately: on a skewed set a judge that says "pass" to everything can score high on accuracy while catching nothing.

    Running a human evaluation without wasting it

    • Pilot the guideline first. Have 2–3 reviewers label 30–50 items, measure agreement, read every disagreement, rewrite the rules, repeat. Do not start the main run until agreement stops improving.
    • Prefer narrow, checkable questions to a single "rate quality 1–5". "Does the answer state the correct fee?" and "Does it cite a clause that exists?" agree far better than "is it good?". Binary or three-level scales are usually more consistent than seven.
    • Pairwise comparison ("which of A and B is better?") is easier for humans than absolute scoring and is the basis of preference leaderboards. It needs many comparisons to rank reliably and says nothing about whether either answer is acceptable.
    • Choose the reviewer. A compliance officer for policy answers, a native speaker of the right Arabic register for tone, a crowd worker for simple factual checks. Domain experts are scarce and expensive, so spend them on the hard items and use them to calibrate cheaper reviewers.
    • Blind and randomise. Hide which system produced each answer, shuffle the order, and balance position, since reviewers favour the first or the longer answer.
    • Size by the decision. The uncertainty on a pass rate depends on the number of items, as in this table for an 80% pass rate:
    Items judged95% interval on an 80% pass rateSmallest difference you could trust between two systems (roughly)
    25± 16 pointsabout 22 points
    50± 11 pointsabout 16 points
    100± 8 pointsabout 11 points
    200± 5.5 pointsabout 8 points
    400± 4 pointsabout 5.5 points

    The third column treats the two systems as scored on different items. If both answer the same items, a paired comparison is tighter, and F2 and the statistics-for-evals lesson show how.

    M5.4 · Building test sets from real failures

    An evaluation set is a sample of the situations you want the system to handle. The question is where to get it. Three sources, with different properties.

    SourceStrengthWeakness
    Public benchmarkFree, comparable across models.Not your documents, products, languages or users. Often seen in training. Tells you about models, not about your system.
    Invented by the teamFast, and good for the first week.Reflects what the team imagines users ask. Too clean and too easy.
    Real traffic and real failuresReflects actual users, phrasing, mixed Arabic and English, typos, and the system's own weak spots.Needs logging, privacy care and labelling effort. Biased by how you find the failures.

    The practical loop, which practitioners such as Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar teach under the name error analysis:

    1. Collect traces. Full records of real or realistic interactions: question, retrieved context, tool calls, answer. Start with around a hundred.
    2. Read them and write free notes. One person (ideally a domain expert) writes what went wrong in plain words, without a predefined checklist. Pre-set categories like "hallucination" make you see only those.
    3. Group the notes into failure modes and count them. Typically five to ten categories explain most of the failures. A model can propose groupings; a human should check them.
    4. Turn each mode into test cases with a clear pass condition. Some are checkable by code (the answer must include the disclaimer, must not contain an account number); others need a rubric and a judge validated against human labels.
    5. Keep going until new traces stop revealing new modes (saturation). One heuristic is twenty fresh traces with nothing new. Repeat after any significant change to prompt, model, data or product.

    Where the traces come from matters, because random reading finds mostly successes. Thumbs-down and complaints, escalations to a human agent, analyst corrections, answers that low-scoring judges flagged, long or looping conversations and red-team attempts all enrich for failures. The price of enrichment is that you stop measuring prevalence. Simulation 4 shows both sides.

    What a good case looks like

    • The input exactly as it arrived, with the context the system saw. Not a tidied version.
    • What good looks like: a reference answer for factual cases, or a checklist ("states the 90-day notice period; does not promise a rate; answers in Arabic") for open ones. A checklist is easier to agree on than a model answer.
    • The failure mode tag, so you can report pass rate by mode and see which fix moved which number.
    • Provenance and date: where it came from, who labelled it, which guideline version. Disagreement scores for the set belong here too.

    How to keep the set honest

    • Split it. Tune prompts and retrieval on a development portion. Keep a held-out portion you look at rarely. If you edit the system until every case passes, you have fitted the set, as with any test set (F4.5).
    • Keep a random, unfiltered sample as well, labelled the same way. The failure set tells you what to fix; the random sample tells you how often things fail overall.
    • Keep fixed regression cases. Every fixed bug becomes a permanent test that runs on each change, in the same way as a unit test (F10.3).
    • Handle data with care. Real customer conversations contain names, account numbers and IDs. Remove or mask them, restrict who can read the set, and check where it may be stored under the bank's data-residency and privacy rules. Do not paste production cases into a public tool or a hosted judge without approval. Which rules apply is a question for your privacy and compliance teams, not something to assume.
    • Refresh it. Users, products and the model change. A set from last March misses this March's failures. Add new cases each cycle, retire cases about discontinued products, and track the date mix.
    • Mind selection. A set made only from cases the current system fails on will make a replacement model that fails differently look better than it is. Pair it with the random sample (F2.6).

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Internal copilots for staff (B7). Reviewers are compliance or product experts, and disagreements are mostly guideline gaps about what counts as a complete answer.
    • Customer-facing assistants in Arabic and English. Tone and register differences are legitimate; factual errors and missing disclosures are not. Keep these apart in the rubric.
    • Document extraction and credit memos. Field-level correctness is checkable with a gold set; summary quality needs panels and agreement tracking (see A10).
    • Complaint and KYC text classification. The rare class (alleged fraud, adverse media) is exactly where kappa is least stable. Report counts and intervals.
    • Model risk review. Written evidence that the test set was built, labelled, split and refreshed by a defined process is part of what makes an evaluation defensible.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Three live threads: how well LLM judges track humans (and whether to trust the number), whether to keep human disagreement rather than collapse it, and a fast-maturing practitioner craft around error analysis. The theory of agreement is old and settled; the practice for generative systems is still being written.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026. Searches were light; items below are ones I could confirm from titles and abstracts or search summaries, and some details are from memory.

    1. 1960–79
      Chance-corrected agreement and rater models

      Cohen introduced kappa for two raters. Dawid and Skene later modelled each rater's error rates and the true label jointly, the ancestor of modern label-aggregation methods.

      Cohen, Educational and Psychological Measurement, 1960; Dawid & Skene, Applied Statistics, 1979
    2. 2008–11
      Agreement for language tasks, and alpha made practical

      Artstein and Poesio surveyed the coefficients for computational linguistics. Krippendorff's alpha, which handles any number of raters, missing data and several scale types, became the common default.

      Artstein & Poesio, Computational Linguistics, 2008; Krippendorff, 2011
    3. 2015
      "Truth is a lie"

      Aroyo and Welty argued that the idea of a single true label per item is a myth in many tasks and that disagreement carries signal.

      Aroyo & Welty, AI Magazine, 2015
    4. 2022
      Human label variation as a research programme

      Plank's position paper set out why variation should be kept in data and evaluation rather than averaged away. Related work trained models on individual annotators' judgements.

      Plank, EMNLP 2022 (arXiv 2211.02570); Davani et al., TACL 2022
    5. 2023
      LLM judges reach roughly human-level agreement

      The MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena study reported that a strong LLM judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time, about the level humans agree with each other, while documenting position, length and self-preference biases. (Figure from memory of the paper.)

      Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv 2306.05685)
    6. 2025
      Raw agreement overstates judge reliability

      The "Rating Roulette" study found LLM judges inconsistent with themselves across repeated runs. On MT-Bench it reports human-human Krippendorff's alpha of 0.478 and LLM judges at 0.239 to 0.426, while raw accuracy looked high (0.827). Other studies find judges close to humans on ranking systems but variable on exact scores. Task, scale and judge differ across studies, so the numbers are not comparable.

      Rating Roulette, EMNLP Findings 2025 (arXiv 2510.27106); search summaries of related relevance-judgement work
    7. 2025
      Learning with Disagreements shared task, third edition

      LeWiDi-2025 covered four datasets including ordinal ratings and explanations, with two tracks: predicting the distribution of human opinions and predicting individual annotators. LLM-based systems led the leaderboards.

      LeWiDi-2025 overview, NLPerspectives workshop
    8. 2025–26
      Error analysis becomes standard practice for LLM apps

      Practitioner guides converge on one workflow: read real traces, open-code the problems, group into a taxonomy, turn modes into binary checks, validate any judge against human labels with true-positive and true-negative rates, and feed new production traces back in. Much of this is vendor or blog material; the numbers (about 100 traces, 5–10 categories, 20 traces to saturation) are heuristics, not standards.

      Husain & Shankar, AI Evals FAQ; Shankar et al., "Who validates the validators?" (arXiv 2404.12272)
    9. 2026
      Benchmarks scored against disagreement

      A case study on multimodal benchmarks proposes evaluating separately where annotators agree and where they split, using Brier scores on soft labels that penalise confident answers on items experts dispute. I could read only the summary.

      arXiv 2603.19744, 2026 (preprint)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Quoting raw agreement

      "Reviewers agree 93% of the time." On a set that is 95% acceptable, that is barely better than chance.

      Spot itAsk for kappa or alpha, the class balance and the item count, plus an interval.
    • Averaging disagreement away

      A split of 3 to 2 becomes "problem" and the ambiguity vanishes, along with the lesson about the guideline.

      Spot itAsk how many items had a split vote and what the team learned from reading them.
    • Judging a judge by accuracy

      A judge that passes everything scores 95% on a set that is 95% good.

      Spot itAsk for its true-positive and true-negative rates against humans, and for agreement compared to human-human agreement on the same items.
    • Guidelines written once

      The rubric was drafted before anyone read real outputs, then never updated. Agreement is low and nobody knows why.

      Spot itLook for a guideline version history and a pilot round with measured agreement.
    • A test set that is all successes, or all failures

      Random reading finds few failures. A failure-only set says nothing about overall rate and overstates any new model.

      Spot itAsk how cases were chosen and whether a random sample sits alongside.
    • Tuning on the test set

      Prompts are edited until every case passes. The set then measures how well you memorised it.

      Spot itAsk whether a held-out portion exists that the builders have not been looking at.
    • Stale set

      Scores are stable and good while users complain. The set predates new products and phrasings.

      Spot itLook at the date distribution of cases and compare with the last month's real traffic.
    • Contamination and leakage

      Cases reach a prompt as examples, a fine-tuning set or a public benchmark. The model has seen the answers.

      Spot itCheck whether test cases appear in any prompt, few-shot example or training data file.
    • Real customer data in the wrong place

      Unmasked conversations exported to a spreadsheet or a third-party labelling tool.

      Spot itAsk where the set is stored, who can read it, how identifiers were removed and who approved the labelling vendor.
    • One expert, treated as truth

      A single reviewer labels everything, so personal habits are baked in and agreement cannot be measured.

      Spot itAsk what share of items had a second labeller.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We can't know whether the assistant is getting better without a test, and the best test is made of its real mistakes. We'll collect a few hundred cases from actual use, have our own experts label them against a written standard, and check that two experts agree with each other, because if they don't, no score means much. Every time we fix a problem, it becomes a permanent check, so the system cannot quietly get worse. It needs a few weeks of expert time, and the customer data in it stays inside our controls.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd build the set by error analysis on real traces: open-code a first hundred, group into a failure taxonomy, and turn each mode into a case with a checklist or reference answer. Labels come from domain experts against a versioned guideline, piloted until Krippendorff's alpha stabilises, with an interval, and every split reviewed and classified as guideline gap, rater error, ambiguous item or real difference of view. I'd keep a development and a held-out split plus a random unfiltered sample for prevalence, and validate any LLM judge against human labels by true-positive and true-negative rate, benchmarked against human-human agreement on the same items.”
    “Our reviewers agree 92% of the time. Is that good enough?”

    I can't tell from that number. If most answers are acceptable, two reviewers would agree about that often by chance. I'd want kappa or alpha, the share of problem answers, the number of items and an interval. Then I'd read the disagreements: if they are guideline gaps, fixing the guideline is cheap and lifts the number fast.

    “Two experts disagree. Which one is right?”

    First find out why. If the guideline is silent, neither is wrong and we write the rule. If one is making a mistake, adjudicate. If the question is ambiguous, fix the question. If it is a stable difference of view, such as tone for a customer group, keep both and decide the target audience. Adjudication to a single answer is for tasks with a fact of the matter.

    “Why not just use the public benchmark?”

    It tells us about the model on someone else's tasks, probably ones the model has seen. It does not contain our products, our documents, Arabic and English mixed in one message, or our users' phrasing. I'd use it to shortlist models and our own failure set to decide whether the system is ready and what to fix.

    “Can an LLM judge replace the human reviewers?”

    It can replace some of the volume once it has been validated, not the humans who validate it. I'd compare it with human labels on the same items, check its agreement against how well humans agree with each other, look at true-positive and true-negative rates separately, and re-check periodically, since model and prompts change. Humans stay on the hard and high-risk items.

    “How many test cases do we need?”

    It depends on the difference we need to detect. With 100 items, an 80% pass rate is only known to about ±8 points. Start with 100 to 200 well-chosen cases to find and fix the big problems, which is enough to learn a lot, and grow toward several hundred per important failure area before we claim a small improvement.

    “Is it fine to use real customer chats?”

    Only under the bank's data rules. I'd mask identifiers, restrict access, keep the set inside approved infrastructure, and check with privacy and compliance before using any external labelling or hosted judging service. I won't assume what the regulators require; I'd ask and document the answer.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Two reviewers each right 95% of the time (independent) judge items where 5% are real problems. What is their raw agreement and roughly their kappa?

    Raw agreement = 0.95² + 0.05² = 0.905. Each says "problem" with probability 0.05 × 0.95 + 0.95 × 0.05 = 0.095, so chance agreement = 0.095² + 0.905² ≈ 0.828. Kappa = (0.905 − 0.828) ÷ (1 − 0.828) ≈ 0.45. Excellent-looking raw agreement, moderate kappa, because the rare class dominates the difficulty.

    2. Over 100 items, both reviewers say pass on 90, both say fail on 3, and they split 4 and 3. Compute kappa.

    po = 0.93. A passes 94, B passes 93, so pe = 0.94 × 0.93 + 0.06 × 0.07 = 0.878. Kappa = 0.052 ÷ 0.122 ≈ 0.43.

    3. Reviewers are right 80% of the time, independently. What is the label accuracy with a majority of three?

    P(at least 2 of 3 right) = 0.8³ + 3 × 0.8² × 0.2 = 0.512 + 0.384 = 89.6%. Provided errors are independent. If the reviewers share a bias, the gain is smaller.

    4. Why does label noise make two models look more alike than they are?

    A wrong label can turn a correct model answer into a "miss" or a wrong answer into a "hit". The measured score is pulled toward 50% by a factor equal to 2a − 1 (where a is label accuracy) in the binary case, so true differences shrink. With a = 0.85, a 4-point gap measures as 2.8.

    5. Name the four causes of annotator disagreement and a different fix for each.

    Guideline gap (write the rule, add examples), rater error or drift (calibrate, shorten sessions, adjudicate), ambiguous item (fix or specify the context), legitimate difference of view (keep the spread, define the audience, soft labels).

    6. Your LLM judge agrees with a reviewer on 90% of items. The human-human agreement on the same items is 94%, and 92% of items are "pass". Is the judge good?

    Not yet shown. With 92% passes, a judge that always says pass already agrees about 92% of the time. Compare chance-corrected agreement for judge-human and human-human on the same items, and check how many true failures the judge catches (true-negative rate) and how often it wrongly fails good answers.

    7. Why should a failure-mined test set be paired with a random sample?

    The failure set shows what to fix but says nothing about how often the system fails, and it favours a new model that fails differently from the old one. A random labelled sample estimates the overall rate and catches new problems the old system never produced.

    8. You have read 60 traces and found six failure modes, and the last 20 traces added none. What next?

    You have reached a rough saturation for this slice of traffic. Turn each mode into cases with pass conditions, hold out part of them, add a random sample, and schedule another round after the next major change or when new traffic types arrive. It does not prove no other mode exists, only that this traffic is unlikely to show more soon.
