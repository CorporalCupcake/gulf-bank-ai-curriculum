    When Humans Disagree, and Test Sets Built from Real Failures

    Why raw agreement flatters you, what a human label can and cannot be worth, what to do when two expert reviewers split, and why a few hundred cases mined from real failures will improve a GenAI system faster than any public benchmark.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceHuman judgement is the reference every other GenAI metric is checked against, so measure how consistent it is (chance-corrected, with each disagreement diagnosed), then spend it on a test set built from your own system's real failures.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two compliance reviewers each mark 200 assistant answers "acceptable" or "problem". They agree on 93%. The project lead calls that excellent.

    It is not. Only 5% of answers are problems, so most agreement is two people writing "acceptable" again and again. It is like two people guessing "dry" for tomorrow's weather in a desert: they agree constantly and prove nothing.

    • Human judgement is the ruler, and rulers have error (M5.3). Every automatic metric and LLM judge is checked against human labels. If the humans are inconsistent, you cannot tell a good model from a slightly worse one.
    • The best questions come from your own failures (M5.4). Your system fails in specific, repeated ways: it mixes up two similar products, ignores a circular's date, answers an Arabic question in English. Collect those and you have a test set that measures what you need to fix.
    Hold onto thisNever quote raw agreement alone. Give a chance-corrected number, the item count and the class balance, then ask what each disagreement was about. A disagreement is information about your task, not just noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.3 · Measuring agreement properly

    Cohen's kappa asks: of the agreement that was possible beyond luck, how much did the two raters achieve? Like a golf handicap, it scores you against what a guesser would do, not against zero. 0 means no better than chance, 1 means perfect, negative means worse than chance.

    Example. Two reviewers agree on 93 of 100 answers, but chance alone would give about 88%. Kappa is near 0.42: only moderate.

    • How to read it: 0.4 to 0.6 is usually called moderate, 0.6 to 0.8 substantial, above 0.8 near-perfect. These are rules of thumb, so judge by what a disagreement costs the decision.
    • Misreading to avoid: low kappa with high raw agreement does not mean bad raters. It usually means a rare class (Simulation 1). Never compare kappa across datasets with different class balance, and show label frequencies beside it.

    Krippendorff's alpha reads like kappa but handles many raters, missing ratings and 1–5 scales, and is the usual default for evaluation work. For rankings, Kendall's tau asks whether raters order systems the same way (1 identical, 0 unrelated, −1 reversed).

    Kappa and alpha are unstable on rare classes, so put an interval on them. And a low value says "something is inconsistent", not what. That needs the diagnosis below.

    Why people disagree, and what to do about each kind

    Disagreement has four causes with different cures. Sort every cluster of disagreements into one.

    CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
    Guideline gapThe rubric is silent. "Faithful" does not say whether omitting a fee clause counts, so reviewers fill the gap differently.Write the rule, add worked examples, relabel. Commonest and cheapest fix.
    Rater error or driftOne rater is an outlier, labels worsen late in a session, or a rater has a private rule.Calibration session, feedback, shorter batches, adjudicate affected items.
    Ambiguous itemThe question is underspecified ("Can I withdraw it?" with no account named).Fix, remove or add the missing context.
    Legitimate difference of viewTone, formality, dialect. Informed raters stably differ.Keep the spread as a soft label, name the target audience, or sample raters to match customers.

    So what is the "right" label?

    Three defensible answers. Choose by task.

    • Adjudicate to one gold label. A senior reviewer resolves each split using the guideline. Best when a fact of the matter exists. It hides leftover ambiguity.
    • Majority vote. Cheap and robust if raters err independently (Simulation 3). It fails when the majority shares a bias, such as English-first raters judging Arabic fluency.
    • Keep the distribution. If 3 of 5 experts say "too blunt", the label is 60% blunt. These soft labels suit questions of taste.

    Rule: adjudicate when there is a fact, keep the spread when there is taste, and always keep the raw ratings. With many raters and sparse overlap, as in crowd work, statistical models can also weight careful raters more.

    Noisy labels set a ceiling on what you can measure

    Noisy labels shrink differences, like measuring with a stretchy tape. A wrong label can turn a correct answer into a "miss" or a wrong one into a "hit", which pulls every score towards 50%. If each rater is right 85% of the time, a true 4-point gap between two models shows up as only about 2.8.

    • What a change means: better labels widen the gap you can see, so you need fewer items to detect a real improvement.
    • The cure: more raters per item and better guidelines. Simulation 3 shows how fast majority voting repairs independent noise, and where it stops.

    Human agreement as the yardstick for an LLM judge

    A judge (M5.2) should agree with humans about as well as humans agree with each other. That human-human agreement is the ceiling: demanding 0.9 from a judge when reviewers reach 0.45 asks more than the task allows. Compare both on the same items with the same statistic, and report the judge's true-positive rate (share of real passes it passes) and true-negative rate (share of real failures it catches) separately. A judge that says "pass" to everything scores high accuracy on a skewed set while catching nothing.

    Running a human evaluation without wasting it

    • Pilot the guideline first. 2–3 reviewers label 30–50 items, you measure agreement, read every disagreement, rewrite the rules and repeat. Start the main run once agreement stops improving.
    • Ask narrow, checkable questions rather than "rate quality 1–5". "Does the answer state the correct fee?" agrees far better than "is it good?".

    Size by the decision. For an 80% pass rate, 100 items give roughly ±8 points and 400 give ±4, so small differences between systems need hundreds of items. Judging the same items for both systems is tighter (see the statistics-for-evals lesson).

    M5.4 · Building test sets from real failures

    SourceStrengthWeakness
    Public benchmarkFree, comparable across models.Not your documents, products, languages or users. Tells you about models, not your system.
    Invented by the teamFast; good for week one.Reflects what the team imagines users ask. Too clean and easy.
    Real traffic and failuresReal phrasing, mixed Arabic and English, typos, the system's actual weak spots.Needs logging, privacy care and labelling effort.

    The loop is often called error analysis:

    1. Collect about a hundred traces: full records of question, retrieved context and answer.
    2. Read and write free notes in plain words. Preset labels like "hallucination" make you see only those.
    3. Group notes into failure modes and count them. Five to ten usually explain most failures.
    4. Turn each mode into cases with a clear pass condition, then stop when new traces reveal no new modes. Repeat after any major change.

    Random reading finds mostly successes. Enrich for failures with thumbs-down, complaints, escalations and analyst corrections. The price is that you stop measuring how often things fail. Simulation 4 shows both sides.

    What a good case looks like

    • The input as it arrived, with the context the system saw. Not tidied.
    • What good looks like: a reference answer for factual cases, or a checklist ("states the 90-day notice period; does not promise a rate") for open ones. Checklists are easier to agree on.
    • A failure-mode tag, so you can see which fix moved which number.

    How to keep the set honest

    • Split it. Tune on a development portion and keep a held-out portion you rarely look at. Edit until every case passes and you have fitted the set.
    • Keep a random, unfiltered sample. The failure set says what to fix; the random sample says how often things fail, and stops a new model that fails differently looking better than it is.
    • Protect the data. Real chats contain names and account numbers. Mask them, restrict access, and do not paste production cases into a public tool or hosted judge without approval. Which rules apply is for privacy and compliance to say.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting raw agreement

      "Reviewers agree 93%." On a set that is 95% acceptable, that is barely better than chance.

      Spot itAsk for kappa or alpha, class balance, item count and an interval.
    • Unexamined labels

      A 3 to 2 split is averaged into "problem", one expert labels everything, or the rubric was never piloted. The ambiguity, and the lesson about the guideline, vanish.

      Spot itAsk how many items had a split vote, what share had a second labeller, and where the guideline version history is.
    • Judging a judge by accuracy

      A judge that passes everything scores 95% on a set that is 95% good.

      Spot itAsk for true-positive and true-negative rates against humans, and judge-human versus human-human agreement on the same items.
    • A test set that is all successes, or all failures

      Random reading finds few failures. A failure-only set says nothing about overall rate and overstates a new model.

      Spot itAsk how cases were chosen and whether a random sample sits alongside.
    • Tuned, stale or leaked set

      Prompts are edited until every case passes, the set predates new products, or cases reached a prompt or training file. Scores look good while users complain.

      Spot itAsk whether a held-out portion exists, compare case dates with last month's traffic, and check whether test cases appear in any prompt or training file.
    • Real customer data in the wrong place

      Unmasked conversations in a spreadsheet or third-party labelling tool.

      Spot itAsk where the set is stored, who can read it, how identifiers were removed and who approved the vendor.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Two reviewers are each right 95% of the time and only 5% of items are real problems. Why is raw agreement about 90% while kappa is only moderate?

    Most items are easy "acceptable" ones, so much of the agreement would happen by chance. Kappa strips that out and is left with the rare class, where the difficulty sits. Excellent raw agreement, moderate kappa.

    2. Two reviewers agree on 93 of 100 items. What do you need to know before calling that good?

    The class balance. If 94% of items are acceptable, chance alone gives about 88% agreement and kappa is only about 0.42. Ask for kappa or alpha, the item count and an interval.

    3. Why does a majority of three reviewers give better labels than one, and when does it help less?

    One reviewer's slip is outvoted by the other two, provided errors are independent. If reviewers share a bias, the vote repeats it and the gain shrinks. Each extra pair of reviewers also helps less than the last.

    4. Why does label noise make two models look more alike than they are?

    A wrong label can turn a correct answer into a "miss" or a wrong one into a "hit", which pulls every score towards 50%. The true gap between models shrinks, so you need better labels or more items to see it.

    5. Name the four causes of annotator disagreement and a different fix for each.

    Guideline gap (write the rule, add examples), rater error or drift (calibrate, shorten sessions, adjudicate), ambiguous item (fix or specify context), legitimate difference of view (keep the spread, define the audience, soft labels).

    6. Your LLM judge agrees with a reviewer on 90% of items. Human-human agreement on the same items is 94%, and 92% of items are "pass". Is the judge good?

    Not yet shown. A judge that always says pass already agrees about 92% of the time. Compare chance-corrected agreement for judge-human and human-human on the same items, and check how many true failures the judge catches (true-negative rate) and how often it wrongly fails good answers.

    7. Why pair a failure-mined test set with a random sample?

    The failure set shows what to fix but not how often the system fails, and it favours a new model that fails differently. A random labelled sample estimates the overall rate and catches new problems.

    8. You have read 60 traces and found six failure modes, and the last 20 traces added none. What next?

    Rough saturation for this traffic. Turn each mode into cases with pass conditions, hold some out, add a random sample, and schedule another round after the next major change or new traffic type. It does not prove no other mode exists.
