    M5.3 · M5.4 Day one ★ About 42 minutes

    When Humans Disagree, and Test Sets Built from Real Failures

    Why raw agreement flatters you, what a human label can and cannot be worth, what to do when two expert reviewers split, and why a few hundred cases mined from real failures will improve a GenAI system faster than any public benchmark.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceHuman judgement is the reference every other GenAI metric is checked against, so measure how consistent it is (chance-corrected, with each disagreement diagnosed), then spend it on a test set built from your own system's real failures.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two compliance reviewers each mark 200 assistant answers "acceptable" or "problem". They agree on 93%. The project lead calls that excellent.

    It is not. Only 5% of answers are problems, so most agreement is two people writing "acceptable" again and again. Two reviewers flipping coins weighted 95/5 would agree about 90% of the time. Think of two people guessing "dry" for tomorrow's weather in a desert: they agree constantly and prove nothing.

    • Human judgement is the ruler, and rulers have error (M5.3). Every automatic metric and LLM judge is validated against human labels. If the humans are inconsistent, you cannot tell a good judge, or a good model, from a slightly worse one. Measure the ruler first.
    • The best questions come from your own failures (M5.4). Public benchmarks measure what someone else cared about. Your system fails in specific, repeated, boring ways: it mixes up two similar products, ignores a circular's date, answers an Arabic question in English. Collect those, label them carefully, and you have a test set that measures what you need to fix.
    Hold onto thisNever quote raw agreement alone. Give a chance-corrected number, the item count and the class balance, then ask what each disagreement was about. A disagreement is information about your task, not just noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.3 · Measuring agreement properly

    People can disagree on three layers: is the answer factually right, is it faithful to the source, and is it good (clear, toned well, complete, safe). Agreement is highest on the first and lowest on the third. The more your rubric leans on taste, the more disagreement to expect.

    Cohen's kappa asks: of the agreement that was possible beyond luck, how much did the two raters achieve? Like a golf handicap, it scores you against what a guesser would do, not against zero.

    κ=po−pe1−pe

    po is the share of items where the raters agree. pe is the agreement expected if each rater labelled at random with their own label frequencies.

    Worked example. 100 answers: both say "acceptable" on 90, both say "problem" on 3, and they split on 7 (4 and 3). po = 0.93. Rater A says acceptable on 94 and B on 93, so pe = 0.94 × 0.93 + 0.06 × 0.07 = 0.878. Kappa = (0.93 − 0.878) ÷ (1 − 0.878) ≈ 0.42. A 93% agreement is only moderate.

    • What it means: the share of the gap between chance and perfect that the raters closed. 0 is no better than chance, 1 is perfect, negative is worse than chance.
    • How to read it: conventional bands (Landis and Koch, 1977) call 0.4 to 0.6 moderate, 0.6 to 0.8 substantial and above 0.8 near-perfect. They are rules of thumb. Judge by what the disagreement costs the decision. Human-labelled GenAI quality tasks often land around 0.4 to 0.7.
    • What a change means: going from 0.4 to 0.6 cuts the unclosed gap from 60% to 40%, a third fewer avoidable disagreements. Going from 0.90 to 0.95 halves it. The same raw gain is worth more near the top.
    • Misreading to avoid: low kappa with high raw agreement does not mean bad raters. It usually means a rare class (Simulation 1). So never compare kappa across datasets with different class balance, and always show label frequencies beside it.

    Other agreement statistics follow the same logic: all are chance-corrected, and 0 means chance, 1 means perfect. Pick by setup.

    SituationUseWhat it adds, and how to read it
    Two raters, categoriesCohen's kappaRead as above. Sensitive to class balance.
    Many raters, each item seen by a fewKrippendorff's alphaHandles missing ratings, any number of raters and ordinal or interval scales. Read like kappa. The usual default for evaluation work.
    Same number of raters on every itemFleiss' kappaCohen's idea extended to a panel.
    1–5 or 1–7 ratingsWeighted kappa or ordinal alphaA 4 versus a 5 costs less than a 1 versus a 5, as a near-miss should.

    Two cautions. Kappa and alpha are unstable on rare classes: with 12 problems in 400 items, a handful of changed labels moves them a lot, so put an interval on them (bootstrap the items, F2.2). And a low value says "something is inconsistent", not what. That needs the diagnosis below.

    Why people disagree, and what to do about each kind

    There is no single answer to "when humans disagree, what is right?", because disagreement has four causes with different cures. Sort every cluster of disagreements into one.

    CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
    Guideline gapThe rubric is silent. "Faithful" does not say whether omitting a fee clause counts, so reviewers fill the gap differently.Write the rule, add worked examples, relabel. Commonest and cheapest fix.
    Rater error or driftOne rater is an outlier, labels worsen late in a session, or a rater has a private rule.Calibration session, feedback, shorter batches, adjudicate affected items.
    Ambiguous itemThe question is underspecified ("Can I withdraw it?" with no account named).Fix, remove or add the missing context. An assistant that answers without asking is arguably the real failure.
    Legitimate difference of viewTone, formality, dialect. Informed raters stably differ.Keep the spread as a soft label, name the target audience, or sample raters to match customers.

    So what is the "right" label?

    Four defensible answers. Choose by task.

    • Adjudicate to one gold label. A senior reviewer resolves each split using the guideline. Best when a fact of the matter exists. It hides leftover ambiguity.
    • Majority vote. Cheap and robust if raters err independently (Simulation 3). It fails when the majority shares a bias, such as English-first raters judging Arabic fluency by reading speed.
    • Model the raters. Dawid-Skene-style methods (1979) estimate each rater's reliability and the true label together, so a careful rater counts for more. Worth it with many raters and sparse overlap, as in crowd work.
    • Keep the distribution. If 3 of 5 experts say "too blunt", the label is 60% blunt. These soft labels come from the perspectivist line of research (Plank, 2022). A model that confidently says "100% fine" on a split item should be penalised, which a proper scoring rule such as the Brier score does.

    Rule: adjudicate when there is a fact, keep the spread when there is taste, and always keep the raw ratings.

    Noisy labels set a ceiling on what you can measure

    Noisy labels shrink differences, like measuring with a stretchy tape. Say each rater is right 85% of the time, independently. A model truly correct 82% of the time is scored against those labels at 0.82 × 0.85 + 0.18 × 0.15 = 72.4% (right when the label is right, or wrong when the label is wrong too). A truly 86% model measures 75.2%. The true 4.0-point gap shows as 2.8.

    • What it means: measured score = true score × a + (1 − true score) × (1 − a), where a is label accuracy. It pulls every score toward 50% by a factor of 2a − 1.
    • What a change means: better labels directly widen the gap you can see, so you need fewer items to detect a real improvement.
    • The cure: more raters per item and better guidelines. Simulation 3 shows how fast majority voting repairs independent noise, and where it stops.

    Human agreement as the yardstick for an LLM judge

    A judge (M5.2) should agree with humans about as well as humans agree with each other. Human-human agreement is the ceiling: if reviewers reach alpha 0.45, a judge at 0.40 against them performs like a reviewer, and demanding 0.9 asks more than the task allows. Compare both on the same items with the same statistic. Also report the judge's true-positive rate (share of real passes it passes) and true-negative rate (share of real failures it catches) separately. A judge that says "pass" to everything scores high accuracy on a skewed set while catching nothing.

    Running a human evaluation without wasting it

    • Pilot the guideline first. 2–3 reviewers label 30–50 items, you measure agreement, read every disagreement, rewrite the rules and repeat. Start the main run once agreement stops improving.
    • Ask narrow, checkable questions rather than "rate quality 1–5". "Does the answer state the correct fee?" agrees far better than "is it good?". Binary or three-level scales beat seven.
    • Pairwise comparison ("A or B?") is easier than absolute scoring and drives preference leaderboards. It needs many comparisons and says nothing about whether either answer is acceptable.
    • Choose the reviewer. A compliance officer for policy, a native speaker of the right register for Arabic tone, a crowd worker for simple facts. Spend scarce experts on hard items and to calibrate cheaper reviewers.
    • Blind and randomise. Hide the system, shuffle order and balance position, since reviewers favour the first or longer answer.
    • Size by the decision. More items, tighter interval. For an 80% pass rate:
    Items judged95% interval on an 80% pass rateSmallest difference you could trust between two systems (roughly)
    25± 16 pointsabout 22 points
    50± 11 pointsabout 16 points

    M5.4 · Building test sets from real failures

    A test set is a sample of the situations you want handled. Three sources:

    SourceStrengthWeakness
    Public benchmarkFree, comparable across models.Not your documents, products, languages or users. Often seen in training. Tells you about models, not your system.
    Invented by the teamFast; good for week one.Reflects what the team imagines users ask. Too clean and easy.
    Real traffic and failuresReal phrasing, mixed Arabic and English, typos, the system's actual weak spots.Needs logging, privacy care and labelling effort. Biased by how you find failures.

    The loop, taught by practitioners such as Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar as error analysis:

    1. Collect traces: full records of interactions (question, retrieved context, tool calls, answer). Start with about a hundred.
    2. Read and write free notes. One person, ideally a domain expert, writes what went wrong in plain words. Preset labels like "hallucination" make you see only those.
    3. Group notes into failure modes and count them. Five to ten usually explain most failures. A model can propose groupings; a human checks.
    4. Turn each mode into cases with a clear pass condition: code-checkable (must include the disclaimer, must not contain an account number) or a rubric with a judge validated against humans.
    5. Stop at saturation: new traces reveal no new modes (one heuristic: twenty fresh traces, nothing new). Repeat after any major change to prompt, model, data or product.

    Random reading finds mostly successes. Enrich for failures with thumbs-down, complaints, human-agent escalations, analyst corrections, low judge scores, long or looping conversations and red-team attempts. The price is that you stop measuring prevalence. Simulation 4 shows both sides.

    What a good case looks like

    • The input as it arrived, with the context the system saw. Not tidied.
    • What good looks like: a reference answer for factual cases, or a checklist ("states the 90-day notice period; does not promise a rate; answers in Arabic") for open ones. Checklists are easier to agree on.
    • A failure-mode tag, so you can report pass rate by mode and see which fix moved which number.
    • Provenance and date: source, labeller, guideline version, and the set's agreement scores.

    How to keep the set honest

    • Split it. Tune on a development portion and keep a held-out portion you rarely look at. Edit until every case passes and you have fitted the set (F4.5).
    • Keep a random, unfiltered sample, labelled the same way. The failure set says what to fix; the random sample says how often things fail.
    • Keep regression cases. Every fixed bug becomes a permanent test run on each change, like a unit test (F10.3).
    • Protect the data. Real chats contain names, account numbers and IDs. Mask them, restrict access, and check storage against the bank's data-residency and privacy rules. Do not paste production cases into a public tool or hosted judge without approval. Which rules apply is for privacy and compliance to say.
    • Refresh it. A set from last March misses this March's failures. Add cases each cycle, retire discontinued products, track the date mix.
    • Mind selection. A set of only the current system's failures flatters a replacement that fails differently. Pair it with the random sample (F2.6).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting raw agreement

      "Reviewers agree 93%." On a set that is 95% acceptable, that is barely better than chance.

      Spot itAsk for kappa or alpha, class balance, item count and an interval.
    • Averaging disagreement away

      A 3 to 2 split becomes "problem" and the ambiguity, and the lesson about the guideline, vanish.

      Spot itAsk how many items had a split vote and what the team learned from them.
    • Judging a judge by accuracy

      A judge that passes everything scores 95% on a set that is 95% good.

      Spot itAsk for true-positive and true-negative rates against humans, and judge-human versus human-human agreement on the same items.
    • Guidelines written once

      The rubric was drafted before anyone read real outputs and never updated. Agreement is low and nobody knows why.

      Spot itLook for guideline version history and a pilot round with measured agreement.
    • A test set that is all successes, or all failures

      Random reading finds few failures. A failure-only set says nothing about overall rate and overstates a new model.

      Spot itAsk how cases were chosen and whether a random sample sits alongside.
    • Tuning on the test set

      Prompts are edited until every case passes, so the set measures how well you memorised it.

      Spot itAsk whether a held-out portion exists that builders have not been looking at.
    • Stale set

      Scores are stable and good while users complain. The set predates new products and phrasings.

      Spot itCompare the date distribution of cases with last month's real traffic.
    • Contamination and leakage

      Cases reach a prompt as examples, a fine-tuning set or a public benchmark. The model has seen the answers.

      Spot itCheck whether test cases appear in any prompt, few-shot example or training file.
    • Real customer data in the wrong place

      Unmasked conversations in a spreadsheet or third-party labelling tool.

      Spot itAsk where the set is stored, who can read it, how identifiers were removed and who approved the vendor.
    • One expert, treated as truth

      One reviewer labels everything, so personal habits are baked in and agreement cannot be measured.

      Spot itAsk what share of items had a second labeller.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Two reviewers each right 95% of the time (independent) judge items where 5% are real problems. What is their raw agreement and roughly their kappa?

    Raw agreement = 0.95² + 0.05² = 0.905. Each says "problem" with probability 0.05 × 0.95 + 0.95 × 0.05 = 0.095, so chance agreement = 0.095² + 0.905² ≈ 0.828. Kappa = (0.905 − 0.828) ÷ (1 − 0.828) ≈ 0.45. Excellent raw agreement, moderate kappa, because the rare class carries the difficulty.

    2. Over 100 items, both reviewers say pass on 90, both say fail on 3, and they split 4 and 3. Compute kappa.

    po = 0.93. A passes 94, B passes 93, so pe = 0.94 × 0.93 + 0.06 × 0.07 = 0.878. Kappa = 0.052 ÷ 0.122 ≈ 0.43.

    3. Reviewers are right 80% of the time, independently. What is the label accuracy with a majority of three?

    P(at least 2 of 3 right) = 0.8³ + 3 × 0.8² × 0.2 = 0.512 + 0.384 = 89.6%, if errors are independent. Shared bias shrinks the gain.

    4. Why does label noise make two models look more alike than they are?

    A wrong label can turn a correct answer into a "miss" or a wrong one into a "hit". In the binary case the measured score is pulled toward 50% by a factor of 2a − 1 (a is label accuracy). With a = 0.85, a 4-point gap measures as 2.8.

    5. Name the four causes of annotator disagreement and a different fix for each.

    Guideline gap (write the rule, add examples), rater error or drift (calibrate, shorten sessions, adjudicate), ambiguous item (fix or specify context), legitimate difference of view (keep the spread, define the audience, soft labels).

    6. Your LLM judge agrees with a reviewer on 90% of items. Human-human agreement on the same items is 94%, and 92% of items are "pass". Is the judge good?

    Not yet shown. A judge that always says pass already agrees about 92% of the time. Compare chance-corrected agreement for judge-human and human-human on the same items, and check how many true failures the judge catches (true-negative rate) and how often it wrongly fails good answers.

    7. Why pair a failure-mined test set with a random sample?

    The failure set shows what to fix but not how often the system fails, and it favours a new model that fails differently. A random labelled sample estimates the overall rate and catches new problems.

    8. You have read 60 traces and found six failure modes, and the last 20 traces added none. What next?

    Rough saturation for this traffic. Turn each mode into cases with pass conditions, hold some out, add a random sample, and schedule another round after the next major change or new traffic type. It does not prove no other mode exists.
