    What Would Change the Answer? Partial Dependence, Counterfactuals and the Limits of Explanation

    How to see what a model does as one input moves, why an average can hide the whole story, how to tell a declined customer what would flip the decision, and why every one of these explanations describes the model and not the world.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePartial dependence and ICE plots ask "what does the model do if I move this input?", counterfactuals ask "what is the smallest realistic change that flips the decision?", and both answer questions about the model's behaviour only, so they can be wrong about the customer's world when features are correlated, when the model is one of many equally good ones, or when the model is retrained.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two questions come up about any credit model within a week of it going live.

    The first comes from the risk committee: "Does higher card utilisation raise the model's default risk, and by how much?" The model is a gradient-boosted ensemble with 200 trees. There is no coefficient to read. So you do an experiment on the model: take real customers, set everyone's utilisation to 20%, record the average predicted risk, then set it to 30%, 40%, and so on. Plot the average against utilisation. That curve is a partial dependence plot (PDP). Draw one line per customer instead of the average and you have ICE plots (individual conditional expectation).

    The second comes from a relationship manager: "Mr Khalid was declined for a personal finance. What would he need to change to be approved?" You search for the smallest change in his application that moves the score across the cut-off: a counterfactual. "Reduce your card balances to 30% of limits and complete 6 more months in your job" is a counterfactual, and it is a different thing from "the main reasons were your missed payments and utilisation".

    Both tools are what-if experiments on the model. That is their strength: they work on any model, however opaque. It is also their limit, and the third half of this lesson. An experiment on a model tells you what the model would say. It does not tell you what would happen to Mr Khalid if he did it.

    Hold onto thisAn average of different effects is not a typical effect. A flat PDP can mean "no effect for anyone" or "strong, opposite effects that cancel". Only the individual lines tell you which. And everything these plots show is the model's behaviour; whether the same is true of customers is a separate, causal question.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Partial dependence and ICE (F8.3)

    Let the model be f, the feature of interest xj, and everything else xC (the "complement"). The ICE curve for customer i keeps that customer's other features fixed and sweeps xj across a grid. The partial dependence is the average of the ICE curves over the data (Friedman, 2001; Goldstein et al., 2015):

    PDj(v)=1n∑i=1nf(v,xC(i))

    For each grid value v, overwrite feature j with v in every row, run the model, average the predictions. Cost: n × grid-size predictions, so use a few thousand rows, not millions. Always say which output you plot: probability (what a manager reads) or log-odds (where additive effects look straight).

    Three practical points about reading them.

    • Always draw the ICE lines under the PDP. Simulation 1 is the reason. If the lines are roughly parallel, the average is a fair summary. If they fan out, cross or split, the model contains an interaction: the effect of utilisation depends on something else (employer type, tenure, income band). Centring the lines at the left edge (c-ICE) makes it easy to compare slopes without being distracted by the different starting risks.
    • Mind the scale. A model that is additive in log-odds gives non-parallel lines on the probability scale, because the sigmoid squashes the same push differently at 2% and at 30%. Not every fan is an interaction.
    • Trees give staircases. A boosted tree is piecewise constant, so a PDP looks jagged. Do not over-read a single step; look for the broad shape. Validators also use the plot as a sanity check: where the business expects a monotone relationship (more missed payments should not lower risk), a PDP that dips is worth investigating, and many boosting libraries can enforce monotone constraints.

    Also useful: a two-feature PDP (a heat map) for one suspected interaction, and a histogram or rug along the axis showing where the data actually sits, which matters for the next problem.

    The catch: the PDP asks about customers who do not exist

    Setting everyone's salary to AED 40,000 while keeping their credit limits creates a customer with a AED 40,000 salary and a AED 8,000 limit, or an AED 8,000 salary and a AED 120,000 limit. When two features move together, many of those combinations are rare or absent in the data. The model has never seen them, so what it returns there is extrapolation: a tree returns whatever the nearest leaf holds, a neural net returns something smooth and unconstrained. The PDP averages those guesses in with the sensible points and gives them equal weight.

    There is a second, subtler issue even when the model is perfectly good. The PDP deliberately breaks the link between salary and limit. It answers "what does the model do with salary, holding limit as it is?". If you instead average over customers who really have that salary (a "marginal plot" or M-plot), the limit rides along with salary and you measure the model's response to both at once. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions. Accumulated local effects (ALE; Apley and Zhu, 2020) are the usual compromise: look at small steps in salary using only customers whose actual salary is near that step, and add up the local changes. ALE stays on the data and removes the double counting.

    The rule that follows: interpret a PDP only where there is data (show the rug), distrust it for strongly correlated features, and reach for ALE or conditional methods when correlation is the point. Notice also that the model here is fully known and still the PDP and the "real" answer differ. That is not a bug in the plot; it is the difference between intervening on a feature and observing it. F8.5 comes back to that.

    Counterfactuals and adverse-action reasons (F8.4)

    A counterfactual explanation says: "if your application had been x′ instead of x, the decision would have been approval." Wachter and colleagues (2017) framed it as a search: find the closest x′ such that the model's output crosses the decision boundary.

    x′=argminx′∈𝒜cost(x,x′)such thatf(x′)≤cut-off

    𝒜 is the set of changes that are actually allowed, and that is where all the substance lies. Age and nationality cannot be changed and should not be used. Missed payments in the last 12 months cannot be undone, only waited out. Tenure rises one month per month and cannot be bought. "Cost" should reflect effort to the customer, not distance in raw units: paying down AED 5,000 of card balance is not the same as AED 5,000 more salary.

    Three families of methods exist. For a linear or scorecard model, the search is exact and cheap (Ustun et al., 2019, call the result "actionable recourse"). For black boxes, search algorithms vary the inputs and query the model; DiCE (Mothilal et al., 2020) generates several diverse alternatives rather than one, so the customer has choices. For trees, one can enumerate the leaves that approve and find the nearest. In all cases you add constraints (immutable, directional, bounded) and a plausibility check: a counterfactual that asks for a customer who is 25 years old with 30 years of tenure is useless.

    Do not confuse counterfactuals with the other thing you are asked for, reasons for decline. These are different questions:

    Reason codes ("why")Counterfactual / recourse ("what would change it")
    QuestionWhich inputs pushed this score the wrong way, compared with a reference?What is the cheapest allowed change that flips the decision?
    Typical methodScorecard points below the best attainable per variable; or Shapley-type attributions on a complex model (F8.1 – F8.2).Constrained search over actionable features.
    Top answer can be"Two missed payments": the biggest contributor, and impossible to change today."Reduce card utilisation and wait six months": the cheapest route.

    Simulation 3 puts both side by side for one applicant, and then breaks the advice by retraining the model.

    What the simulation shows about counterfactuals in practice:

    • The biggest reason and the best advice differ. A notice that lists only contributors says "missed payments, tenure, utilisation". The customer can act on two of those three, and only one of them quickly.
    • Counterfactuals are conditional on this model, this month. Retrain, change the cut-off, or let the bureau data refresh, and the nearest approved point moves. If you tell customers what to do, you have made a promise the next model release may break. Banks handle this with wording ("based on our current criteria"), with stable scorecards, and by testing candidate advice against the next model before release.
    • Advice changes behaviour, and behaviour changes meaning. If everyone pays cards down just before applying, utilisation stops carrying the information it once did (the model is gamed), and the next retrain will lean on something else. Features the customer can move cheaply are the least trustworthy ones in the long run.
    • Recourse costs can be unequal across groups. Two customers with the same score may face very different effort to cross the line. Comparing the cost of recourse across customer groups is a fairness check that standard parity metrics do not capture (see G2).
    • It is not the same as a causal claim. "Reduce utilisation and you will be approved" is a statement about the model. Whether reducing utilisation actually makes the customer less likely to default, or merely hides the symptom, is the F9 causal question (F9.2).

    On the rules. In the United States, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require lenders to give specific, principal reasons for an adverse decision. The CFPB has said in guidance (Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03, as summarised by law firms and trade bodies; the circular text itself should be checked) that using a complex algorithm is no excuse, that reasons must describe the factors actually considered or scored, and that picking from a sample checklist is not enough. That is the clearest published example of a regulator tying model complexity to explanation duty. I have not confirmed what specific adverse-action wording rules CBUAE or SAMA apply to consumer credit decisions, and I would not assert one in a meeting. Ask Compliance which consumer-protection and data-protection provisions govern your product, and treat the US position as a design reference, not as local law. See A12 for how to read local expectations.

    The limits of post-hoc explanation (F8.5)

    "Post-hoc" means the explanation is produced after the fact, from outside, for a model already trained: PDP, ICE, Shapley attributions, LIME, counterfactual search. All of them probe the model and report what it does. Four limits follow.

    1. A model is not the world. The model learned correlations in last year's data. If it uses "premium card tier" because premium customers happen to be richer, an attribution will say "premium tier lowers risk". Upgrading a customer to premium would change the model's output, and change nothing about their risk. The explanation is a faithful description of the model and a false description of causation.
    2. Correlated features share the credit arbitrarily. When two inputs carry the same information, which one a flexible model leans on is partly an accident of training. Retrain with another random seed and the importance can move from one to the other with no loss in accuracy. Any explanation of "the" model is really an explanation of one member of a family of equally good models (the Rashomon effect).
    3. Explanations are themselves estimates. Shapley values are approximated with sampling and a choice of background data; LIME depends on a perturbation scheme. A January 2026 paper calls the resulting variation "explanation multiplicity": the same person, model and decision can get different attribution rankings when only the explainer settings change, and top-ranked features are less stable than magnitudes suggest. Brunet et al. (2022) found that models with similar accuracy can give widely different local explanations.
    4. Explanations can be persuasive and wrong. Adebayo et al. (2018) found that some popular saliency methods produce nearly identical maps from a trained network and from one with random weights, so they were explaining the image, not the model. Slack et al. (2020) showed that a deliberately biased classifier can be built to look unbiased to LIME and SHAP audits, because the explainers query inputs the real data never contains. Rudin (2019) argues that for high-stakes decisions one should prefer models that are interpretable by design over explaining black boxes after the fact.

    None of this means the tools are useless. It means each has a job and a scope.

    You want to knowPost-hoc tools areWhat else you need

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These tools sit between model building, regulation and customer communication.

    F8.1 · F8.2 · Global and local explanationsThe attribution methods this lesson leans on

    Permutation importance and Shapley values give the "which features" view. PDP/ICE give the "what shape" view, and counterfactuals give the "what to change" view.

    F4.3 · F4.4 · Trees and boostingWhy the PDP looks like a staircase

    The models most banks run on tabular data are the ones that need these tools, and monotone constraints are the tree-side answer to a dipping PDP.

    F4.1 · F4.2 · RegressionA coefficient is a built-in PDP

    In a logistic regression the PDP on the log-odds scale is a straight line with the coefficient as its slope. Interpretable models need none of this machinery.

    F2.5 · GLMsScorecards and exact recourse

    Linear log-odds is why Simulation 3 can solve for the cheapest plan exactly. The scorecard remains the most explainable credit model there is.

    F2.6 · ConfoundingWhy the model's story is not causal

    A feature the model uses may stand in for something the customer cannot change. This is confounding turned inside a model.

    F9.2 · Causal inferenceIntervening versus observing

    The difference between the PDP and the real-customer curve in Simulation 2 is the difference between do(x) and conditioning on x.

    F7.4 · CalibrationWhich scale you plot on

    PDP on probability only makes sense if the probabilities mean something; cut-off and counterfactual distance both depend on calibration.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringRetrains change explanations

    Track not only score drift but explanation drift: when the top reasons shift between model versions, customer advice and reason codes shift with them.

    B4 · PD, LGD, EAD and IFRS 9The credit models being explained

    Scorecards and PD models are where decline reasons, monotonic shapes and validator questions are most formal.

    G2 · FairnessProxies and recourse

    A proxy for a protected attribute can sit inside an excellent model. Explanations alone rarely expose it; group outcome tests do.

    G3 · Model governanceWhat validators ask for

    Validators routinely request PDPs, reason-code logic and stability tests. Knowing the limits lets you pre-empt the follow-up.

    A12 · Regulatory expectationsWhat local supervisors expect

    Where explainability duties sit in the Gulf, and how to find out what applies to your product.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Retail credit decisions. Adverse-action reasons, appeal handling, validator review of variable shapes. This is where counterfactuals and reason codes matter most.
    • Model validation. PDP/ICE per key variable as a standard exhibit, with monotonicity and out-of-support checks.
    • Fraud and AML alerts. Analysts want "why this alert" in a line. Local attributions help triage; they should not be mistaken for evidence of fraud.
    • Marketing and retention. "What would make this customer less likely to leave" is a counterfactual question that is really a causal one, so be careful what you promise from the model alone.
    • LLM-based assistants. A model's own written justification is a post-hoc explanation too, with the same limits (R4).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    PDP and ICE are settled tools. The live arguments are about whether post-hoc explanation is good enough for high-stakes decisions, how stable explanations are, and what counts as legitimate recourse.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Reading the PDP and ignoring the ICE lines

      "Tenure has no effect on risk." The average is flat because new-to-bank customers and long-standing ones move in opposite directions.

      Spot itAsk to see the individual curves or a split by segment. A flat PDP with wide ICE spread is an interaction.
    • Interpreting regions with no data

      The curve extends to a salary-to-limit mix nobody has. The model's guess is presented as a finding.

      Spot itCheck for a rug or histogram on the axis. Trim the plot to the 5th–95th percentile, or use ALE.
    • PDP with highly correlated features

      Income and limit plotted separately, each looking modest or contradictory.

      Spot itLook at the correlation between the plotted feature and the rest. Above about 0.7, use ALE or a two-feature plot.
    • Treating an attribution as a cause

      "Premium card holders are safer, so let's push upgrades."

      Spot itAsk what the customer could do to change the feature, and whether that would change the outcome. If the answer needs an experiment, say so.
    • Counterfactuals that ask for the impossible

      Advice to be older, to have a different nationality, or to erase last year's arrears.

      Spot itCheck the actionability constraints on every feature, and read a sample of generated advice as a customer would.
    • Advice that expires

      The customer does what was asked and is declined by the next model release.

      Spot itTest stored counterfactuals against the candidate model before it goes live. Check wording for a "current criteria" qualifier.
    • One-seed, one-background explanations

      A single Shapley run is quoted as "the" reason.

      Spot itRe-run with different seeds and backgrounds. If the top three reasons change, the explanation is not stable enough for a notice.
    • Passing a model on its explanations

      "The importance chart looks sensible, so the model is fair."

      Spot itAsk for outcome tests across groups and for a check that the model works when inputs are moved off the data (the Slack et al. attack).
    • Explanations of different models mixed in one document

      PDPs from last year's model illustrate this year's.

      Spot itEvery exhibit should carry the model version and the data window it was computed on.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a credit or business head · 30 seconds
    “For a declined customer we can say two things: why the model scored them as it did, and what would change the outcome. They are not the same. The biggest reason might be a missed payment they cannot undo, while the quickest route is paying down cards and waiting six months. I'd want us to give the second kind of advice only with the right caveat, because it describes our current model, and a retrain can change it. And it shows what the model rewards, not what causes good repayment.”
    To a validator or technical interviewer
    “I'd show PDP with ICE for each key variable, with a rug so we only read where there is data, and ALE where features are strongly correlated. Reason codes would be tested for stability across explainer seeds and background sets, and mapped to a policy, not generated free-form. Counterfactuals would use actionability constraints and be re-run against each candidate model. I'd treat all of this as description of the model; any causal claim goes through a separate experiment.”
    “The partial dependence plot for tenure is flat. Can we drop the variable?”

    Not on that evidence. A flat average can hide opposing effects in different customer groups. I'd look at the ICE lines or split by segment. Then I'd check importance by permutation and test the model's performance without the variable. If all agree it does nothing, drop it.

    “Why not just use SHAP for the decline reasons?”

    It can work, but I'd test it first. Attributions depend on background data and approximation settings, and top-ranked reasons can change between runs. They also explain the model, not the customer's situation, and may name a feature the customer cannot change. I'd add stability tests and a policy for mapping attributions to reason text, or use a scorecard where the reasons are exact.

    “Is a counterfactual the same as telling the customer how to get approved?”

    Only if it is actionable, realistic and survives the next model update. A raw nearest-point search may ask for something impossible or implausible. And it is a statement about the model; it doesn't guarantee that the change reduces the customer's real risk. I'd phrase it as guidance against current criteria, not a promise.

    “Two models have the same accuracy but different important features. Which is right?”

    Possibly neither, and both can be fine. When features are correlated, equally accurate models can lean on different ones. I'd check which is more stable over time, which relies on features available and appropriate at decision time, and which is easier to defend to the customer and regulator. If the story matters more than a point of AUC, pick the simpler or more stable one.

    “Can I use explanations to prove the model isn't biased?”

    No. Explanations can show the model does not use a protected attribute directly, but proxies carry the same information, and explainers can be fooled. Fairness needs outcome testing across groups, and ideally an independent review. Explanations are supporting evidence at best.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A PDP for utilisation is almost flat, but the average ICE slope across customers is zero because half rise and half fall. What do you do?

    Do not conclude utilisation has no effect. Show the ICE lines, and look for the variable that splits customers into the rising and falling groups (an interaction). Test the model without the feature. A flat PDP means the model's average response is flat, not that nobody's prediction moves.

    2. Why can a PDP be misleading when salary and credit limit are correlated at 0.9?

    It sets salary to values while keeping other customers' limits, which creates salary-and-limit combinations that rarely exist. The model's output there is extrapolation, and it gets the same weight as real regions. Also it separates the effects the data ties together. ALE or conditional plots, plus a rug showing where the data is, are the remedies.

    3. In Simulation 2 the model's true salary coefficient is −0.8 and the limit coefficient is +0.6. With correlation 0.9, roughly what log-odds slope per sd of salary do real customers show?

    PDP slope: −0.8. Among real customers, a one sd rise in salary comes with a 0.9 sd rise in limit on average, so the slope is about −0.8 + 0.6 × 0.9 = −0.26. The model has not changed; the question has.

    4. How does a reason code differ from a counterfactual?

    A reason code ranks which inputs pushed this customer's score the wrong way relative to a reference. A counterfactual finds the cheapest allowed change that flips the decision. The top reason may be unchangeable (missed payments), while the cheapest counterfactual uses other levers.

    5. A customer follows your counterfactual advice and is declined by the next model release. Where did the process fail, and what would you change?

    The advice was tied to one model version and presented as a promise. Fixes: wording tied to current criteria, testing stored counterfactuals against candidate models before release, avoiding advice on features likely to change weight, and giving margin beyond the bare threshold so small retrains do not reverse it.

    6. Model B uses card tier and has an AUC almost equal to Model A, which uses the real driver. Name two reasons to prefer A and one reason you might still ship B.

    Prefer A because its explanations point at something that causes the outcome, and because the proxy relationship may break (a change in card policy or customer mix). Also B will degrade faster under drift. You might ship B if the real driver is unavailable at decision time, or costly or intrusive to collect, with monitoring on the proxy relationship.

    7. Why can't an explanation prove a model is not using a protected attribute?

    Other features can carry the same information, and the explanation will credit them. Explainers also probe the model on inputs that may not look like real data, and a model can behave differently there. Use outcome tests across groups and independent validation.
