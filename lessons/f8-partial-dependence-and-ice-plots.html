    What Would Change the Answer? Partial Dependence, Counterfactuals and the Limits of Explanation

    How to see what a model does as one input moves, why an average can hide the whole story, how to tell a declined customer what would flip the decision, and why every one of these explanations describes the model and not the world.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePartial dependence and ICE plots ask "what does the model do if I move this input?", counterfactuals ask "what is the smallest realistic change that flips the decision?", and both describe the model, not the customer's world, so they can mislead when features are correlated, when many equally good models exist, or after a retrain.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two questions arrive within a week of any credit model going live.

    Risk committee: "Does higher card utilisation raise default risk?" A boosted model has no coefficient to read, so run an experiment on it. Set everyone's utilisation to 20%, record the average predicted risk, then repeat at 30%, 40% and so on. That average, plotted, is a partial dependence plot (PDP). One line per customer instead of the average gives ICE plots (individual conditional expectation).

    Relationship manager: "Mr Khalid was declined. What would he need to change?" Search for the smallest change to his application that moves the score across the cut-off: a counterfactual. "Cut card balances to 30% of limits and complete 6 more months in your job" is a counterfactual. "Your main reasons were missed payments and utilisation" is not.

    Both are what-if experiments on the model. That is why they work on any model, and also their limit: they say what the model would do, not what would happen to Mr Khalid.

    Hold onto thisAn average of different effects is not a typical effect: a hospital's average patient temperature can be normal while half its patients have fevers. A flat PDP means "no effect for anyone" or "strong opposite effects that cancel"; only the individual lines say which.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Partial dependence and ICE (F8.3)

    An ICE curve takes one customer, sweeps one feature across a grid and keeps everything else fixed. The partial dependence is the average of those curves. It reads as "the model's average output if everyone had this value". Use a few thousand rows to keep it fast, and say whether you plot probability (what managers read) or log-odds.

    • Always draw ICE lines under the PDP. Parallel lines mean the average is a fair summary. Fanning or crossing lines mean an interaction: utilisation matters differently by employer type, tenure or income. Centring the lines at the left edge compares slopes without the different starting risks.
    • Mind the scale. A model that is additive in log-odds gives non-parallel lines in probability. Not every fan is an interaction.
    • Use it as a sanity check. More missed payments should not lower risk. A dip is worth investigating, and many boosting libraries can enforce monotone constraints. Trees give staircases, so read the broad shape.

    The catch: the PDP asks about customers who do not exist

    Setting everyone's salary to AED 40,000 while keeping their limits creates people with an AED 8,000 limit. It is like testing a shoe in every size on every foot, including size 14 on a child. The model has never seen such customers, so its output is a guess, and the PDP averages those guesses in at equal weight.

    There is a second issue even with a perfect model. The PDP deliberately breaks the link between salary and limit. Looking only at real customers with that salary lets limit ride along, so you measure the response to both. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Accumulated local effects (ALE) are the usual compromise: they use only customers near each value, so they stay on the data.

    Rule: read a PDP only where there is data (show a rug of tick marks along the axis), distrust it for strongly correlated features, and use ALE when correlation is the point. The model here is fully known and the two answers still differ: intervening on a feature is not the same as observing it (F8.5 returns to this).

    Counterfactuals and adverse-action reasons (F8.4)

    A counterfactual explanation says "had your application been X instead, you would have been approved." Like a GPS reroute: the nearest road that still reaches the destination. The substance is in which changes are allowed. Age and nationality cannot change and should not be used. Missed payments can only be waited out. Tenure rises one month per month and cannot be bought. "Cost" should reflect customer effort: paying down AED 5,000 of balance is not the same as earning AED 5,000 more.

    For scorecards the search is exact and cheap; for black boxes, tools such as DiCE return several diverse alternatives. Always add constraints (immutable, directional, bounded) and a plausibility check: advice that needs a 25-year-old with 30 years of tenure is useless. Do not confuse counterfactuals with reasons for decline:

    Simulation 3 puts both side by side for one applicant, then breaks the advice by retraining.

    • The biggest reason and the best advice differ. A notice listing contributors says "missed payments, tenure, utilisation". The customer can act quickly on only one.
    • Counterfactuals hold for this model, this month. Retrain, move the cut-off or refresh bureau data and the nearest approved point moves. Mitigate with wording ("based on our current criteria") and by testing candidate advice against the next model before release.
    • It is not a causal claim. "Reduce utilisation and you will be approved" describes the model. Whether it makes the customer safer or merely hides the symptom is a causal question (F9.2). Advice also changes behaviour: if everyone pays cards down just before applying, utilisation stops carrying information. Recourse effort can also differ across groups, a fairness check standard metrics miss (see G2).

    On the rules. In the US, ECOA and Regulation B require specific reasons for an adverse decision, and the CFPB says a complex algorithm is no excuse. I have not confirmed what adverse-action wording rules CBUAE or SAMA apply to consumer credit, so do not assert one in a meeting. Ask Compliance and treat the US position as a design reference. See A12.

    The limits of post-hoc explanation (F8.5)

    "Post-hoc" means produced after training, from outside: PDP, ICE, Shapley, LIME, counterfactual search. All probe the model and report what it does. Four limits follow.

    1. A model is not the world. If the model uses "premium card tier" because premium customers happen to be richer, an attribution says "premium lowers risk". Upgrading a customer changes the model's output and nothing about their risk.
    2. Correlated features share credit arbitrarily. Two inputs carrying the same information are like two co-authors on one paper: who gets the credit is partly accident. Retrain with another seed and importance can swap with no loss in accuracy. You are explaining one member of a family of equally good models.
    3. Explanations are estimates. Shapley values depend on sampling and background data; LIME on its perturbation scheme. Same person, same model, different settings can give different top reasons.
    4. Explanations can persuade and be wrong. Researchers have built a deliberately biased classifier that passes LIME and SHAP audits, because explainers query inputs that real data never contains. Where stakes are high, consider models that are interpretable by design.

    The tools are not useless; each has a scope.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reading the PDP and ignoring the ICE lines

      "Tenure has no effect on risk." The average is flat because new-to-bank customers and long-standing ones move in opposite directions.

      Spot itAsk to see the individual curves or a split by segment. A flat PDP with wide ICE spread is an interaction.
    • Interpreting regions with no data, or correlated features

      The curve extends to a salary-to-limit mix nobody has, and the model's guess is presented as a finding.

      Spot itCheck for a rug on the axis and the correlation with other features. Above about 0.7, use ALE or a two-feature plot.
    • Treating an attribution as a cause

      "Premium card holders are safer, so let's push upgrades."

      Spot itAsk what the customer could do to change the feature, and whether that would change the outcome. If the answer needs an experiment, say so.
    • Counterfactuals that ask for the impossible, or expire

      Advice to be older, or the customer does what was asked and is declined by the next model release.

      Spot itCheck the actionability constraints, read a sample of advice as a customer would, and test stored counterfactuals against the candidate model.
    • One-seed, one-background explanations

      A single Shapley run is quoted as "the" reason.

      Spot itRe-run with different seeds and backgrounds. If the top three reasons change, the explanation is not stable enough for a notice.
    • Passing a model on its explanations

      "The importance chart looks sensible, so the model is fair."

      Spot itAsk for outcome tests across groups, and carry the model version and data window on every exhibit.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A PDP for utilisation is flat because half the ICE lines rise and half fall. What do you do?

    Do not conclude it has no effect. Show the ICE lines and find the variable that splits the rising and falling groups. Flat means the average response is flat, not that nobody's prediction moves.

    2. Why can a PDP be misleading when salary and credit limit are correlated at 0.9?

    It sets salary while keeping other customers' limits, creating combinations that rarely exist. The model's output there is a guess, weighted like real regions. Remedies: ALE and a rug.

    3. In Simulation 2 the model charges for salary and for limit, and the two are correlated at 0.9. Why do real customers show a much weaker salary effect than the PDP?

    The PDP changes salary and holds limit still. Among real customers, higher salary comes with higher limit, and the limit effect works against the salary effect, so they partly cancel. The model has not changed; the question has.

    4. How does a reason code differ from a counterfactual?

    A reason code ranks which inputs pushed this customer's score the wrong way relative to a reference. A counterfactual finds the cheapest allowed change that flips the decision. The top reason may be unchangeable (missed payments), while the cheapest counterfactual uses other levers.

    5. A customer follows your counterfactual advice and is declined by the next model release. Where did the process fail, and what would you change?

    The advice was tied to one model version and presented as a promise. Fixes: wording tied to current criteria, testing stored counterfactuals against candidate models before release, and adding margin beyond the bare threshold.

    6. Model B uses card tier and has an AUC almost equal to Model A, which uses the real driver. Name two reasons to prefer A and one reason you might still ship B.

    Prefer A because its explanations point at a real cause, and because the proxy link may break, so B degrades faster under drift. Ship B if the real driver is unavailable at decision time or costly to collect, with monitoring on the proxy relationship.

    7. Why can't an explanation prove a model is not using a protected attribute?

    Other features can carry the same information and take the credit. Explainers also probe off-data inputs, where a model can behave differently. Use outcome tests across groups and independent validation.
