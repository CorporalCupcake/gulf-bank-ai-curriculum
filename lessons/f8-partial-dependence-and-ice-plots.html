    Partial Dependence and Counterfactuals: What Would Change the Answer?

    Part 1

    What these tools are

    In one sentenceThese are what-if experiments on a model: change one input, watch the prediction, and see what moves.

    Take a model that predicts exam failure. There are three experiments to know:

    • Partial dependence plot (PDP): set every student's phone time to 20%, record the average predicted risk, then repeat at 30%, 40% and so on. The plotted averages are the PDP.
    • ICE plot (individual conditional expectation): the same sweep, but one line per student instead of the average.
    • Counterfactual: for one flagged student, find the smallest change that would push her below the cut-off. It is like "had you left 20 minutes earlier, you would have caught the train".

    All three work on any model. They show what the model would do, not what would happen to a real student.

    How they differ from similar things

    • Feature importance says how much a feature matters. A PDP says how: rising, falling, or flat.
    • SHAP splits one prediction among features. A counterfactual instead asks what change would flip it.
    • A reason for decline ("two missed payments") names the biggest push. A counterfactual names the cheapest allowed change. The top reason can be something nobody can change this year.
    Part 2

    PDP and ICE: the shape of an effect

    Here is the trap. An average can hide opposite stories. Below, the bold line is the PDP, and it is flat. "Phone time does not matter" looks true. The thin lines are individual students, and half of them climb while half fall.

    How it works

    1. Choose one feature and a grid of values, such as 10% to 90% phone time in 20 steps.
    2. For one student, set the feature to each grid value and keep everything else as it is. Record the prediction each time. That is her ICE line.
    3. Repeat for a few thousand students.
    4. Average the lines. That average is the PDP.

    How to read it

    • Parallel lines mean the average is a fair summary: everyone reacts alike.
    • Fanning or crossing lines mean an interaction: the feature matters differently depending on other features, such as sleep or subject.
    • A flat PDP means either no effect for anyone, or strong opposite effects that cancel. Only the ICE lines say which.
    • A dip that should not exist is worth investigating. More missed lessons should not lower the chance of failing.
    • Tree models give staircases. Read the broad shape, not each step.

    Always draw ICE lines under the PDP. Draw tick marks along the axis to show where the real data is.

    The catch: made-up students

    Setting every student to 15 revision hours a week while keeping their coffee habits creates students who revise 15 hours on no coffee at all. The model has rarely seen such students, so its output there is a guess, and the PDP averages the guesses in. This gets worse when features are strongly linked, such as salary and credit limit.

    Accumulated local effects (ALE) are the usual fix. They split the feature into bins, measure the change in prediction within each bin using only students already in it, and add the changes up. They stay on real data, but are harder to explain to non-specialists.

    Part 3

    Counterfactuals: what would change the answer?

    A counterfactual finds the nearest point on the other side of the model's decision line. It is like a satnav rerouting you to the closest road that still reaches your destination.

    How it works

    1. Start from the flagged student's inputs.
    2. Set the rules. Some things cannot change (age), some only move one way (months of history), and some have a limit.
    3. Search for the closest allowed point where the prediction crosses the cut-off. For a simple scorecard this is exact. For a black box it is an optimisation. Tools such as DiCE return several different options, so the person can choose a route.
    4. Check it is realistic. Advice that needs a 25-year-old with 30 years of experience is useless.

    How to read it

    • "Cut phone time to 40% and revise 2 more hours" means the model would stop flagging her at that point. It is a recipe, not a promise.
    • Several options are better than one. Effort differs: paying down AED 5,000 is not the same as earning AED 5,000 more.
    • No option within the rules is a valid answer. Say so plainly.
    • Models get retrained. A student who follows the advice may still be flagged next term, because the line moved.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    ToolQuestionScopeWatch out for
    PDPOn average, how does the output move with this input?Global shapeHides opposite effects
    ICEHow does it move for each person?IndividualMessy with many lines
    ALEThe same, but staying on real dataGlobal shapeHarder to explain
    CounterfactualWhat is the cheapest allowed change?One caseNeeds rules and a realism check
    SHAP / reason codesWhy this answer?One caseNot a recipe for change

    These tools are strong at:

    • Working on any model, including black boxes.
    • Sanity checks: does the model respond to inputs in a sensible direction?
    • Useful guidance for the person affected.

    They are weak at:

    • Showing causes. They test the model, not the world. "The model would flip" is not "her outcome would change".
    • Correlated inputs, where the experiment invents unrealistic people.
    • Staying valid over time, since a retrained model draws a new line.

    In practice: draw ICE lines under every PDP, use ALE when features are strongly linked, and constrain every counterfactual.

    Show the formula

    PDP at a value v = the average of the model's prediction over all students, after setting this one feature to v for each of them. ICE is the same without the average.

    In banking, a decline notice gives the reasons, while a counterfactual gives the customer a route to approval, such as "reduce card balances and wait six months".

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting a flat PDP

      Flat can mean no effect, or opposite effects cancelling. Look at the ICE lines.

    • Reading the plot where there is no data

      The edges of a PDP rest on made-up students. Show where the real data lies.

    • Treating a counterfactual as a promise

      It describes the model today. Retraining or other changes can flip the result again.

    • Suggesting impossible changes

      Without rules, the nearest fix may be a different age or a past event. Always constrain it.

    1. What is the difference between a PDP and an ICE plot?

    ICE has one line per student. The PDP is the average of those lines.

    2. A PDP is flat. Does the feature matter?

    Not necessarily either way. The ICE lines show whether nobody reacts, or whether opposite reactions cancel.

    3. Why can a PDP be misleading with correlated features?

    It sets one feature while holding the others, creating unrealistic cases the model has hardly seen. ALE avoids this by staying near real data.

    4. How does a counterfactual differ from a reason for decline?

    A reason names what pushed the score the wrong way, even if it cannot change. A counterfactual names the cheapest allowed change that flips the decision.

    5. Why must a counterfactual have rules?

    Otherwise it may ask for changes that are impossible or unrealistic, such as a different age.
