    Smaller, Faster, Cheaper: Quantization, Pruning, Distillation, Latency Budgets and LLM Serving

    How a model is squeezed to fit a smaller machine and what that costs in accuracy, how a fraud score survives a 30-millisecond budget, and why one GPU can serve hundreds of chatbot users at once.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRunning a model is mostly about moving numbers through memory, not about arithmetic, so you make it cheaper by storing fewer or smaller numbers (quantization, pruning, distillation), by never waiting on the slowest part of a request (latency budgets) and by keeping the GPU busy with many users at once (batching).

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A GPU is a very fast chef in a tiny kitchen. Cooking is quick. The slow part is walking to the cold store for ingredients. Cook one plate per trip and most of the shift is walking. Cook forty plates per trip and the walk is shared.

    An 8-billion-parameter model at 16 bits is 16 GB of weights. To produce one token the GPU reads all 16 GB from memory. At about 3.35 TB/s (an H100 SXM) that takes about 5 ms. The arithmetic takes a fraction of that. So the GPU waits for memory, not maths. This is memory-bound, and it explains nearly everything here.

    Three families of tricks follow.

    • Move fewer bytes. Store weights in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 (quantization), remove weights that barely matter (pruning), or train a small model to imitate a big one (distillation).
    • Never wait on the slowest piece. A fraud decision has a fixed window and the model is one step in it. The latency budget divides that window. The danger is the slow tail, not the average.
    • Share each trip to memory. Read the weights once and serve many users together (batching).
    Hold onto thisCompression is a trade, not a free lunch: every method gives back some accuracy for memory, speed or cost. Measure the loss on your own task and data instead of trusting "4-bit loses almost nothing". In real-time systems the number that matters is the slow 1%.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Quantization: fewer bits per weight

    A weight is normally a 16-bit float (FP16 or BF16, see F6.5). Quantization stores a small integer plus a shared scale instead. 8 bits gives 256 levels, 4 bits only 16. It is like rounding prices to the nearest 5 dirhams: smaller to store, slightly wrong.

    The simplest scheme, symmetric absmax rounding: take a block of weights, find the largest magnitude m, space levels evenly from −m to +m, and round each weight to its nearest level. To rebuild a weight, multiply the integer by the scale.

    s=max|w|2b−1−1,w^=s·round(w/s)

    b is the bit count, s the step between levels. At 4 bits there are 7 steps each side of zero. A weight's rounding error is at most half a step, so error scales with the largest weight in the block. Each bit removed roughly doubles the step, so error climbs fast below 4 bits.

    Outliers are the enemy. If one weight is 15 times larger than the rest, the step s stretches to cover it, and the ordinary small weights all round to zero or one level. It is like measuring everyone in a room with a ruler marked only in metres because a giraffe walked in. LLMs have such outliers, especially in a few activation channels, which is why naive 8- and 4-bit conversion once ruined models that looked fine in FP16 (Dettmers et al., 2022). The cure is group-wise quantization: many small blocks, each with its own scale. A block of 128 weights costs one extra 16-bit scale, so "4-bit" really means about 4.125 bits per weight. An outlier now ruins only its own block.

    That is plain round to nearest. Smarter methods keep the same storage but pick better integers.

    • GPTQ quantizes a layer column by column and adjusts the remaining weights to cancel the error just introduced, using a small calibration set (Frantar et al., 2022).
    • AWQ notices that about 1% of weights matter far more because they multiply large activations, and scales those channels up before rounding (Lin et al., 2024).
    • SmoothQuant shifts difficulty from activations to weights with per-channel rescaling, so both fit in 8 bits (Xiao et al., 2023).

    Very low bits (2–3, QuIP, AQLM) are in the edge lesson. Four distinctions for meetings:

    ChoiceOptionsWhy it matters
    What is quantizedWeights only (W4A16), or weights and activations (W8A8, FP8, W4A4)Weight-only saves memory and speeds memory-bound decoding. Adding activations also speeds the arithmetic (long inputs, big batches) but loses accuracy more easily.
    WhenPost-training (PTQ) or quantization-aware training (QAT)PTQ takes minutes to hours and is the default. QAT trains with rounding in the loop and recovers more accuracy at 4 bits and below, at the cost of a training run.
    FormatINT8, INT4, FP8, FP4 (MXFP4, NVFP4)Floating-point formats spend bits on range, so cope better with outliers. Speed-up needs native hardware support.
    What stays high precisionKV cache, embeddings, last layer, normsThe KV cache (M1.5) can dominate memory at long context and is often quantized separately. Sensitive layers usually stay at 16 bits.

    Two caveats. Memory saved is not speed gained: a 4-bit model on a GPU without native 4-bit support may unpack to 16-bit inside the kernel. Error is uneven: perplexity barely moves while a rarely used skill degrades first, such as arithmetic, Arabic dialects or long-context retrieval. Always evaluate on your own task.

    Pruning: removing what barely matters

    Pruning sets weights to zero. The classic rule is magnitude pruning: remove the weights closest to zero, like trimming the faintest strokes from a drawing. Han et al. (2015) showed many networks lose 80–90% of weights this way with short retraining. The "lottery ticket" work (Frankle and Carbin, 2019) asked why.

    Sparse is not the same as fast. Zeros scattered arbitrarily ("unstructured") leave the matrix the same shape, and normal GPU kernels multiply the zeros anyway. More structure buys more real speed and costs more accuracy:

    TypeWhat is removedReal speed-upAccuracy cost
    UnstructuredIndividual weights anywhereLittle, unless sparse kernels are used. Memory savings need a sparse storage format.Lowest
    Semi-structured (2:4)In every group of 4 consecutive weights, exactly 2 are zeroNVIDIA's sparse tensor cores (Ampere onward) can use this pattern. Up to about 2x on the matrix multiplications themselves; end-to-end gains are smaller.Moderate, usually recovered by short fine-tuning
    StructuredWhole neurons, attention heads or layersReal, on any hardware, because the matrices shrinkHighest, needs retraining or distillation to recover

    Retraining LLMs is expensive, so one-shot methods dominate. SparseGPT prunes a GPT-scale model in hours and holds quality near 50% sparsity (Frantar and Alistarh, 2023). Wanda scores each weight by its magnitude times the typical size of its input, with no weight update (Sun et al., 2024). A small weight on a huge input can matter more than a large weight on a tiny one. The simulation shows this.

    Distillation: teaching a small model to imitate a big one

    Quantization and pruning edit a model. Distillation builds a new, smaller one, like an apprentice learning from a master's worked answers, not just the answer key. A large teacher runs on many inputs. A small student learns to reproduce the teacher's outputs, not only the labels.

    Why beat training on labels? The teacher's full output carries more than the label. For a five-class transaction classifier, the label says "legitimate". The teacher says "legitimate 91%, card-testing 7.5%, account takeover 1%...", telling the student this transaction resembles card-testing more than a mule account. Hinton and colleagues (2015) called this dark knowledge: the relative sizes of the small probabilities.

    Those probabilities are tiny, so the teacher's logits are divided by a temperature T before the softmax. Higher T flattens the distribution and exposes the hidden structure.

    ClassTeacher logitProbability, T = 1Probability, T = 4
    Legitimate7.091.2%45.1%
    Card testing4.57.5%24.1%
    Account takeover2.51.0%14.6%
    Merchant fraud1.00.2%10.1%
    Mule account−1.00.03%6.1%

    The student's loss blends cross-entropy against the true label (F6.1) with the divergence (F1.7) between its softened outputs and the teacher's, the second term usually scaled by T² so the gradients stay comparable. For language models there are three forms:

    • Logit (soft-label). Match the teacher's whole next-token distribution. Richest signal, but needs the teacher's logits, which closed APIs do not give.
    • Sequence-level. The teacher writes answers or reasoning traces and the student fine-tunes on them as text. Works with any API and is how many small reasoning models were made in 2025. Check the teacher's licence terms.
    • Pruning plus distillation. Prune a big model to a smaller shape, then retrain by distilling from the original, using a fraction of the usual tokens (Muralidharan et al., 2024; Llama 3.2 1B and 3B were announced as made this way).

    Distillation costs a training run but gives a model smaller in every sense: parameters, compute per token, KV cache. It can also specialise. A 1B model distilled for one job, such as classifying complaints in Arabic and English, can match a general 70B model there and lose everywhere else.

    Latency budgets in real-time scoring

    A card payment is approved or declined while the customer stands at the till. The fraud score is one link in a chain with a fixed deadline: network call, feature fetch (F3.4), model, rules, response. A latency budget gives each stage a number of milliseconds that adds up to less than the deadline, plus a plan for when a stage is late. Ask your payments team for the real deadline rather than assuming one.

    • Percentiles, not averages. A train that is on time on average but a quarter-hour late one day in a hundred still ruins commutes. If the mean is 12 ms but p99 (the 99th percentile) is 60 ms, one request in a hundred misses the deadline: at 100 payments a second, one customer every second. Meet the budget at p99 or p99.9.
    • The slowest of several calls sets the pace. Ten parallel lookups, each slow 1% of the time: chance at least one is slow is 1 − 0.9910 ≈ 9.6%. Dean and Barroso (2013) called this "the tail at scale": fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness. Run the lookups one after another and the delays add instead.
    • A fallback when the clock runs out. Approve on a cheap rule, use a smaller model, or score on the features that arrived. Which one is a business and risk decision, and it must be logged so monitoring (P2.4) sees it.

    How teams buy back milliseconds, most reliable first:

    • Precompute features. Keep running totals ready in an online store (P1.3) so one lookup replaces twelve queries.
    • Co-locate and keep connections warm. Model next to feature store, connections reused. Cold starts and garbage-collection pauses are classic p99 spikes.
    • Use a simpler model on the hot path. A gradient-boosted tree (F4.4) scores in under a millisecond on CPU. A big neural net or LLM may not fit. Common pattern: a fast model for everyone, a slow strong one only for the uncertain middle band or after approval.
    • Compress. A quantized or distilled model runs faster and fits a cheaper machine.
    • Hedge and time out. Send a duplicate to a second replica if the first is slow, and cut off anything over its slice.

    LLM serving and batching engines

    A language model writes one token at a time, and each token needs a full pass that reads all the weights from memory. That is the memory-bound problem from Part 1. Reading the weights costs the same whether the GPU multiplies them by one user's data or sixty-four users'. So batch many users together and share the read, like a bus instead of sixty-four taxis.

    Per-user speed barely changes as the batch grows, while total throughput rises almost in proportion, until a limit is hit. Either compute becomes the bottleneck, or (often first) KV-cache memory runs out (M1.5): each user's conversation needs its own cache, which grows with context length.

    tstep≈max(weights+B·KV per usermemory bandwidth,2·parameters·Bcompute speed)

    B is the batch size. For an 8B model in FP16 the weights are 16 GB, so the memory term is about 4.8 ms at B = 1 and barely grows with B. The compute term grows with B but starts tiny. They cross near B of about 300 on an H100 at peak numbers; real engines reach half to two-thirds of peak, so read this as shape, not benchmark.

    Four ideas turned this into modern engines such as vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.

    • Continuous batching. Like a restaurant that seats new guests the moment a table frees, instead of waiting for the slowest table in the group. Static batching waits for the longest answer while short ones sit idle. Continuous batching lets a finished request leave and a waiting one join at every step (Orca; Yu et al., 2022).
    • PagedAttention. Like virtual memory in an operating system. One contiguous KV block per user wastes memory. vLLM stores it in small fixed pages, so memory is almost fully used and a shared system prompt can be shared between users. Kwon et al. (2023) reported 2–4x higher throughput at the same latency.
    • Chunked prefill and prefill/decode split. Reading a long prompt (prefill) is compute-heavy. Generating (decode) is memory-heavy. Mixing them stalls other users, so engines cut prompts into chunks or run the phases on different GPUs.
    • Speculative decoding. A junior drafts, the senior checks. A small draft model proposes several tokens and the big model verifies them in one pass, keeping those it agrees with (Leviathan et al., 2023). Output matches the big model alone. The speed-up depends on how often the draft is right.

    Cost per token falls sharply with batch size. A busy shared GPU is far cheaper per token than a private idle one, which is why a central model platform beats each team renting its own. A vendor's price per million tokens is really the price of a well-batched GPU.

    Two speeds, two jobs. Time to first token (driven by prefill) is how long until the answer starts. Tokens per second (driven by decode) is how fast it streams. Interactive assistants need both. Overnight summarising needs neither and wants maximum total throughput in huge batches. Know which you are sizing.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging a compressed model on a general benchmark

      "Perplexity is unchanged, so 4-bit is fine." Meanwhile Arabic extraction, numeric reasoning or long-document recall has dropped.

      Spot itAsk for before-and-after on your own task set, split by language and hardest cases, not one average.
    • Confusing memory saved with speed gained

      A 4-bit model fits a small GPU but runs no faster, because the hardware unpacks it to 16-bit.

      Spot itAsk for measured tokens per second on the target GPU and engine, not the compression ratio.
    • A sparse model that is no quicker

      "We pruned 70%." The matrices are the same size and the kernels dense, so latency is unchanged.

      Spot itAsk what pattern and what kernel. Measure latency, not zeros.
    • Quoting the average latency

      "Scoring takes 12 ms." The p99 is 70 ms and the deadline is 30.

      Spot itAsk for p95, p99 and p99.9 under realistic load, and the share of requests hitting the fallback.
    • Load testing at the wrong load

      Latency is fine at 10 requests a second and collapses on salary day, when queues build.

      Spot itAsk what request rate was tested, whether it was a burst, and how the system degrades above capacity.
    • An unmonitored fallback

      The slow path silently approves everything on a default rule for hours.

      Spot itAsk for the fallback rate on a dashboard with an alert. A rising fallback rate is a fraud-loss event.
    • Teacher mistakes inherited by the student

      A distilled model copies the teacher's errors and biases, just as confidently.

      Spot itEvaluate the student against human labels, not agreement with the teacher.
    • Benchmarks from a different setting

      "The engine does 10,000 tokens per second": short prompt, large batch, favourable draft model.

      Spot itAsk for prompt and output length, concurrency and time to first token on your hardware.
    • Sizing for the average user

      Plans assume 2,000-token contexts, then staff paste 60-page documents and the KV cache fills the GPU.

      Spot itSize from the context-length distribution and the memory limit, not the mean.
    • Skipping re-validation after compression

      The compressed model goes live under the old model's sign-off, yet its scores and calibration have shifted.

      Spot itAsk whether it passed the same validation and shadow run as any new model version (G3.2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. How much memory do the weights of a 70-billion-parameter model need at 16 bits, 8 bits, and 4 bits with groups of 128 (16-bit scale per group)?

    16-bit: 70e9 × 2 bytes = 140 GB. 8-bit: 70 GB. 4-bit: 4 + 16/128 = 4.125 bits per weight, so 70e9 × 4.125 ÷ 8 ≈ 36 GB. The KV cache and runtime overhead come on top.

    2. Why does one huge outlier weight ruin 4-bit quantization of a whole tensor, and what is the fix?

    The largest weight sets the scale, so the step between levels becomes very wide and ordinary small weights round to zero. Fix: a separate scale per small group (for example 128 weights), so an outlier damages only its own group. AWQ and SmoothQuant also rescale channels to tame outliers.

    3. A team prunes 60% of the weights with magnitude pruning. Latency does not improve. Why, and what could help?

    Unstructured zeros leave the matrices the same size, and ordinary GPU kernels still multiply them. Speed needs a hardware-supported pattern (2:4 sparsity at 50%), sparse kernels, or structured pruning of whole neurons or heads, usually followed by retraining or distillation.

    4. Why does distillation with a temperature give the student more than hard labels would?

    A hard label says only which class is right. The softened probabilities also show how the wrong classes relate, for example that this transaction looks more like card testing than a mule account. Temperature flattens the distribution so the small probabilities are big enough to learn from.

    5. A payment decision has 8 feature lookups in parallel, each slow 1% of the time. What fraction of requests includes at least one slow lookup? What if they run one after another?

    In parallel: 1 − 0.998 ≈ 7.7%, because the request is as slow as its slowest call. In sequence the same fraction meets a slow lookup, but delays also add, so the typical time rises too. Either way p99 is set by the lookup tail, not the median.

    6. Why does an 8B model on an H100 give roughly the same tokens per second per user at batch 1 and batch 32, yet 30 times the total?

    Decoding is memory-bound: each step reads all the weights once, whatever the number of users. More users add only a small KV-cache read and a little arithmetic, so step time barely grows while each step yields 32 tokens instead of one. It stops when the KV cache fills memory or arithmetic becomes the bottleneck.

    7. What problem does continuous batching solve over static batching?

    Static batching runs a group until the longest answer finishes, so finished slots sit idle. Continuous batching refills a slot the moment a request ends. PagedAttention solves a different problem: wasted KV-cache memory.

    8. A vendor says the 4-bit version of a model is "lossless". What do you ask?

    Which method and group size; which tasks and languages were measured, and how big the differences are with intervals; whether the speed-up was measured on our hardware; and whether the calibration data resembled our domain. Then run our own evaluation.
