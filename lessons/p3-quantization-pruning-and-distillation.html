    Making Models Smaller and Faster: Quantization, Pruning and Distillation

    Part 1

    What it is and why it matters

    In one sentenceThree ways to make a trained model smaller and cheaper to run: store its numbers with fewer bits (quantization), delete the numbers that barely matter (pruning), or train a small model to copy a big one (distillation).

    Our running example is a language model with 8 billion numbers (its weights). Stored as 16-bit numbers, that is 16 GB. To produce each word, the GPU must read all 16 GB from memory.

    The GPU calculates faster than it fetches. So fewer bytes means faster answers, more users per GPU, and a model that fits hardware you own.

    How the three differ

    • Quantization keeps every weight but stores each with fewer bits. Like rounding prices to the nearest 5 dirhams.
    • Pruning sets some weights to zero. Like trimming the faintest strokes from a drawing.
    • Distillation builds a new, smaller model that learns from the big one. Like an apprentice learning from a master's answers.

    All three are a trade: smaller, with some quality possibly lost. Measure that on your own task.

    Part 2

    Quantization: fewer bits per weight

    1. Find the biggest weight in a block of weights.
    2. Lay out evenly spaced levels up to that size. 8 bits gives 256 levels, 4 bits gives 16.
    3. Round each weight to its nearest level and store the small whole number plus the shared spacing (the scale).
    4. At run time, turn the whole numbers back into approximate weights.

    At 4 bits our 8B model shrinks from 16 GB to about 4 GB.

    How to read it

    • 8 bits is usually close to lossless and halves memory.
    • 4 bits is the common sweet spot for big models, with a small loss when done with a good method.
    • 3 bits or fewer is where quality drops fast, especially for models under about 10B weights.

    Outliers are the enemy. The scale is set by the biggest weight. One weight 15 times bigger than the rest stretches the levels so far that ordinary weights all round to zero. The fix is a separate scale for each small group of weights, say 128. Those extra scales mean "4-bit" really costs about 4.1 bits per weight.

    Smarter rounding. Know these names and ask which was used.

    • GPTQ rounds one column at a time and nudges the remaining weights to cancel each error.
    • AWQ protects the roughly 1% of channels that matter most, found by looking at the values flowing through the model.
    • FP4 is a 4-bit floating-point grid. Recent GPUs multiply it directly, so it saves time as well as memory.
    • Below 4 bits, QuIP# and AQLM reach usable quality near 2 bits for the largest models only.

    Two caveats. Memory saved is not always speed gained: a GPU without 4-bit support may unpack weights to 16 bits first. And errors are uneven: rare skills, such as long numbers or Arabic dialects, degrade first.

    The same idea for the KV cache

    The KV cache is the model's working memory of the text so far. Each user needs their own, and it grows with conversation length. It can be quantized to 8 or 4 bits too, or shrunk by design with fewer key-value heads (grouped-query attention). Dropping old tokens is lossy and can forget page 90. Two tricks cost no quality: FlashAttention (same result, less memory traffic) and PagedAttention in the vLLM engine (cache stored in small pages, so little is wasted).

    Part 3

    Pruning: deleting weights

    Pruning sets weights to zero. Magnitude pruning removes the weights closest to zero. Many networks lose 80 to 90% of their weights this way with a little retraining.

    But zeros are not speed. A matrix full of scattered zeros is the same shape, and ordinary GPU code multiplies the zeros anyway. Speed needs a pattern the hardware can skip.

    • Unstructured: individual weights. Lowest accuracy cost, little speed-up.
    • Semi-structured (2:4): NVIDIA GPUs from Ampere onward can exploit it. Moderate cost.
    • Structured: whole neurons, attention heads or layers. Real speed anywhere, but the highest accuracy cost, and it needs retraining.

    Retraining a big language model is expensive, so one-shot methods dominate. Wanda scores each weight by its size times how large its input usually is: a small weight on a busy input can matter more than a big one on a quiet input.

    Part 4

    Distillation: a small model learns from a big one

    Distillation builds a new model. A large teacher answers many inputs, and a small student learns to reproduce its answers.

    A plain label says "legitimate". The teacher also shows how it hesitates, so the student learns this payment looks a bit like card-testing.

    The small probabilities are called dark knowledge (Hinton). A temperature setting flattens the teacher's answers so those small numbers are big enough to learn from.

    For language models there are two forms:

    • Logit distillation: match the teacher's full probability for every next word. Richest signal, but needs the teacher's internals, so closed APIs do not offer it.
    • Sequence-level: the teacher writes answers or reasoning, and the student fine-tunes on that text. Works through any API. This is how the distilled DeepSeek-R1 models inherited step-by-step reasoning. Check the teacher's licence first.

    A small student distilled for one job, such as sorting complaints in Arabic and English, can match a giant at that job. It cannot beat its teacher and copies its mistakes.

    Part 5

    Which one to use

    MethodWhat changesPick it whenAvoid when
    8-bit weightsHalf the memoryA safe first stepEven 8-bit does not fit
    4-bit (GPTQ, AWQ)About a quarter of the memoryA 30B to 70B model on one or two GPUsSmall models on exact-number tasks, until tested
    PruningFewer weights2:4 on supporting GPUs, or you can afford retrainingOrdinary GPUs with scattered zeros
    DistillationA new small modelA narrow, steady, high-volume task, on CPU or edgeOpen-ended tasks needing broad knowledge

    They are strong at:

    • Fitting hardware you own. 4-bit takes a 70B model from 140 GB to about 36 GB, which fits one 80 GB card, with no retraining.
    • Faster answers and more users per GPU. Halving the bytes nearly doubles one user's speed, and frees room for more conversations.

    They are weak at:

    • Hidden damage in rare cases such as long numbers and Arabic. Averages will not show it.

    In practice: switch on the free, exact tricks first (FlashAttention, a paged engine such as vLLM). Then try 8-bit, then a published 4-bit AWQ or GPTQ model. Compare each with full precision on 200 of your own examples, split by language and length.

    Show the formula

    Weight memory = number of weights × bits per weight ÷ 8 bytes. 8 billion × 16 ÷ 8 = 16 GB. At 4 bits: 4 GB. Scales and the KV cache add a little more.

    In banking, this shows up in on-premises assistants and Arabic document extraction under data-residency rules, and in complaint triage by a small distilled classifier. Fraud and credit scorecards are already small tabular models, so none of this applies to them.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting "no loss"

      The vendor measured English multiple-choice tasks, and yours is Arabic contract extraction. Test on 200 of your own documents.

    • Judging by the average

      A 0.5-point drop on average can hide big losses on long numbers, negations and low-resource languages. Break results down by language and length.

    • Expecting speed from small files

      Scattered zeros and unpacked 4-bit weights save space, not time. Measure tokens per second on your target hardware.

    • Forgetting the model changed

      Model risk signed off the 16-bit model, and operations deploys a 4-bit one. Each compressed version needs its own validation evidence.

    1. How big is an 8B model at 16 bits, and at 4 bits?

    16 GB and about 4 GB (a little more for scales). Size is weights times bits divided by 8.

    2. Why does one huge outlier weight hurt quantization?

    It sets the scale, so the levels stretch to cover it and ordinary weights round to zero. A separate scale per small group limits the damage.

    3. Why does pruning 80% of the weights not make the model 5 times faster?

    Scattered zeros leave the matrix the same shape, and ordinary GPUs still multiply them. Speed needs a pattern such as 2:4, or removing whole rows and heads.

    4. What does a distilled student learn that plain labels would not teach it?

    How the teacher spreads its doubt: for instance, that this payment looks like card-testing more than like a mule account.

    5. Which tricks cost no quality?

    FlashAttention and PagedAttention. They are exact, so switch them on first.
