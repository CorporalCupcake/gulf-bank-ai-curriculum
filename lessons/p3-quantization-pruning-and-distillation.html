    Smaller, Faster, Cheaper: Quantization, Pruning, Distillation, Latency Budgets and LLM Serving

    How a model is squeezed to fit a smaller machine and what that costs in accuracy, how a fraud score survives a 30-millisecond budget, and why one GPU can serve hundreds of chatbot users at once.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRunning a model is mostly about moving numbers through memory, so you make it cheaper by storing fewer or smaller numbers (quantization, pruning, distillation), by never waiting on the slowest step of a request (latency budgets) and by keeping the GPU busy with many users at once (batching).

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A GPU is a very fast chef in a tiny kitchen. Cooking is quick. The slow part is walking to the cold store. Cook one plate per trip and the shift is mostly walking. Cook forty per trip and the walk is shared.

    An 8-billion-parameter model at 16 bits is 16 GB of weights, and producing one token means reading all of it from memory. The GPU waits for memory, not for arithmetic. This is memory-bound, and it explains nearly everything below. Three families of tricks follow.

    • Move fewer bytes. Store weights in fewer bits (quantization), remove weights that barely matter (pruning), or train a small model to imitate a big one (distillation).
    • Never wait on the slowest piece. A fraud decision has a fixed window. The latency budget divides it among the steps. The danger is the slow tail, not the average.
    • Share each trip to memory. Read the weights once and serve many users together (batching).
    Hold onto thisCompression is a trade, not a free lunch. Measure the loss on your own task and data instead of trusting "4-bit loses almost nothing". In real-time systems the number that matters is the slow 1%.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Quantization: fewer bits per weight

    Quantization stores each weight as a small integer plus a shared scale, instead of a 16-bit float. 8 bits gives 256 levels, 4 bits only 16. It is like rounding prices to the nearest 5 dirhams: smaller to store, slightly wrong.

    Outliers are the enemy. The scale is set by the largest weight in a block. If one weight is 15 times bigger than the rest, the levels stretch to cover it and the ordinary weights all round to zero. It is like measuring a room with a ruler marked only in metres because a giraffe walked in. The cure is group-wise quantization: a separate scale for each small block (say 128 weights), so an outlier ruins only its own block. The extra scales mean "4-bit" really costs about 4.125 bits per weight.

    Smarter methods keep the same storage but pick better integers. GPTQ adjusts remaining weights to cancel each rounding error. AWQ protects the roughly 1% of weights that matter most. SmoothQuant shifts difficulty from activations to weights so both fit in 8 bits. You only need to know these exist and ask which one was used. Very low bits (2 to 3) are in the edge lesson. Two distinctions come up in meetings.

    • Weights only, or weights and activations. Weight-only saves memory and speeds memory-bound decoding. Quantizing activations too speeds the arithmetic but loses accuracy more easily.
    • After training (PTQ), or during it (QAT). PTQ takes minutes to hours and is the default. QAT recovers more accuracy at 4 bits and below, at the cost of a training run.

    Two caveats. Memory saved is not speed gained: on a GPU without native 4-bit support the model may unpack to 16-bit inside the kernel. Error is uneven: overall scores barely move while a rarely used skill, such as arithmetic or Arabic dialects, degrades first. Always evaluate on your own task.

    Pruning: removing what barely matters

    Pruning sets weights to zero. The classic rule, magnitude pruning, removes the weights closest to zero, like trimming the faintest strokes from a drawing. Many networks lose 80 to 90% of weights this way with short retraining.

    But sparse is not the same as fast. Zeros scattered anywhere leave the matrix the same shape, and normal GPU kernels multiply the zeros anyway. Real speed needs structure:

    • Unstructured: individual weights. Lowest accuracy cost, little speed-up without special kernels.
    • Semi-structured (2:4): two of every four weights are zero. NVIDIA GPUs from Ampere onward can exploit it. Moderate cost, usually recovered by short fine-tuning.
    • Structured: whole neurons, heads or layers. Real speed on any hardware, highest accuracy cost, needs retraining.

    For LLMs, retraining is expensive, so one-shot methods dominate. Wanda scores each weight by its size times how large its input typically is: a small weight on a huge input can matter more than a large weight on a tiny one. The simulation shows this.

    Distillation: teaching a small model to imitate a big one

    Quantization and pruning edit a model. Distillation builds a new, smaller one, like an apprentice learning from a master's worked answers, not just the answer key. A large teacher runs on many inputs, and a small student learns to reproduce its outputs.

    Why beat plain labels? For a transaction, the label says "legitimate". The teacher says "legitimate 91%, card-testing 7.5%, account takeover 1%...", which tells the student this one resembles card-testing more than a mule account. Hinton called this dark knowledge: the relative sizes of the small probabilities. A temperature setting flattens the teacher's probabilities so those small ones become big enough to learn from (at T = 4, card testing rises from 7.5% to 24%).

    For language models there are two common forms.

    • Logit (soft-label). Match the teacher's whole next-token distribution. Richest signal, but closed APIs do not expose it.
    • Sequence-level. The teacher writes answers or reasoning traces and the student fine-tunes on them as text. Works with any API and is how many small reasoning models were made. Check the teacher's licence terms.

    Distillation costs a training run but gives a model smaller in every sense: parameters, compute, KV cache. It can also specialise: a 1B model distilled for one job, such as classifying complaints in Arabic and English, can match a general 70B model there and lose everywhere else.

    Latency budgets in real-time scoring

    A card payment is approved or declined while the customer stands at the till. The fraud score is one link in a chain with a fixed deadline: network call, feature fetch, model, rules, response. A latency budget gives each stage some milliseconds, adding up to less than the deadline, plus a plan for when a stage is late. Ask your payments team for the real deadline.

    • Percentiles, not averages. A train on time on average but a quarter-hour late one day in a hundred still ruins commutes. If the mean is 12 ms but p99 (the 99th percentile) is 60 ms, one request in a hundred misses the deadline: at 100 payments a second, one customer every second.
    • The slowest of several calls sets the pace. Ten parallel lookups, each slow 1% of the time, give about a 10% chance that at least one is slow. Fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness. Run them one after another and the delays add instead.
    • A fallback when the clock runs out. Approve on a cheap rule, use a smaller model, or score on the features that arrived. That is a business and risk decision, and it must be logged so monitoring (P2.4) sees it.

    How teams buy back milliseconds, most reliable first:

    • Precompute features in an online store (P1.3), so one lookup replaces twelve queries.
    • Use a simpler model on the hot path. A gradient-boosted tree scores in under a millisecond on CPU. Common pattern: a fast model for everyone, a slow strong one only for the uncertain middle band.
    • Compress, and time out. A quantized or distilled model runs faster. Cut off anything over its slice, and keep connections warm: cold starts are classic p99 spikes.

    LLM serving and batching engines

    A language model writes one token at a time, and each token needs a full pass reading all the weights. Reading them costs the same whether the GPU serves one user or sixty-four, so batch many users together and share the read, like a bus instead of sixty-four taxis.

    Per-user speed barely changes as the batch grows, while total throughput rises almost in proportion, until a limit is hit. Usually KV-cache memory runs out first (M1.5): each user's conversation needs its own cache, which grows with context length.

    Modern engines such as vLLM add three ideas worth knowing.

    • Continuous batching. Like a restaurant that seats new guests the moment a table frees, instead of waiting for the slowest table. A finished request leaves and a waiting one joins at every step.
    • PagedAttention. Like virtual memory in an operating system: the KV cache is stored in small pages, so little memory is wasted and more users fit.
    • Speculative decoding. A junior drafts, the senior checks. A small model proposes several tokens and the big one verifies them in one pass. Output matches the big model alone; the speed-up depends on how often the draft is right.

    Cost per token falls sharply with batch size. A busy shared GPU is far cheaper per token than a private idle one, which is why a central model platform beats each team renting its own.

    Two speeds, two jobs. Time to first token is how long until the answer starts. Tokens per second is how fast it streams. Interactive assistants need both. Overnight summarising wants maximum total throughput. Know which you are sizing.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging a compressed model on a general benchmark

      "Perplexity is unchanged, so 4-bit is fine." Meanwhile Arabic extraction, numeric reasoning or long-document recall has dropped.

      Spot itAsk for before-and-after on your own task set, split by language and hardest cases, not one average.
    • Confusing memory saved with speed gained

      A 4-bit model fits a small GPU but runs no faster, because the hardware unpacks it to 16-bit.

      Spot itAsk for measured tokens per second on the target GPU and engine, not the compression ratio.
    • A sparse model that is no quicker

      "We pruned 70%." The matrices are the same size and the kernels dense, so latency is unchanged.

      Spot itAsk what pattern and what kernel. Measure latency, not zeros.
    • Quoting the average latency

      "Scoring takes 12 ms." The p99 is 70 ms and the deadline is 30.

      Spot itAsk for p95, p99 and p99.9 under realistic load, and the share of requests hitting the fallback.
    • An unmonitored fallback

      The slow path silently approves everything on a default rule for hours.

      Spot itAsk for the fallback rate on a dashboard with an alert. A rising fallback rate is a fraud-loss event.
    • Sizing and load-testing for the average

      Plans assume 2,000-token contexts and calm traffic, then staff paste 60-page documents and salary day arrives. The KV cache fills and queues build.

      Spot itAsk for the context-length distribution, the request rate and burst tested, and how the system degrades above capacity.
    • Skipping re-validation after compression

      The compressed model goes live under the old model's sign-off, yet its scores and calibration have shifted. A distilled student also inherits the teacher's mistakes.

      Spot itAsk whether it passed the same validation and shadow run as any new model version (G3.2), judged against human labels.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Roughly how does the memory for a 70-billion-parameter model change from 16-bit to 8-bit to 4-bit with groups?

    It halves each time: about 140 GB, 70 GB, then a little over 35 GB (the group scales add a bit). The KV cache and runtime overhead come on top.

    2. Why does one huge outlier weight ruin 4-bit quantization of a whole tensor, and what is the fix?

    The largest weight sets the scale, so the steps become very wide and ordinary small weights round to zero. Fix: a separate scale per small group, so an outlier damages only its own group.

    3. A team prunes 60% of the weights with magnitude pruning. Latency does not improve. Why, and what could help?

    Unstructured zeros leave the matrices the same size, and ordinary GPU kernels still multiply them. Speed needs a hardware-supported pattern (2:4), sparse kernels, or structured pruning of whole neurons or heads, usually followed by retraining or distillation.

    4. Why does distillation with a temperature give the student more than hard labels would?

    A hard label says only which class is right. The softened probabilities also show how the wrong classes relate, for example that this transaction looks more like card testing than a mule account. Temperature makes those small probabilities big enough to learn from.

    5. A payment decision makes 8 feature lookups, each slow 1% of the time. Why is the request slow far more than 1% of the time, and does running them in sequence help?

    In parallel, the request is as slow as its slowest call, so any one slow lookup is enough: roughly 8% of requests. Running them in sequence does not help, because delays then add up and the typical time rises too. Either way p99 is set by the lookup tail, not the median.

    6. Why does an 8B model on an H100 give roughly the same tokens per second per user at batch 1 and batch 32, yet far more in total?

    Decoding is memory-bound: each step reads all the weights once, whatever the number of users. More users add only a small KV-cache read, so step time barely grows while each step yields 32 tokens instead of one. It stops when the KV cache fills memory or arithmetic becomes the bottleneck.

    7. What problem does continuous batching solve over static batching?

    Static batching runs a group until the longest answer finishes, so finished slots sit idle. Continuous batching refills a slot the moment a request ends. PagedAttention solves a different problem: wasted KV-cache memory.

    8. A vendor says the 4-bit version of a model is "lossless". What do you ask?

    Which method and group size; which tasks and languages were measured and how big the differences are; whether the speed-up was measured on our hardware; and whether the calibration data resembled our domain. Then run our own evaluation.
