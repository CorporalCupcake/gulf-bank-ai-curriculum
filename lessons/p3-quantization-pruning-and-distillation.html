    Smaller, Faster, Cheaper: Quantization, Pruning, Distillation, Latency Budgets and LLM Serving

    How a model is squeezed to fit a smaller machine and what that costs in accuracy, how a fraud score survives a 30-millisecond budget, and why one GPU can serve hundreds of chatbot users at once.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRunning a model is mostly about moving numbers through memory, not about arithmetic, so you make it cheaper by storing fewer or smaller numbers (quantization, pruning, distillation), by never waiting on the slowest part of a request (latency budgets) and by keeping the GPU busy with many users at once (batching).

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a chef in a tiny kitchen. The chef cooks very fast. The slow part is the walk to the cold store to fetch ingredients. If the chef cooks one plate at a time, most of the shift is spent walking. If the chef cooks forty plates from one trip, the walk is shared and the kitchen becomes efficient.

    A modern GPU is that chef. An 8-billion-parameter language model stored at 16 bits is 16 GB of weights. To produce one token, the GPU must read all 16 GB from its memory. At about 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth (the figure for NVIDIA's H100 SXM) that read alone takes about 5 ms. The arithmetic on those weights takes a fraction of that. So the GPU waits for memory, not for maths. This is called being memory-bound, and it explains nearly everything in this lesson.

    Three families of tricks follow from it.

    • Move fewer bytes. Store each weight in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 (quantization), remove weights that barely matter (pruning), or train a smaller model to imitate a big one (distillation). The model is cheaper to hold, to move and often to run.
    • Never wait on the slowest piece. A fraud decision has a fixed time window, and the model is only one of many steps. The latency budget is how you divide that window, and the dangerous part is the slow tail, not the average.
    • Share each trip to memory. Read the weights once and use them for many users' requests together (batching). That is how one GPU serves a whole call-centre's worth of assistants.
    Hold onto thisCompression is a trade, not a free lunch. Every method gives back some accuracy in exchange for memory, speed or cost. The professional move is to measure that loss on your own task, with your own data, instead of trusting a headline such as "4-bit loses almost nothing". And in real-time systems, the number that matters is the slow 1%, not the average.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Quantization: fewer bits per weight

    A weight is normally stored as a 16-bit floating-point number (FP16 or BF16, see F6.5). Quantization replaces it with a small integer plus a shared scale. With 8 bits you have 256 levels; with 4 bits, only 16. To rebuild an approximate weight, multiply the integer by the scale.

    The simplest scheme, symmetric absmax rounding, works like this. Take a block of weights. Find the largest magnitude, call it m. Divide the range −m to +m into evenly spaced levels. Round every weight to its nearest level.

    s=max|w|2b−1−1,w^=s·round(w/s)

    b is the number of bits and s the step between levels. At 4 bits there are 7 steps each side of zero. The rounding error on a weight is at most half a step, so the error is proportional to the largest weight in the block. That one fact explains the rest of this section.

    Outliers are the enemy. If one weight in a block is 15 times larger than the rest, the step s has to stretch to cover it, and the ordinary weights, which are small, all get rounded to zero or to a single level. Large language models are known for such outliers, particularly in a few activation channels, which is why naive 8-bit and 4-bit conversion once ruined models that looked fine in FP16 (Dettmers et al., 2022). The standard cure is to use many small blocks, each with its own scale, called group-wise quantization. A block of 128 weights costs one extra 16-bit scale, so a "4-bit" model really uses about 4.125 bits per weight. An outlier then ruins only its own block.

    That is the simplest method, round to nearest. Smarter methods keep the same storage but choose better integers.

    • GPTQ quantizes a layer one column at a time and adjusts the remaining unrounded weights to cancel the error just introduced, using second-order information from a small calibration set (Frantar et al., 2022).
    • AWQ notices that about 1% of weights matter far more because they multiply large activations. It scales those channels up before rounding so they keep precision (Lin et al., 2024).
    • SmoothQuant shifts the difficulty from activations to weights with a per-channel rescaling, so that both can be held in 8 bits (Xiao et al., 2023).

    Details at 2–3 bits (QuIP, AQLM, rotations) are in the edge lesson on very low-bit quantization. Four distinctions you will use in meetings:

    ChoiceOptionsWhy it matters
    What is quantizedWeights only (W4A16), weights and activations (W8A8, FP8, W4A4)Weight-only saves memory and speeds up memory-bound decoding. Quantizing activations as well speeds up the arithmetic too, which matters for long inputs and big batches. It is harder to do without losing accuracy.
    WhenPost-training (PTQ) or quantization-aware training (QAT)PTQ takes minutes to hours on a calibration set and is the default. QAT trains with rounding in the loop and recovers more accuracy at 4 bits and below, at the price of a training run.
    FormatINT8, INT4, FP8, FP4 variants (MXFP4, NVFP4)Floating-point formats spend bits on range, so they cope with outliers better. The speed-up needs hardware that supports the format natively.
    What stays high precisionKV cache, embeddings, last layer, normsThe KV cache (M1.5) can dominate memory at long context. It is often quantized separately, and the sensitive layers are commonly left at 16 bits.

    Two honest caveats. First, "memory saved" and "faster" are different things. A 4-bit model on a GPU without native 4-bit support may unpack to 16-bit inside the kernel, giving you memory savings with little speed-up. Second, quantization error does not show up evenly. Averages such as perplexity barely move while a rarely used skill, such as arithmetic, a low-resource language like Arabic dialects, or long-context retrieval, degrades first. So always evaluate on your own task.

    Pruning: removing what barely matters

    Pruning sets some weights to zero and, ideally, skips them. The oldest and most common rule is magnitude pruning: remove the weights closest to zero. Small weights contribute little to the output, so removing them costs little. Han and colleagues (2015) showed that many networks can lose 80–90% of their weights this way with a short retraining, and the "lottery ticket" line of research (Frankle and Carbin, 2019) asked why.

    The catch is the difference between sparse and fast. Setting weights to zero in an arbitrary pattern ("unstructured" sparsity) leaves the matrix the same shape. Normal GPU kernels multiply the zeros anyway, so nothing speeds up unless specialised sparse kernels exist. There are three levels of structure, each giving more real speed and costing more accuracy:

    TypeWhat is removedReal speed-upAccuracy cost
    UnstructuredIndividual weights anywhereLittle, unless sparse kernels are used. Memory savings need a sparse storage format.Lowest
    Semi-structured (2:4)In every group of 4 consecutive weights, exactly 2 are zeroNVIDIA's sparse tensor cores (Ampere onward) can use this pattern. Up to about 2x on the matrix multiplications themselves; end-to-end gains are smaller.Moderate, usually recovered by short fine-tuning
    StructuredWhole neurons, attention heads or layersReal, on any hardware, because the matrices shrinkHighest, needs retraining or distillation to recover

    For large language models, retraining is expensive, so one-shot methods dominate. SparseGPT prunes a GPT-scale model in hours and holds quality at around 50% sparsity (Frantar and Alistarh, 2023). Wanda scores each weight by its magnitude times the typical size of the input it multiplies, which needs no weight update at all (Sun et al., 2024). A small weight on a huge input can matter more than a large weight on a tiny one. The simulation shows exactly this.

    Distillation: teaching a small model to imitate a big one

    Quantization and pruning edit a model. Distillation builds a new, smaller one. A large teacher model is run on lots of inputs. A small student is trained to reproduce the teacher's outputs, not just the labelled answers.

    Why would that beat training the student on the original labels? Because the teacher's full output carries more information than the label. Take a transaction-category classifier with five classes. The label for one transaction says "legitimate" (a one-hot vector). The teacher's probabilities say "legitimate 91%, card-testing 7.5%, account takeover 1%...", which tells the student that this transaction resembles card-testing more than a mule account. Hinton and colleagues (2015) called this dark knowledge: the relative sizes of the small probabilities.

    The small probabilities are tiny, so the method softens the teacher's outputs with a temperature T. Divide the logits by T before the softmax. Higher T flattens the distribution and makes the hidden structure visible.

    ClassTeacher logitProbability, T = 1Probability, T = 4
    Legitimate7.091.2%45.1%
    Card testing4.57.5%24.1%
    Account takeover2.51.0%14.6%
    Merchant fraud1.00.2%10.1%
    Mule account−1.00.03%6.1%

    The student's loss is a blend: cross-entropy against the true label (F6.1) plus the divergence (F1.7) between its own softened outputs and the teacher's softened outputs, with the second term usually scaled by T² so the two gradients stay comparable. For language models, the same idea has several forms:

    • Logit (soft-label) distillation. Match the teacher's whole next-token distribution at every position. It gives a very rich signal, but you need the teacher's logits, which a closed API does not give you.
    • Sequence-level distillation. Have the teacher write answers (or reasoning traces) and fine-tune the student on them as ordinary text. This works with any API and is how many small reasoning models were produced in 2025. It is also where the licence terms of the teacher matter.
    • Pruning plus distillation. Prune a large model into a smaller shape, then retrain it by distilling from the original. This was used to derive small models from larger ones with a fraction of the usual training tokens (Muralidharan et al., 2024; Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B models were announced as made this way).

    Compared with quantization, distillation costs a training run but gives a model that is smaller in every sense: fewer parameters, less compute per token, a smaller KV cache. It can also be specialised. A 1B model distilled for one narrow job, such as classifying complaints in Arabic and English, can match a general 70B model on that job, and will lose on everything else.

    Latency budgets in real-time scoring

    A card payment is approved or declined while the customer waits at the till. The fraud score is one step inside a chain with a fixed deadline: the network call, fetching features (F3.4), the model, rules, and the response. Where the whole decision must land inside tens of milliseconds, the team writes a latency budget: a number of milliseconds for each stage that adds up to less than the deadline, with a plan for what happens when a stage is late. The exact deadline is set by your card scheme, processor and payments team, so ask for the real figure rather than assuming one.

    Three ideas make budgets work.

    • Percentiles, not averages. If the mean is 12 ms but the 99th percentile (p99) is 60 ms, then one request in a hundred blows the deadline. At 100 payments a second, that is one customer a second. The budget must be met at p99 or p99.9.
    • Waiting for the slowest of several calls. If a score needs ten feature lookups in parallel, the request is as slow as the slowest of the ten. If each lookup is slow 1% of the time, the chance at least one of ten is slow is 1 − 0.9910 ≈ 9.6%. Dean and Barroso (2013) called this "the tail at scale": fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness. If the lookups are made one after another instead, delays add up.
    • A fallback when the clock runs out. When a stage exceeds its slice, the system must still answer: approve on a cheap rule, use a smaller model, or score on the features that did arrive. Which fallback is used is a business and risk decision, and it must be logged so that monitoring (P2.4) sees it.

    How teams buy back milliseconds, in rough order of how often it works:

    • Precompute features. Keep running totals and counters ready in an online store (P1.3) so the request does one lookup, not twelve queries.
    • Co-locate and keep connections warm. Putting the model next to the feature store, and reusing connections, removes network time. A cold start or a garbage-collection pause is a classic source of p99 spikes.
    • Use a smaller or simpler model on the hot path. A gradient-boosted tree model (F4.4) scores in well under a millisecond on a CPU. A large neural net or an LLM may not fit. A common pattern is a fast model for everything and a slow, stronger one only for the uncertain middle band, or run asynchronously after approval.
    • Compress. This is where Part 2's first three topics earn their keep: a quantized or distilled model runs in less time and fits a cheaper machine.
    • Hedge and time out. Send a duplicate request to a second replica if the first is slow, and cut off anything that exceeds its slice.

    LLM serving and batching engines

    A language model produces text one token at a time. Each new token needs a full pass through the model, and each pass starts with reading all the weights from memory. That is the memory-bound problem from Part 1. The key observation: reading the weights once costs the same whether the GPU is multiplying them by one user's data or by sixty-four users' data. So batch many users together and share the read.

    This is the whole economics of serving. Per-user speed barely changes as the batch grows, but total throughput rises almost in proportion, until one of two limits is hit. One limit is compute (the arithmetic finally becomes the bottleneck). The other, often reached first, is memory for the KV cache (M1.5): each user's running conversation needs its own cache, and it grows with their context length.

    tstep≈max(weights+B·KV per usermemory bandwidth,2·parameters·Bcompute speed)

    B is the batch size. For an 8B model in FP16, the weights are 16 GB and the left-hand term is about 4.8 ms at B = 1 and barely grows with B. The compute term grows in proportion to B but starts tiny. They cross near B of roughly 300 on an H100, if you take the manufacturer's peak numbers. In practice engines reach perhaps half to two-thirds of those peaks, so use these as shape, not as a benchmark.

    Four ideas turned this principle into modern serving engines such as vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.

    • Continuous batching. Early servers grouped requests into a fixed batch and waited for the longest answer to finish before starting the next group, while shorter answers sat idle. Continuous (or "iteration-level") batching, introduced in Orca (Yu et al., 2022), lets a finished request leave and a waiting one join at every single step. The simulation below puts a number on the waste.
    • PagedAttention. Storing each user's KV cache as one contiguous block wastes memory through fragmentation and over-reservation. vLLM splits it into small fixed pages, like operating-system virtual memory, so memory is used almost fully and prefix pages (a shared system prompt) can be shared between users. Kwon et al. (2023) reported 2–4x higher throughput than earlier systems at the same latency.
    • Chunked prefill and prefill/decode split. Reading a long prompt (prefill) is compute-heavy, while generating (decode) is memory-heavy. Mixing them stalls other users. Engines now cut long prompts into chunks, or run the two phases on different GPUs.
    • Speculative decoding. A small draft model proposes several tokens, and the big model checks them all in one pass, accepting those it agrees with (Leviathan et al., 2023). The output is the same as the big model alone, but faster, because verifying several tokens costs about the same as generating one. The speed-up depends on how often the draft is right, so it is workload-dependent.

    Two things worth noticing in the numbers. First, the cost per million tokens falls sharply with batch size, which is why a shared, busy GPU is far cheaper per token than a private, idle one, and a central model platform beats each team renting its own. Second, the gap between "one user" and "sixty-four users" is the reason that when a vendor quotes a price per million tokens, they are really selling a well-batched GPU.

    There is a second axis you need for planning: time to first token (how long until the answer begins, driven by prefill) versus tokens per second (how fast it then streams, driven by decode). Interactive assistants care about both. Overnight document summarising cares about neither and wants the highest total throughput: run it in huge batches. Know which kind of workload you are sizing.

    How it connects

    Efficiency touches nearly every deployed model. These are the main joins.

    M1.5 · KV cacheThe memory that limits your batch

    The KV cache is why long contexts cost so much at serving time. Serving engines are largely programs for managing it well.

    M2.5 · Model choice, cost and latencyChoosing the model size

    Compression changes the options on that menu: a distilled or quantized open model may sit where only a large hosted one used to.

    P4.2 · GPUs and why they matterBandwidth versus compute

    The memory-bound idea comes straight from GPU architecture. Know the bandwidth and memory of the cards you are given.

    P4.3 · On-premises deploymentFitting models on the hardware you own

    Data-residency rules often mean running models in your own data centre on a fixed number of GPUs. Compression is how you fit a capable model inside that limit.

    P5.1 · Designing a real-time fraud systemThe budget in context

    The latency budget is the spine of that design. This lesson supplies the tail-latency reasoning.

    P2.3 · P2.4 · Deployment and monitoringShip it safely

    A compressed model is a different model. It needs shadow runs, canary releases and monitoring like any new version, including fallback rates and p99 latency.

    P1.3 · Feature storesWhere the milliseconds go

    Fetching features is usually the biggest slice of a real-time budget, and the main source of tail latency.

    F6.5 · Low-precision trainingNumber formats

    FP16, BF16 and FP8 are the same formats used to train. Quantization for inference is the other end of that story.

    F7.4 · CalibrationCompression can shift scores

    If a fraud model is quantized or distilled, its probabilities can shift slightly. Re-check calibration and the alert threshold before release.

    R1.1 · Mixture of expertsBig but cheap per token

    MoE models hold many parameters but read only a few per token, which shifts what is memory-bound and what is not.

    R12 · Very low-bit quantization, pruning and distillationThe frontier of the same ideas

    GPTQ, AWQ, FP4, KV-cache compression and on-premises distillation in more depth.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card and payment fraud. The tightest budget in the building. The model is usually a small tree ensemble or compact network, and the engineering effort goes into features, tails and fallbacks (B1.1).
    • Staff copilots and document assistants. Hosted or on-premises LLMs shared by hundreds of staff. Throughput per GPU decides the bill, and batching is the main lever.
    • On-premises LLMs under residency or confidentiality rules. A quantized open-weight model may be the only one that fits the GPUs you are allowed to use. Check licence, security review and evaluation, not just the size.
    • Arabic and bilingual workloads. Small multilingual models and aggressive compression can hurt Arabic more than English, since there is less Arabic in calibration data and training. Evaluate separately.
    • Overnight batch jobs. Large-scale KYC document extraction or complaint tagging wants the highest throughput, not low latency, so uses the biggest batches and cheapest quantization that pass the accuracy test.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basic methods are settled and built into the tools. The live questions are how low the bit count can go, which formats the hardware supports natively, how to serve many users and long contexts cheaply, and how to get small models that reason well.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging a compressed model on a general benchmark

      "Perplexity is unchanged, so 4-bit is fine." Meanwhile Arabic extraction, numeric reasoning or long-document recall has dropped.

      Spot itAsk for the before-and-after on your task set, split by language and by hardest cases, not only an average.
    • Confusing memory saved with speed gained

      A 4-bit model loads on a small GPU but runs no faster, because the hardware unpacks it to 16-bit.

      Spot itAsk for measured tokens per second on the target GPU and engine, not the compression ratio.
    • A sparse model that is no quicker

      "We pruned 70%." The matrices are the same size and the kernels dense, so latency is unchanged.

      Spot itAsk what pattern was used and what kernel runs it. Measure latency, not zeros.
    • Quoting the average latency

      "Our scoring takes 12 ms." The p99 is 70 ms and the deadline is 30.

      Spot itAsk for p95, p99 and p99.9 under realistic load, and for the share of requests that hit the fallback.
    • Load testing at the wrong load

      Latency is fine at 10 requests a second and collapses at salary day or a holiday peak, when queues build.

      Spot itAsk what request rate was tested, whether it was a burst, and how the system degrades above capacity.
    • An unmonitored fallback

      The slow path silently approves everything on a default rule for hours.

      Spot itAsk for the fallback rate on the dashboard and an alert on it. A rising fallback rate is a fraud-loss event.
    • Teacher mistakes inherited by the student

      A distilled model faithfully copies the teacher's errors and biases, and sounds equally confident.

      Spot itEvaluate the student against human labels, not only against agreement with the teacher.
    • Benchmarks from a different setting

      "The engine does 10,000 tokens per second." That was a short prompt, a large batch and a favourable draft model.

      Spot itAsk for prompt length, output length, batch or concurrency, and time to first token, on the hardware you will use.
    • Sizing for the average user

      Plans assume 2,000-token contexts, then staff paste 60-page documents and the KV cache fills the GPU.

      Spot itSize from the context-length distribution and the memory limit, not the mean.
    • Skipping re-validation after compression

      The compressed model goes live under the old model's sign-off, yet its scores and calibration have shifted.

      Spot itAsk whether the compressed model passed the same validation and shadow run as any new model version (G3.2).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Running these models costs money mainly because the graphics cards spend their time fetching the model from memory. There are three levers. We can shrink the model with little loss, serve many users at once on the same card, and design real-time decisions around the slowest one percent of requests rather than the average. Together these can cut the cost per answer several times over. I would want to prove the smaller version holds up on our own Arabic and English cases first, and that it has passed the same validation as any new model.”
    To a technical interviewer or platform lead
    “For serving I'd start with a weight-only 8-bit or 4-bit AWQ or GPTQ model on a continuous-batching engine such as vLLM, sized from the KV-cache budget at our real context lengths. I'd measure time to first token and tokens per second at target concurrency, not peak throughput. For the real-time fraud path I'd budget the stages at p99, parallelise and precompute features, keep a cheap model on the hot path with a logged fallback, and move any LLM step off the critical path. For a narrow text task I'd consider distilling a small model from a larger one, validated against human labels.”
    “Is a 4-bit model as good as the 16-bit one?”

    On average benchmarks, often within a point or two; on specific skills it can be noticeably worse, and the difference depends on the method, the model and the task. I would not accept a claim in either direction. I'd run our own evaluation set, including Arabic and the hardest cases, on both and compare with intervals.

    “Why not just buy more GPUs?”

    Sometimes that is right. But a GPU running one request at a time is mostly idle, so batching and quantization can multiply what the cards we already have can do. We should know our utilisation and cost per million tokens before buying, and under residency rules extra hardware also brings lead times.

    “Our fraud model takes 12 ms on average. Why is the team worried?”

    Because the deadline applies to every request, not the average. If the slow 1% takes 60 ms, one payment in a hundred misses the window and takes the fallback. With many parallel lookups the chance is higher, since the request waits for the slowest. I'd ask for p99 and p99.9 under peak load and the fallback rate.

    “Can a small model replace the big LLM for classifying complaints?”

    Often yes, for a fixed set of categories. A small model fine-tuned, or distilled from a larger one's labels, can match it on that narrow task at a fraction of the cost and latency. The check is a human-labelled test set in both languages, plus a plan for the examples it gets wrong. It will not generalise outside that task.

    “What does vLLM actually do for us?”

    It keeps the GPU busy. It batches requests continuously so users join and leave at every step, and it stores each user's cache in pages so memory is not wasted. For a shared internal assistant, that is typically the difference between a few concurrent users per card and many. It does not make the model better; it makes serving it cheaper.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. How much memory do the weights of a 70-billion-parameter model need at 16 bits, 8 bits, and 4 bits with groups of 128 (16-bit scale per group)?

    16-bit: 70e9 × 2 bytes = 140 GB. 8-bit: 70 GB. 4-bit with scales: 4 + 16/128 = 4.125 bits per weight, so 70e9 × 4.125 ÷ 8 ≈ 36 GB. Plus the KV cache and runtime overhead, which can be large.

    2. Why does one huge outlier weight ruin 4-bit quantization of a whole tensor, and what is the fix?

    The scale is set by the largest weight, so the step between levels becomes very wide. Ordinary small weights fall below half a step and round to zero. The fix is a separate scale for each small group (for example 128 weights), so an outlier damages only its own group. Methods such as AWQ and SmoothQuant also rescale channels to tame outliers.

    3. A team prunes 60% of the weights in a model with magnitude pruning. Latency does not improve. Why, and what could?

    Unstructured zeros do not shrink the matrices, and ordinary GPU kernels still multiply them. Real speed needs a pattern the hardware supports (2:4 sparsity on NVIDIA tensor cores, at 50%), sparse kernels, or structured pruning that removes whole neurons or heads, usually followed by retraining or distillation.

    4. Why does distillation with a temperature give the student more than hard labels would?

    A hard label says only which class is right. The teacher's softened probabilities also say how the wrong classes relate, for example that this transaction looks more like card testing than like a mule account. That is extra signal about the shape of the problem per example. Temperature flattens the distribution so the small probabilities are large enough to learn from.

    5. A payment decision has 8 feature lookups in parallel, each slow 1% of the time. What fraction of requests includes at least one slow lookup? What if they run one after another?

    In parallel: 1 − 0.998 ≈ 7.7% of requests wait for a slow lookup, since the request is as slow as its slowest call. In sequence the same fraction meets a slow lookup, but the delays also add, so the typical time rises as well. Either way the p99 is dominated by the tail of the lookups, not by the median.

    6. Why does an 8B model on an H100 produce roughly the same tokens per second per user at batch 1 and batch 32, yet 30 times the total?

    Decoding is memory-bound: each step reads all the weights once, whatever the number of users. Adding users adds a small KV-cache read and a little arithmetic, so the step time barely grows while every step now produces 32 tokens instead of one. The effect stops when the KV cache fills GPU memory or the arithmetic becomes the bottleneck.

    7. What problem does continuous batching solve over static batching?

    In static batching, a group of requests runs until the longest finishes, so finished slots sit idle. With answer lengths varying widely, a large share of the GPU's work is wasted. Continuous batching refills a slot the moment a request ends, at every step. PagedAttention handles a different problem: wasted KV-cache memory.

    8. A vendor says the 4-bit version of a model is "lossless". What do you ask?

    Which method and group size; which tasks and languages were measured and how big the differences are with intervals; whether the speed-up was measured on the hardware we will use; and whether calibration data resembled our domain. Then run our own evaluation before accepting it.
