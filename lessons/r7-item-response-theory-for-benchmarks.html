    Which Questions Tell You Something? Item Response Theory and Prediction-Powered Inference

    Two ideas for the same problem: evaluation is expensive and noisy. Item response theory says which test questions carry information. Prediction-powered inference says how to use a cheap automatic judge without letting its bias into your numbers.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceNot every test question is equally informative, so model each one's difficulty and discrimination and choose or weight accordingly; and not every label has to be human, because a small set of trusted labels can correct a big pile of cheap model judgements and still give you an honest confidence interval.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a 60-question exam used to rank candidates. Eight questions are so easy that everyone gets them right. Eight are so hard, or so badly worded, that everyone gets them wrong. Five have an answer key that is simply incorrect, so the strongest candidates tend to lose marks on them. The final score adds all 60 up. Those 21 questions contribute nothing useful, or worse than nothing, and the score is still reported to one decimal place.

    AI benchmarks are built the same way: thousands of items, one average. Most of the time nobody asks which items separate a strong model from a weak one. Psychometricians, the people who design human exams, asked that question a century ago. Their answer, item response theory (IRT), gives each question two numbers: how hard it is and how sharply it separates people of different ability. It treats the test taker's ability as something to be estimated, not read off as a raw percentage.

    The second problem is the cost of the answers. Suppose your bank's Arabic and English support assistant has produced 50,000 answers this month. Having a compliance expert grade each one is out of the question. Having another LLM grade them is cheap, but that judge has its own biases: it may be too generous, or favour long answers. What can you honestly say about the true share of acceptable answers? Prediction-powered inference (PPI) is a recipe: have experts grade a few hundred, compare their grades with the judge's on those same few hundred, and use the difference to correct the judge's verdict on the other 49,000. The result is an estimate that is not biased by the judge, with a confidence interval you can defend, and which is tighter than using the few hundred alone.

    Three things to take from it.

    • A benchmark score is a measurement, not a fact. Like any measurement it has an error bar (F2.1), and some of its parts are broken. Averaging everything equally is a choice, and often a poor one.
    • Information is concentrated. A question is informative about a model when the model has a real chance of getting it right or wrong. That is a statement about the pair, not the question alone.
    • Cheap labels are fine if you pay for a correction. A model's predictions are an excellent helper and a dangerous substitute. The small gold sample is what makes the helper trustworthy.
    Hold onto thisTwo questions to ask of any evaluation number. "Which of these items were actually doing the work?" and "Where did the labels come from, and how do we know the cheap ones are not skewed?" IRT answers the first; PPI answers the second.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The item curve: difficulty, discrimination and information

    Each model has a latent ability θ (theta), a single number on an arbitrary scale, usually centred at zero. Each item has a difficulty b, the ability at which the model has a 50% chance of getting it right, and a discrimination a, how steeply that chance rises with ability. The most common version, the two-parameter logistic (2PL) model, says:

    P(correct)=σ(a(θ−b))=11+e−a(θ−b)

    σ is the logistic sigmoid from F2.5. It is logistic regression with one feature, the model's ability, and an intercept and slope per item. Fix a = 1 for every item and you get the Rasch (1PL) model. Add a floor c for lucky guesses on multiple choice and you get the 3PL. The ability and the item parameters are estimated together by maximum likelihood (F1.2) or Bayesian fitting from a matrix of models by items, each cell a 0 or 1.

    The second quantity is how much a single item tells you about a model's ability. For the 2PL, the Fisher information at ability θ is

    I(θ)=a2·P(θ)·(1−P(θ))

    It peaks at θ = b, where P = 0.5, with value a²/4. Add up the information from all items a model answers and the standard error of its ability estimate is about 1 ÷ √(total information). Two lessons: an item is only informative near its difficulty, and doubling the discrimination quadruples the information.

    So "which questions tell you something" has a precise answer. For models near the ability you care about, the best items are those with probability near 50% and a steep curve. Items everyone passes, or everyone fails, have P(1−P) close to zero. An item with negative discrimination, where stronger models do worse, is almost always a wrong key, an ambiguous question or a leak. In one example of the approach, the Land and Bikel paper in Part 4 uses this to flag likely mislabelled benchmark items.

    Where does the ability scale come from? Nowhere external. It is whatever makes the model fit, and it is only defined up to a shift and a stretch. That is why IRT scores compare models on the same fitted item set and are not absolute. It is the same story as Elo ratings from chess or the Bradley–Terry model used by the Chatbot Arena: Bradley–Terry is essentially Rasch applied to head-to-head comparisons, where "item" is the opponent.

    Using it: audit items, pick a smaller test, score models

    Fitting a 2PL to a model-by-item response matrix gives three practical uses.

    • Audit. Flag items with near-zero or negative discrimination, items nobody passes, and items everybody passes. They are candidates for removal or re-checking, and a model that fails an item every stronger model fails is more likely seeing a bad key than a real weakness.
    • Shrink. If you only need to rank models, a well-chosen 100 items can stand in for several thousand. Select items that discriminate and cover the ability range, or run adaptive testing, where the next item shown to a model is the most informative at its current ability estimate, as computerised adaptive exams do for people.
    • Score better. An ability estimate weights hard, discriminating items more than easy ones. Two models with the same accuracy can have different abilities if one passed the harder items.

    The catch is that all of this relies on a panel of models whose answers you already have. The next simulation builds a 60-item benchmark with the traps above, calibrates item quality on one panel of 40 models, and tests the shortlist on a different 60 models. Calibration on a separate panel matters for the same reason F4 splits data: choosing items on the very responses you then score would flatter the result.

    Real versions of this are more refined: a full IRT fit gives each item its parameters, clustering picks items that represent the original distribution, and sometimes a correction maps the short-test score back onto the full-benchmark scale. The 2024 papers tinyBenchmarks and metabench (Part 4) report that a few hundred items can recover the original ranking and scores of popular benchmarks closely. The limits are just as important, and they come up in Part 5.

    Prediction-powered inference: a small gold set corrects a large cheap set

    Now the labels. You want a population quantity, such as the true share of acceptable assistant answers, the true fraud rate in a segment, or the true recall of a model on a population of cases. You have n items with a trusted label Y (an expert review, a confirmed investigation) and N items, many more, with only a model's prediction f (an LLM judge, a classifier). The two obvious estimators are both flawed.

    • Classical: average Y over the n labelled items. Valid, but wide, because n is small.
    • Plug-in: average f over all the N items. Narrow, but it inherits the judge's bias, and its narrow interval is actively misleading. It shrinks to a precise wrong answer.

    PPI starts from the plug-in estimate and subtracts the judge's measured bias, estimated on the gold set. Informally: "the judge says 69%; on the 300 items we double-checked, it ran 9 points high; so the answer is about 60%".

    θ^PPI=1N∑j=1Nf(X~j)judge on the many+1n∑i=1n(Yi−f(Xi))correction from the few

    Its variance is Var(f)/N + Var(Y − f)/n. With N large, the first term is negligible, so the width is set by how variable the judge's errors are, not how variable the labels are. If the judge is mostly right, the errors are mostly zero, their variance is small and the interval is narrower than the classical one. If the judge is poor, Var(Y − f) can exceed Var(Y) and PPI is worse than ignoring the judge. The PPI++ refinement (Angelopoulos, Duchi and Zrnic, 2023) multiplies the judge term by a tuning weight λ between 0 and 1 chosen from the data, which makes the result never meaningfully worse than the classical one. Both versions give valid intervals either way.

    Why does the interval stay valid? Because the correction term is an unbiased estimate of the judge's average error, computed on items sampled from the same population. The judge can be arbitrarily wrong; that only affects width, not validity. Two conditions carry the weight:

    • The labelled items must be a random sample of the population you want to describe, or an independently weighted one with known sampling probabilities. Labelling only the cases that looked hard, or only the ones that were escalated, breaks the guarantee.
    • The judge must not have seen those labels in building the prediction, or you must cross-fit so each label is predicted by a judge that never trained on it. Prompt-tuning your LLM judge on the gold set and then using the same gold set to correct it is the typical leak.

    Three details that matter in practice.

    • The saving is limited by judge quality. In the default setting above the PPI interval is about 22% narrower (±4.3 points against ±5.5), worth roughly 1.6 times as many gold labels. With a much better judge it is more. A paper on LLM judges at the frontier (Dorner et al., 2024) argues that, because a judge cannot be better than the labels it is checked against, the gain from using it is bounded: roughly, you can expect no more than about double the effective labels when the judge is the best available model. I am recalling that result from memory; treat the factor as an order of magnitude.
    • Spend labels where they teach most. Stratified and active variants sample more gold labels in the regions where the judge is uncertain, with known sampling weights so validity holds.
    • It works for more than averages. The original paper covers quantiles, regression coefficients and odds ratios. For model evaluation, the quantities are accuracy, a win rate between two models, recall at a threshold or an AUC.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    Both ideas belong to the same family: treat evaluation as statistics, not as a leaderboard.

    R7.1 – R7.2 · METR time horizonsAnother latent-scale measure

    The time-horizon metric fits a curve of success against task length, a close cousin of an item curve, with human completion time standing in for difficulty.

    A4 · Statistics for evalsError bars on every score

    The practical rules of intervals, paired comparisons and sample size for agent and LLM evaluation. This lesson supplies two techniques that sit inside that toolkit.

    M5.1 – M5.4 · LLM-as-judge, annotatorsWhere the cheap judge comes from

    M5 covers judge prompts and their biases. PPI is how you use such a judge in a number that has to be defended.

    M5.3 · Annotator agreementThe gold labels are noisy too

    PPI assumes the gold label is the truth. If experts disagree, the target itself is blurry, and no estimator removes that.

    F2.1 – F2.2 · Intervals and the bootstrapThe classical baseline

    The "gold only" interval in Simulation 3 is the standard proportion interval. PPI intervals can also be bootstrapped.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsIRT is logistic regression

    An item curve is a logistic regression of correctness on ability. Rasch and 2PL are mixed models with a person effect and an item effect.

    F1.2 · LikelihoodFisher information

    Item information is Fisher information: curvature of the log-likelihood. The same quantity bounds how precisely any estimator can pin down a parameter.

    F2.3 · F2.7 · PowerHow many labels do we need?

    PPI changes the answer: a good judge lowers the number of gold labels needed for a given width.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionAnother valid-by-design tool

    Conformal methods also use a small calibration set to give guarantees about a flexible model. PPI targets population quantities; conformal targets individual predictions.

    B1 · Delayed labelsCheap predictions, slow truth

    For fraud, the confirmed label arrives late and only where someone looked. A random confirmed sample plus model scores is exactly the PPI setup.

    F3.2 · Imbalanced dataFew positives, wide intervals

    For rare events the count of positives sets the interval, so a helper that tightens it is worth a lot. The unflagged random review sample is the gold set.

    G3 · ValidationWhat a validator will ask

    Validators want evidence that an evaluation number is unbiased and that its uncertainty is stated. PPI gives a documented route.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Choosing a test set for a customer-service assistant. You have 20,000 historical chats in Arabic and English. Which 300 do you ask experts to grade? IRT-style thinking says: not only the easy ones, and drop any that experts disagree on for reasons of ambiguity.
    • Comparing candidate models cheaply. A short, informative test lets you screen ten models in an afternoon before the expensive evaluation of two.
    • Estimating quality across a whole month's output. An LLM judge scores all answers; compliance reviews a random 400; PPI gives the share of acceptable answers with an interval.
    • Estimating a rate when truth is slow. Share of document-extraction fields that are wrong (trade finance, KYC), or precision of alerts, with model scores on everything and checks on a random sample.
    • Reporting to model risk. The documentation can state the sampling design, the judge, the correction and the interval, which is easier to defend than "an LLM scored it".
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    IRT is old and well established in education; its use on AI benchmarks is young and growing. PPI is from 2023 and is moving quickly into LLM evaluation tooling. The argument now is about robustness: do the item parameters transfer across languages and model families, and how far can a judge's help go?

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with two light searches. Items marked "from memory" I did not re-verify; 2026 items are mostly preprints seen via abstracts.

    1. 1960
      Rasch and the origin of IRT

      Georg Rasch's measurement model and, in the decades after, the two- and three-parameter models and computerised adaptive testing. Standard in educational testing for decades.

      Rasch, 1960; Lord & Novick, 1968 (from memory)
    2. 2019–21
      IRT arrives in machine learning

      Early papers used IRT to analyse classifiers at the instance level, to compare NLP test sets, and to show that evaluation examples are not equally informative.

      Martínez-Plumed et al., AIJ 2019; Vania et al., ACL 2021; Rodriguez et al., ACL 2021 (from memory)
    3. 2023
      Prediction-powered inference

      Angelopoulos and colleagues introduced the estimator with valid intervals using a small labelled set and a large set of predictions, for means, quantiles and regression coefficients. It appeared in Science. PPI++ followed with tuned weighting and computational improvements.

      Angelopoulos et al., Science, 2023; Angelopoulos, Duchi & Zrnic, arXiv 2311.01453
    4. 2024
      IRT to shrink LLM benchmarks

      tinyBenchmarks and metabench used IRT to select small item sets from large benchmarks that reproduce the original scores and rankings. Reported errors are low, a few percent, for common benchmarks (from memory; check the papers for the exact numbers).

      Polo et al., arXiv 2402.14992; Kipnis et al., arXiv 2407.12844
    5. 2024
      Judges used as data, and their limits

      AutoEval-style work applied PPI-like estimators to model evaluation with synthetic judgements, and a paper on the limits of LLM judges argued that the gain is bounded by judge-versus-gold agreement.

      Boyeau et al., arXiv 2403.07008; Dorner et al., arXiv 2410.13341 (from memory)
    6. 2026
      Auditing benchmarks and generalising IRT

      An EMNLP 2026 paper uses an IRT-based indicator to flag likely mislabelled items, reporting 95% precision on the top 200 items across seven benchmarks, using responses from 114 models. Other 2026 work fits item parameters for 41,871 items across 11 benchmarks, tests whether IRT-based item selection holds across languages, and applies adaptive IRT to safety benchmarks, where about ten adaptive items estimated scores with 18–34% lower error than ten random ones.

      Land & Bikel, EMNLP 2026 (arXiv 2605.30504); PSN-IRT, AAAI 2026; Dhar et al., Findings of EMNLP 2026; arXiv 2608.05086
    7. 2026
      PPI becomes tooling for LLM judges

      Preprints compare PPI with other bias-corrected estimators for noisy judge evaluations, report that naive intervals on judge scores are too narrow, and describe libraries that consolidate PPI variants for GenAI and agent evaluation. One preprint pools labels across tasks to shrink intervals when labels are scarce.

      arXiv 2601.05420; arXiv 2605.31278 (GLIDE); arXiv 2605.29249; arXiv 2609.35815 (all preprints)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Reading "ability" as an absolute skill level

      IRT scores exist only relative to the fitted item set and model panel. A model's θ in one study cannot be compared with its θ in another without linking.

      Spot itAsk which items and which models were in the fit. If either differs between two reported numbers, they are not comparable.
    • One dimension is not enough

      2PL assumes one ability explains everything. A benchmark mixing Arabic reading, arithmetic and legal knowledge has several, and one number blurs them.

      Spot itCheck whether the fit improves a lot with two dimensions, or whether item residuals cluster by topic or language.
    • Too few models to fit the items

      Item parameters from a handful of models are very noisy, and models from one family share quirks.

      Spot itAsk how many models, how diverse, and whether item parameters are stable under resampling the models.
    • Shortlists tuned to today's models

      A short test chosen to separate current models can fail to separate a new model class, for example a reasoning model whose failures differ.

      Spot itCheck the shortlist against new models on the full set periodically; watch for rank disagreement.
    • Contaminated items look "easy"

      An item the training data included is passed by every recent model. IRT calls it easy and uninformative, when the real problem is leakage.

      Spot itItems that older models fail but newer ones all pass, with no capability reason, deserve a contamination check.
    • A non-random gold sample

      Experts reviewed the escalated cases, or the ones the judge flagged. The PPI correction is then biased and the interval claims coverage it does not have.

      Spot itAsk how the gold items were chosen. Anything other than random (or known-probability) selection is a red flag.
    • Judge tuned on the gold set

      Prompts were refined until the judge agreed with the 300 reviewed cases, then the same 300 were used to correct it. The correction term shrinks artificially.

      Spot itAsk whether the correction items were held out from every judge design choice. If not, collect a fresh sample.
    • Treating gold as truth when experts disagree

      Inter-annotator agreement of 70% means the "true" label is itself noisy, so PPI's interval is valid for the average expert verdict, not for an objective fact.

      Spot itLook for agreement statistics (M5.3). Define the target as "what the policy panel would conclude" and say so.
    • Distribution shift between gold and unlabelled

      Gold labels were collected in March from English chats; the unlabelled set is April and half Arabic. The correction does not transfer.

      Spot itCompare the covariates and judge-score distribution of gold and unlabelled data. They should match, or be reweighted.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We can't have experts read every answer our assistant gives, so we use an automatic checker for all of them and have experts check a random few hundred. The few hundred tell us how far off the checker is, and we correct for it. The result is a quality rate for the whole month with a stated margin of error, instead of a number we'd have to take on trust. It also tells us how many more expert hours would buy a tighter figure.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd estimate the acceptance rate with prediction-powered inference: judge scores on the full set, a bias correction from a randomly drawn gold sample that the judge design never saw, and a confidence interval from the variance of the judge's errors. I'd compare against the gold-only interval to show what the judge buys, and use the PPI++ weighting so a weak judge can't make things worse. For the benchmark side, I'd audit items with an IRT fit on a diverse model panel and drop items with low or negative discrimination before anyone averages.”
    “Why not just use the LLM judge's score?”

    Because it has a systematic bias we can't see without human labels, and its interval, based on thousands of items, would look very precise while being precisely wrong. The simulation shows nearly every judge-only interval missing the true value. A small gold sample lets us measure and remove the bias.

    “How many expert labels do we need?”

    It depends on the width we want and on how well the judge agrees with experts. Without the judge, a proportion near 60% needs about 370 labels for ±5 points. A decent judge cuts that by something like a third to a half. I'd run the width formula for our judge's measured error rate on a pilot sample of 100 before committing.

    “Our benchmark has 5,000 items. Do we need them all?”

    Probably not for ranking candidate models, but we should find out. I'd fit item difficulty and discrimination on the models we've already run, drop broken items, and test a shortlist against the full set on held-out models. For the final sign-off on a chosen model I'd still run the full set, since the shortlist is tuned to the models we had.

    “Two models score 71% and 73%. Is the second better?”

    Not on that evidence alone. Run both on the same items, compute the paired difference with an interval, and check how many items actually separate them. Many items are answered the same way by both, so the information sits in a minority of items. An IRT view says the same: most of the benchmark is uninformative about this particular pair.

    “Can we use the judge to pick which examples to label?”

    Yes, but with care. Choosing the uncertain ones is efficient, but the selection probabilities must be known and used as weights, otherwise the estimate is biased. A simple version is to sample at random within bands of judge score. Pure "label the ones the judge dislikes" is the red flag from Part 5.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. An item has a = 2 and b = 0. What is the chance a model of ability θ = 1 gets it right, and what is the item's maximum information?

    P = σ(2 × 1) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−2) ≈ 0.88. Maximum information is a²/4 = 1.0, reached at θ = 0 where P = 0.5. At θ = 1, information is 4 × 0.88 × 0.12 ≈ 0.42.

    2. Why is an item that every model passes useless for ranking those models, even though it is "correct"?

    P(1−P) is close to zero, so it separates nobody: it adds the same constant to every score. It only inflates the headline accuracy.

    3. An item's discrimination is estimated as −0.8. What are the most likely explanations?

    A wrong or ambiguous answer key, a question where plausible reasoning leads to the wrong answer, or a very noisy estimate from few models. Check the item by hand and re-estimate with a larger, more varied model panel before removing it.

    4. 10,000 answers are judged by an LLM, 70% acceptable. On a random gold sample the judge ran 8 points high. What is the PPI point estimate?

    70% minus 8 points, so 62%. The correction is the average of (gold − judge) on the gold sample, which is −8 points.

    5. Why can PPI be wider than just using the gold labels?

    Its width depends on the variance of Y − f. When the judge is nearly random, that variance exceeds the variance of Y alone (in Simulation 3 at 50%/50%, 0.49 against 0.24). PPI++ shrinks the judge's weight toward zero in that case so you fall back to the gold-only interval.

    6. A team labels the 300 answers the judge scored lowest and uses them as the gold set. What is wrong?

    The gold set is not a random sample, so the average judge error on it is not the average error on the population. The correction is biased and the interval has no coverage guarantee. Sample at random, or with known probabilities and weights.

    7. You pick a 100-item shortlist from a 5,000-item benchmark using nine existing models. A new reasoning model arrives. What can go wrong?

    The shortlist was selected to separate the old models. The new model may fail different items, and rankings on the shortlist may differ from the full set. Run the new model on the full benchmark at least once and check rank agreement before relying on the shortlist.
