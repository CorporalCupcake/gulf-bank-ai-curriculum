    Which Questions Tell You Something? Item Response Theory and Prediction-Powered Inference

    Two ideas for expensive, noisy evaluation: which test questions carry information, and how to use a cheap judge without its bias.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceNot every test question is equally informative, and a small set of trusted labels can correct a big pile of cheap judgements.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a 60-question exam where eight questions are so easy everyone passes, eight are so hard everyone fails and five have a wrong answer key. Those 21 add nothing, yet the total is reported to one decimal place. AI benchmarks are built the same way: thousands of items, one average. Item response theory (IRT) rates each question's difficulty and discrimination, so you see which ones carry information.

    The second problem is cost. Experts cannot grade 50,000 answers a month. Prediction-powered inference (PPI) has experts grade a few hundred, measures how far a cheap LLM judge drifts from them, and subtracts that drift on the rest.

    Hold onto thisAsk two things of any evaluation number. Which items were doing the work? Where did the labels come from, and are the cheap ones skewed?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The item curve: difficulty, discrimination and information

    Each model has an ability. Each item has a difficulty, the ability at which it is passed half the time, and a discrimination, how sharply the pass chance rises with ability. Think of a high-jump bar: its height is difficulty, and discrimination is how cleanly it separates jumpers from non-jumpers.

    Read discrimination like this: near zero is a coin flip for everyone, 1 to 2 is healthy, and negative means stronger models do worse. That usually points to a wrong key, an ambiguous question or a leak. An item informs only near its own difficulty, so one everyone passes or fails tells you nothing, like asking a chess grandmaster how the knight moves.

    Ability has no absolute anchor, so scores compare models only on the same fitted items, as with Elo in chess.

    Using it: audit items, pick a smaller test, score models

    Fitting the model to a models-by-items grid gives three uses.

    • Audit. Flag items with near-zero or negative discrimination, and items everyone passes or nobody passes.
    • Shrink. A well-chosen 100 items can rank models about as well as thousands.
    • Score better. Two models with equal accuracy differ if one passed the harder items.

    The short test must be chosen on one panel of models and checked on different ones, or the result flatters itself.

    Real versions use a full IRT fit. tinyBenchmarks and metabench (2024) report that a few hundred items recover the rankings of popular benchmarks closely.

    Prediction-powered inference: a small gold set corrects a large cheap set

    You want a true rate, such as the share of acceptable answers. You have a few items with trusted labels and a huge pile scored only by a cheap judge. Averaging the gold labels alone is valid but wide. Averaging the judge is narrow but inherits its bias, so you get a precise wrong answer.

    PPI takes the judge's average and subtracts the bias measured on the gold set: "the judge says 69%; on the 300 we double-checked it ran 9 points high; so about 60%." A bad judge only costs width, and a near-random one can make PPI wider than gold alone (PPI++ down-weights it). Validity needs two things: the gold sample must be random, and the judge must not have been tuned on those same labels.

    The saving is limited by judge quality. In the default setting the PPI interval is about 22% narrower, worth roughly 1.6 times the gold labels. Expect modest gains, not miracles. The same idea works beyond averages, for accuracy, win rate, recall or AUC.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reading "ability" as absolute skill

      Ability exists only relative to the fitted items and model panel, and a handful of similar models gives noisy item estimates.

      Spot itAsk which items and how many, diverse models were in each fit. If they differ, numbers are not comparable.
    • One dimension is not enough

      A benchmark mixing Arabic reading, arithmetic and legal knowledge has several abilities, not one.

      Spot itItem residuals cluster by topic or language.
    • Shortlists tuned to today's models

      A test that separates current models may fail on a new class. Leaked items also look "easy", when the real problem is contamination.

      Spot itCheck the shortlist against the full set on new models, and investigate items only recent models all pass.
    • A bad gold sample

      Gold labels chosen from escalated cases, or used to tune the judge as well as correct it, make the correction biased.

      Spot itAsk how gold items were chosen and whether they were held out from judge design.
    • Treating gold as truth, or ignoring shift

      Experts disagree, so PPI covers the average expert verdict, not an objective fact. A gold set from March English chats will not correct an April, half-Arabic set.

      Spot itCheck agreement statistics (M5.3) and compare the two sets' covariates.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is an item every model passes useless for ranking, even though it is "correct"?

    It separates nobody. It adds the same amount to every score and only inflates headline accuracy.

    2. An item's discrimination is estimated at -0.8. What are the likely explanations?

    A wrong or ambiguous key, a question where plausible reasoning gives the wrong answer, or a noisy estimate from few models. Check by hand before removing it.

    3. Why can PPI be wider than gold labels alone?

    Its width depends on how variable the judge's errors are. A near-random judge makes errors more variable than the labels themselves. PPI++ down-weights the judge and falls back to gold-only.

    4. A team labels the 300 answers the judge scored lowest and uses them as the gold set. What is wrong?

    The sample is not random, so the judge's error there is not its error on the whole population. The correction is biased and the interval overclaims. Sample randomly, or with known weights.

    5. You pick a 100-item shortlist using nine existing models, then a new reasoning model arrives. What can go wrong?

    The shortlist was tuned to separate the old models, and the new one may fail different items. Run it on the full benchmark at least once and check rank agreement.
