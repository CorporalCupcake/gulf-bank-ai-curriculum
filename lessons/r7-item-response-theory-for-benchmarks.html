    Smarter Benchmarks: Item Response Theory

    Part 1

    What item response theory is

    In one sentenceItem response theory (IRT) gives every question a difficulty and a sharpness, so you can see which benchmark questions actually tell models apart.

    Picture a 60-question exam where eight questions are so easy that everyone passes, eight are so hard that everyone fails, and five have a wrong answer key. Those 21 add nothing, yet the total is quoted to one decimal place. AI benchmarks are built the same way: thousands of questions (called "items") boiled down to one average.

    Two numbers per item. Difficulty is the ability at which the item is passed half the time. Discrimination is how steeply the chance rises with ability. Think of a high-jump bar: its height is the difficulty, and discrimination is how cleanly it separates jumpers from non-jumpers.

    How it differs from a plain average score

    • An average counts every item equally. An easy item everyone passes adds as much as a sharp one.
    • IRT weighs items by how much they separate models. It also gives models an ability score, so two models with equal accuracy differ if one passed the harder items.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Collect a grid. Rows are models, columns are items, and each cell is pass or fail. You need enough varied models: a few dozen similar ones give wobbly results, while over a hundred spanning sizes and families give steady ones.
    2. Fit the curves. Standard software estimates every item's difficulty and discrimination and every model's ability together, choosing the numbers that make the observed grid most probable.
    3. Inspect the items. Flag the ones that are too easy, too hard or oddly shaped.
    4. Use the result. Fix or drop bad items, build a shorter test, and score models on ability rather than raw accuracy.

    How to read an item

    • Discrimination near zero is a coin flip for everyone: the item says nothing about ability.
    • Discrimination of 1 to 2 is healthy.
    • Negative discrimination means stronger models do worse. This usually points to a wrong answer key, an ambiguous question or a leak.
    • Difficulty far from your models means the item is useless to you. An item informs only near its own difficulty, like asking a chess grandmaster how the knight moves.

    Ability has no absolute anchor. Scores compare models only on the same fitted items, like Elo ratings in chess.

    Part 3

    What it is used for, and whether you can use it today

    • Audit. Flag items with near-zero or negative discrimination, and items everyone or nobody passes. Hand-checking the twenty worst items typically finds real key errors, and fixing them improves every score on the benchmark.
    • Shrink. A well-chosen 100 items can rank models about as well as thousands. tinyBenchmarks and metabench (2024) report that a few hundred items recover the rankings of popular benchmarks closely.
    • Score better. Ability reflects which items a model passed, not just how many.

    Keep items with high discrimination and spread their difficulties across the range where your models sit. Choose them on one panel of models and check them on different ones, or the result flatters itself.

    Strong at: finding broken or useless questions, cutting evaluation cost, and telling apart models with equal accuracy.

    Weak at: small or very similar model panels, and benchmarks with only a handful of items per topic. The scores are relative to the fitted panel, not absolute.

    Usable today? Yes for audits and shortlisting: it is standard statistics with ready software. The same logic powers Bradley-Terry ratings in head-to-head model arenas, where the opponent plays the role of the item.

    Show the formula

    Chance of a correct answer = 1 ÷ (1 + e−a × (ability − b)), where b is the item's difficulty and a its discrimination. When ability equals b the chance is 50%. A larger a makes the curve steeper.

    In banking, run an audit before trusting an internal model-evaluation set: a few broken questions can quietly distort every comparison.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting a single average

      Easy, impossible and mis-keyed items all feed the total. Look at which items carry information.

    • Fitting on too few models

      A few dozen similar models give unstable item estimates. Use a varied panel.

    • Choosing and testing the short test on the same models

      The ranking looks better than it is. Choose on one panel, check on another.

    • Treating ability as absolute

      It only compares models on the same fitted items.

    1. What do difficulty and discrimination mean?

    Difficulty is the ability at which an item is passed half the time. Discrimination is how sharply the pass chance rises with ability.

    2. An item has negative discrimination. What do you do?

    Hand-check it. Stronger models doing worse usually means a wrong key, an ambiguous question or a leak.

    3. Why is an item everyone passes useless?

    It does not separate models, so it carries no information about ability.

    4. Two models both score 70%. Can IRT tell them apart?

    Yes. The one that passed the harder, sharper items gets the higher ability.

    5. Why use a 2PL rather than Rasch for an audit?

    Rasch gives every item the same discrimination, so it cannot spot flat or reversed items.
