    Statistics for Evals, and Judges You Can Trust

    Why "version B scored 3 points higher" usually means nothing, how many test cases you need to say something, and how to use an LLM as a grader without letting its position, length and self-preference biases decide your results.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn eval score is a sample estimate with an error bar, comparisons need paired tests on the same cases, and an LLM judge is a noisy, biased instrument that you must measure against human labels and then correct for, not a source of ground truth.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A team tests a credit-policy assistant on 100 questions. Version 1 gets 82 right, version 2 gets 85. The slide says "+3 points". A manager asks to ship.

    Like polling 100 voters, a fresh set of 100 questions could give 79 and 84, or 86 and 83, with nothing changed in the models. The questions are a sample of everything customers might ask, so the score is an estimate with an error bar (F2.1, F2.2).

    Free-text answers can't be string-matched, so another LLM grades them. That judge is a measuring instrument with biases, like a scale that reads heavy: it may favour the first answer, the longer one, or text that sounds like itself. A biased judge on a small test set gives a number that looks precise and isn't.

    • Every score needs an interval. It says what the data can support. On a few hundred cases most "improvements" sit inside it.
    • Compare on the same cases. Easy questions cancel; only disagreements matter (Simulation 2).
    • A judge is an instrument to calibrate. Check it against humans, measure how it errs, correct for it (Simulations 3 and 4).
    Hold onto thisBefore believing a difference, ask: how many cases? Same cases for both? Really independent? How was grading done, and does it agree with humans? Most eval disputes die at one of these.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Error bars on an eval score (A4.1)

    The standard error (SE) is the typical wobble of a score between samples, like the spread of poll results. Score each case right or wrong; the true success rate is p. With n independent cases:

    SE=p(1−p)n

    At p = 0.85, n = 100: √(0.1275 ÷ 100) = 3.6 points, so a 95% interval is about ±7 points. Read it as: the true rate plausibly lies anywhere in that range, so differences smaller than it are noise. Error is largest near 50%. Halving the bar costs four times the cases.

    The textbook "±1.96 SE" interval fails where LLM evals live: small n and scores near 100%. It can exceed 100%, or have zero width at 50 out of 50. Use the Wilson interval (or a Bayesian Beta interval), which Simulation 1 draws. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova (2025) warn against normal intervals below a few hundred items.

    Two things widen the bar further.

    • Clustered questions. 10 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios are not 500 independent draws, just as 500 students from 50 families aren't 500 independent opinions. Effective sample size is about n ÷ (1 + (m − 1)ρ), with m the cluster size and ρ the correlation inside a cluster. Anthropic's guidance (Miller, 2024) recommends clustered standard errors.
    • Model randomness. At non-zero temperature the same question gets different answers. Averaging several samples cuts this noise but not the question-sampling noise, so repeating a run does not replace more questions. Report the two separately.

    For 1 to 5 ratings or rubric averages, the SE of the mean is s ÷ √n (s = sample standard deviation). The bootstrap (F2.2), resampling questions with replacement, works for any metric.

    Comparing two systems, and how many cases you need (A4.1)

    The natural mistake is an interval per model and a check for overlap. That is too strict and too blind. Both models answered the same questions, so look at each question's pair of results. It is like two runners timed on the same course: course difficulty cancels. Questions both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better. Only discordant ones matter (A right, B wrong, or the reverse). McNemar's test and a paired bootstrap use exactly this.

    The noise in a paired difference depends on how often the models disagree (a fraction q), not on how often each is right. Similar models rarely disagree, so pairing is much sharper. Cases needed to detect a true gap d at 80% power and 5% significance:

    npaired=(zα/2q+zβq−d2)2d2

    zα/2 = 1.96 (5% two-sided), zβ = 0.84 (80% power), d = true accuracy gap, q = share of questions where the models disagree. Example: d = 3 points, q = 10%: about 870 questions. Unpaired, you need about 2,600 per model. This is F2.4's power logic with discordant pairs as the sample. Halving the gap you want to detect roughly quadruples the cases.

    • Reuse the same test set and report the paired difference with an interval, not two scores.
    • Size the set before building it. If the rule is "ship at +2 points or more", compute n. If you can't afford it, say now that the test can't resolve 2 points.
    • Many comparisons, many false winners. Try 20 prompts on 200 cases and the best score is inflated by selection, like picking the best of 20 coin-flip streaks. Confirm on fresh cases or correct for multiple comparisons (F2.3).
    • Stop peeking. Adding cases until a result turns "significant" inflates false positives. Fix n in advance, or use a sequential method.

    LLM-as-judge: the biases (A4.2)

    Show a strong model the question, the answer(s) and a rubric, and ask for a verdict. It is cheap, scales, and agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other on open-ended chat (Zheng et al., 2023). Like a tired referee, it also has known habits:

    • Position bias. In pairwise comparisons the judge favours the first (sometimes second) answer regardless of content. Swapping the order can flip the winner (Wang et al., 2023).
    • Length bias. Longer answers score higher even when the extra adds nothing. Length-controlled AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024) removes it and changed rankings.
    • Self-preference. A judge rates its own family's text higher, more so the better it recognises its own output (Panickssery et al., 2024).
    • Style effects. Confident tone, formatting, lists and citations raise scores. Judges are usually less reliable in Arabic or mixed-language text; measure it, don't assume.
    • Weak at verification. On maths, code and facts it may approve fluent wrong answers. Where you can run a check (execute the SQL, look up the figure), do that instead.

    Model the first three as additive shifts on the judge's log-odds of choosing X over Y (the F1.1 and F2.5 scale):

    logit(P(judge picks X))=δ±b+l·extra length+s

    δ = true quality gap (what careful humans would pick), b = position bias (added if X is first, subtracted if second), l = length bias per unit of extra length, s = self-preference when X is from the judge's family. Zero bias and δ = 0 means 50:50; each +0.7 log-odds is roughly a 2:1 tilt. A model for intuition only.

    Calibrating the judge against humans (A4.2)

    Mitigations (swap order, explicit rubrics, reasons before the verdict, a different-family judge, equal-length comparison) lower bias but don't remove it, and without a human reference you can't tell how much is left. Treat the judge like any classifier (F7.1): hand-grade a sample, then measure.

    For a pass/fail judge, measure on a human-labelled set its sensitivity (share of truly good answers it passes) and specificity (share of truly bad answers it fails). It works like a smoke alarm: 80% specificity means 20% false alarms, here bad answers passed, and the more bad answers there are, the more the pass rate inflates. With judge pass rate o, the corrected true pass rate is

    p^=o+spec−1sens+spec−1

    The Rogan-Gladen correction from epidemiology, which strips a test's errors from measured prevalence. Example: o = 78%, sens = 92%, spec = 80%: (0.78 + 0.80 − 1) ÷ 0.72 = 80.6%. Sens + spec = 1 means the judge is a coin flip and the correction breaks; it needs to be well above 1.

    Sensitivity and specificity are themselves estimated from a small human set, so the corrected score has two uncertainties: the test set and the calibration set. A naive interval ignores the second and is overconfident. Lee et al. (ICML 2026) give an estimator and interval covering both, and show how to split the labelling budget between good and bad answers. Prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) similarly uses a small human set to de-bias a large machine-labelled one, with valid intervals.

    • Keep a gold set. A few hundred human-labelled answers, built from real failures (M5.4) and graded by two raters with agreement measured (M5.3), make every judge number defensible. Re-check whenever the judge model or prompt changes.
    • Judge to compare, not to certify. Relative results with order swapped are sturdier than absolute scores. An uncalibrated "94% safe" should not reach a risk committee.
    • Prefer checks that need no judge. Exact match on a figure, SQL execution, schema validation or a retrieval hit are cheaper and unbiased. Keep the judge for what only language can assess.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Common in vendor decks and internal reports, each with its quickest check.

    • A single number with no interval

      "Accuracy rose from 82% to 85%." On 100 cases that is within noise.

      Spot itAsk for the number of cases and the paired interval on the difference.
    • Overlapping-interval reasoning

      Concluding there is no difference because two separate intervals overlap, or a difference because they do not.

      Spot itAsk whether the comparison was paired on the same questions. If not, ask for it.
    • Pseudo-replication

      500 questions that are paraphrases of 50 scenarios, or many questions about one document, treated as 500 independent samples.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were generated. If there are groups, ask for clustered errors.
    • Picking the best of many variants

      Twenty prompts tested on the same set; the winner is announced with its test score.

      Spot itAsk how many variants were tried and whether the winning score came from fresh, unseen cases.
    • Judge used in one order

      Model A always in the first slot of a pairwise prompt.

      Spot itAsk for results with order swapped, and the share of pairs where the verdict flips.
    • Longer is better

      The new prompt makes answers verbose and the judge score goes up. Customers do not agree.

      Spot itPlot judge score against answer length. If they track, compare at matched length or add a conciseness criterion.
    • The judge grades its own family

      The system under test and the judge are the same model.

      Spot itCheck the model names. Re-grade with a different family and see if the ranking holds.
    • Uncalibrated absolute scores

      "The judge says 94% of answers are safe," with no human check.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity and specificity against human labels, and for how many were labelled.
    • Test set leaked into the prompt or training

      The score is excellent because the system has seen the questions.

      Spot itAsk when the set was written, who had access, and whether a fresh private set gives the same result (see A4.7).
    • Averaging away the subgroup

      Overall 90% hides 70% on Arabic dialect questions that make up 8% of cases.

      Spot itAsk for results by language, product and risk tier, each with its own interval and count.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening. The numeric ones need only Part 2's formulas.

    1. A model gets 90 of 100 questions right. Roughly what is the 95% interval, and what does that say about a competitor at 93%?

    SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 100) = 3 points, so about ±6: 83% to 95% (Wilson: about 83% to 94%). The competitor's 93% is inside it. Without a paired comparison on the same questions, you can't call them different.

    2. You want to halve the width of an interval. What happens to the number of cases?

    Width scales with 1 ÷ √n, so halving it needs four times the cases. 100 becomes 400, and 400 becomes 1,600.

    3. Why does pairing two models on the same questions need fewer cases?

    Questions both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better, and they are most of the data. The noise depends on the disagreement rate, far lower than each model's error rate. Only discordant questions count.

    4. You built 400 questions by writing 8 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios. What is your effective sample size, assuming a within-scenario correlation of 0.5?

    Design effect = 1 + (8 − 1) × 0.5 = 4.5. Effective n = 400 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 89. Your interval is as wide as for 89 independent questions, not 400.

    5. A judge shown A first prefers A 60% of the time and prefers it only 40% of the time when A is shown second. What is A's order-corrected win rate, and what does the gap show?

    The average of 60% and 40% is 50%: no real difference. The 20-point gap is position bias. One order alone would have crowned a winner either way.

    6. A judge passes 70% of answers. On the human-labelled set its sensitivity is 90% and specificity is 75%. What is the corrected pass rate?

    (0.70 + 0.75 − 1) ÷ (0.90 + 0.75 − 1) = 0.45 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 69%. The errors nearly cancel here. At specificity 60% it would be (0.70 + 0.60 − 1) ÷ 0.50 = 60%: the bias depends on how the two errors balance.

    7. You tested 15 prompt variants on the same 200 questions and the best scored 6 points above baseline. Why is that not a 6-point improvement?

    The maximum of 15 noisy scores is biased upward; part of the win is luck. Re-run the winner and baseline on fresh questions, or correct for multiple comparisons. Expect the gain to shrink.

    8. Why is "the judge agrees with itself 97% of the time" weak evidence?

    It measures repeatability, and a biased judge can be perfectly repeatable, like a scale that always reads 2 kg heavy. Validity needs an independent reference (human labels), which gives sensitivity, specificity and the corrections.
