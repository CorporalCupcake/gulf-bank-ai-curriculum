    Statistics for Evals, and Judges You Can Trust

    Why "version B scored 3 points higher" usually means nothing, how many test cases you need to say something, and how to use an LLM as a grader without letting its position, length and self-preference biases decide your results.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn eval score is a sample estimate with an error bar, comparisons need paired tests on the same cases, and an LLM judge is a noisy, biased instrument that you must measure against human labels and then correct for, not a source of ground truth.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A team tests a credit-policy assistant on 100 questions. Version 1 gets 82 right, version 2 gets 85. The slide says "+3 points". A manager asks to ship.

    Like polling 100 voters, a fresh set of 100 questions could give 79 and 84, or 86 and 83, with nothing changed in the models. The questions are a sample of everything customers might ask, so the score is an estimate with an error bar (F2.1, F2.2).

    Free-text answers can't be string-matched, so another LLM grades them. That judge is a biased instrument, like a scale that reads heavy: it may favour the first answer, the longer one, or text that sounds like itself. On a small test set the result looks precise and isn't.

    • Every score needs an interval. On a few hundred cases most "improvements" sit inside it.
    • Compare on the same cases. Only disagreements matter (Simulation 2).
    • A judge is an instrument to calibrate. Check it against humans and correct for it (Simulations 3 and 4).
    Hold onto thisBefore believing a difference, ask: how many cases? Same cases for both? Really independent? Does the grader agree with humans?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Error bars on an eval score (A4.1)

    A score is a sample estimate. Its wobble between samples, like the spread of poll results, shrinks only with the square root of the number of cases. At 85% on 100 cases the 95% interval is about ±7 points, and differences smaller than that are noise. The bar is widest near 50%, and halving it costs four times the cases.

    The textbook "±2 standard errors" interval fails for small samples and scores near 100%: it can exceed 100%, or have zero width at 50 out of 50. Use the Wilson interval, which Simulation 1 draws. For any other metric, the bootstrap (F2.2) works: resample questions and see how the score moves.

    Two things widen the bar further.

    • Clustered questions. 10 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios are not 500 independent draws, just as 500 students from 50 families aren't 500 independent opinions. The effective sample is closer to the number of scenarios (Miller, 2024, recommends clustered standard errors).
    • Model randomness. At non-zero temperature the same question gets different answers. Repeating a run does not replace more questions.

    Comparing two systems, and how many cases you need (A4.1)

    Don't compare two separate intervals for overlap. Both models answered the same questions, so look at each question's pair of results, like two runners timed on the same course: difficulty cancels. Questions both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better. Only the discordant ones matter. McNemar's test and a paired bootstrap use exactly this.

    Similar models rarely disagree, so pairing is much sharper. Detecting a 3-point gain when the models disagree on 10% of questions takes about 870 paired questions, against roughly 2,600 per model with separate sets. Halving the gain you want to detect roughly quadruples the cases.

    • Reuse the same test set and report the paired difference with an interval, not two scores.
    • Size the set before building it. If the rule is "ship at +2 points", work out the cases needed. If you can't afford them, say now that the test can't resolve 2 points.
    • Many comparisons, many false winners. The best of 20 prompts is inflated by luck, like the best of 20 coin-flip streaks. Confirm on fresh cases (F2.3), and don't keep adding cases until a result turns "significant".

    LLM-as-judge: the biases (A4.2)

    Show a strong model the question, the answer(s) and a rubric, and ask for a verdict. It is cheap, scales, and agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other on open-ended chat (Zheng et al., 2023). Like a tired referee, it also has habits:

    • Position bias. It favours the first (sometimes second) answer regardless of content. Swapping the order can flip the winner (Wang et al., 2023).
    • Length bias. Longer answers score higher even when the extra adds nothing (Dubois et al., 2024).
    • Self-preference. A judge rates its own family's text higher (Panickssery et al., 2024).
    • Style and weak verification. Confident tone raises scores and fluent wrong answers can pass on maths and facts. Judges are often less reliable in Arabic; measure it. Where you can run a check (execute the SQL), do that instead.

    Averaging both orders cancels position bias. Length and self-preference don't depend on order, so they stay.

    Calibrating the judge against humans (A4.2)

    Mitigations (swap order, explicit rubrics, a different-family judge) lower bias but don't remove it. Treat the judge like any classifier (F7.1): hand-grade a sample, then measure two things.

    • Sensitivity: how many truly good answers it passes.
    • Specificity: how many truly bad answers it fails.

    Think of a smoke alarm. At 80% specificity, one bad answer in five slips through as a pass, so the pass rate is inflated. Knowing both rates lets you correct it: a judge that passes 78% with 92% sensitivity and 80% specificity implies a true rate of about 81%. If the two rates add up to barely over 100%, the judge is close to a coin flip and no correction helps.

    The rates come from a small human set, so the corrected score has two uncertainties, the test set and the calibration set. A naive interval ignores the second and is overconfident (Lee et al., ICML 2026).

    • Keep a gold set. A few hundred human-labelled answers, built from real failures (M5.4) and graded by two raters (M5.3). Re-check whenever the judge model or prompt changes.
    • Judge to compare, not to certify. An uncalibrated "94% safe" should not reach a risk committee.
    • Prefer checks that need no judge. Exact match on a figure, SQL execution or schema validation are cheaper and unbiased.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Common in vendor decks and internal reports, each with its quickest check.

    • A single number with no interval

      "Accuracy rose from 82% to 85%." On 100 cases that is within noise.

      Spot itAsk for the number of cases and a paired interval on the difference.
    • Overlapping-interval reasoning

      Concluding "no difference" because two separate intervals overlap.

      Spot itAsk whether the comparison was paired on the same questions.
    • Pseudo-replication

      500 paraphrases of 50 scenarios treated as 500 independent samples.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were generated, and for clustered errors.
    • Picking the best of many variants

      Twenty prompts tested on one set; the winner is announced with its test score.

      Spot itAsk how many variants were tried and whether the score came from fresh cases.
    • An unchecked judge

      Model A always shown first, answers getting longer as scores rise, or the judge grading its own family.

      Spot itAsk for order-swapped results, scores against length, and a re-grade by a different family.
    • Uncalibrated absolute scores

      "The judge says 94% of answers are safe," with no human check.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity and specificity against human labels.
    • Averaging away the subgroup

      Overall 90% hides 70% on Arabic dialect questions that are 8% of cases.

      Spot itAsk for results by language, product and risk tier, each with an interval.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening.

    1. A model gets 90 of 100 questions right and a competitor gets 93 on a different test set. What can you say?

    Very little. The true score could plausibly be anywhere from about 83% to 95%, so 93% sits inside the error bar. You need a paired comparison on the same questions.

    2. You want to halve the width of an interval. What happens to the number of cases?

    You need four times as many. 100 cases become 400, and 400 become 1,600.

    3. Why does pairing two models on the same questions need fewer cases?

    Questions both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better, and they are most of the data. Only the disagreements carry information.

    4. You wrote 8 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios, giving 400 questions. Do you have 400 independent samples?

    No. Paraphrases of one scenario tend to be right or wrong together, so you effectively have far fewer. Ask for clustered errors.

    5. A judge prefers A 60% of the time when A is shown first, and 40% when A is shown second. What does that tell you?

    Averaged over both orders, A wins 50%: no real difference. The 20-point gap is position bias.

    6. A judge passes 70% of answers. It passes 90% of truly good answers but also 25% of truly bad ones. Can you take 70% at face value?

    Not without checking. Its errors go both ways and here nearly cancel, so the true rate is about 69%. If it passed 40% of bad answers, the true rate would be nearer 60%. Measure both error rates against human labels.

    7. You tested 15 prompt variants on the same 200 questions and the best scored 6 points above baseline. Why is that not a 6-point improvement?

    The best of 15 noisy scores is biased upward; part of the win is luck. Re-run it on fresh questions and expect the gain to shrink.

    8. Why is "the judge agrees with itself 97% of the time" weak evidence?

    It measures repeatability, and a biased judge can be perfectly repeatable, like a scale that always reads 2 kg heavy. You need human labels as a reference.
