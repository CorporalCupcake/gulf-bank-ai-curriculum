    Statistics for Evals, and Judges You Can Trust

    Why "version B scored 3 points higher" usually means nothing, how many test cases you need to say something, and how to use an LLM as a grader without letting its position, length and self-preference biases decide your results.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn eval score is a sample estimate with an error bar, comparisons need paired tests on the same cases, and an LLM judge is a noisy, biased instrument that you must measure against human labels and then correct for, not a source of ground truth.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A team builds an Arabic and English assistant that answers staff questions about credit policy. They write 100 test questions. Version 1 gets 82 right. After a prompt change, version 2 gets 85. The slide says "+3 points". A manager asks to ship it.

    Run the same experiment again with a fresh set of 100 questions and the scores could easily come out 79 and 84, or 86 and 83. Nothing about the models changed. The test questions were a sample of everything customers might ask, and a different sample gives a different score. An eval score is an estimate of how the system would do on the whole population of questions, and every estimate carries an error bar. F2.1 and F2.2 taught this for any statistic; this lesson applies it to the specific habits of LLM testing.

    A second problem sits on top. Free-text answers cannot be graded by string matching, so the team asks another LLM to grade them. That judge is also a measuring instrument, and instruments have biases: it may prefer the answer shown first, the longer answer, or answers that sound like itself. A biased, noisy judge added to a small test set gives a number that looks precise and is neither.

    Three ideas to carry through the rest of the lesson.

    • Every score needs an interval. Not a decoration: the interval says which conclusions the data can support. On a few hundred cases, most "improvements" are within it.
    • Compare on the same cases. Both models answer every question, so the easy questions cancel out and only disagreements matter. This is a large saving in cases, shown in Simulation 2.
    • A judge is an instrument to be calibrated. Check it against humans on a sample, measure how it errs, and correct for that error, as you would with any sensor (Simulations 3 and 4).
    Hold onto thisBefore believing a difference between two LLM systems, ask four things. How many cases? Were both run on the same cases? Are the cases really independent? How was the grading done and how well does it agree with humans? Most eval disputes die at one of those questions.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Error bars on an eval score (A4.1)

    Suppose each test case is scored right or wrong, and the system's true success rate over all possible cases is p. With n independent cases the measured rate has a standard error

    SE=p(1−p)n

    At p = 0.85 and n = 100: √(0.1275 ÷ 100) = 3.6 points, so a 95% interval is about ±7 points. The error is largest near 50% and smaller near 0% or 100%, but the square root of n means that a bar half as wide costs four times the cases.

    The textbook "± 1.96 SE" interval misbehaves exactly where LLM evals often live: small n and scores near 100%. It can run past 100%, or collapse to zero width when you score 50 out of 50. Use the Wilson interval (or a Bayesian Beta interval), which is what Simulation 1 draws. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova (2025) argue that normal-approximation intervals should not be trusted for fewer than a few hundred items for just this reason.

    Two things widen the bar beyond that formula, and both are common in LLM work.

    • Clustered questions. If your 500 questions are 10 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios, or 20 questions about the same document, they are not 500 independent draws. The effective sample size is about n ÷ (1 + (m − 1)ρ), where m is the cluster size and ρ the correlation inside a cluster. Anthropic's evals guidance (Miller, 2024) recommends clustered standard errors for exactly this case.
    • Model randomness. At non-zero temperature the same model answers the same question differently each time. Sampling several answers per question and averaging reduces this noise, but the question-sampling noise above remains, so repeating a run is no substitute for more questions. Report the two separately if you can.

    For scores that are not right/wrong, such as a 1 to 5 rating or a rubric average, use the same logic with the sample standard deviation: the standard error of the mean is s ÷ √n. The bootstrap (F2.2), resampling questions with replacement, works for any metric, including ones with no clean formula.

    Comparing two systems, and how many cases you need (A4.1)

    The natural mistake is to compute an interval for each model and check whether they overlap. That is both too strict and too blind. Better, because both models answered the same questions, to look at each question's pair of results. Questions both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better. Only the discordant ones matter: A right and B wrong, or the reverse. McNemar's test and a paired bootstrap on the difference use exactly this.

    The consequence is large. The standard error of the difference between paired scores depends on how often the two models disagree (a fraction q of cases), not on how often each is right. Two similar models disagree rarely, so a paired comparison is much sharper than comparing two independent scores. The approximate number of cases needed to detect a true difference d with 80% power at a 5% significance level is

    npaired=(zα/2q+zβq−d2)2d2

    zα/2 = 1.96 for a 5% two-sided test and zβ = 0.84 for 80% power; d is the true accuracy gap and q the share of questions on which the two models disagree. Example: d = 3 points, q = 10%: about 870 questions. Unpaired, each model on its own different questions, you would need about 2,600 per model. The power logic is F2.4's, with discordant pairs as the sample.

    Practical rules that follow:

    • Reuse the same test set and report paired differences with an interval, not two separate scores.
    • Plan the size before you build the set. If the decision is "ship if at least +2 points", work out n from the formula. If you cannot afford it, say now that the test cannot resolve a 2-point change.
    • Many comparisons, many false winners. Try 20 prompts on the same 200 cases and pick the best, and the winner's score is inflated by selection. Hold out fresh cases for the final check, or correct for multiple comparisons (F2.3).
    • Stop peeking. Adding cases until a difference turns "significant" inflates false positives. Fix n in advance, or use a method designed for sequential looks.

    LLM-as-judge: the biases (A4.2)

    The basic recipe is simple: show a strong model the question, the answer (or two answers) and a rubric, and ask for a verdict. It scales, it is cheap, and with a strong judge it agrees with human raters about as often as raters agree with each other on open-ended chat tasks (Zheng et al., 2023). The same study also named the failures, and later work confirmed them:

    • Position bias. In pairwise comparisons the judge favours the first (or sometimes the second) answer regardless of content. Wang et al. (2023) showed that merely swapping the order could flip which model "won".
    • Length (verbosity) bias. Longer answers get higher scores even when the extra length adds nothing. Dubois et al. (2024) built a length-controlled AlpacaEval to remove it, which changed leaderboard rankings.
    • Self-preference. A judge tends to rate text from its own model family higher, and Panickssery et al. (2024) found that this grows with the judge's ability to recognise its own output.
    • Style and authority effects. Confident tone, formatting, lists and citations raise scores. In Arabic or mixed-language settings, judges are also usually less reliable than in English; treat that as something to measure, not assume.
    • Weak at hard verification. On maths, code and factual claims a judge may approve fluent wrong answers. Where a check can be run (execute the SQL, look up the figure), run it instead.

    You can model the first three as additive shifts on the judge's log-odds of choosing answer X over answer Y (F1.1 and F2.5 use the same scale):

    logit(P(judge picks X))=δ±b+l·extra length+s

    δ is the true quality gap (what a careful human panel would pick), b the position bias, added when X is shown first and subtracted when second, l the length bias per unit of extra length, and s self-preference when X came from the judge's own family. This is a model for intuition: real biases depend on the pair and the prompt.

    Calibrating the judge against humans (A4.2)

    Mitigations (swap order, use rubrics with explicit criteria, ask for reasons before the verdict, use a judge from a different family, compare at equal length) lower the bias. They do not remove it, and you cannot know how much is left without a human reference. So treat the judge like any classifier (F7.1): grade a sample by hand, then measure.

    Take a pass/fail judge. On a human-labelled calibration set, measure its sensitivity (share of truly good answers it passes) and specificity (share of truly bad answers it fails). A judge with 92% sensitivity and 80% specificity passes 20% of bad answers, and the more bad answers there are, the more that inflates the pass rate. If the judge's overall pass rate is o, the corrected estimate of the true pass rate is

    p^=o+spec−1sens+spec−1

    This is the Rogan-Gladen correction from epidemiology, where a test's errors are removed from a measured prevalence. Example: o = 78%, sens = 92%, spec = 80%: (0.78 + 0.80 − 1) ÷ 0.72 = 80.6%. The correction only works if sens + spec is well above 1, that is, the judge is informative at all.

    The catch is that sensitivity and specificity are themselves estimated from a small human-labelled set, so the corrected estimate has two sources of uncertainty: the test set and the calibration set. A naive interval ignores the second and is too confident. Lee et al. (ICML 2026) give an estimator and interval that account for both, and show how to split the human-labelling budget between good and bad answers. A related family, prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023), uses a small human-labelled set to remove the bias of a large machine-labelled set and gives valid intervals.

    Three working habits come out of this section.

    • Keep a gold set. A few hundred human-labelled answers, built from real failures (M5.4) and graded by two raters with agreement measured (M5.3), is the asset that makes every judge number defensible. Re-check the judge on it whenever the judge model or prompt changes.
    • Use judges to compare, not to certify. Pairwise or relative results, with order swapped, are more robust than absolute scores. An absolute "94% safe" from an uncalibrated judge should not reach a risk committee.
    • Prefer checks that need no judge. Exact-match on a figure, a SQL execution result, a schema validation or a retrieval hit are cheaper and unbiased. Reserve the judge for what only language can assess.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Model-risk validation. A validator will ask how many test cases, how they were chosen, what the interval is, and who graded. Having this ready speeds approval of any LLM use case.
    • Release gates. "Do not ship if the unsafe-answer rate could be above 1%" is a statement about an upper confidence bound, and demonstrating a rate under 1% takes hundreds of clean cases. The rule of three says that 300 clean cases give an upper 95% bound of about 1% (3 ÷ 300).
    • Prompt and model changes. Every change to a customer-facing assistant is a comparison. Paired testing on a stable set, with a pre-set threshold, makes this routine instead of an argument.
    • Arabic and English performance. Report each language separately with its own interval. A good overall score can hide a weak Arabic (or dialect) subgroup that has too few cases to show up.
    • Compliance Q&A and KYC summaries. Judges grading regulatory answers should be checked against compliance staff labels, where the cost of a pass on a wrong answer is highest.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Statistical practice for LLM evals has moved from "report one number" towards intervals, paired designs and calibrated judges, though many leaderboards and internal reports still lag. The live questions are how to correct judge error properly, how to cut human labelling cost, and what to do with small, expensive agent evals.

    1. 2023
      Judges work, with named biases

      MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena showed strong-LLM judges agree with humans about as well as humans agree with each other on chat tasks, and documented position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias. Other work showed that reordering answers can flip rankings.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in vendor decks and in internal reports. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • A single number with no interval

      "Accuracy rose from 82% to 85%." On 100 cases that is within noise.

      Spot itAsk for the number of cases and the paired interval on the difference.
    • Overlapping-interval reasoning

      Concluding there is no difference because two separate intervals overlap, or a difference because they do not.

      Spot itAsk whether the comparison was paired on the same questions. If not, ask for it.
    • Pseudo-replication

      500 questions that are paraphrases of 50 scenarios, or many questions about one document, treated as 500 independent samples.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were generated. If there are groups, ask for clustered errors.
    • Picking the best of many variants

      Twenty prompts tested on the same set; the winner is announced with its test score.

      Spot itAsk how many variants were tried and whether the winning score came from fresh, unseen cases.
    • Judge used in one order

      Model A always in the first slot of a pairwise prompt.

      Spot itAsk for results with order swapped, and the share of pairs where the verdict flips.
    • Longer is better

      The new prompt makes answers verbose and the judge score goes up. Customers do not agree.

      Spot itPlot judge score against answer length. If they track, compare at matched length or add a conciseness criterion.
    • The judge grades its own family

      The system under test and the judge are the same model.

      Spot itCheck the model names. Re-grade with a different family and see if the ranking holds.
    • Uncalibrated absolute scores

      "The judge says 94% of answers are safe," with no human check.

      Spot itAsk for sensitivity and specificity against human labels, and for how many were labelled.
    • Test set leaked into the prompt or training

      The score is excellent because the system has seen the questions.

      Spot itAsk when the set was written, who had access, and whether a fresh private set gives the same result (see A4.7).
    • Averaging away the subgroup

      Overall 90% hides 70% on Arabic dialect questions that make up 8% of cases.

      Spot itAsk for results by language, product and risk tier, each with its own interval and count.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The new version scored three points higher on a hundred questions. With a test that small, the margin of error is about seven points either way, so we can't yet say it is better. If we use the same questions for both versions and test about nine hundred, we can detect a three-point gain with confidence. That's a few days' work and it stops us shipping on noise.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I report Wilson or bootstrap intervals, use clustered errors where questions share a scenario, and compare versions with paired tests on a fixed set sized in advance from the expected gain and disagreement rate. For LLM judges I swap order, control for length, avoid same-family judging, and calibrate against a human-labelled set to get sensitivity and specificity. I correct the pass rate and widen the interval for calibration uncertainty, and keep a private gold set to re-check the judge whenever it changes.”
    “How many test cases do we need?”

    It depends on the decision. To show a rate with a ±5-point interval near 80%, about 250. To detect a 3-point gain between two versions on the same questions, about 900 if they disagree on 10% of them. To show a failure rate is below 1%, about 300 clean cases. I'd write the decision rule first and compute the number from it.

    “Can we just use GPT or Claude to grade everything? It's much cheaper.”

    For development and triage, yes. For a number that goes to a risk committee, only after calibration: grade a few hundred answers by hand, measure how often the judge passes bad answers and fails good ones, correct for that, and show the wider interval. Where a programmatic check exists, use that and keep the judge for what needs language.

    “Why not just run it five times and average?”

    That reduces the randomness of the model's sampling. It does not reduce the randomness of which questions you chose, and that is usually the larger part. Both matter: more questions for the second, repeats for the first, and a paired comparison to make the best use of each.

    “The judge prefers our new version. Is that real?”

    I'd check four things: order swapped, length-matched, judge from a different family from both systems, and a human spot-check on the cases where it disagreed with the old one. If it survives all four, I'd trust it, and I'd give the size of the gain with an interval, not the direction alone.

    “Do we need human labels at all if the judge agrees with itself?”

    Yes. Agreement with itself measures consistency, not correctness. A judge can be consistently wrong, for example always passing fluent but unsupported answers. Humans are the reference that tells you the error rates to correct for, and a few hundred well-chosen labels are enough to start.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A model gets 90 of 100 questions right. Roughly what is the 95% interval, and what does that say about a competitor at 93%?

    SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 ÷ 100) = 3 points, so about ±6: roughly 83% to 95% (Wilson is a little lower, about 83% to 94%). The competitor's 93% sits inside it. Without a paired comparison on the same questions, you cannot call them different.

    2. You want to halve the width of an interval. What happens to the number of cases?

    Width scales with 1 ÷ √n, so halving it needs four times the cases. 100 becomes 400, and 400 becomes 1,600.

    3. Why does pairing two models on the same questions need fewer cases?

    Questions both models get right or wrong carry no information about which is better, and they are most of the data. The noise in the difference depends on the disagreement rate, which for similar models is far lower than the error rate of each. The sample that counts is the discordant questions.

    4. You built 400 questions by writing 8 paraphrases of each of 50 scenarios. What is your effective sample size, assuming a within-scenario correlation of 0.5?

    Design effect = 1 + (8 − 1) × 0.5 = 4.5. Effective n = 400 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 89. Your interval is as wide as for 89 independent questions, not 400.

    5. A judge shown A first prefers A 60% of the time and prefers it only 40% of the time when A is shown second. What is A's order-corrected win rate, and what does the gap show?

    Average of 60% and 40% is 50%: no real difference. The gap of 20 points is position bias. If you had run only one order you would have declared a winner in either direction.

    6. A judge passes 70% of answers. On the human-labelled set its sensitivity is 90% and specificity is 75%. What is the corrected pass rate?

    (0.70 + 0.75 − 1) ÷ (0.90 + 0.75 − 1) = 0.45 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 69%. Here the errors nearly cancel. Had specificity been 60%, it would be (0.70 + 0.60 − 1) ÷ 0.50 = 60%, so the bias depends on how the two errors balance.

    7. You tested 15 prompt variants on the same 200 questions and the best scored 6 points above baseline. Why is that not a 6-point improvement?

    The maximum of 15 noisy scores is biased upward: some of that win is luck. The honest check is to run the chosen variant and the baseline on fresh questions, or to apply a correction for multiple comparisons. Expect the gain to shrink.

    8. Why is "the judge agrees with itself 97% of the time" weak evidence?

    It measures repeatability. A biased judge can be perfectly repeatable. Validity needs comparison with an independent reference, human labels, from which you get sensitivity, specificity and the corrections.
