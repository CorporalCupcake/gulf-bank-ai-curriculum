    Customers as a Portfolio: Segmentation, Lifetime Value and Personalisation Within the Rules

    How to group a customer base so the groups mean something, what a customer is worth over years rather than this quarter, why that number is easy to get subtly wrong, and how to make offers relevant while compliance can still sign them off.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank's customers are an uneven portfolio of long-lived assets: segment them by what you can act on, value them by discounted future margin net of risk and cost, and personalise by filtering to what is allowed and suitable first, then ranking what is left by expected value.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A retail bank has two million customers. The marketing head asks a fair question: "Who are they, and which ones matter?" Two instincts fight each other. One says treat everyone alike, because it is simpler and fairer. The other says there are really a few types, and each deserves a different conversation. Both are partly right, and the work of this lesson is deciding where each one wins.

    Think like a portfolio manager. A fund does not hold one stock and does not treat all stocks as equal. It asks which holdings drive returns, which carry hidden risk, and how they move together. A customer base has the same shape. A small share of customers usually produces a large share of profit. Some customers look valuable today and leave in a year. Some look unremarkable today and will be worth far more in ten years, because they are young and will take a mortgage, a business account and a family of products. A balance sheet cannot see that. A lifetime value can.

    Three ideas, in order of how they build on each other.

    • Segmentation (B6.3) is choosing the groups. A good segment is one you would treat differently. A group that is statistically tidy but gets the same email as everyone else is decoration.
    • Customer lifetime value (B6.3) puts a number on each customer or segment: the margin you expect over the whole relationship, discounted to today, after funding, risk and servicing costs, and allowing for the chance they leave. It turns "who matters" into something you can compare and budget against.
    • Personalisation within suitability rules (B6.4) is the action step. A model can find the offer most likely to be accepted. A bank also has to make sure the offer is one the customer may be contacted about, can afford, understands and is suited to. In a regulated bank those are not afterthoughts. They are the first filter.
    Hold onto thisAverages hide the structure. Before you design a campaign, a service model or a model of value, ask three things. How concentrated is the value? Which groups behave differently enough to treat differently? And what is each allowed to be offered? Everything else follows from those.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Segmentation: choosing groups you can act on

    There are four common ways to cut a customer base, and they answer different questions.

    BasisExample segmentsGood forWeakness
    Who they are (demographic, life stage)Young salaried expatriates; families with school-age children; retirees; SME owners.Product design, tone, channel. Easy to explain.People of the same age behave very differently. Some attributes (nationality, gender) must not drive treatment at all.

    RFM: the workhorse that is still hard to beat

    The simplest behavioural segmentation is RFM: recency (how long since the last activity), frequency (how often), monetary (how much). Score each customer into thirds or fifths on each, and combine. In a bank, "activity" might be a card transaction, a login, or a deposit. It is transparent, cheap, and needs only a transaction table. Its fault is that it describes the past and treats all three scores as equally informative.

    Clustering (k-means and Gaussian mixtures, F4.6) finds groups from many behaviour features at once. The mechanism: choose k, drop k centres, assign each customer to the nearest, move each centre to the average of its members, repeat until nothing moves. Two cautions the cross-validation lesson already raised. The clusters depend on your features and scaling, so change either and the groups change. And there is no true k to recover; you choose the smallest number of groups that are stable, distinct and actionable.

    Four tests tell a useful segmentation from a pretty one.

    • Actionable. Would you do something different for each segment? If two segments get the same treatment, merge them.
    • Distinct. Do they differ on outcomes you care about (balance growth, product uptake, attrition), not only on the features used to build them? Clustering on spend and then "discovering" that segments differ in spend proves nothing.
    • Stable. Rerun on a different month or a bootstrap sample (F2.2). If a quarter of customers change segment each time, the groups are noise. Some movement is real: that is segment migration, and a transition matrix of who moves where is itself a useful early warning.
    • Describable. A segment you cannot name in one sentence will not survive a steering committee. Name them from their behaviour, not from a label you hoped to find.

    One more habit: segment on information that existed at the time, and keep segmentation separate from sensitive attributes. If a segment is in effect a proxy for nationality or gender and receives systematically worse terms, that is a fairness problem (G2) however neat the clustering was.

    Lifetime value: the formula and the real decisions inside it

    CLV=∑t=0T−1m·rt(1+d)t

    m is margin per active year, r the chance the customer is still active a year later (so rt is the chance they are still here at year t), d the discount rate, T the horizon. Each year's margin is shrunk twice: once because the customer may be gone, once because money later is worth less than money now. With m = 1,800, r = 0.85, d = 8%, T = 5 the factor is 1 + 0.787 + 0.619 + 0.487 + 0.383 = 3.28, giving about AED 5,900.

    The formula is three lines. All the difficulty is in what goes into m and r.

    Margin has to be net. A bank earns on both sides of the balance sheet, and revenue is not margin. Typical components:

    • Net interest income on loans (interest earned less the internal cost of the money lent) and on deposits (the value of the funds, credited at an internal transfer price, less what the customer is paid). Treasury's funds transfer pricing curve is what makes a deposit customer's value visible; see B5.
    • Fees and interchange, less rewards and cashback.
    • Expected credit loss on lending products (B4). A highly profitable borrower can have negative value once expected loss is subtracted. Leaving this out is the most common way CLV flatters risky customers.
    • Cost to serve (branch visits, call-centre minutes, relationship manager time) and acquisition and retention cost.
    • Where the bank uses it, a capital charge: lending uses regulatory capital that has a cost.

    Retention is a customer-level, product-level question. Banking is a mix. Some relationships are contractual: a mortgage or a fixed deposit has a start and an end you can see. Others are non-contractual: a current account or a card can sit unused, and "churn" is never announced. For non-contractual relationships you cannot observe the exit, so you must model the probability the customer is still active. The churn lesson (B6.1 · B6.2) covers how to define and predict that; here we use its output as r.

    Three ways to estimate it, in rising order of flexibility:

    A strong practical default is to model probability of being active and margin when active separately, then combine. It lets you explain each piece to the business, and it lets the first piece come from the same survival and churn tooling you already trust.

    The lesson of Simulation 3 is that population retention rises with age. This is survivor selection, the same effect that makes the oldest accounts the stickiest. Two practical consequences. First, a segment-level CLV from average retention can understate the value of a base made of very different customers, and a customer-level model is better than a segment average. Second, never read "customers who have been with us longest are the most loyal" as proof that tenure causes loyalty; it may be only that the fickle ones left.

    There is one deeper trap, and it is the same one the causal-inference lesson (F9.3) warns about. High CLV does not mean high return on spending money on that customer. A customer worth AED 40,000 who would have stayed anyway gains nothing from a retention gift. What you want to know is the change in value that an action causes, which is uplift, not level. A CLV model tells you where the stakes are. An experiment tells you whether acting pays.

    Personalisation within suitability rules

    Personalisation means choosing, for each customer, what to say, when, and in which channel. The well-known architecture for it, often called next-best-action, has a clear order. The order is the whole point for a regulated bank.

    1. Eligibility and permission. Does the customer have a valid marketing consent for this channel? Are they on a do-not-contact list? Is the product open to them at all (age, residency, existing product)?
    2. Suitability and affordability. Would this product be appropriate for this person? For credit, can they afford it (a debt-burden ratio or equivalent affordability test)? For investments, is there a completed risk profile and does the product match it? Is the customer flagged as vulnerable (recent bereavement, financial difficulty, very elderly), where extra care or no sales contact applies?
    3. Product and customer preferences. For example, a customer who has chosen Islamic-only banking should not be shown a conventional product, and a product description must match what the bank's Sharia supervisory board approved.
    4. Scoring. Only now do models rank what is left: probability of accepting, value if accepted (margin net of expected loss), and ideally the uplift the offer causes.
    5. Arbitration and contact policy. Limits on how often to contact someone, channel preference, and competing campaigns.
    6. Logging and explanation. Store why each offer was shown and which rules passed, so a complaint, an audit or a regulator's question can be answered months later.

    The reason for this order is that rules are hard constraints and models are soft preferences. If a model is trained to predict acceptance, it will happily rank an unaffordable loan first for exactly the customer who is most likely to say yes, because desperate customers do say yes. Filtering afterwards is dangerous: it is easy to forget a rule, and the model never learns what is allowed. Filtering first guarantees the ranking only ever sees permissible choices. Simulation 4 makes this concrete.

    Three details separate a sound system from one that merely passes a demo.

    • The cost of rules varies, and where it is large the value was never real. In Simulation 4, Sara (Islamic-only) gives up only about 17% of expected value, because the Islamic equivalent of the product is nearly as good. Omar gives up most of his, and that is the right outcome: the "value" was the bank profiting from a loan he could not carry, and it would have come back as arrears, complaints and write-offs. When your CLV already includes expected credit loss, the best affordable offer is in fact the most valuable one.
    • Not contacting is a legitimate action. Khalid has no consent, so the right output is nothing, or a non-marketing service message where the rules permit. "No offer" must be a first-class result, not an error.
    • Personalisation that uses sensitive attributes needs the fairness lens. Use behaviour and need, not nationality, gender or religion as drivers, and audit offer rates and terms across groups (G2). Use only data you are permitted to use for marketing (purpose limitation and consent).

    On modelling itself: the scorer behind step 4 is a recommender or propensity model (F9.5). A bandit (F9.7) can learn which of the allowed options works while still exploring a little, which is safe only because the allowed set is fixed first. Uplift models answer "who is changed by this offer", which is the question that pays. The batch machinery for running this on millions of customers monthly is in P5.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson sits between prediction and action. It uses outputs from earlier models and must respect constraints from later ones.

    B6.1 · B6.2 · Churn and retentionWhere retention r comes from

    Lifetime value is retention times margin, discounted. The churn model supplies the first factor, and its definition of "gone" decides whether the CLV is meaningful.

    F4.6 · ClusteringFinding groups, testing stability

    The mechanics of k-means and how to check whether clusters are real. Use its stability tests on every segmentation.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisTime to leave, with censoring

    The right tool when customers are still active and their story is unfinished. It gives the survival curve that CLV integrates over.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceValue is not uplift

    A high-CLV customer who would have stayed anyway gains nothing from your offer. Uplift and experiments tell you where acting pays.

    F9.5 · F9.7 · Recommenders and banditsThe scoring step

    Step 4 of next-best-action. A bandit explores within the allowed set.

    B4 · Credit riskNet the margin of expected loss

    A lending offer's value is margin minus expected credit loss. Without it, personalisation steers toward the riskiest borrowers.

    B5 · TreasuryDeposit value and funding cost

    A deposit's margin is the funds transfer price less the rate paid. Stable deposits are worth more to liquidity planning too.

    G2 · Fairness and privacyWho gets which offer

    Segments and offers must not become a proxy for protected traits, and consent and purpose limits bound what data may be used.

    P5 · Campaign architectureRunning it at scale

    Monthly batch scoring, suppression lists, holdout groups for measurement and the feedback loop into the next model.

    G3 · MonitoringSegments drift, rules change

    Segments and value models age. Monitor stability, migration and offer-acceptance, and re-review when products or regulations change.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Retail relationship strategy. Deciding who gets a relationship manager, a priority line, a branch visit or a digital-only experience. CLV and potential, not balance alone, should set the tiers.
    • Acquisition and cross-sell budgets. How much can you spend to win or deepen a customer? Not more than their discounted net value, and less again by the uplift you can prove.
    • Salary-transfer and payroll customers. In the Gulf, a salary landing in the account is a strong signal of a primary relationship and of future product need. It is a natural anchor for behavioural segments, and the account's loss to a competitor is the churn event that matters.
    • Remittance, expatriate and SME segments. Distinct needs and distinct risk. A segmentation that blends them into "mass market" loses most of the signal.
    • Islamic and conventional customers. Product eligibility and wording constraints are a hard filter in step 3 of the pipeline, and the margin definitions differ (profit rate rather than interest).
    • Collections and hardship. Vulnerable and distressed customers are excluded from sales contact and moved to a care journey, which is a personalisation decision too.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The methods for segmentation and lifetime value are mature; the live questions are whether complex models beat simple ones on realistic data, how to move from predicting value to predicting change in value, and how generative AI changes the message while the rules stay the same.

      Generative AI for the message, not the decision

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Segments that are only a restatement of the features

      Cluster on spend, then report that the high-spend cluster spends more.

      Spot itAsk how the segments differ on an outcome not used to build them: retention, product uptake, credit loss.
    • Segments that nobody treats differently

      Nine segments, one marketing calendar.

      Spot itAsk what each segment will receive that the others will not. If the answer is the same, merge.
    • Unstable clusters presented as truth

      A new run next quarter finds different groups, and last quarter's strategy is obsolete.

      Spot itAsk for the share of customers who keep their segment across two runs or bootstrap samples.
    • Revenue treated as margin

      Lifetime value built from fees and interest income with no funding cost, credit loss or cost to serve.

      Spot itAsk for the margin definition line by line. If expected credit loss is missing, risky borrowers will look like the best customers.
    • No discounting, an unbounded horizon, or one average retention

      A "lifetime" that sums forty years undiscounted, or applies one churn rate to a mixed base.

      Spot itAsk for the horizon, the discount rate and whether retention varies by customer. Compare against a simple historic cohort.
    • Mistaking value for return on action

      Spending most on the highest-CLV customers, who would have stayed anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the control group. No holdout means no evidence the spend changed anything.
    • Future information inside the value model

      Features built after the cut-off date leak, and value predictions look excellent.

      Spot itCheck that every feature is dated before the prediction point and that the test is a later period.
    • Rules applied after the model, or only some of them

      Top-scored offers are generated, then a compliance team is asked to catch the bad ones.

      Spot itAsk where in the pipeline eligibility, affordability and consent are applied, and whether a test confirms that a blocked offer can never be produced.
    • Optimising acceptance rather than customer outcome

      The model learns that customers in difficulty accept credit offers, and targets them.

      Spot itLook at who is selected by the top decile. Check arrears, complaints and attrition after acceptance, not only click-through.
    • Proxy discrimination

      Segments that map almost one-to-one onto nationality or gender, with different offers and terms.

      Spot itCross-tabulate segment against protected attributes, even if those attributes were not features, and compare offer rates and terms.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our customers are an uneven portfolio: a small share carries a large share of profit, and the average customer describes almost nobody. I'd group them into a handful of segments we can actually treat differently, and put a value on each customer that counts what they will earn over the years, after funding cost, credit risk and servicing. Then offers: the system first removes anything we are not allowed or not suited to offer that person, and only then picks the best of what is left. That protects customers and the bank, and in most cases it costs us very little.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd define margin net of transfer-priced funding, expected credit loss and cost to serve, and estimate CLV as survival times margin, discounted over a bounded horizon, starting with a probabilistic or survival baseline and a cohort check before anything deeper. Segments get tested for actionability and stability on a later period. For personalisation, eligibility, consent, affordability and vulnerability are hard constraints applied before scoring, with logged reason codes; ranking within the allowed set uses expected value, ideally uplift, measured against a control group. I'd audit offer rates across protected groups.”
    “How many segments should we have?”

    As few as give different treatments. A common answer is four to eight at the top level, with behavioural flags within them. Pick the number by what you can act on and by stability across two runs, not by an elbow plot alone. If two segments would get the same offers, same channel and same service, merge them.

    “Why not just rank customers by balance or revenue?”

    Balance is a snapshot and revenue ignores cost. A large borrower may be loss-making after expected credit loss; a young salaried customer with a small balance may be worth far more over ten years. Lifetime value, net of risk and funding, is the quantity decisions about service and spend should rest on.

    “Which customers should we spend retention budget on?”

    Not simply the highest-value ones. The ones where the offer changes the outcome: valuable, at risk of leaving, and responsive. That needs uplift, measured with a holdout. High-value customers who would have stayed anyway are the most expensive way to waste a retention budget.

    “Our model says this customer is very likely to take a loan. Why can't we send it?”

    Likelihood to accept is not the same as suitability. If the customer's debt burden is already above our affordability limit, is flagged as in difficulty, or has not given marketing consent, the offer is blocked before the model is consulted. A high acceptance score for a stretched customer is a warning, not a target.

    “Doesn't filtering by rules destroy the value of personalisation?”

    The filter removes offers that were either not permitted or likely to turn into arrears and complaints. In our example the value given up was small where a close permitted alternative existed (Sara), and large where the top offer was unaffordable or not consented, which is value the bank should not have counted. The important thing is that ranking happens inside the allowed set, so the model's effort goes to offers we can really send.

    “Can we use an LLM to personalise the messages?”

    For wording, yes, within limits. The decision on who gets what stays with the filtered pipeline. The model writes from approved templates and approved claims in the customer's language, outputs are checked for unapproved promises, and everything is logged. Letting a model invent product terms or tone for a vulnerable customer is the failure to design against.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A customer earns AED 2,000 margin per active year, retention is 80%, the discount rate is 10%, and you look three years ahead, counting year 1 as undiscounted. What is the lifetime value?

    Factor per year: 0.8 ÷ 1.1 = 0.7273. Sum over t = 0, 1, 2: 1 + 0.7273 + 0.5289 = 2.2562. Times 2,000 gives about AED 4,512.

    2. Why can raising retention by five points matter more than raising margin by five per cent?

    Retention compounds. It scales every later year's expected margin, and the effect grows with the horizon, whereas a five per cent margin rise is a flat five per cent of the total. At 85% retention and a five-year view, five more points of retention add roughly 9% to lifetime value in Simulation 2.

    3. Half your customers retain at 95% a year, half at 60%. What is year-1 retention, and what does a single-retention model get wrong?

    Year-1: 0.5 × 0.95 + 0.5 × 0.60 = 77.5%. Applying 77.5% every year predicts that 0.7755 ≈ 28% remain after five years. The actual figure is 0.5 × 0.955 + 0.5 × 0.605 ≈ 0.5 × 0.774 + 0.5 × 0.078 ≈ 43%. The fickle customers leave early, so the remaining base is increasingly loyal, and the naive model understates value.

    4. A segmentation shows that the "High spenders" cluster has the highest card spend. Is that a finding?

    No. The clusters were built from spend, so they differ on spend by construction. The test is whether they differ on something not used to build them, such as retention, product uptake or loss rate, and whether the same groups appear in a later period.

    5. Why should a high-lifetime-value customer not automatically get the retention offer?

    Value measures how much is at stake, not how much the offer will change. If the customer would have stayed anyway, the gift is a pure cost. Target on the expected change in value (uplift), estimated from an experiment with a control group.

    6. Why apply eligibility and suitability rules before the scoring model rather than after?

    Models rank by what is likely to be accepted, and the customers most likely to accept a credit offer are often those who can least afford it. Filtering first guarantees that a blocked offer can never be produced, lets the model learn within the allowed set, and gives you an auditable reason code for every decision. Filtering after relies on someone catching each violation.

    7. What belongs in "margin" for a deposit customer and for a lending customer?

    Deposit: the funds transfer price credited to the deposit minus the rate paid, plus fees, minus cost to serve. Lending: interest earned minus funding cost, plus fees, minus expected credit loss, cost to serve and often a capital charge. Revenue alone is not margin.
