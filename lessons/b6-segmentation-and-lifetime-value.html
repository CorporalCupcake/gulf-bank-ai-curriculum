    Customers as a Portfolio: Segmentation, Lifetime Value and Personalisation Within the Rules

    How to group a customer base so the groups mean something, what a customer is worth over years rather than this quarter, why that number is easy to get subtly wrong, and how to make offers relevant while compliance can still sign them off.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank's customers are an uneven portfolio of long-lived assets: segment them by what you can act on, value them by discounted future margin net of risk and cost, and personalise by filtering to what is allowed and suitable first, then ranking what is left by expected value.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of the customer base as a fund, not a single stock. A few holdings drive most returns, some carry hidden risk, and some look dull today but grow. The marketing head asks: "Who are they, and which ones matter?" Treating everyone alike is simple and fair, but customers differ. Where each view wins is the work of this lesson.

    A small share of customers usually produces a large share of profit. A young salaried customer may look unremarkable now and be worth far more in ten years (mortgage, business account, family products). A balance sheet cannot see that. A lifetime value can. Three ideas build on each other:

    • Segmentation (B6.3) is choosing the groups. A good segment is one you would treat differently. Like sorting post into piles: a pile that gets the same handling as every other pile is pointless.
    • Customer lifetime value (CLV) (B6.3) is the margin you expect over the whole relationship, discounted to today, after funding, risk and servicing costs, allowing for the chance they leave. It makes "who matters" comparable and budgetable.
    • Personalisation within suitability rules (B6.4) is the action step. A model finds the offer most likely to be accepted. The bank must first check the customer may be contacted, can afford it, and is suited to it. Those checks come first, not last.
    Hold onto thisAverages hide structure. Before any campaign or value model, ask: how concentrated is the value, which groups behave differently enough to treat differently, and what is each allowed to be offered?

    How it really works

    Segmentation: choosing groups you can act on

    RFM: the workhorse that is still hard to beat

    RFM scores each customer on recency (time since last activity), frequency (how often) and monetary (how much), in thirds or fifths, then combines them. In a bank, "activity" is a card transaction, login or deposit. It is transparent, cheap and needs only a transaction table. It describes the past and weights all three scores equally.

    Clustering (k-means, Gaussian mixtures, F4.6) groups customers on many behaviour features at once. Choose k, drop k centres, assign each customer to the nearest, move each centre to its members' average, repeat until nothing moves. Clusters change if you change features or scaling, and there is no true k: pick the smallest number that is stable, distinct and actionable.

    Four tests separate a useful segmentation from a pretty one.

    • Actionable. Each segment gets a different treatment. If two get the same, merge them.
    • Distinct. They differ on outcomes (balance growth, uptake, attrition), not just the features used to build them. Clustering on spend and "discovering" they differ in spend proves nothing.
    • Stable. Rerun on another month or a bootstrap sample (F2.2). If a quarter of customers switch each time, it is noise. Real movement is segment migration, and a transition matrix of who moves where is a useful early warning.
    • Describable. If you cannot name it in one sentence, it will not survive a steering committee.

    Segment on information that existed at the time, and keep sensitive attributes out. A segment that proxies for nationality or gender and gets worse terms is a fairness problem (G2) however neat the clustering.

    Lifetime value: the formula and the real decisions inside it

    CLV=∑t=0T−1m·rt(1+d)t

    m is margin per active year, r the chance of still being active a year later (rt is the chance of being here at year t), d the discount rate, T the horizon. Each year's margin shrinks twice: the customer may be gone, and later money is worth less (like a bus that may not come, and a fare that costs more tomorrow). With m = 1,800, r = 0.85, d = 8%, T = 5 the factor is 1 + 0.787 + 0.619 + 0.487 + 0.383 = 3.28, giving about AED 5,900.

    Read it as a rent roll: each year the customer "pays" margin, but you discount for the chance they have moved out. A CLV of AED 5,900 means the expected present value of the relationship is that much, not that the customer will pay it. Raising r lifts every later year at once, so retention often beats margin. The mistake to avoid is treating CLV as a certain number: it is only as good as m and r, and that is where the difficulty lies.

    Margin must be net. Revenue is not margin. Components:

    • Net interest income: on loans, interest less internal funding cost; on deposits, the funds transfer price credited less what the customer is paid. Treasury's funds transfer pricing curve makes deposit value visible (B5).
    • Fees and interchange, less rewards and cashback.
    • Expected credit loss (B4). A profitable borrower can be negative once it is subtracted. Omitting it is the commonest way CLV flatters risky customers.
    • Cost to serve (branch, call centre, RM time) and acquisition and retention cost.
    • A capital charge on lending, where the bank uses one.

    Retention is customer- and product-level. A mortgage or fixed deposit is contractual: the end is visible. A current account or card is non-contractual: it can go quiet and churn is never announced, so you model the probability of still being active. The churn lesson (B6.1 · B6.2) supplies that as r.

    A strong default is to model probability of being active and margin when active separately, then multiply. Each piece is explainable, and the first reuses your survival and churn tooling.

    Population retention rises with age because the fickle leave first. This is survivor selection, like a marathon where the slow runners drop out and the field gets faster. Two consequences. A segment CLV built on average retention understates a mixed base, so a customer-level model is better. And "longest-tenured customers are most loyal" does not show tenure causes loyalty; the fickle ones may simply be gone.

    One deeper trap, from causal inference (F9.3): high CLV does not mean high return on spending on that customer. A customer worth AED 40,000 who would have stayed anyway gains nothing from a retention gift. You want the change an action causes (uplift), not the level. CLV shows where the stakes are; an experiment shows whether acting pays.

    Personalisation within suitability rules

    Personalisation picks what to say, when, and in which channel. The standard design, next-best-action, works like airport security before the duty-free shop: checks first, browsing after. The order is the point.

    1. Eligibility and permission. Valid marketing consent for this channel? Not on a do-not-contact list? Product open to them (age, residency, existing product)?
    2. Suitability and affordability. Appropriate for this person? Credit: can they afford it (debt-burden ratio or similar)? Investments: completed risk profile that matches? Flagged vulnerable (bereavement, financial difficulty, very elderly), where extra care or no sales contact applies?
    3. Product and customer preferences. An Islamic-only customer must not see a conventional product, and wording must match what the Sharia supervisory board approved.
    4. Scoring. Only now do models rank what is left: acceptance probability, value if accepted (margin net of expected loss), ideally uplift.
    5. Arbitration and contact policy. Contact frequency, channel preference, competing campaigns.
    6. Logging and explanation. Store why each offer was shown and which rules passed, so a complaint or regulator's question can be answered months later.

    Rules are hard constraints; models are soft preferences. A model trained on acceptance will rank an unaffordable loan first for the customer most likely to say yes, because desperate customers do say yes. Filtering afterwards is risky: a rule gets forgotten, and the model never learns what is allowed. Filtering first guarantees the ranking only sees permissible choices.

    Three details separate a sound system from one that merely passes a demo.

    • Where rules cost a lot, the value was never real. Sara (Islamic-only) gives up about 17% of expected value because the Islamic equivalent is nearly as good. Omar gives up most of his, rightly: the "value" was profit from a loan he could not carry, which returns as arrears and write-offs. With expected credit loss in CLV, the best affordable offer is the most valuable one.
    • Not contacting is a legitimate action. Khalid has no consent, so the output is nothing, or a service message where permitted. "No offer" must be a first-class result, not an error.
    • Sensitive attributes need the fairness lens. Drive offers by behaviour and need, not nationality, gender or religion, and audit offer rates and terms across groups (G2). Use only data permitted for marketing (purpose limitation and consent).

    The step-4 scorer is a recommender or propensity model (F9.5). A bandit (F9.7) can learn which allowed option works while exploring a little, safe only because the allowed set is fixed first. Uplift models ask who is changed by the offer. Running this monthly on millions of customers is covered in P5.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Segments that are only a restatement of the features

      Cluster on spend, then report that the high-spend cluster spends more.

      Spot itAsk how the segments differ on an outcome not used to build them: retention, product uptake, credit loss.
    • Segments that nobody treats differently

      Nine segments, one marketing calendar.

      Spot itAsk what each segment will receive that the others will not. If the answer is the same, merge.
    • Unstable clusters presented as truth

      A new run next quarter finds different groups, and last quarter's strategy is obsolete.

      Spot itAsk for the share of customers who keep their segment across two runs or bootstrap samples.
    • Revenue treated as margin

      Lifetime value built from fees and interest income with no funding cost, credit loss or cost to serve.

      Spot itAsk for the margin definition line by line. If expected credit loss is missing, risky borrowers will look like the best customers.
    • No discounting, an unbounded horizon, or one average retention

      A "lifetime" that sums forty years undiscounted, or applies one churn rate to a mixed base.

      Spot itAsk for the horizon, the discount rate and whether retention varies by customer. Compare against a simple historic cohort.
    • Mistaking value for return on action

      Spending most on the highest-CLV customers, who would have stayed anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the control group. No holdout means no evidence the spend changed anything.
    • Future information inside the value model

      Features built after the cut-off date leak, and value predictions look excellent.

      Spot itCheck that every feature is dated before the prediction point and that the test is a later period.
    • Rules applied after the model, or only some of them

      Top-scored offers are generated, then a compliance team is asked to catch the bad ones.

      Spot itAsk where in the pipeline eligibility, affordability and consent are applied, and whether a test confirms that a blocked offer can never be produced.
    • Optimising acceptance rather than customer outcome

      The model learns that customers in difficulty accept credit offers, and targets them.

      Spot itLook at who is selected by the top decile. Check arrears, complaints and attrition after acceptance, not only click-through.
    • Proxy discrimination

      Segments that map almost one-to-one onto nationality or gender, with different offers and terms.

      Spot itCross-tabulate segment against protected attributes, even if those attributes were not features, and compare offer rates and terms.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A customer earns AED 2,000 margin per active year, retention is 80%, the discount rate is 10%, and you look three years ahead, counting year 1 as undiscounted. What is the lifetime value?

    Per-year factor 0.8 ÷ 1.1 = 0.7273. Sum for t = 0, 1, 2: 1 + 0.7273 + 0.5289 = 2.2562. Times 2,000 is about AED 4,512.

    2. Why can raising retention by five points matter more than raising margin by five per cent?

    Retention compounds: it scales every later year, and the effect grows with the horizon. A five per cent margin rise is a flat five per cent. At 85% retention over five years, five more points add roughly 9% to lifetime value in Simulation 2.

    3. Half your customers retain at 95% a year, half at 60%. What is year-1 retention, and what does a single-retention model get wrong?

    Year 1: 0.5 × 0.95 + 0.5 × 0.60 = 77.5%. Repeating 77.5% predicts 0.7755 ≈ 28% remain after five years. Actual: 0.5 × 0.955 + 0.5 × 0.605 ≈ 0.5 × 0.774 + 0.5 × 0.078 ≈ 43%. The fickle leave early, so the base gets more loyal, and the naive model understates value.

    4. A segmentation shows that the "High spenders" cluster has the highest card spend. Is that a finding?

    No. The clusters were built from spend, so they differ on spend by construction. Test whether they differ on something not used to build them (retention, uptake, loss rate) and whether the same groups appear in a later period.

    5. Why should a high-lifetime-value customer not automatically get the retention offer?

    Value measures what is at stake, not how much the offer changes. If they would have stayed anyway, the gift is pure cost. Target the expected change in value (uplift), estimated from an experiment with a control group.

    6. Why apply eligibility and suitability rules before the scoring model rather than after?

    Customers most likely to accept credit are often those who can least afford it. Filtering first guarantees a blocked offer can never be produced, lets the model learn within the allowed set, and gives an auditable reason code per decision. Filtering after relies on someone catching each violation.

    7. What belongs in "margin" for a deposit customer and for a lending customer?

    Deposit: funds transfer price credited minus rate paid, plus fees, minus cost to serve. Lending: interest earned minus funding cost, plus fees, minus expected credit loss, cost to serve and often a capital charge. Revenue alone is not margin.
