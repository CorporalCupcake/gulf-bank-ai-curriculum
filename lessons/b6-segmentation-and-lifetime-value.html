    Segmentation and Customer Lifetime Value

    Part 1

    What segmentation and lifetime value are

    In one sentenceSegmentation splits customers into groups you would treat differently, and customer lifetime value (CLV) estimates how much profit one customer will bring over the whole relationship, in today's money.

    Think of the customer base as an investment fund. A few holdings drive most of the profit, and some look dull today but will grow.

    Our running example: a bank with a few hundred thousand customers that wants to decide who gets which message and how much effort to spend keeping them.

    How it differs from similar things

    • Segment vs cluster. Clustering is one algorithm for finding groups. A segment is any group you can act on, however you found it.
    • CLV vs revenue. Revenue is what comes in. CLV is what is left after funding cost, expected credit losses and the cost of serving the customer.
    • CLV vs balance. A young salaried customer may hold little today but be worth far more in ten years.
    Part 2

    How it works

    Choosing segments

    There are four common ways to cut the base:

    • Who they are (life stage). Good for product design, but people of the same age behave differently.
    • What they are worth (value tiers). Good for service levels, but it misses the young high-potential customer.
    • What they do (behaviour). The most predictive, and good for triggers and early warnings.
    • What they need (first home, sending money abroad). Gives timely offers, but needs are often guessed.

    A simple, transparent method is RFM: score each customer on recency (how recently they were active), frequency (how often) and monetary value (how much). It needs only a transaction table and is hard to beat.

    Working out lifetime value

    1. Start with net margin per active year: income minus funding cost, expected credit loss and cost to serve.
    2. Estimate the chance the customer is still here each year (retention).
    3. Multiply: expected margin = margin × chance still here.
    4. Shrink later years by a discount rate, because money later is worth less than money today.
    5. Add up the years over a sensible horizon, usually 3 to 10 years.

    The result is an expected present value, not a promise. A customer will not pay AED 2,900 exactly: some leave early, some stay for years.

    Part 3

    How to read it

    Two customers, both AED 2,000 margin a year. Retention 95% vs 60%
    The 95% customer is worth far more, and the gap widens every year, because retention scales every later year.
    Retention up 5 points vs margin up 5%
    Margin is a flat 5% gain. Retention compounds. At 85% retention over five years, five more points add roughly 9% to CLV.
    High CLV
    This shows where the stakes are, not where spending pays. A customer worth AED 40,000 who would have stayed anyway gains nothing from a retention gift. Test it with a control group and look at the change an action causes (uplift).
    A segment that "spends more"
    If you built the clusters from spend, of course they differ on spend. A real finding is a difference on something you did not use, such as attrition or loan uptake.

    Four tests for a useful segmentation

    • Actionable. Each segment gets a different treatment. If two get the same, merge them.
    • Distinct. They differ on outcomes, not just on the inputs used to build them.
    • Stable. Rerun next month. If a quarter of customers switch segment, it is noise.
    • Describable. If you cannot name it in one sentence, nobody will use it.

    Averages hide loyal customers

    Say half your customers retain at 95% a year and half at 60%. One "average" rate of 77.5% understates value. The fickle customers leave first, so the people who remain are increasingly loyal.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ApproachIdeaCatch
    Historic average by segmentAdd up what past customers in the segment really producedLooks backwards. New products have no history.
    "Buy till you die" (BG/NBD)Each customer transacts at their own rate and may silently drop outSuits shopping better than salary-driven banking
    Machine learning on future valuePredict next-12-month margin from today's featuresNeeds a clean cut-off date. Values are skewed and many are zero.
    • Strong at: showing where value is concentrated, and which groups need different treatment.
    • Weak at: telling you whether an action works (that needs an experiment), and at products with no history.
    • A strong default: model the chance the customer is active and the margin when active separately, then multiply.

    Offers within the rules

    When a model picks the next offer, apply the rules first and the ranking second, like airport security before the duty-free shop.

    1. Eligibility and consent: may we contact them, and can they have the product?
    2. Suitability: can they afford it, and does it suit them? An Islamic-only customer must never see a conventional product.
    3. Scoring: only now rank what is left by acceptance chance and value.

    Rules are hard limits and the model gives soft preferences. A model trained on acceptance would rank an unaffordable loan first, because desperate customers say yes. "Send nothing" must be a valid answer.

    Show the formula

    CLV = margin in year 1 + margin × retention ÷ (1 + discount) in year 2 + margin × retention² ÷ (1 + discount)² in year 3, and so on. Each year multiplies by retention ÷ (1 + discount). With 0.8 ÷ 1.1 ≈ 0.73, a bar is about 73% of the one before.

    In banking, segments must not stand in for nationality, gender or religion. If a segment lines up with one of these and gets worse terms, that is a fairness problem however neat the clustering.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating revenue as margin

      Leaving out funding cost and expected credit loss makes risky borrowers look like the best customers. Ask for the margin line by line.

    • Circular or unstable segments

      Clusters built on spend will differ on spend. Check outcomes you did not use, and rerun on another period.

    • One average retention rate, no discounting

      Summing forty years undiscounted, or applying one churn rate to a mixed base, gives a CLV nobody should trust.

    • Applying rules after the model

      Generating top-scored offers and then asking compliance to catch bad ones lets violations through. Filter first.

    1. Why does CLV shrink each year twice?

    The customer may have left (retention), and money received later is worth less than money today (discounting).

    2. Why can five more points of retention beat a 5% margin rise?

    Retention scales every later year, so its effect compounds. A margin rise is a flat 5%.

    3. The "high spenders" cluster has the highest card spend. Is that a finding?

    No, the clusters were built from spend. Test them on an outcome not used to build them, such as attrition.

    4. Should the highest-CLV customer always get the retention offer?

    No. CLV shows what is at stake, not how much the offer changes. If they would stay anyway, the offer is pure cost. Target uplift, measured with a control group.

    5. Why filter by eligibility and suitability before scoring?

    The customers most likely to accept credit are often those who can least afford it. Filtering first means a blocked offer can never be produced.
