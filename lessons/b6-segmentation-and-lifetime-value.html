    Customers as a Portfolio: Segmentation, Lifetime Value and Personalisation Within the Rules

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of the customer base as a fund, not a single stock. A few holdings drive most returns, some carry hidden risk, and some look dull today but grow. A young salaried customer may look unremarkable now and be worth far more in ten years (mortgage, business account, family products). A balance sheet cannot see that. A lifetime value can. Three ideas build on each other:

    • Segmentation (B6.3) is choosing the groups. A good segment is one you would treat differently.
    • Customer lifetime value (CLV) (B6.3) is the margin you expect over the whole relationship, in today's money, after funding, risk and servicing costs, allowing for the chance they leave.
    • Personalisation within suitability rules (B6.4) is the action step. A model finds the offer most likely to be accepted, but the bank must first check the customer may be contacted, can afford it and is suited to it.
    Hold onto thisAverages hide structure. Before any campaign or value model, ask: how concentrated is the value, which groups behave differently enough to treat differently, and what is each allowed to be offered?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Segmentation: choosing groups you can act on

    Four common ways to cut a customer base, answering different questions.

    BasisExample segmentsGood forWeakness
    Who they are (life stage)Young salaried expatriates; families; retirees; SME owners.Product design, tone, channel.Same-age people behave very differently. Some attributes (nationality, gender) must not drive treatment.
    What they are worth (value tiers)Priority; mass affluent; mass market.Service model and budget.Judges the past. Misses the young high-potential customer.
    What they do (behaviour)Salary spent in a week; spends abroad; saves steadily; dormant.Triggers and early warning. Most predictive.Needs good features (F3.4) and shifts over time.

    RFM scores each customer on recency (time since last activity), frequency (how often) and monetary (how much). It is transparent, cheap and needs only a transaction table, which is why it is still hard to beat. Clustering (F4.6) lets an algorithm group customers on many behaviours at once. There is no true number of clusters: pick the smallest number that is stable, distinct and actionable.

    Four tests for a useful segmentation:

    • Actionable. Each segment gets a different treatment. If two get the same, merge them.
    • Distinct. They differ on outcomes (attrition, uptake), not just the features used to build them. Clustering on spend and "discovering" they differ in spend proves nothing.
    • Stable. Rerun on another month. If a quarter of customers switch segment, it is noise.
    • Describable. If you cannot name it in one sentence, it will not survive a steering committee.

    Keep sensitive attributes out. A segment that proxies for nationality or gender and gets worse terms is a fairness problem (G2) however neat the clustering.

    Lifetime value: the formula and the real decisions inside it

    Lifetime value adds up the margin from each year, but shrinks every year twice: the customer may have left, and later money is worth less than money today. A CLV of AED 5,900 is an expected present value, not a promise the customer will pay it. Raising retention lifts every later year at once, so it often beats a margin rise. CLV is only as good as the margin and retention you feed it.

    Margin must be net. Revenue is not margin. Take off:

    • Funding cost. On loans, interest less internal funding cost; on deposits, the value Treasury credits less what the customer is paid (B5).
    • Expected credit loss (B4). A profitable borrower can turn negative once it is subtracted. Omitting it is the commonest way CLV flatters risky customers.
    • Cost to serve, acquisition cost, rewards and cashback.

    Retention. A mortgage is contractual: the end is visible. A current account or card is non-contractual: it goes quiet without announcing churn, so you model the probability of still being active (B6.1 · B6.2).

    Three ways to estimate it:

    ApproachIdeaCatch
    Historic average by segmentAdd up the margin past customers in the segment actually produced.Backward-looking. New products have no history.
    "Buy till you die" models (BG/NBD)Each customer transacts at their own rate and may silently drop out. Fit from recency, frequency and spend.Fits retail purchases better than salary-driven banking.
    Machine-learning on future valuePredict next-12-month margin from features at a cut-off date.Must respect point-in-time rules (F3.1). Value is skewed and zero-heavy.

    A strong default: model probability of being active and margin when active separately, then multiply.

    Population retention rises with age because the fickle leave first. This is survivor selection, like a marathon where the slow runners drop out and the field gets faster. So "longest-tenured customers are most loyal" does not show tenure causes loyalty; the fickle ones may simply be gone.

    One deeper trap (F9.3): high CLV does not mean high return on spending on that customer. A customer worth AED 40,000 who would have stayed anyway gains nothing from a retention gift. You want the change an action causes (uplift), not the level. CLV shows where the stakes are; an experiment shows whether acting pays.

    Personalisation within suitability rules

    The standard design, next-best-action, works like airport security before the duty-free shop: checks first, browsing after. The order is the point.

    1. Eligibility and consent. May we contact them, and is the product open to them?
    2. Suitability. Can they afford it, does it match their risk profile, are they vulnerable? An Islamic-only customer must not see a conventional product.
    3. Scoring. Only now do models rank what is left: acceptance probability, value if accepted, ideally uplift.
    4. Contact policy and logging. Store why each offer was shown so a complaint can be answered months later.

    Rules are hard constraints; models are soft preferences. A model trained on acceptance will rank an unaffordable loan first for the customer most likely to say yes, because desperate customers do say yes. Filtering first guarantees the ranking only sees permissible choices.

    • Where rules cost a lot, the value was never real. Omar gives up most of his expected value, rightly: it was profit from a loan he could not carry, which returns as arrears and write-offs.
    • Not contacting is a legitimate action. Khalid has no consent, so the output is nothing. "No offer" must be a first-class result, not an error.
    • Sensitive attributes need the fairness lens. Drive offers by behaviour and need, not nationality, gender or religion, and audit offer rates across groups (G2, consent).

    The scorer is a recommender or propensity model (F9.5). Running this monthly at scale is covered in P5.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Segments that are circular, unstable or never treated differently

      Cluster on spend, then report that the high-spend cluster spends more. Or a new run next quarter finds different groups. Or nine segments, one marketing calendar.

      Spot itAsk how segments differ on an outcome not used to build them, what share keep their segment across two runs, and what each will receive that the others will not.
    • Revenue treated as margin

      Lifetime value built from fees and interest income with no funding cost, credit loss or cost to serve.

      Spot itAsk for the margin definition line by line. If expected credit loss is missing, risky borrowers will look like the best customers.
    • No discounting, an unbounded horizon, or one average retention

      A "lifetime" that sums forty years undiscounted, or applies one churn rate to a mixed base.

      Spot itAsk for the horizon, the discount rate and whether retention varies by customer.
    • Mistaking value for return on action

      Spending most on the highest-CLV customers, who would have stayed anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the control group. No holdout means no evidence the spend changed anything.
    • Rules applied after the model, or optimising acceptance over outcome

      Top-scored offers are generated, then compliance is asked to catch the bad ones. Or the model learns that customers in difficulty accept credit, and targets them.

      Spot itAsk where eligibility, affordability and consent are applied, and check arrears and complaints after acceptance, not only click-through.
    • Proxy discrimination

      Segments that map almost one-to-one onto nationality or gender, with different offers and terms.

      Spot itCross-tabulate segment against protected attributes and compare offer rates and terms.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Two customers each earn AED 2,000 margin a year. One has 95% retention, the other 60%. Which is worth more, and why does the gap grow with the horizon?

    The 95% customer, by a wide margin. Retention scales every later year, so each extra year multiplies the difference. Discounting shrinks later years a little, but the loyal customer is still there to earn them.

    2. Why can raising retention by five points matter more than raising margin by five per cent?

    Retention compounds: it scales every later year, and the effect grows with the horizon. A five per cent margin rise is a flat five per cent. At 85% retention over five years, five more points add roughly 9% to lifetime value in Simulation 2.

    3. Half your customers retain at 95% a year, half at 60%. What does a single average retention rate get wrong?

    It treats everyone as 77.5% retained every year. In reality the fickle customers leave early, so the base gets more loyal over time and far more customers remain after five years than the average predicts. The naive model understates value.

    4. A segmentation shows that the "High spenders" cluster has the highest card spend. Is that a finding?

    No. The clusters were built from spend, so they differ on spend by construction. Test whether they differ on something not used to build them (retention, uptake, loss rate) and whether the same groups appear in a later period.

    5. Why should a high-lifetime-value customer not automatically get the retention offer?

    Value measures what is at stake, not how much the offer changes. If they would have stayed anyway, the gift is pure cost. Target the expected change in value (uplift), estimated from an experiment with a control group.

    6. Why apply eligibility and suitability rules before the scoring model rather than after?

    Customers most likely to accept credit are often those who can least afford it. Filtering first guarantees a blocked offer can never be produced, lets the model learn within the allowed set, and gives an auditable reason code per decision. Filtering after relies on someone catching each violation.

    7. What belongs in "margin" for a deposit customer and for a lending customer?

    Deposit: funds transfer price credited minus rate paid, plus fees, minus cost to serve. Lending: interest earned minus funding cost, plus fees, minus expected credit loss, cost to serve and often a capital charge. Revenue alone is not margin.
