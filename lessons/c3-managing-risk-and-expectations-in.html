    Managing Risk, Expectations and People

    Part 1

    What managing expectations means

    In one sentenceManaging risk and expectations means telling people early what could go wrong and how long things might really take, so that nothing arrives as a surprise.

    Take one running example. At kickoff of a new alert-prioritisation model, the sponsor asks: "When can we go live?" You have not seen the data yet, but a number is expected, so you say "about six months".

    That number now becomes the date you are judged against. In month four someone finds it was never realistic, and nobody saw it coming. The fault was not effort. It was how the uncertainty was communicated.

    Think of a commute. It takes 30 minutes on a good day, a crash adds an hour, and nothing gets it below 25. Project dates behave the same way: delays stack up, but nothing can finish before the minimum.

    Part 2

    How to manage it

    How it differs from software delivery

    In ordinary software, "will it work?" is mostly a question of effort. In machine learning you often cannot know whether the data supports, say, 70% recall until a first version exists. So you plan cheap tests first, expensive commitments later.

    Five steps

    1. Name the risks. Sort them into four kinds (picture below), because each needs a different fix.
    2. Give a range, not a date. Quote P50 and P80 (explained below) and say which one you plan around.
    3. Name what narrows the range. "I will give you a firmer date once the baseline is measured in week five."
    4. Keep a short risk register. Five to eight items, each with an owner, an early warning sign and a response.
    5. Escalate on a rule. Agree triggers up front, such as a milestone slipping two weeks. Escalating is then the process working, not a failure.
    Part 3

    How to read a date, and how to lead people

    PercentilesP50 is the date you are as likely to beat as to miss. P80 is the date you beat four times in five.
    "Six months" is really the P50
    You have a coin flip of missing it. If others depend on you, plan near P80 instead, perhaps eight months.
    Six tasks, each 50-50 to beat its "likely" time
    All six beating them together is rare, like six traffic lights all green. The total usually lands later than the sum of the "likely" times.
    Warning sign "validation queue over three weeks"
    Someone can act on that. "Delays possible" cannot be acted on.
    A good update, in order
    Where we are against what we said; what we learned; what it does to the date, as a range; what we need from you, by when.

    Leading people: no surprises either

    • Feedback: describe an observed behaviour, its effect, and a next step, soon after it happens. Test: could a camera have filmed it? "Careless" cannot be acted on. "Show the number of positives next to every metric" can.
    • Example: "In Tuesday's model review you showed the AUC without the test-set size, and the validators questioned the whole result."
    • Safety: people only raise doubts, such as a suspected data leak, if it feels safe. Google's Project Aristotle found this the strongest team trait.
    • Delegating: settle three things. What outcome do I need? What can this person decide alone? When do I hear how it is going?
    Part 4

    Which way to communicate

    ApproachWhat the listener hearsUse it when
    One dateA promiseAlmost never, early on
    Range with a reasonA planKickoff and every update
    Range plus a narrowing eventA plan that firms upWhen the sponsor wants more certainty

    Strong at: removing surprises, keeping sponsors' trust, giving early options. Weak at: sponsors who insist on one date. Offer a date for narrowing the range, a firm date for the next gate, and what would let you commit sooner. Never give a precise date you do not believe.

    In banking, validation and data-access queues are often longer than the modelling itself, so involve validators in the first month and start those queues first. In a Gulf setting, give feedback in private, ask before telling, and make disagreement explicitly welcome.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting the middle as a promise

      "About six months" becomes a commitment by repetition. Quote P80 and a range, and say what the date rests on.

    • Watermelon status

      Green outside, red inside: "on track" until the week it is not. Ask when the earliest warning sign appeared. If it was weeks before, the reporting rule is wrong.

    • Moving the goalposts

      Changing the test set or metric so the promise is met. If the evaluation changed after the target was agreed, say so openly.

    • Feedback saved for the review

      The first real conversation should not be the year-end rating. Give specific feedback within days.

    1. Why is the chance of beating the sum of the "likely" times so low?

    Delays are lopsided, so long outcomes dominate the total. Many small "likely" times add up to something later.

    2. What is the difference between P50 and P80, and which do you plan around?

    P50 is as likely missed as beaten. P80 is beaten four times in five. Plan near P80 when others depend on you.

    3. What should a risk register entry contain besides the risk?

    An owner, an observable early warning sign and a response. The warning sign makes it usable.

    4. Why does "bad news does not improve with age" matter?

    Early, you can offer options. Late, you can only apologise.

    5. Rewrite "your analysis was sloppy" as specific feedback.

    "In this morning's pack the segment table did not add to the total, so I corrected it before it reached the CRO. Can we agree a reconciliation check before it goes out?"
