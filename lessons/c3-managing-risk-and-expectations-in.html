    Managing Risk and Expectations, and Leading People

    Why a single delivery date is almost always a wrong answer, how to say what is uncertain early and in a form people can act on, and how to give feedback that changes behaviour when you start leading a team.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceUncertainty does not go away when you hide it, it just turns into a surprise with your name on it, so give ranges and decision dates early, name the risks that could move them, and use the same candour, specific and kind, when you give feedback to the people you lead.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    In the kickoff meeting for a new alert-prioritisation model, the sponsor asks: "When can we go live?" The room waits. You have not seen the data. You do not know how long the model risk team's queue is. You say "about six months" because a number is expected, and because everyone around the table is nodding at six.

    Six months is a plausible middle. But a plan is a chain of steps, each of which can slip, and slips are lopsided: a task can run three weeks late, but it will never finish three weeks before it has started. So the realistic finish sits to the right of the sum of the sensible estimates. Six months becomes the date the project is judged against, and it is wrong by design. In month four someone discovers it, in a steering committee, with a senior audience and no warning.

    None of that is a failure of effort. It is a failure of communication about uncertainty. Almost every delivery problem in AI work looks like this: the work was uncertain in ways you could see early, and the people who relied on it were told something more certain than what you knew.

    The second half of this lesson is the same idea pointed inward. When you lead people, the thing most likely to hurt them is a surprise: a performance conversation that arrives in a year-end review rather than in week six, when it could still change something. Honest, early, specific information is how you manage risk with a project and with a person.

    Hold onto thisExpectations are set by what people hear, not what you meant. A single number is heard as a promise. A range with a reason is heard as a plan. The skill is making the range and the reason easy to say, and saying them before anyone has to ask.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C3.5 Managing risk and expectations in AI delivery

    Why AI work is harder to promise than software work. In ordinary software, "does it work?" is mostly a matter of effort. In ML, there is a second question that effort does not settle: can the data support this at all? You often cannot know whether a fraud model can reach 70% recall at your alert capacity until you have built a first version. That uncertainty is not laziness or poor planning. It is the nature of the work, and it is why a plan for an AI project should be an experiment ladder (cheap tests first, expensive commitments later) rather than a fixed march to a date.

    Four kinds of risk, because they need different handling.

    • Feasibility risk: the signal may not be in the data. Handled by an early baseline and a go/no-go gate (see Baselines).
    • Schedule risk: access, approvals, validation queues, integration. Often larger than the modelling itself in a regulated bank. Handled by ranges and by starting the slow queues first.
    • Operating risk: drift, bad labels, a bad day in production. Handled by monitoring, a rollback path and a human fallback.
    • Conduct and compliance risk: unfair outcomes, unexplainable decisions, data used beyond its purpose. Handled by involving risk and compliance early and leaving a paper trail. The CBUAE's 2026 guidance note on AI (Part 4) puts weight here.

    Schedules are sums of skewed numbers. Each task has a best case, a likely case and a bad case. The bad case is further from "likely" than the good case is, because delays stack on top of the likely duration but nothing can go below the minimum. Add several such tasks and two things happen. The total is longer than the sum of the "likely" values, and it is much less likely to land on or below it. If each task has a 50% chance of beating its estimate, finishing six of them all on time is far rarer than one in two.

    The honest summary is therefore a percentile. "P50" is the date you are as likely to beat as to miss. "P80" is the date you would beat four times out of five. Sponsors planning around your delivery want something close to P80. Teams hearing a P50 as a commitment are being set up to fail. Try this below.

    How the range gets narrower. Early on the honest range is wide, perhaps 2x from best to worst. It narrows when you buy information: data access granted, a baseline measured, validation scoped. So say what event will narrow it, not only the range: "I will give you a firmer date after the baseline is measured in week five." That sentence turns an uncomfortable answer into a plan, and it gives your sponsor a date on which the uncertainty gets smaller.

    The risk register people actually use. A long list of risks that nobody reads is theatre. A working one has five to eight items, each with: what could happen, how likely and how bad, a named owner, an early warning sign, and what you will do about it. The warning sign is the part people skip, and it is the useful part. "Validation queue exceeds three weeks" is something you can observe and act on. "Delays possible" is not.

    Escalate on a rule, not on a feeling. Agree up front what triggers a conversation: a milestone slipping by more than two weeks, a metric below an agreed floor, a dependency that you cannot unblock yourself. Then it is never a personal confession. It is the process working. The rule for timing is simple: bad news does not improve with age. Early, you can offer options. Late, you can only apologise.

    What a good update contains. Four things, in this order. Where we are against what we said. What changed or what we learned. What it does to the date or the result, as a range. What we need from you, with a date. Do not bury the third point in the fifth slide.

    C3.6 Leading people and giving feedback

    Most data scientists become team leads because they are good at the work, not because they have practised leading. The change is that your output is now other people's output. The things that determine whether a team does well are mostly not technical. Google's internal study of its own teams (Project Aristotle) found that psychological safety, the shared belief that you will not be punished for speaking up with a mistake, a question or a doubt, was the strongest of the team traits they tested. In AI work that matters directly: the person who first suspects the labels are leaking will only say so if saying so feels safe.

    Feedback that works has four parts. It is about an observed behaviour and not a trait. It says what effect that had. It comes soon. And it ends with a shared view of what to do next. A common frame is Situation, Behaviour, Impact (SBI), taught by the Center for Creative Leadership: "In Tuesday's model review (situation), you showed the AUC without the test-set size (behaviour), and the validators questioned the whole result (impact)." Notice what is missing: any word about the person's character. "Careless" cannot be acted on. "Show the number of positives next to every metric" can.

    Praise is feedback too. Specific praise tells someone what to repeat. "Great job" teaches nothing. "Your leakage check caught the post-event field before validation did, and it saved us two weeks" teaches a lot.

    Delegation in three questions. What outcome do I need (not which steps)? What can this person decide alone? When do I want to hear how it is going? A task delegated without the second question is returned to you for every decision, and one delegated without the third is returned to you late.

    In a Gulf setting. Teams are often mixed in nationality, seniority, and language. Direct criticism in front of others can cost someone face in ways a Western management book would not warn you about, and hierarchy may make a junior colleague unwilling to disagree with you at all. This is not a reason to avoid feedback. It is a reason to give it in private, to build in asking before telling ("how did that review feel to you?"), and to make disagreement explicitly welcome. Individuals differ more than cultures do, so ask rather than assume. See Scoping and running an AI project for the wider context on working across cultures.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Risk and expectations are where the other Track C skills meet reality. Leading people is where the same ideas turn inward.

    C1 · BaselinesThe cheapest risk reduction

    A baseline in week two tells you whether the problem is feasible before anyone has promised a date.

    C2 · Explaining uncertaintyAnswer first, range included

    The range-with-a-reason update is the executive-summary habit applied to a project.

    C2 · Validators and risk committeesThe slowest queue

    Model validation is usually the biggest schedule risk. Engage the validators in week one, share the design before the model exists.

    C3.1 · C3.2 · First 90 days and stakeholdersTrust is the buffer

    Sponsors who trust you accept ranges more easily. Early honesty is how that trust is built.

    C3.3 · C3.4 · Scoping and cultureScope is the first lever

    When the date moves, scope is the thing you trade. Agree up front which parts are essential.

    F7 · Metrics and driftOperating risk is measurable

    Early warning signs for production are metrics with thresholds. Agree them before launch.

    F2.1 · IntervalsSame idea, applied to dates

    An interval on recall and a range on a date are the same honesty. A range is not weakness, it is a measurement.

    B1 · Fraud deliveryDelayed labels delay evidence

    If labels take six weeks to mature, you cannot know a fraud model's real recall until then. Say so in the plan.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Fraud and AML models. Promised recall depends on labels that arrive late. Commit to a measurement date, not just a number.
    • Credit models and IFRS 9. Validation, governance sign-off and regulatory notice periods make schedule risk large. Start those queues first.
    • GenAI copilots and document tools. Pilots look good in a demo and weaker at scale. Agree a quality floor and a hand-back to humans before launch, not after.
    • Your own team. A new hire's first project is a feedback opportunity in week two, not in the year-end review.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    This is not a research field in the same way as the earlier tracks, so the evidence is a mix of surveys, regulator guidance and management research. I flag below how well each item is established.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with two light searches. Most coverage of the survey figures is secondary; I did not read the primary Gartner or MIT NANDA documents, so treat the numbers as headlines.

    1. 1979
      Optimism bias in plans

      Kahneman and Tversky described the planning fallacy: people underestimate their own tasks' durations even when they know similar tasks ran late. Bent Flyvbjerg's later work on large projects supports the same pattern, and recommends "reference class forecasting" (use the outcomes of similar past projects) as the fix.

      Kahneman and Tversky, 1979 (cited from memory); Flyvbjerg, 2006
    2. 2011
      Model risk guidance becomes the bank norm

      The US Federal Reserve and OCC issued SR 11-7 on model risk management, with the principle of "effective challenge" and independent validation. It is the template banks worldwide, including in the Gulf, borrow from. Local rules differ, so check CBUAE and SAMA requirements for your bank directly.

      Federal Reserve SR 11-7
    3. 2015–16
      Project Aristotle and psychological safety

      Google's study of 180 teams found psychological safety to be the most important dynamic. Amy Edmondson's academic work from 1999 on team learning is the underlying research.

      Edmondson, 1999; Google re:Work
    4. 2025
      Most AI pilots do not pay off, as reported

      A widely quoted MIT Project NANDA report (2025) said about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no measurable revenue or cost effect within six months. Gartner (June 2025) forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing cost, unclear value and weak risk controls. Both are surveys and forecasts, and the methodology of the first has been questioned. Read them as a prompt to define value and risk early, not as hard rates.

      MIT Project NANDA, 2025; Gartner press release, 25 June 2025 (via secondary coverage)
    5. 2026
      CBUAE guidance on AI in financial services

      The Central Bank of the UAE issued a guidance note on consumer protection and the responsible adoption of AI and machine learning by licensed financial institutions (reported dates of 11 or 23 February 2026; I could not reconcile them). Secondary summaries say it covers governance, explainability, fairness, accountability and data protection, keeps the institution responsible for outsourced AI, and recommends an inventory of AI models and the ability to suspend a system quickly. Reports disagree on whether it is mandatory or advisory, and it appears to exclude DIFC and ADGM. Read the text at the CBUAE rulebook, not a summary.

      CBUAE, 2026 (read via press release and law-firm summaries)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each of these is common. Each has a quick check.

    • Quoting the middle as a promise

      "About six months" becomes a commitment by repetition.

      Spot itAsk whether the number is a P50 or a P80, and who heard it as what.
    • Promising a metric before seeing the data

      "We will reach 90% accuracy" in the kickoff slide.

      Spot itCheck for a stated metric with no baseline, no interval and no date for the first measurement.
    • Watermelon status

      Green on the outside, red inside. Reports say on track until the week it is not.

      Spot itAsk what the earliest warning sign was and when it was first seen. If the answer is "weeks before", the reporting rule is wrong.
    • Risk register as paperwork

      Fifty risks, no owners, never reviewed.

      Spot itOpen the last version. If no item has an owner, a warning sign and a recent change, nobody uses it.
    • Moving the goalposts to hit the number

      Changing the test set or the metric definition so the promise is met.

      Spot itCheck whether the evaluation set or the metric changed since the target was agreed.
    • Leaving validation to the end

      The model is done and then waits in a queue for ten weeks.

      Spot itLook for validator involvement in the first month's plan.
    • Feedback saved for the review

      The first real conversation about someone's work is a year-end rating.

      Spot itAsk the team when they last got specific feedback. If the answer is more than a month, that is the problem.
    • Feedback as a verdict on the person

      "You're not a strategic thinker."

      Spot itCan you name the date, the behaviour and the effect? If not, it is a judgement.
    • Becoming the bottleneck

      You are still the best coder, so you take the hard tasks and the team stops growing.

      Spot itCount the decisions that wait on you. If your calendar is the critical path, delegate more.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a sponsor asking for a date · 30 seconds
    “Today my honest range is five to eight months, and I'd plan around seven. Two things move it most: how quickly we get data access and the validation queue. I'll give you a firmer date in week five, once the baseline is measured and validation has scoped the work. If it slips past two weeks I will tell you that day, with options.”
    To your team, giving feedback
    “In Tuesday's review you showed the AUC without how many fraud cases were in the test set, and the validators questioned the whole result. Next time can you put the count and an interval next to every metric? You otherwise asked good questions on the feature work, so I'd like you to present it again on Thursday with that change. How did the review feel to you?”
    “Why can't you just give us a date?”

    Because I would be giving you the optimistic one. Each step has a chance of slipping and none can finish early, so the date I would be most comfortable defending is later than the sum of my guesses. I can give you a range now, the event that narrows it, and the date I'll update you.

    “The business wants 90% by quarter end. What do you do?”

    Ask what decision the 90% supports and what today's process achieves. Offer to measure a baseline within two weeks and agree the target then. If it is not feasible, say so with the evidence, and propose what is: a smaller scope, a later date or a human-in-the-loop version.

    “A team member keeps missing deadlines. Where do you start?”

    With a private conversation that starts by asking, not accusing. Name the specific dates, ask what is getting in the way, and listen. It is often unclear scope, a blocked dependency, or work they are unsure how to start. Then agree small checkpoints. Only after that does a pattern become a performance matter.

    “How do you handle a senior colleague who disagrees with your estimate?”

    Treat it as information. Ask what they have seen that I have not, ideally from a similar past project, and walk through the assumptions. If we still differ, present both ranges to the sponsor with the reasons rather than quietly picking one.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Six tasks each have a 50% chance of beating their "likely" estimate. Why is the chance of beating the sum of those estimates so much lower than 50%?

    Because delays are lopsided: the bad case is further above "likely" than the good case is below it. Adding up, long outcomes dominate. In Simulation 1 the chance of finishing by the sum of the likely values is about 1%, not 50%.

    2. What is the difference between P50 and P80, and which would you quote to a sponsor?

    P50 is the date you are as likely to miss as to beat. P80 is beaten four times in five. Quote a range and say which you would plan around, usually close to P80 for anything with a downstream dependency.

    3. What should a risk register entry contain, besides the risk?

    Likelihood and impact, an owner, an observable early warning sign and the response. The warning sign is what makes it usable.

    4. Why is "bad news does not improve with age" more than a slogan?

    Early, you still have options (scope, date, resources). Late, you only have an explanation. Each week of delay also makes the eventual conversation worse because people ask why they were not told.

    5. Rewrite "your analysis was sloppy" as SBI feedback.

    For example: "In this morning's pack (situation), the segment table did not add to the total, and the totals did not match last week's (behaviour), so I had to correct it before it went to the CRO (impact). Can we agree a reconciliation check before it goes out?"

    6. Why does psychological safety matter in a modelling team in particular?

    Many serious modelling errors, such as leakage, bad labels, and a result that is too good, are first suspected by junior people. If raising a doubt feels risky, the doubt reaches validators or production instead of you.

    7. A sponsor will not accept a range. What can you offer?

    A date for narrowing it, a staged commitment (a firm date for the next gate rather than the end), and a clear statement of what would let you commit sooner, for example earlier data access. Do not give a precise date you do not believe.
