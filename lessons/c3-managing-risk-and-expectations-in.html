    Managing Risk and Expectations, and Leading People

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    At kickoff for a new alert-prioritisation model, the sponsor asks: "When can we go live?" You have not seen the data or the model risk team's queue. You say "about six months" because a number is expected.

    Think of a commute. It takes 30 minutes on a good day, a crash adds an hour, and nothing gets it below 25. Project delays are lopsided in the same way, so the realistic finish sits later than your sensible estimates suggest. Six months becomes the date you are judged against, and in month four someone discovers it is wrong, with no warning.

    That is a failure to communicate uncertainty, not a failure of effort. Leading people is the same idea pointed inward: the most damaging thing is a surprise, such as a problem raised in the year-end review instead of week six.

    Hold onto thisA single number is heard as a promise. A range with a reason is heard as a plan. Give both before anyone asks.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C3.5 Managing risk and expectations in AI delivery

    AI work is harder to promise than software. In software, "does it work?" is mostly effort. In ML, you often cannot know whether the data supports 70% recall at your alert capacity until a first version exists. So plan an experiment ladder: cheap tests first, expensive commitments later.

    Four kinds of risk need different handling.

    • Feasibility: the signal may not be in the data. Fix: early baseline and a go/no-go gate (see Baselines).
    • Schedule: access, approvals, validation queues. Often bigger than the modelling in a bank. Fix: ranges, and start the slow queues first.
    • Operating: drift, bad labels, a bad day in production. Fix: monitoring, rollback, human fallback.
    • Conduct and compliance: unfair or unexplainable decisions. Fix: involve risk and compliance early.

    Schedules are sums of skewed numbers. Delays stack above "likely" but nothing goes below the minimum, so the total lands later than the sum of the "likely" values. It is like six traffic lights: each is 50-50 green, but all six together is far rarer.

    Summarise with a percentile. P50 is the date you are as likely to beat as miss. P80 is beaten four times in five. A P50 heard as a commitment sets the team up to fail, so when others depend on you, plan near P80. Try it below.

    Name the event that narrows the range. An honest early range may be 2x wide. It narrows as you buy information: "I will give you a firmer date once the baseline is measured in week five."

    A usable risk register has five to eight items, each with an owner, an early warning sign and a response. The warning sign is the useful part: "validation queue exceeds three weeks" can be acted on, "delays possible" cannot.

    Escalate on a rule, not a feeling. Agree triggers up front, such as a milestone slipping two weeks. Escalating is then the process working. Bad news does not improve with age. Early, you offer options. Late, you apologise.

    A good update, in order: where we are against what we said; what we learned; what it does to the date, as a range; what we need from you, by when.

    C3.6 Leading people and giving feedback

    When you lead, your output is other people's output. Google's Project Aristotle found psychological safety (the belief that you will not be punished for raising a mistake or doubt) the strongest team trait. In AI work it is concrete: the person who first suspects label leakage only says so if it feels safe.

    Feedback that works describes an observed behaviour, says its effect, comes soon, and ends with a next step. The Situation, Behaviour, Impact (SBI) frame fits: "In Tuesday's model review, you showed the AUC without the test-set size, and the validators questioned the whole result." Test: could a camera have filmed it? "Careless" cannot be acted on. "Show the number of positives next to every metric" can. Praise works the same way: "great job" teaches nothing, "your leakage check saved two weeks" tells them what to repeat.

    Delegate with three questions. What outcome do I need? What can this person decide alone? When do I hear how it is going? Skip the second and every decision comes back to you.

    In a Gulf setting, criticism in front of others can cost face, and a junior may not disagree with you at all. Give feedback in private, ask before telling, and make disagreement explicitly welcome. Individuals differ more than cultures, so ask rather than assume. See Scoping and running an AI project.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting the middle as a promise

      "About six months" becomes a commitment by repetition; a metric like "90% accuracy" is promised before anyone has seen the data.

      Spot itIs the number a P50 or P80, and does it have a baseline and a first-measurement date?
    • Watermelon status

      Green outside, red inside: "on track" until the week it is not.

      Spot itAsk when the earliest warning sign appeared. "Weeks before" means the reporting rule is wrong.
    • Risk register as paperwork

      Fifty risks, no owners, never reviewed.

      Spot itNo item has an owner, a warning sign and a recent change.
    • Leaving validation to the end

      The model is done, then waits ten weeks in a queue.

      Spot itNo validator involvement in the first month's plan.
    • Moving the goalposts

      Changing the test set or metric definition so the promise is met.

      Spot itDid the evaluation set or metric change since the target was agreed?
    • Feedback saved for the review, or aimed at the person

      The first real conversation is a year-end rating, or a verdict like "you're not strategic".

      Spot itCan you name the date, behaviour and effect? Has the team had specific feedback this month?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Six tasks each have a 50% chance of beating their "likely" estimate. Why is the chance of beating the sum so much lower than 50%?

    Delays are lopsided, so long outcomes dominate the total. In Simulation 1 the chance is about 1%.

    2. What is the difference between P50 and P80, and which would you quote to a sponsor?

    P50 is as likely missed as beaten. P80 is beaten four times in five. Quote a range and say which you plan around, usually near P80 when others depend on you.

    3. What should a risk register entry contain, besides the risk?

    An owner, an observable early warning sign and the response. The warning sign makes it usable.

    4. Why is "bad news does not improve with age" more than a slogan?

    Early, you have options. Late, you have an explanation.

    5. Rewrite "your analysis was sloppy" as SBI feedback.

    "In this morning's pack, the segment table did not add to the total, so I had to correct it before it went to the CRO. Can we agree a reconciliation check before it goes out?"

    6. Why does psychological safety matter in a modelling team in particular?

    Leakage, bad labels and too-good results are often first suspected by juniors. If raising a doubt feels risky, it reaches validators or production instead of you.

    7. A sponsor will not accept a range. What can you offer?

    A date for narrowing it, a firm date for the next gate, and what would let you commit sooner. Never give a precise date you do not believe.
