    Batch Scoring, Fallbacks and Cost: Designing AI Systems That Run Every Day

    How a nightly propensity and campaign scoring job is really built, what should happen when the model or an API is down, and how to turn tokens, GPUs and storage into a budget a finance team will believe.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA production AI system is a model plus a schedule, a contract on what it outputs, a plan for the day a part breaks, and a price per decision; design all four on purpose, starting from what a wrong or missing answer costs the business.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most money-making ML in a retail bank is a nightly job. At 2 a.m. it scores 500,000 customers on how likely each is to take a personal finance offer, card upgrade or savings product, and writes a file. By 8 a.m. SMS, the app and branch dashboards read it. Nobody watches. It fails in boring ways: a late upstream table, a wrong-dated file, an opted-out customer contacted anyway.

    Think of a bakery: the recipe (model) is the easy part. The system is the schedule, the check that ingredients arrived, the plan if the oven breaks, and the cost per loaf. Three questions turn a notebook into a system.

    • What decision does the score drive, and how many can we act on? Contacting everyone above 0.5 is almost never right. The cut comes from the value of a response, the cost of a contact and call-centre capacity (P5.3).
    • What happens when a part breaks? Model server, feature lookup, external LLM API, the job itself. Which answer does the customer get instead, and does it err safe or err open (P5.4)?
    • What does one decision cost, and at ten times the volume? Tokens, GPU hours, storage, people. Most of it fits on one page before any code (P5.5).

    One discipline underlies all three: write down the failure and cost of each choice before choosing. The real-time sibling is P5.1–P5.2; this lesson covers batch plus the shared machinery for breakage and money.

    Hold onto thisBatch is the default until latency forces otherwise: cheaper, easier to test, audit and fix. Add real-time only for decisions that cannot wait until morning, and give every path a fallback and a price.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Batch propensity and campaign scoring (P5.3)

    A nightly scoring job is a kitchen line: seven stations, each with its own way to go wrong.

    StageWhat happensDesign rule
    1. SnapshotFix an as-of date; read each table as it stood then.Point-in-time discipline as in F3.1. Training and scoring build features the same way, ideally from one definition (P1).
    2. EligibilityRemove closed accounts, opt-outs, deceased, under-18s, vulnerable-customer flags, recent complaints, sanctions or fraud holds.Filter before scoring and again after, from a fresh consent table. Consent can change between 2 a.m. and 10 a.m.
    3. Feature buildBehaviour features from transactions, balances, products.Check row counts and null rates against yesterday. Stop if a key table is empty or stale.
    4. ScoreApply the registered model to every eligible customer.Pin the model version and write it to the output. Never "latest".
    5. DecideTurn scores into actions: who gets which offer, in what order, within contact limits.Keep separate from the model. Business rules change weekly; the model quarterly.
    6. PublishWrite a table or file with run date, model version and row count.Idempotent: re-running the same day replaces that day's output, never doubles it.
    7. MonitorScore distribution, volumes, freshness and, weeks later, actual responses.Alert on a moved score distribution (F7.7), not only a crashed job.

    Score, then decide: the cut is an economic choice

    Like a fisherman deciding how many nets to cast: keep casting while the expected catch pays for the net. A propensity model gives each customer a probability p of responding. Contact when p × value > cost, i.e. when p exceeds cost ÷ value. A response worth AED 400 of margin and a contact costing AED 12 gives a break-even of 3%, far below "above 0.5". That is why campaign lists often take a fifth of the base even when the average response is 2%. This needs calibrated scores; if a "3%" customer really responds at 1%, the rule fails.

    The cumulative gains curve sorts customers by score and shows what share of all responders sits in the top 10%, 20% and so on. Lift is the response rate in the contacted group divided by the overall rate: lift 3 in the top decile means responders found at three times a random list's rate. Lift 1 is no better than random. Both are the precision and recall of F7 at a chosen depth. Misreading to avoid: high lift says the model ranks well, not that the campaign caused the sales.

    Three design decisions hide behind that curve.

    • Propensity is not uplift. A model finding likely savings buyers also finds people who would have bought anyway; messaging them is wasted spend and sometimes irritating. Uplift models estimate the change caused by contact and need a randomised holdout (F9.2). The cheap safeguard is a standing control group: hold back a random 5–10% of every list, never contact them, compare responses weeks later. Without it the bank cannot say what the campaign caused (F3.6).
    • Capacity and contact policy bind before the model does. A call centre that rings 8,000 a day; no more than two marketing messages per customer per month; different fatigue rules for SMS and push. In the decide stage: rank by expected value, fill capacity, apply contact limits. Overlapping campaigns compete for one customer, so one arbiter ("next best action") chooses, not each campaign team.
    • Labels arrive late, so fix the window first. "Took the product within 30 days of the message" is defined before training; last night's customers have no label for a month. Evaluate forward in time on past campaigns, and store model version and score date with every contact so responses join back correctly (B1).

    Two Gulf-specific constraints are routine. Consent and channel rules differ by product and jurisdiction; the consent table, not the model team, is the source of truth, and I would not assume CBUAE or SAMA marketing rules without asking compliance. For Islamic products, offers must sit within Sharia-approved terms: the model scores interest, a separate approved process decides what may be offered.

    Making a nightly job safe to re-run

    Like a light switch, not a toggle: running it twice leaves the same state. Write to staging, validate, then swap in atomically, keyed by run date.

    def run(as_of):                       # as_of = a date, passed in, never "today"
    elig   = eligible_customers(as_of)           # consent, status, holds
    feats  = build_features(elig, as_of)         # point-in-time, same code as training
    check(feats, min_rows=400_000, max_null=0.02)  # stop here if the inputs look wrong
    scores = model_v17.predict_proba(feats)[:, 1]
    out    = decide(elig, scores, capacity=8_000, control_share=0.05, seed=as_of)
    write_staging(out, run_date=as_of, model="v17")
    validate_staging(as_of)                      # counts, score distribution vs yesterday
    swap_into_live(as_of)                        # replaces that date, never appends twice

    Note seed=as_of: a seeded control draw picks the same customers on a re-run. Otherwise a re-run quietly moves people between control and treated.

    Fallbacks, rate limits and graceful degradation (P5.4)

    Every component has availability below 100%. A managed LLM API may target 99.9% (about 43 minutes down a month), and chained dependencies multiply: three at 99.9% give about 99.7%, around two hours a month. You cannot remove failure; you choose what happens next. That is graceful degradation: like a car losing power steering but keeping its brakes, quality drops in steps, not all at once. The building blocks:

    • Timeouts. Every call gets a deadline shorter than the caller's own. An unbounded wait lets one slow dependency take down everything upstream.
    • Retries with exponential backoff and jitter. Retry transient errors, wait longer each time, add randomness so a thousand clients do not retry in lockstep. Retry only idempotent operations (or those with an idempotency key); retrying "send SMS" blindly sends it twice. Cap attempts, since retries add load to a service already struggling.
    • Rate limits and queues. A token bucket keeps you under a provider's limit: tokens refill steadily, each call spends one, a burst uses the saved-up bucket. Beyond that, queue work that can wait or shed low-priority traffic first. A 429 "too many requests" is information; do not retry it immediately.
    • Circuit breakers. Like a fuse: after a run of failures, stop calling the broken dependency for a while and go straight to the fallback. It saves time and stops you hammering a recovering service.
    • A fallback ladder. Best to safest: main model; smaller model with fewer features; last night's cached score; a conservative rule. The last rung must run in your own process with no external call, or it is not a fallback.
    def score(txn, deadline_ms=120):
    for tier in [primary, light_model, cached_score, rule]:   # best to safest
        if breaker[tier].open():              # recently failing: skip it
            continue
        try:
            return tier(txn, timeout=deadline_ms / 1000)     # every call has a deadline
        except (Timeout, Unavailable):
            breaker[tier].record_failure()
    return SAFE_DEFAULT                       # never raise to the customer

    Fail open or fail closed: a business decision

    The last rung needs a default, and someone must choose it.

    • Marketing score: fail closed. Send nothing tonight.
    • Card fraud: blocking every payment when the model is down is a self-inflicted outage, so the rule tier lets low-value payments through and challenges high-value or unusual ones.
    • Sanctions or AML screening: failing open can be a compliance breach, so queue the payment for review (B2.2).

    The model team proposes; the business owner and risk function sign off each default, in writing, before launch, not at 3 a.m. For LLM features there is one more rung: the answer you do not generate. A staff copilot that cannot reach its model can show search results and the policy link, or say plainly it is unavailable. A confidently wrong cheap-model answer is worse than an honest "not available" for anything touching customers' money (M4.5).

    The correlation setting is the lesson. A fallback only helps if it fails for different reasons than what it replaces: like a spare key kept in the same bag as the first. Two models on one feature store go down together; so do two LLM providers behind the same cloud region and gateway. The best fallbacks are dull: last night's cached score, a rule in your own process. Test by switching off the dependency and watching what the customer sees. Untested fallbacks fail on the day.

    Two more habits. Monitor how often each rung is used, so a quiet slide to the rule tier is an incident, not a surprise in a quarterly review. And log which rung produced each decision; the validator will ask how many fraud decisions last quarter came from the rule, not the model (G3.4).

    Cost estimation for AI systems (P5.5)

    Cost estimation is a one-page spreadsheet. The usual mistake is leaving a line out. A realistic budget has six.

    LineWhat drives itRule of thumb
    Model inferenceTokens (LLM API), GPU-hours (self-hosted) or CPU time (classic models).Tabular models cost pennies per million predictions. LLM calls cost fractions of a cent to several cents each.
    Retrieval and storageVector index, documents, logs and traces, backups.A million 1,024-number float32 vectors is about 4 GB before index overhead. Full-prompt logs grow fast.
    Data movementEgress between clouds or to on-premises; cross-region copies.Small per GB, large in a hybrid design shipping data both ways daily (P4).
    Evaluation and testingRegression suites, LLM-as-judge, red-teaming, shadow traffic.Often 10–30% of inference cost in development; a judge scoring every output can double the bill.
    Training and tuningRetraining cadence, hyperparameter search, fine-tuning.One-off per release; multiply by retrain frequency.
    People and operationsOn-call, monitoring, model risk validation, security, compliance.Usually the biggest line. One engineer a quarter costs more than the GPUs.

    LLM API arithmetic

    Hosted LLMs charge per million tokens, input and output separately, output several times dearer. A token is about three-quarters of an English word; Arabic often needs more tokens per word, so the same document can cost more in Arabic (M1.1). Like a taxi meter: a flag-fall plus a rate per kilometre, except you are billed per word read and per word written.

    cost per request=Tin·Pin+Tout·Pout1,000,000

    T are tokens per request, P the price per million. Prompt caching bills repeated input (system prompt, policy text) at a fraction; batch endpoints for work that can wait hours are commonly about half price. Example: 1,500 in and 300 out at an illustrative USD 3 and USD 15 per million is 0.0045 + 0.0045 = USD 0.009, about AED 0.033. Times 50,000 requests a day for 30 days is about AED 50,000 a month, before caching.

    Four things matter more than the price list.

    • Context length is the hidden multiplier. 8,000 tokens of retrieved passages per call costs five times the same call with 1,500. Cutting wasted context is usually the cheapest saving (M1.3).
    • Agents multiply calls. An eight-step loop re-sends a growing context each time, so cost grows faster than step count. Budget per task, with a hard cap on steps and spend (M4.1).
    • Route by difficulty. A small model does classification and extraction; the expensive one gets the hard 10%. Routing, caching and batching together often cut a bill by over half, so a central gateway that meters and enforces budgets pays for itself (A15, M2.5).
    • Self-hosting is a step function. Like renting a taxi per ride versus buying a minibus: you buy whole GPUs sized for the peak, at least two for resilience. Below a certain volume the API is cheaper; above it, owned GPUs win if you can keep them busy and staffed. Residency rules may force self-hosting regardless (P4.4). Quantisation fits a model on fewer GPUs (P3).

    Two ways to make the estimate honest. First, use a unit the business recognises: AED per customer scored, per case summarised, per transaction screened. "AED 0.03 per statement query" can be set against the analyst time it saves; a monthly total cannot. Second, give a range (low, expected, high volume, and tokens per request, since real prompts run longer than test ones). Check it against the first month's bill and keep a cost-per-task dashboard. The benefit side is A16.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Operational failures rarely look like ML failures. Each has a quick check.

    • A stale or partial upstream table scored as fresh

      The job succeeds, the file looks normal, half the customers have last week's balances.

      Spot itFreshness and row-count checks before scoring; compare tonight's score distribution with last week's.
    • Contacting people who opted out

      Eligibility was computed at the start; consent changed before the send.

      Spot itAsk when consent was last read relative to the send. Test a customer who opts out mid-run.
    • Non-idempotent re-runs

      After a crash the job re-runs and customers get two offers, or the control group is redrawn.

      Spot itRun it twice in test. Row counts and the control list must match.
    • Training and scoring features built differently

      Trained on a clean extract, scored on a new pipeline with a different "active in last 90 days".

      Spot itCompare a feature's distribution in training and in the live table. Gaps in means or null rates are the sign.
    • Measuring lift without a control group

      "Contacted customers bought at 6%", with no comparison to similar customers left alone.

      Spot itAsk for the holdout response rate. No holdout means the campaign effect is unknown.
    • Correlated fallbacks

      The backup model reads the same feature store, so both go down together.

      Spot itDraw each rung's dependency graph. Any shared box is a shared failure. Switch it off to test.
    • Retry storms

      Clients retry instantly and in unison against a slow service, turning a blip into an outage.

      Spot itLook for no backoff or jitter, no attempt cap, and retries stacked at several layers.
    • A silent drift to the fallback

      The rule tier quietly serves 15% of traffic for three weeks.

      Spot itIs share of decisions by rung on a dashboard with an alert? If not, you cannot know.
    • Budgeting from a test prompt

      The estimate used a 300-token demo prompt. Production carries 4,000 tokens of retrieved text, an agent loops eight times, and the bill is 30 times higher.

      Spot itAsk for the token distribution from realistic traffic, a high percentile, and a spend cap per task.
    • Leaving out people and review

      A cost case with only GPU and API lines, ignoring validation, on-call and compliance.

      Spot itCount lines. No operations or model risk means the number is a floor, not an estimate.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A response is worth AED 250 of margin and a contact costs AED 5. Above what calibrated probability should you contact someone?

    Contact when p x 250 > 5, so p > 5 / 250 = 2%.

    2. Top decile responds at 6.5%; the whole base at 2%. What is the lift, and what else do you need to know?

    Lift = 6.5 / 2 = 3.25. Also: the holdout rate (how many would have responded anyway) and whether the model was tested forward in time on past campaigns.

    3. Why pass the run date into the job and seed the control draw with it?

    So a re-run reproduces the same output. A clock date changes on a retry after midnight, and an unseeded draw moves customers between control and treated, corrupting the experiment.

    4. Three services in series are each 99.9% available. Combined figure and monthly downtime?

    0.999 cubed is about 99.7%. Over 43,200 minutes that is about 130 minutes down. This assumes independent failures; shared causes make it worse.

    5. The main model is down 5% of the time, the light model 2% on its own, independently. What share of requests reach the cached score or the rule? And with a 60% shared cause?

    Independent: 0.05 x 0.02 = 0.1%. With a 60% shared cause, P(light down | main down) = 0.6 + 0.4 x 0.02 = 0.608, so 0.05 x 0.608 = 3.0%, thirty times more.

    6. A payments screening service is unreachable. Fail open or closed?

    For sanctions or AML, not open: hold or queue payments for review. For card fraud, blocking everything is its own outage, so a rule tier usually passes low-risk items. It is a business and risk decision, written down in advance.

    7. 20,000 requests a day, 2,000 input and 400 output tokens, USD 3 and USD 15 per million, no caching. Monthly cost in AED?

    Per request: 2,000 x 3 / 1e6 = 0.006, plus 400 x 15 / 1e6 = 0.006, so USD 0.012. Per month: 0.012 x 20,000 x 30 = USD 7,200, about AED 26,440 at 3.6725.

    8. Why might a cost estimate from a demo prompt be wrong by an order of magnitude?

    Production prompts add retrieved context, history and instructions; agent loops re-send growing context each step; retries and judge-model evaluations add calls. Estimate from realistic traffic, use a high percentile, cap spend per task.
