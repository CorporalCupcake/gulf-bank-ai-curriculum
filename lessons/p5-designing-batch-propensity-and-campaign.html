    Batch Scoring, Fallbacks and Cost: Designing AI Systems That Run Every Day

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most money-making ML in a retail bank is a nightly job. At 2 a.m. it scores 500,000 customers on how likely each is to take an offer and writes a file that SMS, the app and branch dashboards read by 8 a.m. Nobody watches. It fails in boring ways: a late upstream table, a wrong-dated file, an opted-out customer contacted anyway.

    Think of a bakery: the recipe (model) is the easy part. The system is the schedule, the check that ingredients arrived, the plan if the oven breaks, and the cost per loaf. Three questions turn a notebook into a system.

    • What decision does the score drive, and how many can we act on? The cut comes from the value of a response, the cost of a contact and call-centre capacity (P5.3).
    • What happens when a part breaks? Which answer does the customer get instead, and does it err safe or err open (P5.4)?
    • What does one decision cost, and at ten times the volume? Tokens, GPU hours, storage, people (P5.5).

    The real-time sibling is P5.1-P5.2.

    Hold onto thisBatch is the default until latency forces otherwise: cheaper, easier to test, audit and fix. Add real-time only for decisions that cannot wait until morning, and give every path a fallback and a price.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Batch propensity and campaign scoring (P5.3)

    A nightly scoring job is a kitchen line: seven stations, each with its own way to go wrong.

    StageWhat happensDesign rule
    1. SnapshotFix an as-of date; read each table as it stood then.Point-in-time discipline (F3.1); one feature definition for training and scoring (P1).
    2. EligibilityRemove closed accounts, opt-outs, vulnerable customers, sanctions or fraud holds.Filter before scoring and again after, from a fresh consent table.
    3. Feature buildBehaviour features from transactions, balances, products.Compare row counts and null rates with yesterday. Stop if a key table is stale.
    4. ScoreApply the registered model to every eligible customer.Pin the model version and write it to the output.
    5. DecideTurn scores into actions within contact limits.Keep separate from the model: rules change weekly, the model quarterly.
    6. PublishWrite a table with run date, model version and row count.Idempotent: a re-run replaces that day's output, never doubles it.
    7. MonitorScore distribution, volumes, freshness, later responses.Alert on a moved score distribution (F7.7), not only a crash.

    Score, then decide: the cut is an economic choice

    Keep casting nets while the expected catch pays for the net. Contact a customer when their probability of responding times the value of a response beats the cost of the contact. A response worth AED 400 and a contact costing AED 12 breaks even at 3%, far below "above 0.5". That is why campaign lists often take a fifth of the base even when the average response is 2%. It needs calibrated scores: if a "3%" customer really responds at 1%, the rule fails.

    Lift is the response rate in the contacted group divided by the overall rate: lift 3 in the top decile means responders are found at three times a random list's rate; lift 1 is no better than random. The cumulative gains curve shows what share of all responders sits in the top 10%, 20% and so on. High lift says the model ranks well, not that the campaign caused the sales.

    Three design decisions hide behind that curve.

    • Propensity is not uplift. A model finding likely savings buyers also finds people who would have bought anyway. The cheap safeguard is a standing control group: hold back a random 5-10% of every list, never contact them, and compare responses weeks later (F9.2, F3.6).
    • Capacity and contact policy bind before the model does. A call centre that rings 8,000 a day; no more than two marketing messages per customer per month. Rank by expected value, fill capacity, apply limits, and let one arbiter choose across overlapping campaigns.
    • Labels arrive late, so fix the window first. "Took the product within 30 days of the message" is defined before training. Store model version and score date with every contact so responses join back correctly (B1).

    Consent and channel rules differ by product and jurisdiction; the consent table is the source of truth, and I would not assume CBUAE or SAMA marketing rules without asking compliance. For Islamic products, the model scores interest and a separate approved process decides what may be offered.

    Making a nightly job safe to re-run

    Like a light switch, not a toggle: running it twice leaves the same state. Write to staging, validate, then swap in atomically, keyed by run date.

    def run(as_of):                       # a date passed in, never "today"
    elig   = eligible_customers(as_of)
    feats  = build_features(elig, as_of)
    check(feats, min_rows=400_000, max_null=0.02)   # stop if inputs look wrong
    scores = model_v17.predict_proba(feats)[:, 1]
    out    = decide(elig, scores, capacity=8_000, control_share=0.05, seed=as_of)
    write_staging(out, run_date=as_of, model="v17")
    validate_staging(as_of)
    swap_into_live(as_of)                        # replaces that date, never appends

    The seed=as_of means a re-run picks the same control customers.

    Fallbacks, rate limits and graceful degradation (P5.4)

    Every component fails sometimes, and chained dependencies add up: three at 99.9% give about two hours of downtime a month. You cannot remove failure; you choose what happens next. That is graceful degradation: like a car losing power steering but keeping its brakes, quality drops in steps, not all at once.

    • Timeouts and retries. Every call gets a deadline. Retry transient errors with growing waits plus randomness, so a thousand clients do not retry in lockstep, and only for operations safe to repeat: retrying "send SMS" blindly sends it twice.
    • Rate limits and circuit breakers. Stay under a provider's limit and queue work that can wait. Like a fuse, a breaker stops calling a dependency after repeated failures and goes straight to the fallback.
    • A fallback ladder. Best to safest: main model, smaller model, last night's cached score, a conservative rule. The last rung must run in your own process with no external call, or it is not a fallback.

    Fail open or fail closed: a business decision

    The last rung needs a default, and someone must choose it.

    • Marketing score: fail closed. Send nothing tonight.
    • Card fraud: blocking every payment is a self-inflicted outage, so the rule tier lets low-value payments through and challenges unusual ones.
    • Sanctions or AML screening: failing open can be a compliance breach, so queue the payment for review (B2.2).

    The business owner and risk function sign off each default in writing before launch, not at 3 a.m. For LLM features there is one more rung: the answer you do not generate. A confidently wrong cheap-model answer is worse than an honest "not available" for anything touching customers' money (M4.5).

    The correlation setting is the lesson. A fallback only helps if it fails for different reasons than what it replaces, like a spare key kept in the same bag as the first. Two models on one feature store go down together; so do two LLM providers behind the same cloud region. The best fallbacks are dull: last night's cached score, a rule in your own process. Test by switching off the dependency and watching what the customer sees.

    Monitor how often each rung is used, so a quiet slide to the rule tier is an incident, not a surprise. And log which rung produced each decision; the validator will ask (G3.4).

    Cost estimation for AI systems (P5.5)

    Cost estimation is a one-page spreadsheet. The usual mistake is leaving a line out. A realistic budget has six.

    LineWhat drives itRule of thumb
    Model inferenceTokens (LLM API), GPU-hours (self-hosted) or CPU time.Tabular models cost pennies per million predictions; LLM calls cost fractions of a cent to several cents each.
    Retrieval and storageVector index, documents, logs, backups.Full-prompt logs grow fast.
    Data movementEgress between clouds or to on-premises.Small per GB, large in a hybrid design (P4).
    Evaluation and testingRegression suites, LLM-as-judge, red-teaming.A judge scoring every output can double the bill.
    Training and tuningRetraining cadence, fine-tuning.One-off per release; multiply by frequency.
    People and operationsOn-call, monitoring, model risk validation, compliance.Usually the biggest line.

    LLM API costs in plain terms

    Hosted LLMs charge per million tokens, input and output separately, with output several times dearer. A token is about three-quarters of an English word; Arabic often needs more, so the same document can cost more in Arabic (M1.1). Cost per request is just input tokens times the input price plus output tokens times the output price. Repeated input such as a system prompt can be cached at a fraction of the price, and work that can wait hours is commonly about half price on batch endpoints.

    Four things matter more than the price list.

    • Context length is the hidden multiplier. 8,000 tokens of retrieved passages per call costs five times the same call with 1,500. Cutting wasted context is usually the cheapest saving (M1.3).
    • Agents multiply calls. An eight-step loop re-sends a growing context each time, so budget per task with a hard cap on steps and spend (M4.1).
    • Route by difficulty. A small model does classification and extraction; the expensive one gets the hard 10%. Routing, caching and batching together often cut a bill by over half (A15, M2.5).
    • Self-hosting is a step function. You buy whole GPUs sized for the peak. Below a certain volume the API is cheaper; above it, owned GPUs win if you can keep them busy and staffed. Residency rules may force self-hosting regardless (P4.4).

    To make the estimate honest, use a unit the business recognises (AED per customer scored, per case summarised) and give a range for volume and tokens. Check it against the first month's bill. The benefit side is A16.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Operational failures rarely look like ML failures. Each has a quick check.

    • A stale or partial upstream table scored as fresh

      The job succeeds and the file looks normal, but half the customers have last week's balances.

      Spot itFreshness and row-count checks before scoring; compare tonight's score distribution with last week's.
    • Contacting people who opted out

      Eligibility was computed at the start; consent changed before the send.

      Spot itAsk when consent was last read relative to the send.
    • Non-idempotent re-runs

      After a crash the job re-runs and customers get two offers, or the control group is redrawn.

      Spot itRun it twice in test. Row counts and the control list must match.
    • Lift claimed without a control group

      "Contacted customers bought at 6%", with no comparison to similar customers left alone.

      Spot itAsk for the holdout response rate. No holdout means the effect is unknown.
    • Correlated fallbacks and silent slides

      The backup shares a dependency with the primary, or the rule tier quietly serves 15% of traffic for three weeks.

      Spot itDraw each rung's dependencies and switch one off to test. Is share of decisions by rung on an alerting dashboard?
    • Budgeting from a test prompt, or leaving out people

      The estimate used a 300-token demo prompt and only GPU and API lines. Production carries far more text, agents loop, and validation and on-call are missing.

      Spot itAsk for token distribution from realistic traffic, a spend cap per task, and count the lines: no operations or model risk means a floor, not an estimate.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A response is worth AED 250 of margin and a contact costs AED 5. Roughly where does the break-even probability sit, and why is "contact above 0.5" wrong?

    Contact while expected margin beats the contact cost: p above 5 / 250, which is 2%. A cut of 0.5 would throw away most profitable customers.

    2. The top decile responds at 6.5% and the whole base at 2%. What does that lift tell you, and what is it silent about?

    Responders are found at about three times a random list's rate, so the model ranks well. It says nothing about cause: you also need the holdout rate and a forward-in-time test.

    3. Why pass the run date into the job and seed the control draw with it?

    So a re-run reproduces the same output. A clock date changes on a retry after midnight, and an unseeded draw moves customers between control and treated.

    4. Why do three services that are each 99.9% available give a worse system than any one of them?

    Any one failing breaks the chain, so the downtime adds up: about two hours a month instead of 43 minutes. Shared causes make it worse.

    5. Why can a light-model fallback be useless on the day the main model fails, even if it is rarely down on its own?

    If both share a feature store or network path, one fault takes both down. Failures that are rare alone become common together; in the simulation a 60% shared cause lifts the lower-rung share about thirty-fold.

    6. A payments screening service is unreachable. Fail open or closed?

    For sanctions or AML, not open: hold or queue payments for review. For card fraud, blocking everything is its own outage, so a rule tier usually passes low-risk items. It is a business and risk decision, written down in advance.

    7. Which three inputs move an LLM API bill most, and which one do people forget?

    Request volume, tokens per request (input and output, output dearer) and model tier. People forget that context length and agent loops multiply tokens, so a demo prompt understates the real bill.

    8. Why might a cost estimate from a demo prompt be wrong by an order of magnitude?

    Production prompts add retrieved context and history; agent loops re-send growing context each step; retries and judge-model evaluations add calls. Estimate from realistic traffic, use a high percentile, cap spend per task.
