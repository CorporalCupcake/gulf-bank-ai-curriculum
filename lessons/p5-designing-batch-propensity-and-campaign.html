    Designing Batch Scoring Systems

    Part 1

    What a batch scoring system is

    In one sentenceA batch scoring system is a scheduled job that scores every customer in one go (usually overnight) and saves the results for other systems to read the next morning.

    Take a bank that wants to offer a savings product. At 2 a.m. a job scores all 500,000 customers on how likely each is to say yes, picks who to contact, and writes a table. By 8 a.m. the SMS, app and branch tools read that table. Nobody watches it run, so it has to be built to fail safely.

    Think of a bakery. The recipe (the model) is the easy part. The system is the schedule, the check that the ingredients arrived, and the plan for when the oven breaks.

    How it differs from real-time scoring

    • Batch: scores everyone on a schedule. Results are up to a day old, but it is cheap and easy to test, audit and fix.
    • Real-time: scores one customer the moment they act (a card payment, a login). Answers are fresh, but it needs an always-on service with strict speed limits.

    Batch is the default. Add real-time only for decisions that cannot wait until morning.

    Part 2

    How the nightly job works

    1. Snapshot. Pick an as-of date and read every table as it stood on that date. Pass the date in; never use "today", or a re-run gives different answers.
    2. Remove ineligible customers. Drop closed accounts, marketing opt-outs, vulnerable customers and anyone on a sanctions or fraud hold. Check again after scoring, from a fresh consent table.
    3. Build features and check them. Compare row counts and missing values with yesterday. If a key table is late or half empty, stop. A wrong score is worse than no score.
    4. Score. Apply the registered model to each eligible customer. Pin the model version and write it into the output.
    5. Decide. Turn scores into actions: who gets contacted, within contact limits. Keep this step separate from the model, because the rules change weekly and the model changes quarterly.
    6. Publish safely. Write to a staging table, validate it, then swap it in, keyed by run date. Running the job twice must leave the same result, never doubled rows. Seeding any random choice with the date means a re-run picks the same customers.
    7. Monitor. Alert on the score distribution moving, on volumes and on freshness, not only on crashes. A job that runs "successfully" on stale data is the common failure.

    Typical boring failures: a late upstream table, a file with the wrong date, an opted-out customer contacted anyway.

    Part 3

    Scores, cut-offs and how to read them

    Rule of thumbContact a customer when chance of responding × value of a response is more than the cost of the contact.
    A response is worth AED 400, a contact costs AED 12
    Break-even is 12 ÷ 400 = 3%. Contact anyone scoring above 3%, far below the "above 0.5" many people assume.
    The average response rate is 2%
    Only the top fifth or so of customers may clear 3%, so the list is a slice of the base, not everyone.
    Lift 3 in the top 10%
    Lift is the response rate in the contacted group divided by the overall rate. Lift 3 means you find responders at three times the rate of a random list. Lift 1 is no better than random.
    A "3%" customer who really responds at 1%
    The rule now loses money. The cut-off needs calibrated scores, meaning a 3% score really does mean about 3 in 100.

    Three design choices that matter

    • Propensity is not uplift. A model that finds likely savers also finds people who would have saved anyway. Hold back a random 5 to 10% of every list as a control group, never contact them, and compare responses weeks later.
    • Capacity and contact rules bind first. A call centre can ring only so many people a day, and each customer may get at most two marketing messages a month. Rank, fill capacity, apply limits, and let one arbiter settle overlapping campaigns.
    • Fix the label window first. "Took the product within 30 days of the message" is defined before training. Store the model version and score date with every contact so responses join back correctly.
    Part 4

    When batch is the right choice

    ApproachFreshnessCost and effortUse it when
    BatchHours to a day oldLow. Easy to test and re-run.Campaigns, credit limits, churn lists
    Real-timeSecondsHigh. Always-on, strict speed limits.Card fraud, live decisions
    BothMixedHighestNightly base score, real-time adjustment on top

    Batch is strong at:

    • Low cost and simple operations.
    • Easy audit: one table per day, with a model version.
    • Easy repair: fix the bug and re-run the date.

    Batch is weak at:

    • Stale answers: a customer who changed overnight is scored on yesterday.
    • Silent failure: nobody is watching at 2 a.m., so monitoring must do the watching.
    Show the formula

    Campaign profit = (value per response × responders reached) − (cost per contact × customers contacted). Moving down the ranked list adds contacts that are less and less likely to respond, so profit rises, peaks, then falls. Contacting everyone can lose money.

    In banking, consent and channel rules differ by product and jurisdiction, so the consent table is the source of truth and compliance should confirm which central bank marketing rules apply. For Islamic products, the model scores interest and a separate approved process decides what may be offered.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Using "today" inside the job

      A re-run on another day reads different data, so results cannot be reproduced or audited. Pass the as-of date in.

    • Appending instead of replacing

      A re-run doubles that day's rows and customers get contacted twice. Replace the day's output by run date.

    • Only alerting on crashes

      The job can finish happily on stale or half-empty data. Alert on row counts, freshness and the score distribution.

    • Cutting at 0.5 by habit

      The right cut-off comes from value, cost and capacity, not from the middle of the scale.

    1. A response is worth AED 200 and a contact costs AED 10. What is the break-even score?

    10 ÷ 200 = 5%. Contact customers scoring above that, if the scores are calibrated.

    2. Why pass the date into the job instead of reading "today"?

    So a re-run reproduces the same output and any day can be rebuilt or audited.

    3. Why hold back a control group?

    To measure what the campaign caused. Without it you cannot tell responders from people who would have bought anyway.

    4. Row counts are 40% below yesterday. What should the job do?

    Stop and alert. Scoring on a partial table gives wrong answers that look normal.

    5. When is batch the wrong choice?

    When the decision cannot wait, such as blocking a fraudulent card payment as it happens.
