    Batch Scoring, Fallbacks and Cost: Designing AI Systems That Run Every Day

    How a nightly propensity and campaign scoring job is really built, what should happen when the model or an API is down, and how to turn tokens, GPUs and storage into a budget a finance team will believe.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA production AI system is a model plus a schedule, a contract on what it outputs, a plan for the day a part breaks, and a price per decision; design all four on purpose, starting from what a wrong or missing answer costs the business.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most of the machine learning that earns money in a retail bank is not glamorous. Every night at 2 a.m. a job reads the customer tables, scores 500,000 people for how likely each is to take a personal finance offer, a card upgrade or a savings product, and writes a file. By 8 a.m. the SMS system, the app and the branch dashboards read that file. Nobody watches it run. It is the workhorse of retail analytics, and it fails in boring ways: a late upstream table, a score file with the wrong date, a customer who opted out and was contacted anyway.

    Three questions turn a notebook into a system.

    • What decision does the score drive, and how many can we act on? A score is not an outcome. Contacting everyone above 0.5 is almost never right; the right cut comes from the value of a response, the cost of a contact and the capacity of the call centre (P5.3).
    • What happens when a part breaks? A model server, a feature lookup, an external LLM API, a nightly job. Every dependency fails sometimes. The design question is which answer the customer or analyst gets instead, and whether that answer errs safe or errs open (P5.4).
    • What does one decision cost, and what will it cost at ten times the volume? Tokens, GPU hours, storage and the people who keep it alive. The budget is arithmetic, and most of it can be done on one page before anyone writes code (P5.5).

    These three sit under the same discipline: write down the failure and cost of each choice before choosing. The fraud-scoring design in P5.1–P5.2 is the real-time sibling of this lesson; here we cover the batch case, and the shared machinery for breakage and money.

    Hold onto thisBatch is the default until latency forces otherwise. It is cheaper, easier to test, easier to audit and easier to fix. Add a real-time path only for the decisions that cannot wait for tomorrow morning, and give every path a fallback and a price.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Designing batch propensity and campaign scoring (P5.3)

    Start with the shape of the pipeline. A nightly scoring job has about seven stages, and each one has its own way to go wrong.

    StageWhat happensDesign rule
    1. SnapshotFix an as-of date and read each source table as it stood at that date.Same point-in-time discipline as F3.1. Training and scoring must build features the same way, ideally from one feature definition (P1).
    2. EligibilityRemove people who must not be contacted: closed accounts, opt-outs, deceased, under-18s, vulnerable-customer flags, recent complaints, sanctions or fraud holds.Filter before scoring and again after, from a fresh consent table. Consent can change between 2 a.m. and 10 a.m.
    3. Feature buildCompute behaviour features from transactions, balances and products.Validate row counts and null rates against yesterday. Stop the run if a key table is empty or stale.
    4. ScoreApply the registered model version to every eligible customer.Pin the model version and write it into the output. Never "latest".
    5. DecideTurn scores into actions: who gets which offer, in what order, within contact limits.Keep this separate from the model. Business rules change weekly; the model changes quarterly.
    6. PublishWrite the result to a table or file the channels read, with run date, model version and a row count.Make it idempotent: re-running the same day replaces that day's output, never doubles it.
    7. MonitorCheck score distribution, volumes, freshness and, weeks later, actual responses.Alert on a score distribution that moved (F7.7), not only on a job that crashed.

    Score, then decide: the cut is an economic choice

    A propensity model gives each customer a probability p of responding. Contacting someone is worth it when the expected value beats the cost: p × value > cost, so contact when p exceeds cost ÷ value. With a response worth AED 400 of margin and a contact costing AED 12 (SMS, agent minutes, offer handling), the break-even probability is 3%. That is far below "above 0.5", and it is why campaign lists often include a fifth of the base even when the average response rate is 2%.

    The standard picture is the cumulative gains curve: sort customers by score and ask what share of all responders sits in the top 10%, 20% and so on. The lift at a given depth is the response rate in the contacted group divided by the overall rate. A model with lift 3 in the top decile finds responders at three times the rate of a random list. Lift and gains are the language campaign managers use; they are the precision and recall of F7 at a chosen depth.

    Three design decisions hide behind that curve.

    • Propensity is not uplift. A model that finds people likely to buy a savings product also finds people who would have bought it without any message. Marketing spend on them is wasted, and for some products contact irritates. Uplift models estimate the change in response caused by contact, and need a randomised holdout to train (F9.2). The cheap, universal safeguard is a standing control group: hold back a random 5–10% of every list, never contact them, and compare responses weeks later. Without it the bank cannot say what the campaign caused (F3.6).
    • Capacity and contact policy bind before the model does. A call centre that can ring 8,000 people a day, a rule of no more than two marketing messages per customer per month, different fatigue rules for SMS and push. Treat these as constraints in the decide stage: rank by expected value, then fill the capacity, then apply the contact limits. Overlapping campaigns compete for the same customer; one arbiter ("next best action") should choose, not each campaign team.
    • Labels arrive late, and the target needs a window. "Took the product within 30 days of the message" is a definition that must be fixed before training. A customer scored last night will not have a label for a month. Evaluate on past campaigns, forward in time, and keep the model version and score date with every contact so responses can later be joined back correctly (B1).

    For a Gulf bank two further constraints are routine. Marketing consent and channel rules differ by product and jurisdiction; the consent table, not the model team, is the source of truth, and I would not assume details of CBUAE or SAMA marketing rules without asking compliance. And for Islamic products, offers must be framed and priced within the product's Sharia-approved terms; the model scores interest, a separate approved process decides what may be offered.

    Making a nightly job safe to re-run

    The single most useful property of a batch job is that running it twice changes nothing. In practice: write to a staging table, validate it, then swap it in atomically, keyed by run date.

    def run(as_of):                       # as_of = a date, passed in, never "today"
    elig   = eligible_customers(as_of)           # consent, status, holds
    feats  = build_features(elig, as_of)         # point-in-time, same code as training
    check(feats, min_rows=400_000, max_null=0.02)  # stop here if the inputs look wrong
    scores = model_v17.predict_proba(feats)[:, 1]
    out    = decide(elig, scores, capacity=8_000, control_share=0.05, seed=as_of)
    write_staging(out, run_date=as_of, model="v17")
    validate_staging(as_of)                      # counts, score distribution vs yesterday
    swap_into_live(as_of)                        # replaces that date, never appends twice

    Note seed=as_of: the control group is chosen with a seeded random draw, so a re-run picks the same customers. Otherwise a re-run quietly moves people between control and treated.

    Fallbacks, rate limits and graceful degradation (P5.4)

    Every box in your architecture has an availability below 100%. A managed LLM API may publish a target such as 99.9% (about 43 minutes of downtime a month); your own feature store, model server and network add to it. Dependencies in a chain multiply: three components at 99.9% each give about 99.7%, or around two hours down per month. You cannot remove failure. You can choose what the system does next. That is graceful degradation: lose quality in steps, not all at once.

    The building blocks are few and each is worth knowing by name.

    • Timeouts. Every call to something else gets a deadline, shorter than the caller's own deadline. An unbounded wait is how one slow dependency takes down everything upstream.
    • Retries with exponential backoff and jitter. Retry transient errors, wait longer each time, and add randomness so a thousand clients do not retry in lockstep. Retry only operations that are idempotent or carry an idempotency key; retrying "send SMS" without one sends it twice. Cap the number of attempts, because retries multiply load on a service that is already struggling (AWS Builders' Library; Google SRE book).
    • Rate limits and queues. Providers limit requests and tokens per minute. A token bucket on your side keeps you under the limit: tokens refill at a steady rate, each call spends one, and a burst can use the saved-up bucket. Beyond that, queue the work (batch jobs can wait) or shed it (low-priority traffic first). A 429 "too many requests" response is information, not an error to retry immediately.
    • Circuit breakers. After a run of failures, stop calling the broken dependency for a while and go straight to the fallback. This saves time and stops you hammering a service as it recovers (Nygard, Release It!).
    • A fallback ladder. Ordered answers from best to safest: the main model; a smaller or simpler model with fewer features; the last good batch score cached from last night; a conservative rule. The last rung must run in your own process with no external call, otherwise it is not a fallback.
    def score(txn, deadline_ms=120):
    for tier in [primary, light_model, cached_score, rule]:   # best to safest
        if breaker[tier].open():              # recently failing: skip it
            continue
        try:
            return tier(txn, timeout=deadline_ms / 1000)     # every call has a deadline
        except (Timeout, Unavailable):
            breaker[tier].record_failure()
    return SAFE_DEFAULT                       # never raise to the customer

    Fail open or fail closed: a business decision

    When the last rung is reached, someone must decide the default. For a marketing score, fail closed is easy: send nothing tonight. For a card payment fraud score, blocking every transaction when the model is down would be a self-inflicted outage, so the rule tier typically lets low-value payments through and challenges high-value or unusual ones. For sanctions or AML screening, failing open can be a compliance breach, so the system should queue the payment for review instead (B2.2). The model team proposes, but the business owner and risk function sign off on each default, and it is written down before launch, not discovered at 3 a.m.

    For LLM features there is one more rung: the answer you do not generate. A staff copilot that cannot reach its model can show the search results and the policy document link instead of a summary, or say plainly that it is unavailable. A confidently wrong answer from a cheaper fallback model is worse than an honest "not available" for anything involving customers' money (M4.5).

    The correlation setting is the lesson. A fallback only helps if it fails for different reasons than the thing it replaces. Two models that both read from the same online feature store go down together. Two LLM providers reached through the same cloud region and the same gateway share more than their brand. The best fallbacks are the dull ones: a cached score computed last night, a rule that runs in your own process. Practise by switching off the dependency in a test environment and watching what the customer sees. Untested fallbacks are the ones that fail on the day.

    Two more habits. Monitor how often each rung is used, so a quiet migration to the rule tier is noticed as an incident and not discovered in a quarterly review. And record which rung produced each decision, because the validator will ask how many fraud decisions last quarter were made by the rule and not by the model (G3.4).

    Cost estimation for AI systems (P5.5)

    Cost estimation is a one-page spreadsheet. The mistake is not getting the arithmetic wrong; it is leaving a whole line out. A realistic budget has six lines.

    LineWhat drives itRule of thumb
    Model inferenceTokens in and out per request (LLM API), or GPU-hours (self-hosted), or CPU time (classic models).Classic tabular models are almost free to score: pennies per million predictions. LLM calls cost fractions of a cent to several cents each.
    Retrieval and storageVector index size, documents stored, logs and traces retained, backups.A million 1,024-number float32 vectors take about 4 GB before index overhead. Logs of full prompts grow faster than anyone expects.
    Data movementEgress between clouds or to on-premises, cross-region copies.Small per GB, large in a hybrid design that ships data both ways every day (P4).
    Evaluation and testingRegression suites, LLM-as-judge runs, red-teaming, shadow traffic.Often 10–30% of inference cost during development. A judge model scoring every output can double the bill.
    Training and tuningRetraining cadence, hyperparameter search, fine-tuning runs.One-off per release; multiply by how often you retrain.
    People and operationsOn-call, monitoring, model risk validation, security review, compliance.Usually the biggest line. A system that needs one engineer a quarter costs more than its GPUs.

    LLM API arithmetic

    Most hosted LLMs charge per million tokens, separately for input and output, with output several times dearer. A token is roughly three-quarters of an English word; Arabic often needs more tokens per word, so the same document can cost more in Arabic than English (M1.1). The per-request cost is:

    cost per request=Tin·Pin+Tout·Pout1,000,000

    T are tokens per request and P the price per million. Prompt caching lowers the input term: tokens that repeat across requests (system prompt, policy text) are billed at a fraction of the normal rate. Batch endpoints, for work that can wait hours, are commonly discounted by about half. Example: 1,500 input and 300 output tokens at an illustrative USD 3 and USD 15 per million is 0.0045 + 0.0045 = USD 0.009, about AED 0.033 a request. Multiply by 50,000 requests a day for 30 days and the bill is about AED 50,000 a month, before caching.

    Four things matter more than the price list.

    • Context length is the hidden multiplier. Retrieval that stuffs 8,000 tokens of passages into every call costs five times the same call with 1,500. Cutting wasted context is usually the cheapest saving (M1.3).
    • Agents multiply calls. An agent loop of eight steps re-sends a growing context each time. Cost grows faster than the step count. Budget per task, not per call, and set a hard cap on steps and spend (M4.1).
    • Route by difficulty. A small model handles classification and extraction; the expensive one handles the hard 10%. Routing, caching and batching together often cut a bill by more than half, which is why a central gateway that meters and enforces budgets pays for itself (A15, M2.5).
    • Self-hosting is a step function. You buy whole GPUs, sized for the peak, and need at least two for resilience. Below a certain volume the API is cheaper because you pay only for what you use. Above it, owned or reserved GPUs win, provided you can keep them busy and staff them. Data residency and sovereignty rules may force self-hosting regardless of price (P4.4). Quantisation lets a model fit on fewer GPUs (P3).

    Two ways to make the estimate honest. First, work from a unit the business recognises: AED per customer scored, per case summarised, per transaction screened. A figure like "AED 0.03 per statement query" can be compared with the analyst time it saves; a monthly total cannot. Second, give a range, not a point: low, expected and high volume, and the same for tokens per request, because real prompts run longer than test prompts. Then check the estimate against the first month's bill and keep a dashboard of cost per task. When the benefit side is also needed, that is the job of A16.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Operational failures rarely look like machine learning failures. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • A stale or partial upstream table scored as if fresh

      The job succeeds, the file looks normal, and half the customers have last week's balances.

      Spot itAsk for freshness and row-count checks before scoring, and compare tonight's score distribution with last week's.
    • Contacting people who opted out

      Eligibility computed once at the start, consent changed before the send.

      Spot itAsk when consent was last read relative to when messages go out. Test with a customer who opts out mid-run.
    • Non-idempotent re-runs

      The job is re-run after a crash and customers get two offers, or the control group is redrawn.

      Spot itRun it twice in a test environment. Row counts and the control list should be identical.
    • Training and scoring features built differently

      The model was trained on a clean extract and scored on a new pipeline with a different definition of "active in last 90 days".

      Spot itCompare a feature's distribution in training with the live scoring table. Large gaps in means or null rates are the sign.
    • Measuring lift without a control group

      "Contacted customers bought at 6%", with no comparison to similar customers who were not contacted.

      Spot itAsk what the holdout group's response rate was. If there is none, the campaign effect is unknown.
    • Correlated fallbacks

      The backup model reads the same feature store. When it fails, both go.

      Spot itDraw the dependency graph for each rung. Any shared box is a shared failure. Test by switching it off.
    • Retry storms

      Clients retry instantly and in unison against a service that is slow, turning a blip into an outage.

      Spot itLook for retries with no backoff or jitter, no attempt cap, and retries stacked at several layers (client, gateway, SDK).
    • A silent drift to the fallback

      The rule tier quietly serves 15% of traffic for three weeks.

      Spot itIs the share of decisions by rung on a dashboard with an alert? If not, you cannot know.
    • Budgeting from a test prompt

      The estimate used a 300-token demo prompt. Production prompts carry 4,000 tokens of retrieved text, an agent loops eight times and the bill is 30 times higher.

      Spot itAsk for the token distribution from real or realistic traffic, with a high percentile, and a spend cap per task.
    • Leaving out people and review

      A cost case with only GPU and API lines, which ignores validation, on-call and compliance work.

      Spot itCount lines. If operations and model risk are not on the page, the number is a floor, not an estimate.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The model produces a ranked list each night. How far down we go depends on what an accepted offer is worth against what a contact costs, and on how many calls the centre can make. We keep a small group we never contact, so in six weeks we can say how many sales the campaign caused, not just how many happened. If a part of the system fails, it falls back to last night's list rather than stopping, and we track how often that happens.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Nightly job, pinned model version, point-in-time snapshot and consent re-checked at publish. Output is idempotent and keyed by run date, with a seeded control draw. Scores are calibrated so the cut comes from cost over value, then capacity and contact limits are applied as constraints. Each external call has a timeout, bounded jittered retries and a breaker, with a fallback ladder whose rungs fail independently, and we log which rung served each decision. Cost is reported per task as a range, including evaluation and operations.”
    “Why not just contact everyone with a score above 0.5?”

    Because 0.5 is not tied to anything. The right cut is where expected value equals cost: with AED 400 of margin and AED 12 per contact, it is a probability of 3%. That might be the top 20% of customers even when the average response is 2%. It also needs calibrated scores, so I'd check calibration before using that rule, and then apply capacity and contact limits.

    “How do we know the campaign worked?”

    Compare contacted customers with a randomly held-out group scored the same way. Responders in the contacted group include people who would have bought anyway; the holdout tells us how many. Without it, high propensity looks like success even if the campaign did nothing.

    “What happens if the LLM provider is down during month-end?”

    Depends on the feature. For a staff copilot we degrade to search results and the source document with a clear notice, and queue non-urgent summaries. For anything customer-facing and consequential we do not substitute a weaker generated answer silently. Each default is agreed with the business owner and risk beforehand, calls have timeouts and a breaker, and we test the outage path rather than assume it.

    “Can we have a second model as a backup?”

    Yes, if it fails for different reasons. A backup that shares the feature store, network path or cloud region goes down with the primary. A cached score from last night, or a rule that runs in-process, is often a better second line. I'd map the dependencies and test with the shared component switched off.

    “What will this cost?”

    I'd give a unit cost and a range: for example AED 0.02–0.06 per request at expected volumes, from tokens per request measured on realistic prompts, then volume low, expected and high. The total includes evaluation runs, storage, and the operating team, which is usually the largest part. I'd also state where self-hosting becomes cheaper than the API and whether residency rules make that moot.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A response is worth AED 250 of margin and a contact costs AED 5. Above what calibrated probability should you contact someone?

    Contact when p x 250 > 5, so p > 5 / 250 = 2%.

    2. Top decile has a response rate of 6.5%; the whole base responds at 2%. What is the lift, and what else do you need to know before celebrating?

    Lift = 6.5 / 2 = 3.25. You also need to know how many of those would have responded without contact (the holdout rate), and whether the model was evaluated forward in time on past campaigns.

    3. Why do we pass the run date into the job and seed the control draw with it?

    So a re-run reproduces the same output. A date taken from the clock changes on a retry after midnight, and an unseeded draw moves customers between control and treated, which corrupts the experiment and may contact people who were meant to be held out.

    4. Three services in series each have 99.9% availability. What is the combined figure, and the monthly downtime?

    0.999 cubed is about 0.997, so 99.7%. In a 30-day month (43,200 minutes) that is about 130 minutes down. This assumes independent failures; shared causes make it worse.

    5. The main model is down 5% of the time and the light model 2% of the time on its own, independently. What share of requests reach the cached score or the rule?

    Both models must be down: 0.05 x 0.02 = 0.1%. With a 60% shared cause, P(light down | main down) = 0.6 + 0.4 x 0.02 = 0.608, so the share becomes 0.05 x 0.608 = 3.0%, thirty times more.

    6. A payments screening service is unreachable. Should the default fail open or fail closed?

    For sanctions or AML screening, not open: payments that cannot be screened should be held or queued for review. For card fraud, blocking everything is its own outage, so a rule tier usually lets low-risk items through. It is a business and risk decision, written down in advance.

    7. 20,000 requests a day, 2,000 input and 400 output tokens, USD 3 and USD 15 per million, no caching. Monthly cost in AED?

    Per request: 2,000 x 3 / 1e6 = 0.006 plus 400 x 15 / 1e6 = 0.006, so USD 0.012. Per month: 0.012 x 20,000 x 30 = USD 7,200. At 3.6725: about AED 26,440.

    8. Why might a cost estimate based on a demo prompt be wrong by an order of magnitude?

    Production prompts add retrieved context, history and instructions; agent loops re-send growing context on every step; retries and judge-model evaluations add calls. Estimate from realistic traffic, use a high percentile, and cap spend per task.
