    Precision, Recall, Thresholds and Calibration

    Part 1

    What these four ideas are

    In one sentenceA classifier gives each case a score; you pick a threshold above which it says "yes", then measure the result with precision and recall, and check that the score can be trusted as a probability (calibration).

    Our running example is a spam filter. It reads each email and gives it a score from 0 (surely fine) to 1 (surely spam).

    The model only ranks emails from least to most spammy. The threshold (here 0.5) is a separate choice, made by people, and it decides what happens to each email.

    How it differs from accuracy

    • Accuracy gives one number for all mistakes, as if a lost real email and a spam in your inbox were the same.
    • Precision and recall split the mistakes in two, so you can see which kind you are making and trade one for the other.
    • Calibration asks a different question again: not "is the ranking good" but "when it says 80%, is it right 80% of the time?"
    Part 2

    Precision and recall

    Suppose 200 emails arrive and 40 are spam. At a threshold of 0.5 the filter sorts them into four boxes:

    1. Recall: of all real spam, how much did we catch? 32 out of 40 is 80%.
    2. Precision: of everything we flagged, how much really was spam? 32 out of 44 is 73%.
    3. They pull against each other. A lower threshold lifts recall and usually drags precision down. A higher one does the opposite.
    4. So never quote one alone. "Recall 99%" is easy: flag everything.

    Two similar-sounding numbers. The false positive rate is the share of real emails flagged (12 of 160, or 7.5%). It is not precision. F1 blends precision and recall into one number but treats both mistakes as equally bad, so if someone quotes it, ask "at what threshold?" and "do the two mistakes really cost the same?"

    Why rare events surprise people

    Precision depends on how common the thing is. Suppose spam were only 1 email in 500, with the same filter quality: it catches 80% of spam and wrongly flags 1% of real mail. For every 500 emails it catches 0.8 spam but flags about 5 real ones, so precision is about 14%. Most alerts are false even though the filter is 99% accurate on real mail.

    Always test at the real rate, and ask "out of 100 alerts, how many are real?"

    Part 3

    Choosing the threshold

    Three things set the line:

    • Cost of a miss: spam in the inbox is an annoyance. For fraud it can be a large loss.
    • Cost of a false alarm: a lost email, or a blocked customer and an analyst's time.
    • Capacity: if a team can review only 700 alerts a day, the threshold is whatever gives 700.

    Worked reading. Say a miss costs 1,800 and a review costs 30. Flag a case whenever its chance of being bad, times 1,800, is above 30. That is a chance above 30 ÷ 1,800, about 1 in 60. A threshold of 1.7% sounds absurdly low, yet it is right if the numbers are right.

    Choose the threshold on a validation set, never the test set. Re-choose it when the rate, costs or team size change. Many banks use bands instead of one line: block above a high score, ask for a one-time passcode in the middle, and pass the rest.

    ROC and precision-recall curves

    Sliding the threshold from 0 to 1 traces a curve. The ROC curve plots recall against the false positive rate, and its area (AUC) is the chance that a random spam email scores above a random real one: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. The precision-recall curve plots precision against recall.

    • ROC ignores how common the positives are. Good for comparing how well models rank in general.
    • Precision-recall reflects it. Better when positives are rare and someone has to work the alerts.
    • Neither tells you where to put the threshold. Costs and capacity do.
    Part 4

    Calibration: can you trust the number?

    In one sentenceA model is calibrated when its probabilities mean what they say, like a weather forecast: on days it says 30% rain, it rains on about 30% of them.

    To check, group emails by predicted probability and compare with what really happened. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal. Most real models do not.

    How to read it

    • Below the diagonal: overconfident. The model says 90% but is right 70% of the time. Flexible models like boosted trees and neural networks often do this.
    • Calibration and ranking are separate. Halve every probability and the ranking, and so the AUC, is unchanged, but the numbers are now wrong.
    • It matters when the number itself is used: expected loss, a fixed cut-off such as 1.7%, pricing, or blending with another model. If you only rank, it matters less.

    Common causes: overconfident flexible models, resampled training data (see the imbalanced-data lesson), and a changed rate in live data.

    Common fixes, all fitted on recent held-out data and none of which changes the ranking:

    • Temperature scaling: shrinks all confidence by one number. Cures general over- or under-confidence.
    • Platt scaling: fits a small S-curve from score to probability.
    • Isotonic regression: a flexible staircase. Needs plenty of data.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    MeasureAnswersStrong atWeak at
    AccuracyHow often right overall?Balanced classesUseless when positives are rare
    Precision and recallWhat kind of mistakes, at this threshold?Rare events, alert workloadsChange with the threshold
    ROC-AUCHow well does it rank overall?Comparing modelsIgnores how common positives are
    Precision-recall curveWhat will the alert queue look like?Rare positivesDepends on the rate, so quote the rate too
    Calibration plotCan I trust the probability?Cost-based decisionsSays nothing about ranking

    In practice: compare models with AUC or average precision, choose the threshold from costs or capacity, report precision and recall there, and check calibration if anyone will use the probability itself.

    Show the formulas

    Precision = caught ÷ flagged. Recall = caught ÷ all real positives. Flag when chance of positive > cost of a review ÷ cost of a miss.

    In banking, the fraud desk's review capacity, the cost of a missed fraud and calibrated probabilities (also needed for IFRS 9 expected-loss figures) together decide the threshold.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting one number alone

      Precision without recall, or recall without precision, can be made to look perfect. Quote both, at a named threshold.

    • Leaving the threshold at 0.5

      0.5 is a default, not a decision. Set it from costs or capacity.

    • Testing on a balanced sample

      Precision at 50% positives says nothing about live precision. Use the real rate.

    • Assuming a good AUC means good probabilities

      AUC only checks ranking. Check a reliability plot before using the numbers.

    1. A filter flags 50 emails, 40 are really spam, and there were 80 spam in total. What are precision and recall?

    Precision 40 ÷ 50 = 80%. Recall 40 ÷ 80 = 50%.

    2. You raise the threshold. What usually happens to precision and recall?

    Precision tends to rise and recall falls: you flag fewer cases, and the ones you flag are surer.

    3. A miss costs 500 and a review costs 10. Roughly what threshold probability?

    10 ÷ 500 = 2%. Flag anything with more than a 2% chance, provided the probabilities are calibrated.

    4. A model has AUC 0.95 but the alert team is swamped. Why?

    AUC ignores how rare positives are. At a low rate even a small false-alarm rate gives mostly false alerts. Look at precision at your threshold.

    5. The model says 90% but is right 70% of the time. Does that change its AUC?

    No. It is overconfident, a calibration fault, and recalibrating does not change the ranking.
