    Precision, Recall, Thresholds and Calibration

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a card fraud desk at a Gulf bank: 200,000 transactions a day, each scored 0 to 1,000 by a model. Twelve analysts can review about 720 alerts.

    The model only sorts, like an airport scanner that beeps louder for odder bags. Genuine and fraud scores overlap, so the threshold is the score above which a transaction goes to review. That one number decides fraud caught, customers blocked, and whether analysts drown.

    Every threshold is a trade: lower it and you catch more fraud but flag more genuine customers. A better model makes the trade cheaper, never free.

    Hold onto thisThe model ranks. The business chooses the threshold. Show what each choice costs.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The four outcomes and the numbers built from them

    Each transaction lands in one of four boxes, the confusion matrix above. Think of a smoke alarm. Recall: of all real fires, how many did it catch? Precision: of all rings, how many were real?

    Don't confuse precision with the false positive rate, the share of genuine transactions flagged. A 1% rate can still mean most alerts are false. F1 blends precision and recall, but treats a miss and a false alarm as equally bad. If someone quotes it, ask: at what threshold?

    The two pull against each other. A lower threshold lifts recall and usually drags precision down; a higher one does the reverse. So never read one without the other. Accuracy is the share of all transactions classified correctly, and on rare events it is nearly useless, because predicting "genuine" every time already scores about 99.8%. Where a miss and a false alarm cost different amounts, use F-beta (F1 with a dial that weights recall more), or skip the single number and price the outcomes directly, as in the threshold section below.

    Why rare events fool almost everyone

    Precision depends on how common fraud is. A model that catches 80% of fraud and flags only 1% of genuine transactions, with fraud at 0.2% of volume, has precision near 14%. Most alerts are genuine customers, though the model is 99% accurate. Like screening for a disease 1 in 500 people have: most positives are healthy.

    The mechanism is simple. Genuine transactions outnumber fraud 500 to 1, so even a tiny false positive rate applied to a huge pool produces more false alarms than the model catches frauds. Whenever you see a low false positive rate on a rare event, ask: out of 100 alerts, how many are real? The same logic is why fraud models must be tested on data with the live fraud rate, not a convenient balanced sample.

    Watch outPrecision on a balanced test set (say 50% fraud after resampling) says nothing about live precision.

    ROC curves versus precision-recall curves

    Sweeping the threshold traces a curve. The ROC curve plots recall against false positive rate; its area, AUC, is the chance a random fraud outscores a random genuine transaction (0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect). The precision-recall curve plots precision against recall.

    ROC ignores the fraud rate; PR doesn't. PR shows what the desk will actually experience. A random model sits on the diagonal in ROC, but in PR its precision equals the fraud rate, so on rare events the PR curve has far more room to separate good models from mediocre ones. Its one-number summary is average precision.

    Use ROC and AUC to compare models' ranking power in general, or when classes are roughly balanced. Use PR and precision at your operating point when fraud is rare and someone has to work the alerts. Neither tells you where to put the threshold.

    A 0.95 AUC model can still bury the desk. Show precision and recall at the threshold you will use.

    Choosing the threshold

    Three forces set the line: the cost of a miss, the cost of an alert (analyst time plus customer friction) and capacity. Ignoring capacity, flag a transaction when its chance of fraud times the loss exceeds the cost of reviewing it. With a miss at AED 1,800 and an alert at AED 30, that is about a 1-in-60 chance. It needs true probabilities, which is calibration, below.

    The cost of an alert has two parts: analyst minutes, and friction for the genuine customer who is blocked or phoned. Friction is hard to price but not zero, and leaving it out pushes the threshold too low. Fit the threshold on validation data, never the test set, and revisit it whenever the fraud rate, the loss per fraud or the team size changes.

    When capacity binds, team size sets the threshold and the metric is precision at k: of the top k alerts you can work, how many are fraud? Banks often send the middle band to cheap automatic step-up, such as a one-time passcode.

    Calibration: can you trust the probability?

    A model is calibrated when its probabilities mean what they say, like a weather forecast: on days it says 30% rain, it rains on about 30%. A reliability diagram checks this; calibrated means on the diagonal.

    Calibration and ranking are independent: halve every probability and the AUC is unchanged. It usually breaks through overconfident flexible models, resampling that inflates probabilities, or drift in the fraud rate. Cheap fixes such as temperature scaling, Platt scaling or isotonic regression keep the ranking and are fitted on recent held-out data.

    Match the fix to the fault. Temperature scaling divides the model's log-odds by one number, which cures over- or under-confidence. Platt scaling adds a shift as well, which also cures a wrong base rate. Isotonic regression fits a free-form monotone curve, so it handles odd shapes but needs thousands of positives or it overfits.

    Check calibration from about 1% upwards, where decisions are made. In credit risk, poor calibration can misstate IFRS 9 provisions, so validators test it directly.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Precision and recall at a threshold show the real workload. They count alerts and catches directly, so the desk can see what it will be asked to do. Accuracy and ROC AUC hide this.
    • Precision-recall curves stay informative when fraud is rare. Precision uses the false alarms in its denominator, so it reacts to the fraud rate. That is why the curve moves when the base rate does.
    • Cost-based thresholds turn a score into a business decision. Pricing a miss, an alert and customer friction gives a threshold you can defend and re-run when costs change.
    • Calibration makes a score usable as a probability. Once calibrated, one cut-off can be reused across segments, and the probabilities feed provisioning, pricing and expected-loss figures.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • They depend on the fraud rate. Precision measured on a balanced or old sample does not carry over to live data. They must be rechecked after drift.
    • Labels are the ceiling. Unreported or unreviewed fraud counts as genuine, so recall looks better than it is. Chargebacks arrive weeks late, so recent data is under-labelled.
    • Counting cases ignores value. Recall by count can hide missed big tickets. Weight by AED when losses vary widely.
    • A single threshold may not fit all segments. Card-present, online and high-value customers have different base rates and costs, and one global cut-off can be wrong for each.
    • Calibration is not ranking. A perfectly calibrated model can still rank poorly, and a good ranker can be badly calibrated. Check both.

    Alternatives and how to choose

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Precision, recall at a thresholdYou have a chosen operating point and need to describe the desk's workloadYou are comparing models before any threshold exists
    ROC AUCComparing ranking power, with fairly balanced classes or a first model screenFraud is very rare, or alert volume matters
    PR curve / average precisionRare events where analysts work the alertsThe test set's fraud rate differs from production, unless you reweight
    Expected costCosts of a miss and an alert are roughly known and you must justify a thresholdCosts are guesses nobody can defend
    Brier score / calibration curveThe probability itself is used, as in provisioning or pricingOnly the ranking matters

    In practice. Report precision, recall and alerts per day at the planned threshold, plus average precision for model comparison. Choose the threshold on validation data from a cost curve, then check it against team capacity. Recalibrate on recent data every month or quarter. Evaluate out of time, and add a value-weighted view. None of this is heavy on compute; the constraint is enough recent labelled fraud.

    In banking: card and payment fraud thresholds, step-up authentication bands, AML alert tuning where false positives swamp investigators, and IFRS 9 PD calibration where validators test it directly.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Common mistakes, each with its quickest catch.

    • Quoting accuracy or AUC alone

      “99.7% accurate” means little when flagging nothing scores 99.8%. AUC says nothing about alert volume.

      Spot itAsk for precision, recall and daily alerts at the planned threshold.
    • Evaluating on resampled or balanced data

      90% precision on a 50/50 test set can fall below 10% in production, and probabilities from an undersampled model are inflated.

      Spot itCheck the evaluation fraud rate matches production.
    • Threshold tuned on the test set

      Picking the best threshold on the data you report on makes results optimistic.

      Spot itAsk which data chose the threshold. It should be validation data.
    • Counting transactions when the business counts money

      A model can catch 80% of cases but 55% of value if it misses the big tickets.

      Spot itAsk for value-weighted recall (AED).
    • Ignoring seasonal shift

      Ramadan, Eid and summer travel change spending. A threshold set in a quiet month can flood the desk in a busy one.

      Spot itAsk how alert volumes were checked across peaks.
    • Trusting labels that only exist where you looked

      Unflagged, unreported fraud has no label, so recall looks better than it is, and the next model learns the same blind spot.

      Spot itAsk whether a random sample of unflagged transactions is reviewed.
    • Fixing calibration on the data that trained the model

      The model is most overconfident on its own training data, so a calibrator fitted there learns nothing useful.

      Spot itAsk which held-out, recent data the calibrator was fitted on.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A model has 90% recall and a 2% false positive rate, and 0.1% of transactions are fraud. Will most alerts be real fraud?

    No. Genuine transactions outnumber fraud 1,000 to 1, so precision is only about 4%.

    2. Missed fraud costs AED 2,000 and a review costs AED 25. Review a transaction with a 3% chance of fraud?

    Yes, if capacity allows. Break-even is about 1 in 80.

    3. True or false: recalibrating a model improves its AUC.

    False. Calibration methods keep the ranking, and AUC depends only on ranking.

    4. The fraud rate drops from 5% to 0.5%. Which changes: the ROC curve, the PR curve, or both?

    Only the PR curve. ROC compares within each class, so it ignores balance. Precision mixes the classes, so it falls.

    5. A model was calibrated when fraud was 0.4%. Fraud is now 0.2%. What goes wrong with a probability threshold, and how do you fix it?

    Every probability is about twice too high, so too many cross the threshold. Recalibrate on recent data.

    6. Which would you choose to compare two fraud models at 0.05% fraud: ROC AUC or average precision, and why?

    Average precision, alongside AUC. At this rate, a small false positive rate still means many false alarms, which ROC hides and PR shows. Then compare precision and recall at the planned threshold.

    7. Model A has slightly higher AUC but overconfident probabilities. Model B has lower AUC but is calibrated. The bank prices decisions on probabilities. Which would you choose?

    Probably A, after recalibrating it. Temperature or Platt scaling fixes calibration without touching the ranking, so you keep A's edge. If you cannot recalibrate on recent data, B is safer.
