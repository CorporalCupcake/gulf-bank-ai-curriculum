    F7.1 – F7.4 Day one ★ Priority About 35 minutes

    Precision, Recall, Thresholds and Calibration

    How a fraud model's scores turn into decisions, why rare events fool nearly everyone, and how to pick the line that sends a transaction to review.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model only ranks transactions by risk. Where you draw the line, and whether its probabilities can be trusted, are separate decisions, and that's where most of the money is won or lost.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture the card fraud desk at a mid-sized Gulf bank. About 200,000 card transactions come through each day. A model gives each one a risk score from 0 to 1,000, much like the fraud scores card issuers already use. Twelve analysts can each review about 60 cases a day, so the desk can handle roughly 720 alerts.

    The model doesn't decide anything. It sorts. Every transaction gets a score, and genuine transactions mostly score low while fraud mostly scores high. But the two groups overlap. Some fraud looks ordinary, and some genuine customers behave strangely: a first trip to London, a large gold purchase before Eid, a new phone.

    Someone has to choose a threshold: everything above it is flagged for review, and everything below goes through. That single number decides how much fraud you catch, how many genuine customers get blocked, and whether the analysts drown.

    Spend a minute with it, and three things should stand out.

    • Every threshold is a trade. Lower it and you catch more fraud, but you also flag more genuine customers and create more work. No setting gets you all three. A better model shrinks the overlap, so the trade gets cheaper, but it never disappears.
    • Accuracy tells you nothing here. Push the threshold all the way up so nothing is flagged and accuracy reads about 99.8%. You've caught no fraud at all, yet the "accuracy" is excellent, because 99.8% of transactions were genuine anyway.
    • Rarity crushes precision. In real volumes the fraud is a sliver. Even where fraud scores are densest, genuine transactions outnumber it, so most alerts are false alarms unless the model is very good or the threshold is very high.
    Hold onto thisThe model gives you a ranking. The business chooses the threshold. Your job is to show what each threshold costs, in fraud lost, customers annoyed and analyst hours, so the choice is made deliberately rather than by default.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The four outcomes and the numbers built from them

    Once there's a threshold, every transaction lands in exactly one of four boxes: the confusion matrix from the simulation. Every classification metric is just a different ratio of these four counts. The trick is knowing which question each ratio answers.

    Recall=TPTP+FN

    Of all the fraud that happened, how much did we catch? Also called sensitivity, or the true positive rate. Business question: how much fraud gets through?

    Precision=TPTP+FP

    Of everything we flagged, how much was really fraud? Business question: is the analysts' time well spent, and how many good customers are we bothering?

    False positive rate=FPFP+TN

    Of all genuine transactions, what share did we wrongly flag? This sounds like precision's opposite but answers a different question, and confusing the two causes much of the trouble below.

    You'll also meet F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It's a convenient single number for comparing models at a fixed threshold, but it treats a missed fraud and a false alarm as equally bad, which they almost never are. When someone quotes an F1 score, ask: at what threshold, and why that one?

    Why rare events fool almost everyone

    Precision isn't a fixed property of a model. It depends on how common fraud is. Write it out with Bayes' rule and you can see why:

    Precision=R·πR·π+FPR·(1−π)

    R is recall, π is the fraud rate, FPR is the false positive rate.

    Plug in a model that sounds excellent: it catches 80% of fraud and wrongly flags only 1% of genuine transactions. With fraud at 0.2%:

    • Fraud caught: 0.8 × 0.002 = 0.0016 of all transactions
    • False alarms: 0.01 × 0.998 = 0.00998 of all transactions
    • Precision: 0.0016 ÷ (0.0016 + 0.00998) ≈ 13.8%

    So about 6 in every 7 alerts are genuine customers, even though this model is roughly 99% accurate. The 1% false positive rate is applied to a population 500 times bigger than the fraud population, and that swamps everything.

    This is the same base-rate effect behind medical screening paradoxes, and it's the most useful single idea in this lesson. Whenever someone reports a low false positive rate for a rare event, convert it to "out of every 100 alerts, how many are real?"

    Watch outA precision figure measured on a balanced test set, such as 50% fraud after resampling, says nothing about live precision. The formula shows why: change π and precision changes, even though the model is identical.

    ROC curves versus precision-recall curves

    A threshold gives you one point. Sweep the threshold from top to bottom and you get a curve, which describes the model at every possible setting at once. Two curves are standard:

    • ROC curve: recall against false positive rate. The area under it, AUC (or AUROC), has a neat meaning: the chance that a randomly chosen fraud scores higher than a randomly chosen genuine transaction. It measures ranking quality alone.
    • Precision-recall (PR) curve: precision against recall. Its area is usually summarised as average precision.

    The crucial difference: the ROC curve ignores the fraud rate, and the PR curve doesn't. Both axes of the ROC curve are rates within one class, so changing how common fraud is leaves it untouched. Precision mixes the two classes, so the PR curve shows what the fraud desk will actually experience.

    This is why an AUC of 0.95 on a fraud model can come with a fraud desk that hates it. The AUC is honest about ranking but silent about workload. For rare-event problems, show the PR curve, or better, precision and recall at the threshold you'll actually use.

    One nuance is worth knowing because it's recent. Don't conclude that the PR curve is simply better. Each answers a different question. AUC is the right tool for comparing ranking quality across populations with different fraud rates, and average precision can be distorted when you compare groups whose base rates differ. More on that in Part 4.

    Choosing the threshold

    Three forces decide where the line goes, and in a bank all three apply at once.

    • The cost of a miss. A fraud that gets through costs the stolen amount, plus chargeback handling and some reputational damage. Call it L.
    • The cost of an alert. Every flagged transaction uses analyst time, c. A flagged genuine customer adds friction, f: a declined card at the till, a call to the contact centre, sometimes a customer who stops using the card.
    • Capacity. Twelve analysts can't review 4,000 cases a day, however cheap each review is.

    Ignoring capacity for a moment, there's a clean answer. If you knew the true probability p that a transaction is fraud, you should flag it whenever the expected loss from passing it exceeds the expected cost of flagging it. Solve that and you get the break-even probability:

    p*=c+fL+f

    Flag every transaction whose fraud probability is above p*. With L = AED 1,800, c = AED 20 and f = AED 10, p* ≈ 1.7%: flag anything with even a 1-in-60 chance of being fraud.

    That's surprisingly low, and it's why fraud teams would flag far more than they can review if capacity allowed. The rule has a hidden requirement: it only works if the model's probabilities are true. A score labelled "2%" has to mean that about 2 in 100 such transactions really are fraud. That property is calibration, and it's the last piece of this lesson.

    When capacity binds, as it nearly always does, the threshold is really set by the size of the team. The metric that matters then is precision at k: of the top k alerts you can work, how many are fraud? Academic work on card fraud has argued for exactly this framing for years.

    Real banks also rarely use a single threshold. They use bands. Very high scores are declined outright, high scores go to manual review, the middle band gets an automatic step-up (a one-time passcode or 3-D Secure challenge), and everything else is approved. Step-up is the release valve: it's far cheaper than an analyst, so it lets you act on far more transactions than the team could ever review.

    Calibration: can you trust the probability?

    A model is calibrated when its probabilities mean what they say. Take every transaction the model scored at 5%: about 5% of them should turn out to be fraud. Formally:

    P(fraud∣p^=q)=qfor everyq

    You check it with a reliability diagram: group predictions into bins, then plot each bin's average predicted probability against the actual fraud rate in that bin. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal.

    Calibration and ranking are independent. A model can rank perfectly and still be badly calibrated: if it doubles every probability, the ordering is unchanged, so the AUC is identical, but every "2%" really means 1%. Most of the time, miscalibration comes from one of three places:

    • Overconfidence. Modern neural networks and boosted trees often push probabilities towards 0 and 1 more than the evidence supports.
    • Resampling. Fraud models are often trained after throwing away most genuine transactions to balance the classes. That inflates every predicted probability, sometimes massively.
    • Drift. The fraud rate changes after training (a new scam wave, a seasonal shopping peak, a new card product), so the model's sense of what's normal is out of date.

    The fixes are cheap and leave the ranking untouched. Temperature scaling divides the model's log-odds by one number, fixing over- or under-confidence. Platt scaling fits a slope and an intercept, so it fixes confidence and a shifted base rate together. Isotonic regression fits any increasing curve, which is more flexible but needs more data. All three are fitted on recent held-out data, never on the training set.

    The ticks along the bottom tell you something important. Almost every transaction has a predicted probability close to zero. The part of the curve that drives decisions, from about 1% upwards, applies to a tiny slice of traffic. That's why calibration has to be checked where the decisions happen, not averaged over everything. An average calibration error over all transactions can look tiny while the alert-generating region is badly off.

    Calibration matters well beyond fraud. In credit risk, a probability of default is multiplied by an exposure to produce expected credit loss, which sets IFRS 9 provisions on the balance sheet. A model with excellent ranking and poor calibration can misstate provisions by millions. That's why credit validators test calibration explicitly, using tests such as the binomial and Hosmer–Lemeshow tests, rather than only discrimination metrics like AUC or Gini.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These ideas run through almost every other topic in the curriculum. When you meet them elsewhere, you'll recognise the same four boxes.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rule and base ratesPrecision is Bayes' rule

    The precision formula above is Bayes' theorem with different labels. Base-rate neglect is the root of most misread metrics.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataResampling breaks probabilities

    Undersampling and SMOTE can help a model learn, but they distort its probabilities. Correct for them before using any threshold rule.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringCalibration fails first

    When the fraud rate moves, ranking often holds up while calibration drifts. Monitor both, separately.

    B1.3 · Delayed labelsYour precision is measured on a biased sample

    You only learn the truth about transactions you flagged or that were later charged back, weeks later. Missed fraud is undercounted.

    B1.4 · Alert fatigueCapacity sets the threshold

    Low precision at high volume burns analysts out, and tired analysts miss real fraud. Precision at k is a wellbeing metric too.

    B1.5 · Real-time decisionsOne score, several thresholds

    Approve, step up, review, decline: each band is a threshold choice using the same cost logic.

    B4.3 · IFRS 9Calibration is money on the balance sheet

    Probabilities of default are multiplied by exposures. Calibration error turns directly into provisioning error.

    M5.2 · LLM-as-judgeA judge is a classifier

    An LLM grading answers as pass or fail has precision, recall and calibration too, and the same base-rate traps.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionGuarantees instead of hopes

    Conformal methods give error-rate guarantees that hold even when calibration is imperfect.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core ideas are decades old and settled. The live debates are about which summary metric to trust, how to measure calibration properly, and calibration in large language models.

    1. 2001
      Cost-sensitive decisions have a closed-form threshold

      Elkan showed that with calibrated probabilities, the optimal decision threshold follows directly from the cost matrix. That's the p* rule in this lesson.

      Elkan, “The Foundations of Cost-Sensitive Learning”, IJCAI 2001
    2. 2005
      Many strong models produce distorted probabilities

      Boosted trees, SVMs and others rank well but are systematically miscalibrated. Platt scaling and isotonic regression fix this.

      Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, “Predicting Good Probabilities with Supervised Learning”, ICML 2005
    3. 2006
      ROC and PR curves are mathematically linked

      A curve dominates in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space, but areas under the two curves can rank models differently.

      Davis & Goadrich, “The Relationship Between Precision-Recall and ROC Curves”, ICML 2006
    4. 2015
      PR plots are more informative under imbalance

      A widely cited argument for PR over ROC when positives are rare.

      Saito & Rehmsmeier, PLOS ONE 2015
    5. 2015
      Correcting probabilities after undersampling

      Gives a closed-form correction for probabilities from a model trained on undersampled data, using the fraction of genuine cases kept.

      Dal Pozzolo et al., “Calibrating Probability with Undersampling for Unbalanced Classification”, IEEE SSCI 2015
    6. 2017
      Modern neural networks are overconfident

      Deeper, larger networks were found to be worse calibrated than older ones. Temperature scaling, a single parameter, fixed most of it.

      Guo et al., “On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks”, ICML 2017
    7. 2018
      Card fraud should be judged by precision of the alerts you can work

      Argued for precision-at-k given investigator capacity, and dealt with delayed and biased feedback from investigations.

      Dal Pozzolo et al., “Credit Card Fraud Detection: A Realistic Modeling and a Novel Learning Strategy”, IEEE TNNLS 2018
    8. 2019
      The standard calibration metric is biased

      Binned expected calibration error (ECE) depends on the binning and can underestimate miscalibration. Use several diagnostics, not one number.

      Kumar, Liang & Ma, “Verified Uncertainty Calibration”, NeurIPS 2019; Nixon et al., “Measuring Calibration in Deep Learning”, 2019
    9. 2022–23
      LLMs are fairly well calibrated, until post-training

      Pretrained language models' token probabilities are often reasonably calibrated. OpenAI reported that this calibration degraded after RLHF post-training of GPT-4. This is why you can't treat a chatbot's stated confidence as a probability.

      Kadavath et al., “Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know”, 2022; OpenAI, GPT-4 Technical Report, 2023
    10. 2024
      The pushback: PR isn't simply better than ROC

      Argues that AUPRC is not universally superior under class imbalance. It weights errors among the highest scores more heavily and can unfairly favour subgroups with higher base rates, a fairness concern when comparing customer segments.

      McDermott et al., “A Closer Look at AUROC and AUPRC under Class Imbalance”, NeurIPS 2024
    The current consensus, in one linePick the metric that matches the decision. Use precision and recall at the operating threshold, precision at k when capacity binds, AUC for comparing ranking across populations, and always check calibration separately wherever probabilities drive money or policy.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you'll see in real projects, and in AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Reporting accuracy on a rare event

      “The model is 99.7% accurate.” Flagging nothing scores 99.8%.

      Spot itAsk what accuracy the “flag nothing” model gets. If it's close, the number is meaningless.
    • Quoting only AUC for an operational system

      An AUC of 0.95 says nothing about how many alerts the team gets or what share are real.

      Spot itAsk for precision and recall at the planned threshold, and the alert volume per day.
    • Precision measured on resampled or balanced test data

      Precision on a 50/50 test set can look like 90%, and fall below 10% in production.

      Spot itCheck the fraud rate in the evaluation set. It must match production.
    • Threshold tuned on the test set

      Choosing the best threshold on the same data you report results on makes the results optimistic.

      Spot itAsk which data set chose the threshold. It should be a validation set, with results reported on a later, untouched period.
    • Using probabilities from an undersampled model as if they were real

      The scores are inflated, so any probability-based threshold or expected-loss figure is wrong.

      Spot itAsk whether the training data was resampled, and whether the probabilities were corrected or recalibrated afterwards.
    • Counting transactions when the business counts money

      A model can catch 80% of fraud cases but only 55% of fraud value if it misses the big-ticket ones.

      Spot itAsk for value-weighted recall (in AED) alongside count-based recall.
    • Trusting labels that only exist where you looked

      Confirmed fraud comes mostly from transactions you flagged or customers who complained. Unflagged fraud that nobody reports never gets a label, so recall looks better than it is.

      Spot itAsk how fraud labels are created, how long chargebacks take to arrive, and whether a random sample of unflagged transactions is ever reviewed.
    • Ignoring seasonal shift

      Ramadan, Eid, Dubai Shopping Festival and summer travel all change spending patterns. A threshold set in a quiet month can flood the desk in a busy one.

      Spot itAsk how performance and alert volumes were checked across seasonal peaks, using out-of-time data.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't add up

      Typical signs: an F1 score with no threshold stated, an AUC above 0.99 from a random train-test split, or text that says “precision improved” while the printed number went down.

      Spot itRecompute one precision figure by hand from the confusion matrix. If you can't find the confusion matrix, that's your answer.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The model doesn't make decisions. It ranks every transaction by risk. Where we draw the line is a business choice: lower means we catch more fraud but send more genuine customers to review. At the current setting we catch about half the fraud value with a workload our team can handle. Each extra point of fraud caught costs a predictable number of extra alerts, so the real question is whether we add analysts, or use automatic step-up checks, to move the line.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Fraud is under 1% of volume, so I'd evaluate with precision and recall at the operating point, plus precision at k for the team's capacity, rather than AUC alone. I'd set thresholds from expected cost using calibrated probabilities. The break-even probability is c plus f over L plus f. I'd check calibration with reliability diagrams on out-of-time data, recalibrate with Platt or isotonic on a recent window, and correct for any undersampling. I'd also report value-weighted recall, and account for the label bias from only investigating flagged transactions.”
    “Our fraud model is 99% accurate. Is that good?”

    Not by itself. With fraud at 0.2%, flagging nothing is 99.8% accurate. I'd want recall (how much fraud it catches, by count and by value), precision (how many alerts are real), and the alert volume against team capacity.

    “The AUC is 0.96 but the fraud team says the alerts are useless. How?”

    AUC measures ranking and ignores the fraud rate. At a 0.2% fraud rate, even a strong ranking can give low precision at the chosen threshold, so most alerts are false positives. The PR curve or precision at the operating point would have shown this. The fix might be a higher threshold, step-up authentication for the middle band, or better features to separate the overlap.

    “How would you choose the threshold?”

    Start from expected cost: loss per missed fraud, cost per review, and customer friction per false alarm. With calibrated probabilities, flag above p* = (c + f) / (L + f). Then apply the capacity constraint. If the cost-optimal threshold produces more alerts than the team can work, set it by capacity and quantify what more capacity, or automated step-up, would save. Validate on a later time period, and revisit it at seasonal peaks.

    “You undersampled genuine transactions in training. Does that matter?”

    For ranking, usually not much. For probabilities, yes. They're inflated because the model saw fraud far more often than it occurs. I'd correct them analytically using the sampling rate, or recalibrate on a recent sample with the true fraud rate, before using them for thresholds or expected-loss figures.

    “After recalibration the AUC didn't change. So did recalibration do anything?”

    Yes. Recalibration is a monotonic transformation, so the ranking and the AUC stay the same by design. What changes is that the probabilities become trustworthy, which affects cost-based thresholds, expected-loss estimates, and anything that compares scores across models or time periods.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. 100,000 transactions, 0.1% fraud. The model has 90% recall and a 2% false positive rate. What's its precision?

    Fraud: 100. Caught: 90. Genuine: 99,900. False alarms: 2% × 99,900 = 1,998. Precision = 90 ÷ (90 + 1,998) ≈ 4.3%. Fewer than 1 in 20 alerts is fraud.

    2. Missed fraud costs AED 2,000 on average, a review costs AED 25, and you ignore friction. What's the break-even probability?

    p* = (25 + 0) ÷ (2,000 + 0) = 1.25%. Any transaction with more than a 1-in-80 chance of being fraud is worth reviewing, if capacity allows.

    3. True or false: recalibrating a model improves its AUC.

    False. Calibration methods are monotonic, so they preserve the ranking, and AUC depends only on the ranking. Calibration and discrimination are separate properties.

    4. The fraud rate drops from 5% to 0.5%. Which changes: the ROC curve, the PR curve, or both?

    Only the PR curve, assuming the fraud and genuine score distributions themselves stay the same. ROC axes are rates within each class, so they ignore class balance. Precision mixes the classes, so it falls.

    5. A model was calibrated when fraud was 0.4%. Fraud is now 0.2%. You flag on a probability threshold. What goes wrong, and how do you fix it?

    The model still assumes the old base rate, so it overestimates every probability. In odds terms it's about twice too high, so more transactions cross the threshold than should. Fix it by recalibrating the intercept on recent data. Here that's a shift of about −0.69 in log-odds, the natural log of ½. Ranking is unaffected.

    6. Analysts can review 500 alerts a day, but the cost-optimal threshold produces 900. What do you do?

    Set the review threshold so the top 500 alerts go to analysts, measured by precision at 500. Then quantify the expected fraud value sitting in alerts 501–900 and compare it with the cost of more analysts. That's your business case. Also consider automated step-up authentication for that band: it acts on those transactions at a fraction of the cost of a manual review.
