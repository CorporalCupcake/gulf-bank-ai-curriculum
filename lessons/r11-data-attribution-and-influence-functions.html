    Data Attribution and Unlearning

    Part 1

    What data attribution and unlearning are

    In one sentenceData attribution finds which training rows shaped a prediction; unlearning removes chosen rows from a trained model as if they had never been there.

    Take a model that predicts house prices from 10,000 past sales. It prices one house oddly, and you ask: which past sales caused that? That is attribution. Later, one seller asks you to delete their sale from everything. Making the model forget it is unlearning.

    The cleanest definition of both is the same experiment: delete the row, retrain, and see what changes. Attribution measures the change. Unlearning is the instruction to produce the model you would have got anyway. Everything else in this lesson is a cheaper approximation to that experiment.

    How it differs from feature attribution

    • Feature attribution (SHAP and similar) says which columns pushed a prediction: floor area up, distance to the centre down.
    • Data attribution says which rows did: which past sales taught the model this price.
    • Unlearning is not deleting from a database. A trained model is like a cake: the seller's data is baked in with everyone else's, and you cannot lift it back out.
    Part 2

    How it works

    Finding the influential rows

    1. Leave one out. Retrain without the row and compare. Exact, but one full retraining per row.
    2. Influence functions. Instead of retraining, estimate the change with calculus: how hard the row pulls the model (its gradient), divided by how flat the loss surface is (the Hessian, a table of curvatures). Koh and Liang brought this to modern machine learning in 2017.
    3. Scale it up. A billion-parameter model has no usable Hessian. Approximations such as EK-FAC, TracIn (add up how the row's gradient lines up with the test case across training checkpoints) and TRAK (train many models on random subsets) give a shortlist of likely rows.
    4. Check the answer. On a small model, retrain without the top rows and confirm the prediction moves.

    Removing the rows

    1. Retrain from scratch. Exact and simple, but costs a full run.
    2. Shard the training. Train separate models on slices of the data and combine them. A deletion retrains only the slice that held the row. Exact, because the row never touched the others.
    3. One-step update. A single calculation that cancels the rows' pull. Works for small, smooth models and small deletions.
    4. Fine-tuning tricks. Push the model away from the rows (gradient ascent), or train it to refuse. Cheap, but no guarantee.
    Part 3

    How to read the results

    Influence +6 for sale #7
    Removing that sale would lower this house's predicted price by 6 thousand. The sign matters: a row can help one prediction and hurt another.
    One row with a huge score
    Often a mislabel or a data-entry error. Sort rows by how much they hurt a failing case and inspect the top few. This is the best everyday use.
    Influence estimate and true leave-one-out disagree
    Expect this for rows far from the rest (high leverage) and when removing many rows at once. The estimate assumes small changes.
    Unlearned model "passes" its tests
    It means forgotten rows now score like unseen ones, a membership test (R5.8) fails to spot them, and accuracy for everyone else holds, split by segment. It does not prove the information is gone: many weight settings behave identically on any finite test.

    When sharding pays

    • Rare deletions: with 16 shards, 5 requests a month touch about four shards. Cheap.
    • Frequent deletions: 200 requests touch nearly every shard, which is a full retrain anyway.
    • Hidden cost: each shard sees only a fraction of the data, so accuracy drops a little.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    OptionUse it whenAvoid when
    Retrain without the rowsModel retrains in hours or less. Strongest evidence.Training takes weeks, or you no longer hold the data.
    Influence functionsSmall, smooth models; ranking rows for debugging.Deep nets as proof, big deletions, outlying rows.
    TracIn or TRAKLarge networks where a shortlist is enough.You need a guarantee.
    Sharded trainingDeletions are rare and planned from day one.Deletions are frequent.
    Gradient ascent, refusalNo retrain possible; some reduction beats none.You must attest that data is gone.

    Strong at:

    • Debugging data. Mislabelled and leaked rows rise to the top.
    • Small models. Influence on a regularised regression is cheap and checkable.
    • Exact deletion. Sharding gives a real guarantee when deletions are rare.

    Weak at:

    • Large language models. The honest claim is "these rows are plausibly related", not "removing them would change the answer". Treat scores as leads.
    • Certified forgetting for deep nets. No such guarantee exists. Fine-tuning tricks give suppression, not removal.
    • Copies elsewhere. Feature stores, embeddings and backups are untouched by all of this.
    Show the formula

    Shards touched = S × (1 − (1 − 1/S)d), for S shards and d deletion requests. It is less than d because requests sometimes land on the same shard.

    In banking, attribution answers a loan appeal ("which past applicants shaped this decision?") and erasure requests are real, so for tabular models the honest answer is usually a batched retrain; for a vendor's large language model, both ideas are research tools, not production controls.

    Usable today? Yes for small models: retrain, or use exact influence and check it. Not for big models, where you should filter data before training, keep a record of where each dataset went, and control access to retrieval indexes.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading influence on a deep net as cause

      The theory assumes a smooth, bowl-shaped loss. Ask whether anyone checked by retraining, even a smaller model.

    • Counting suppression as unlearning

      A model that stops answering may still hold the knowledge. Ask for a relearning test: does a short fine-tune bring it back?

    • Forgetting the copies

      The data leaves the weights but stays in feature stores, backups, embeddings and downstream models. Keep a lineage of every artefact a dataset fed.

    • Collateral damage

      Unlearning one customer's pattern can hurt similar customers or a whole language. Re-run validation by segment.

    1. What does a row's influence measure?

    How much a prediction would change if that row were removed from training.

    2. Why is the exact method rarely used?

    It needs one full retraining per row. Influence functions estimate the same thing without retraining.

    3. Why does sharding give exact unlearning?

    The deleted row only ever trained one shard, so retraining that shard gives the model you would have had without it.

    4. 200 deletion requests a month on 16 shards. Does sharding help?

    Barely. Nearly every shard is touched, so it costs about a full retrain plus the accuracy lost to sharding.

    5. An LLM refuses to answer about a customer after unlearning. Is the data gone?

    Not proven. Refusal is suppression. Test with paraphrases, other languages and a short fine-tune.
