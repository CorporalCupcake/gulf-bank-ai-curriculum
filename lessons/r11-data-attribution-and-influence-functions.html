    Which Data Made This Prediction, and Can We Take It Back?

    Tracing a prediction to training rows, and making a model forget a customer.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceData attribution asks which training rows shaped a prediction, influence functions estimate it cheaply for simple models and shakily for deep ones, and unlearning asks for the model without those rows, where only retraining gives a real guarantee.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer is declined for a loan and appeals. F8 tools say which features pushed the score down. The model-risk committee may ask: which past applicants shaped this decision? That is data attribution. The cleanest definition: delete one row, retrain, see how the prediction moves.

    Flip it and a customer exercises a right to erasure. The experiment becomes an instruction: produce the model you would have had without that row. That is machine unlearning.

    Hold onto thisAttribution measures the effect of removing data. Unlearning performs the removal. The gold standard for both is "retrain without it"; everything else is an approximation.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What "influence" means, precisely

    There are two ways to ask. Leave-one-out retrains without the row and sees how the prediction moves: exact, but one retraining per row. Influence nudges the row's weight slightly and uses calculus to estimate the shift without retraining.

    Think of a ball in a bowl: a row pulls the model sideways, and a flat bowl lets it roll far. The shortcut multiplies two things. One is how hard the row pulls (its gradient, the direction it pushes the parameters). The other is how flat the bowl is (the Hessian, the table of second derivatives that measures curvature). If the row pushes the model the way the test case wants, it helps that prediction; if opposed, it hurts. Cook did this for linear regression in the 1970s, and Koh and Liang carried it to modern machine learning in 2017.

    The shortcut is accurate for typical rows. A row far out in feature space has high leverage, like a person on the end of a seesaw, and the shortcut understates what deleting it does. For ridge regression the exact answer is the estimate divided by one minus the leverage, so it blows up as leverage nears 1. Simulation 1 shows this.

    Influence is signed and local: a row can help one prediction and hurt another. It also needs a smooth loss, which regularised regression has and neural networks do not. It assumes small changes, so removing one row in 100,000 is fine and removing a whole branch is not (Simulation 2). The theory also assumes a unique minimum and an invertible Hessian. Ridge and regularised logistic regression, the scorecard workhorses, qualify cleanly.

    Making it work on big models

    The exact method needs a matrix with one entry per pair of parameters, impossible at a billion parameters (1018 entries). Each workaround gives up something.

    • Hessian-vector tricks. Never build the matrix; solve for its inverse's effect with conjugate gradients or a stochastic series (LiSSA). Needs many solves per query, and a damping term to cope with non-convexity.
    • Kronecker approximations (EK-FAC). Approximate the matrix layer by layer as a product of two small ones. Used to study language models with tens of billions of parameters. Ignores links between layers, and you still search a shortlist of candidate rows, not the whole corpus.
    • TracIn. No Hessian at all: add up gradient alignments between the row and the test case across saved training checkpoints. Intuitive and cheap, but a heuristic with no leave-one-out guarantee.
    • Datamodels and TRAK. Train many models on random subsets and learn which rows predict which outputs. Predicts subset effects well on average, but needs an ensemble.
    • Shortlist then re-rank. Retrieve similar rows by embeddings, then score only those. Misses influential rows that do not look similar, which is the interesting case.

    For a bank, tabular models are in range of exact tools, and you can test the answer by retraining. For large language models the honest claim is "these rows are plausibly related to this output", not "removing them would change it". Studies on big models found that influence grows more abstract with scale: answers trace to documents with similar themes, not matching words. Good science, awkward audit evidence.

    What attribution is used for

    • Debugging data. Sort rows by how much they hurt a failing case and inspect the top few for mislabels. A human checks, so this works well.
    • Explaining by example. "Customers like you, and how they fared." Colour for an appeal, not proof.
    • Provenance and credit. Which licensed documents shaped an output. This is a causal claim about a model nobody can retrain, so it demands the most trust.
    • Data selection. Drop or reweight rows to improve a model. Treat with care: influence scores often track the real effect of dropping rows poorly in language-model fine-tuning.

    Machine unlearning: what is being asked for

    Deleting a row from a database is a lookup. A trained model is more like a cake: the customer's data is baked in with everyone else's. Exact unlearning means the model you would get training without those rows. Anything else is approximate and needs a way to check it.

    • Retrain from scratch. Exact, but costs a full run.
    • Sharding (often called SISA). Train separate models on slices, with checkpoints inside each slice, and combine by voting or averaging. A deletion retrains only the shard that held the row, from the last checkpoint before it. Exact, because the row never touched the other shards, but each model sees less data.
    • Influence-based update. One Newton-style step that cancels the rows' pull. With added noise it can carry a "certified removal" guarantee, but only for convex models and small forget sets.
    • Fine-tuning heuristics. Gradient ascent (push the model away from the rows), relabelling to random targets, editing a few layers, or training a language model to refuse. Cheap, unguaranteed, and often damaging to neighbouring skills.

    A small model lets you measure the gap to the retrained one; a huge one does not. Under-forgetting leaves the influence in place. Over-forgetting leaves a fingerprint: rows scoring unusually badly show who was removed. A good result scores forgotten rows as the retrained model would, without wrecking accuracy for similar customers or a whole language.

    Sharding: the arithmetic of exact unlearning

    A month of requests hits many shards at once. With 16 shards, 5 requests touch about four; 200 touch all of them, which is a full retrain anyway. Sharding pays only when deletions are rare. The expected number of shards touched is S times (1 minus (1 minus 1/S) to the power d), where d is the number of requests, and it is less than d because requests sometimes collide on a shard.

    How would you know it worked?

    If you can retrain, compare with the retrained model. If not, you can only test behaviour: do forgotten rows score like unseen ones, does a membership-inference attack (R11.3) fail, do paraphrases or Arabic prompts still extract the data, and does a short fine-tune bring it back?

    Also check utility: accuracy on everyone else, split by segment and language, because unlearning one pattern can quietly hurt similar customers.

    A model can pass all of these and still carry the information, because many weight settings behave identically on any finite test set. Read "verified" as "passed these tests".

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Debugging training data. Ranking rows by how much they hurt a failing case puts mislabels and leaked rows near the top, because a wrong label pulls the fit hard against the right answer.
    • Small, smooth models. For regularised regression and logistic scorecards, the influence estimate is cheap and close to true leave-one-out, so you can check it.
    • Case-level explanations. It answers "which past applicants?" which feature attributions cannot.
    • Exact deletion through sharding. When deletions are rare, only one slice retrains, and the guarantee is real because the row never touched the rest.

    Weak at, or do not use when

    • Deep networks and LLMs as causal evidence. The theory assumes a bowl-shaped loss and the approximations add error. Use scores as leads, not proof.
    • Removing many rows at once, or outlying rows. The first-order estimate under-reports both.
    • Any claim of certified forgetting for a deep model. No such guarantee exists. Fine-tuning heuristics give suppression, not removal.
    • Frequent deletions with many shards. Nearly every shard is touched, so you pay a full retrain plus the accuracy loss of sharding.
    • Copies outside the weights. Feature stores, embeddings and backups are untouched by any of this.

    In practice. Try retraining first and measure the cost. If a tabular model retrains in an hour, a monthly batched deletion job is the honest answer, and no research method beats it. For debugging, start with exact influence on a regularised model; check the top rows by retraining without them. Knobs that matter: the damping or regularisation (keeps the Hessian stable), how many rows you remove at once, the number of shards, and checkpoint frequency.

    Compute: exact tools are fine up to millions of rows and thousands of parameters. Beyond that, expect gradients for every candidate row. In a bank, this appears in credit-decision appeals, model-risk reviews, mislabel hunts on fraud and AML alerts, and data-protection erasure requests. For an LLM trained by a vendor, attribution and unlearning are research tools, not production controls. Use today: retrain or filter before training, keep lineage, and control access to RAG indexes. What would change that: unlearning methods with checks that survive relearning and stronger attacks.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reading influence on a deep net as causal fact

      "These documents caused this answer" rests on convex-model theory.

      Spot itAsk whether it was checked by retraining without those rows, even on a smaller model.
    • Ignoring outliers and group removal

      The shortcut understates the effect of an outlying row or of removing many rows at once.

      Spot itAsk how many rows were removed and whether any sit far from the rest.
    • Counting suppression as unlearning

      The model stops answering but still holds the knowledge.

      Spot itAsk for a relearning test and a membership-inference test.
    • Forgetting the copies

      The data leaves the weights but stays in feature stores, backups, embeddings, RAG indexes and downstream models.

      Spot itAsk for lineage: every artefact the dataset fed.
    • Collateral damage

      Unlearning one customer's pattern degrades accuracy for similar customers, or a whole language.

      Spot itRe-run validation by segment, including Arabic and English text.
    • Unverifiable attestations

      Certified removal exists only for specific convex setups.

      Spot itAsk what the guarantee is, under which assumptions, and who checked it.
    • Attribution leaking the data it explains

      Showing "the most similar past customers" to an applicant exposes other people's records.

      Spot itReview any example-based explanation under privacy rules before it leaves the bank.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What does a row's influence on a prediction measure?

    How much the prediction changes if that row is removed or slightly reweighted. The exact version is retraining without it, once per row.

    2. Why do influence tools work for a scorecard but shakily for a large network?

    They assume a smooth bowl-shaped loss with one minimum, which regularised regression has and deep networks do not. Big models also need approximations just to make the sums affordable.

    3. Why can a far-out row defeat the shortcut?

    It pulls the fit towards itself, so the shortcut sees a small error, yet removing it changes the fit a lot. The estimate understates its effect.

    4. A vendor model refuses to discuss a deleted customer but answers again after a short fine-tune. What does that tell you?

    The knowledge was suppressed, not removed. It was still in the weights.

    5. Why is a model that scores far worse than normal on the forgotten rows not a success?

    A retrained model would treat them like any unseen rows. An unusually bad score is a fingerprint showing who was removed.

    6. A tabular credit model retrains in 90 minutes. You get ten erasure requests a month. Do you use influence-based unlearning, sharding or retraining, and why?

    Retrain, in a monthly batch. It is exact, cheap at that size, and easy to defend to an auditor. Sharding would cost accuracy for no gain, and an influence step offers a guarantee you do not need.

    7. A vendor offers "which training documents produced this chatbot answer" for a closed LLM. Would you rely on it in a complaint review, and what would you use instead?

    Not as proof. Scores on big models are approximations that cannot be checked by retraining, so treat them as leads. Rely on logged retrieval sources (RAG citations), your own data lineage, and tests on a smaller model you can retrain.
