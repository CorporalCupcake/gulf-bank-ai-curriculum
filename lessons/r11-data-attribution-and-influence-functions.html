    Which Data Made This Prediction, and Can We Take It Back?

    How influence functions trace a prediction to the training rows behind it, why that works well for small models and shakily for large ones, and what it means to make a trained model forget a customer: the exact way, the approximate ways, and why nobody can yet prove it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceData attribution asks "if this training row had been absent or weighted differently, how would this prediction change?"; influence functions answer it with a one-step calculus approximation that is exact-ish for convex models and unreliable for deep ones, and unlearning is the same question run in reverse, where the only guarantee that really holds is to retrain without the data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer is declined for a personal loan. She appeals. The explanation tools from F8 say which features pushed the score down: low salary stability, two late payments. That is useful. It does not answer a different question the model-risk committee may ask: which past applicants shaped this decision? Perhaps a cluster of 40 mislabelled defaults from one 2021 branch campaign is dragging down everyone who looks like her.

    That is data attribution. Instead of asking "which inputs mattered?" it asks "which training rows mattered?" The cleanest way to define it is a thought experiment: delete one training row, retrain the model, and see how the prediction moves. A row whose deletion changes the prediction a lot is influential. It is also the most expensive possible definition, one full retraining per row.

    Now flip the direction. A customer exercises a right to have their data erased, or a record turns out to have been collected without proper consent. The bank must stop the model carrying that customer's influence. The thought experiment is now an instruction: produce the model you would have had if that row had never been there. That is machine unlearning.

    Hold onto thisAttribution and unlearning are one idea. Attribution measures the effect of removing data. Unlearning performs the removal. The gold standard for both is "retrain without it"; everything clever in this lesson is an approximation to that, and the interesting question is always how good the approximation is.

    Why this is a frontier topic and not a solved one. For a linear model with a few hundred thousand rows, retraining takes seconds, so you just do it. For a model with billions of parameters trained for weeks on a mixture of data nobody fully catalogued, retraining per request is impossible, and a calculus shortcut is all you have. Whether the shortcut can be trusted is an open research question, and regulators, auditors and your own legal team are starting to ask it. Part 2 starts with the case where the shortcut is exact enough to see the mechanism clearly.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    What "influence" means, precisely

    Let θ̂ be the trained parameters, the minimum of the total training loss (plus any regulariser). Take one training row z. Two natural questions:

    • Leave-one-out (LOO). Retrain without z. How does a test prediction or loss change? Exact, but one retraining per row.
    • Upweighting. Give z a tiny extra weight ε in the loss and ask how θ̂ moves per unit ε. A smooth, differentiable version of the same question, which calculus can answer without retraining.

    Cook (1977) did this for linear regression in the 1970s to find outlying observations. Koh and Liang (2017) carried it to modern machine learning. The result, from the implicit function theorem: because θ̂ is a minimum, the total gradient is zero there, and nudging one row's weight tilts the landscape slightly. The parameters slide to the new minimum by an amount set by how curved the landscape is (the Hessian H, the matrix of second derivatives, F1) and how hard the row pulls (its own gradient).

    dθ^dε=−H−1∇ℓ(z,θ^)so the effect on a test loss is−∇ℓ(zt)TH−1∇ℓ(z)

    Read it as a similarity score. ∇ℓ(z) is the direction row z pushes the parameters. ∇ℓ(zt) is the direction the test point would like them pushed. The inverse Hessian H−1 is a distance-warping: directions where the loss is flat (many parameter settings fit equally well) count for more, because the model can move easily there. A training row is helpful for a test point when their gradients align under that warping, and harmful when they oppose. Removing the row is ε = −1/n, which just flips the sign.

    For ridge or linear regression this becomes concrete and the first-order answer can be compared with the exact leave-one-out answer in closed form. The exact removal effect equals the influence estimate divided by (1 − h), where h is the row's leverage (how far out in feature space it sits, between 0 and 1). For ordinary rows h is small and the two agree. For a row far from the others, h is near 1 and the shortcut can badly understate what deleting the row does. Simulation 1 lets you see that.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Influence is local and signed. A row can help one prediction and hurt another. "Most influential row overall" is a different question from "most influential row for this customer's decision".
    • It needs a smooth, convex-ish loss to be trusted. The derivation assumes θ̂ is a unique minimum and that H is invertible. Both hold for ridge regression and regularised logistic regression, the workhorses of bank scorecards. Neither holds cleanly for a neural network.
    • First order means small changes. Removing one row among 100,000 is small. Removing a whole branch's worth of rows is not, and the error grows. Simulation 2 shows this.

    Making it work on big models

    The formula needs H−1, and for a model with 109 parameters H has 1018 entries. You can never write it down. The field has been a sequence of workarounds, and each one gives up something.

    ApproachThe trickWhat it gives up

    EK-FAC / Kronecker approximationsApproximate H layer by layer as a product of two small matrices, so inversion is cheap. Used by Grosse et al.     TracIn (Pruthi et al.)No Hessian at all.     For a bank, the practical split is simple. Tabular models (logistic regression, small nets, boosted trees via related methods) are in range of the exact tools; you can attribute a loan decision to training rows and test the answer by retraining. Large language models are in range only of the approximate ones, and there the honest claim is "these rows are plausibly related to this output", not "removing them would change it".

    One more caution on what the answers look like. In language models, the most influential training texts for a question are often not semantically obvious. Grosse et al. found that influence patterns became more abstract as models grew: a larger model's answer was shaped by documents with similar themes or structure, not simply matching words. That is interesting science and awkward audit evidence.

    What attribution is used for

    Four uses, in rising order of how much trust each demands.

    • Debugging data. Find the mislabelled rows behind a stubborn error. Sort training rows by how much they hurt a failing test case, inspect the top 20. This works well and is the easiest win, because a human checks the result.
    • Explaining a decision by example. "Customers most like you, and how they fared." Supports an appeal or an adverse-action discussion alongside feature-based reasons. Treat as supporting colour, not proof.
    • Provenance and credit. Which licensed documents or which vendor feed contributed to an output. Interesting for copyright and vendor-contract disputes; the most demanding use, because it is a causal claim about a model nobody can retrain.
    • Data selection. Pick or drop training rows to improve a model. An active area, with recent evidence that influence scores often track poorly with the real effect of dropping or reweighting rows in language-model fine-tuning (Part 4).

    Machine unlearning: what is being asked for

    Define the target precisely, because most confusion lives here. Let D be the training set and F the "forget set" to be removed. The exact unlearning target is the model you would get by training on D minus F from scratch, or any model whose distribution over outcomes is identical to it (the "retrain-equivalence" standard). Anything else is approximate unlearning, and it needs a definition of "close enough" and a way to check it.

    Why it is harder than deleting a row from a database. A trained model is not a lookup table. The customer's data has been compressed into millions of weights alongside everyone else's, and no cell holds "customer 4417's contribution". You also cannot just retrain, whenever the model is large, trained once at high cost, or built by a vendor you do not control.

    Three families of method exist.

    FamilyWhat it doesGuaranteeCost
    Retrain from scratchDrop the rows and train again.Exact, by definition.One full training run per batch of requests.
    Structured exact: sharding (SISA)Split data into S shards, train a model per shard (and checkpoint within it), combine by voting or averaging. A deletion retrains only the shard that held the row, from the last checkpoint before it.Exact for the ensemble, because the removed row never touched the other shards.Cheaper retraining, at some accuracy cost from each model seeing 1/S of the data. Works best for models that can be sharded.
    Influence-based updateTake one Newton-style step in parameter space that cancels the forget set's pull: θ' = θ + H−1Σ∇ℓ(z).     Fine-tuning heuristicsGradient ascent on the forget set, pushing the model away from those examples; relabelling to random targets; preference-style objectives; editing a few layers; for language models, training the model to refuse or to produce a plausible replacement.None formal.     What the simulation is showing, in words.

    • Closeness in parameters is not the whole story, but here it is checkable. We can compute the retrained model, so we can measure the gap. For a billion-parameter model we cannot, which is the core problem of Part 5.
    • "Forgotten" has two failure sides. Under-forgetting leaves the customer's influence in place. Over-forgetting, as gradient ascent does, makes the model especially bad on those exact rows. That is itself a fingerprint: an attacker comparing model behaviour on a person's records against a normal model could tell they were removed (sometimes called the Streisand effect of unlearning). A good unlearned model scores the forgotten rows as the retrained model would, neither better nor worse.
    • Utility matters. The business wants a model that still works. Heuristics that destroy accuracy are not a solution, however thoroughly they forget.

    Sharding: the arithmetic of exact unlearning

    Sharding is the one scheme with a clean guarantee, so it is worth seeing what it costs. With S shards, a single deletion retrains one shard: 1/S of the work. But deletion requests arrive in volume. If d requests land in a batch, each hits a random shard, and the expected number of distinct shards touched is

    S(1−(1−1S)d)

    With 16 shards and 5 requests, about 4.4 shards are touched (28% of a full retrain). With 16 shards and 200 requests, essentially all 16 are touched and you have paid for a full retrain anyway. Sharding helps when deletions are rare compared with the number of shards.

    How would you know it worked?

    This is the hard part of the field. For a model you can retrain, the test is direct: compare to the retrained model. For one you cannot, you test behaviour, and behavioural tests are weaker than they look.

    • Forget-set performance. The model should perform on the forgotten rows like a model that never saw them, not better and not far worse.
    • Membership inference (R11.3). Run the attack that guesses "was this row in training?" Unlearning should push its success to the level for unseen rows. This is the strongest practical test, and attacks keep improving.
    • Extraction and probing. For language models, ask directly, paraphrase, ask in another language (Arabic against English), or prompt the model to continue a known passage.
    • Relearning. Fine-tune the unlearned model briefly on a little related data. If the "forgotten" knowledge comes back from a few steps, it was hidden, not removed. Several studies have shown this for heuristic methods.
    • Utility on retained data. Test everything else did not degrade.

    A caution that runs through the literature: a model can pass these tests and still carry the information, and the tests themselves are not agreed. There are also theoretical arguments that exact retrain-equivalence cannot be verified from outside for a general trained model, because many different weight settings can behave identically on any finite test set. Treat any vendor claim of "verified unlearning" as a claim about specific tests passed.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These two topics sit where optimisation, privacy, explanation and governance meet.

    F1 · Gradients and optimisationWhere the Hessian comes from

    Influence is the gradient-and-curvature maths of F1 applied to one training row. If curvature and "flat directions" are fuzzy, revisit it first.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsLeverage and Cook's distance

    Classical diagnostics are influence functions for linear models. Simulation 1 is that, made visible.

    F8 · ExplainabilityFeatures versus rows

    SHAP and counterfactuals explain a prediction through its inputs. Attribution explains it through its training data. A full audit file may need both, and they can disagree.

    R5 · Mechanistic interpretabilityTracing circuits to data

    Recent work uses influence methods to ask which training data built a particular internal feature or circuit, joining attribution to interpretability.

    R11.3 · Memorisation and privacyThe test for unlearning

    Membership inference is both the privacy attack and the main way to check that unlearning worked. Differential privacy is the alternative: limit any row's influence up front.

    A11.3 · Differential privacyBound influence before training

    DP training caps how much any one row can matter, which makes later removal far less urgent. The price is accuracy.

    G2.3 · A12.2 · Privacy and data lawWhy anyone asks for unlearning

    Erasure rights, consent withdrawal and minimisation create the demand. See also the regulator expectations and data protection laws.

    M2.3 · M2.4 · Fine-tuningWhere a customer's data enters a model

    Data in a fine-tune or LoRA adapter is far easier to remove (retrain a small adapter) than data inside a pre-trained base model. RAG avoids the problem: delete the document from the index.

    G3 · Model riskValidation and monitoring

    An unlearned model is a changed model. It needs revalidation, and its monitoring needs to show nothing else shifted.

    R2.3 · Synthetic dataData that has moved on

    Synthetic data generated from a model that saw a customer can carry traces of that customer. Attribution on the downstream model gets murky.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit decisions and appeals. Attribute a declined application to similar past cases, find mislabelled or biased clusters (a past campaign, a branch with sloppy default coding), and use the finding to fix data, not to excuse the model.
    • Fraud and AML models. Locate the labels that mislead: a batch of alerts closed as false positive in haste, which the model then treats as safe. Attribution on a stubborn false negative leads you to them.
    • Consent withdrawal and erasure requests. Marketing, personalisation and churn models trained on customer data. Mostly handled by deleting from the training store and excluding from the next scheduled retrain.
    • Conflicts with retention rules. A bank must keep many records for years (AML, audit). An erasure request may collide with a legal duty to retain. Legal and compliance decide; the data scientist's job is to say precisely what the model contains and what removal would take.
    • LLM copilots and fine-tunes. Staff or customer text used to tune a model. Know which dataset went into which adapter, so removal is retraining one small thing.
    • Vendor models. If a vendor trained on your data, ask what they can attribute and remove, and put it in the contract (A3.6 supply chain risk).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Attribution has moved from "can we compute influence at all?" to "does the number mean what we hope?". Unlearning has moved from clean theory on small models to a sceptical debate about whether heuristics for large language models remove anything.

      Cook's distance

      Influence measured for linear regression: how much do fitted values move if one observation is removed. The statistics ancestor of everything here.

      Cao and Yang proposed making learning algorithms depend on data only through sums, so a deletion updates a few sums rather than retraining.

      Influence functions for modern ML

      Koh and Liang made the classical tool work with Hessian-vector products on neural networks, and used it for debugging data and for building adversarial training examples.

    4. 2020–21
      Fragility on deep networks; first practical unlearning schemes

      Basu et al. found influence estimates in deep networks to be fragile, worse with depth and width. Guo et al. gave certified removal for convex models; Bourtoule et al. introduced SISA sharding with exact guarantees. Pruthi et al. offered the Hessian-free TracIn.

      Basu et al., ICLR, 2021; Guo et al., ICML, 2020; Bourtoule et al., IEEE S&P, 2021; Pruthi et al., NeurIPS, 2020
    5. 2022–23
      Datamodels, TRAK, and influence on 50-billion-parameter language models

      Ilyas et al. and Park et al. cast attribution as a learned linear map from training subset to prediction. Grosse et al. used EK-FAC to scale influence functions to language models up to tens of billions of parameters. Bae et al. clarified why influence on neural nets often does not match leave-one-out.

      Ilyas et al., ICML, 2022; Park et al., ICML, 2023; Grosse et al., arXiv 2308.03296, 2023; Bae et al., NeurIPS, 2022
    6. 2023–24
      Unlearning for LLMs, and benchmarks

      Methods that make a model stop reproducing a book, a person, or hazardous knowledge; benchmarks such as TOFU (fictitious authors) and WMDP (hazardous knowledge). Hu and colleagues showed that a handful of fine-tuning steps on related data can bring supposedly unlearned knowledge back.

      Maini et al., 2024 (TOFU); Li et al., ICML, 2024 (WMDP); Hu et al., 2024 (targeted relearning)
    7. 2024–25
      Benchmarks are weak measures of progress; retrain-equivalence cannot be verified

      Position and theory papers argue that LLM unlearning benchmarks mislead, and that retrain equivalence cannot in general be established for trained models from behaviour.

      arXiv 2410.02879 (2024); arXiv 2510.16629 (2025)
    8. 2026
      Attribution meets interpretability, and a reality check

      Mechanistic Data Attribution (ICML 2026) applies influence functions to trace specific interpretable units, such as induction heads, to training samples, and reports that removing the small set it identifies changes how fast the unit forms while random removal does not. Concept Influence (FAR.AI) attributes behaviour to concept directions rather than single test examples, and reports first-order approximations that match full influence functions much faster. A later preprint reports that influence scores often track poorly with the real effect of deleting, reweighting or rewriting those samples in fine-tuning.

      arXiv 2601.21996 (ICML 2026); FAR.AI blog, February 2026; arXiv 2609.02771 (preprint, not verified)
    9. 2026
      Is "unlearning" the right word?

      Search listings show new relearning-robust methods, an agentic-deployment benchmark (K-Bench), and a position paper arguing the term "machine unlearning" is overused for LLM work, where much of it is output suppression.

      arXiv 2606.27379, arXiv 2609.12808 (listings only; claims not verified)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes to watch for in a vendor pitch, a paper, or an internal proposal.

    • Reading influence on a deep net as a causal fact

      "These ten documents caused this answer." The score estimates a quantity that was derived for convex models and may not match what retraining without them does.

      Spot itAsk whether the claim was checked by retraining without the rows (even on a smaller model), and how well scores correlated with the actual leave-out effect.
    • Ignoring leverage and group removal

      First-order estimates understate the effect of an outlying row (Simulation 1) or of deleting many rows at once (Simulation 2).

      Spot itAsk how many rows are being removed relative to the data size, and whether any are far from the rest in feature space.
    • Counting suppression as unlearning

      The model refuses to answer about the person but still holds the knowledge, and a small fine-tune or clever prompt brings it back.

      Spot itAsk for a relearning test and a membership-inference test, not just "the model no longer says it".
    • Over-forgetting leaves a fingerprint

      Gradient-ascent-style methods make the forgotten rows score unusually badly, so an observer can tell who was removed.

      Spot itCompare the forgotten rows' loss with that of a retrained or never-trained model, and with similar unseen rows. It should match, not be worse.
    • Collateral damage

      Unlearning one customer's pattern degrades accuracy for similar customers, or for a whole language.

      Spot itRe-run the validation suite by segment, including Arabic and English text for language models, and the fairness cuts.
    • Forgetting the copies

      The data is gone from the model but remains in feature stores, caches, backups, embeddings, RAG indexes, adapters, synthetic data and downstream models trained on this model's outputs.

      Spot itTrace lineage: for any dataset, list every artefact it fed. If nobody can, that is the finding.
    • Unverifiable attestations

      "We certify this model has forgotten the request." Certified removal exists only for specific convex setups and noise levels.

      Spot itAsk what the guarantee is, under which assumptions, and who checked it independently.
    • Attribution leaking the data it explains

      Showing "the most similar past customers" to an applicant exposes other people's records.

      Spot itReview any example-based explanation under the privacy rules before it is shown outside the bank.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive or the DPO · 30 seconds
    “For our scorecards and most of our tabular models, honouring a deletion request is straightforward: remove the data and retrain on the normal cycle, and I can tell you exactly what changes. For large language models, the science does not yet let anyone prove that a specific person's data is gone from the weights. So the safe design is to keep personal data out of the model where we can, in retrieval systems and small adapters we can rebuild, and to keep a record of which data fed which model.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Influence functions approximate leave-one-out by a one-step Newton calculation, −∇ℓtestT H−1 ∇ℓtrain. They are reliable for regularised convex models and degrade with non-convexity, high leverage and large removals. For big models you need EK-FAC or TRAK-style approximations, which I'd treat as retrieval of candidate causes, validated by retraining a smaller model. For unlearning, only retraining or sharding is exact; influence-style updates are certified only for convex cases; fine-tuning heuristics need membership inference, relearning and utility checks, and still prove little.”
    “A customer has asked us to delete her data. Do we have to retrain the model?”

    That is a legal question first, so I'd confirm with legal and the DPO what the applicable law and our retention duties require here; I don't want to guess how erasure rights apply to trained weights in the UAE or Saudi context. Technically: delete from the training store and exclude her rows from the next scheduled retrain, which is cheap for tabular models. For a large model, we document what it contains and use the retrain cycle rather than claim an instant weight-level removal.

    “Can you show which training data caused this model's answer?”

    For a regularised tabular model, yes, with good accuracy, and I can check it by retraining. For a large language model I can retrieve the training texts with the highest influence scores, but those are plausible leads, not proof that removing them would change the answer. Recent work reports gaps between influence scores and real effects, so I'd present it as supporting evidence only.

    “The vendor says their LLM supports unlearning. Is that good enough?”

    It depends on what they mean. I'd ask: is it exact (retraining or sharding), certified, or heuristic? What tests show it works: membership inference, relearning attacks, extraction in both Arabic and English? What is the damage to other capabilities? And who verifies it independently? If the answer is "the model no longer outputs it", that is suppression, which is useful but not removal.

    “Why not just use differential privacy so we never have to delete?”

    DP bounds how much any one row can influence the model, which makes individual removal much less pressing and gives a mathematical guarantee. The costs are accuracy, particularly for rare cases like fraud, and engineering effort. It is worth it for sensitive models with broad customer data, but it is not a free replacement for good lineage and a retrain process.

    “Influence says one past loan most affected this decision. Can we tell the customer?”

    I'd be careful. It may expose another person's record, the score may be unreliable, and the regulatory expectation is usually feature-based reasons and the ability to contest. I'd use the finding internally to check data quality, and ask privacy and compliance before anything example-based reaches a customer.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. In one sentence, what does the influence of a training row on a test prediction measure, and what is the cost of the exact version?

    How much the test prediction (or loss) changes if the row is removed or slightly reweighted. The exact version is leave-one-out: one retraining per row, so n retrainings for n rows.

    2. Why does the influence formula contain the inverse Hessian, and why can't you compute it for a large neural network?

    The Hessian measures curvature. Where the loss is flat, the parameters can move a long way for a small push, so a row's gradient has more effect. The inverse accounts for this. For a model with p parameters the Hessian has p2 entries, impossible to store at billions of parameters, so you need Hessian-vector products, Kronecker approximations, or Hessian-free methods.

    3. In Simulation 1 with the far-out row switched on, why does the estimate disagree with true leave-one-out for that row?

    The row has leverage near 1: the fit is pulled strongly toward it, so its residual is small, but removing it changes the fit a lot. The exact removal effect is the first-order estimate divided by (1 − leverage), so as leverage approaches 1 the first-order value badly understates the effect.

    4. You have 8 shards and 3 deletion requests land in a batch. About how many shards do you expect to retrain?

    8 × (1 − (7/8)3) = 8 × (1 − 0.670) ≈ 2.6 shards, about a third of a full retrain. Sometimes two requests land on the same shard, which is why it is slightly under 3.

    5. A vendor's unlearned model refuses to answer questions about a deleted customer. After 20 steps of fine-tuning on unrelated customer data, it answers again. What does this tell you?

    The knowledge was suppressed, not removed: it was still in the weights and the fine-tune undid the suppression. This is the relearning effect reported for heuristic LLM unlearning methods.

    6. Why is a model that scores much worse than normal on the forgotten rows not a successful unlearning?

    A retrained model would have scored those rows like any unseen similar rows. A much worse score is itself information: it marks those rows as ones the model was pushed away from, so a membership-inference attacker can detect who was removed. Success is matching the retrained model, not maximising forgetting.

    7. Name three places a customer's data may persist after you have unlearned it from the model weights.

    The feature store or training snapshots, embeddings and RAG indexes, backups and logs, fine-tuned adapters and downstream or distilled models, synthetic datasets generated from the model, and vendor copies. Lineage tracking is the real control.
