    Which Data Made This Prediction, and Can We Take It Back?

    How influence functions trace a prediction to the training rows behind it, why that works well for small models and shakily for large ones, and what it means to make a trained model forget a customer: the exact way, the approximate ways, and why nobody can yet prove it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceData attribution asks "if this training row had been absent or weighted differently, how would this prediction change?"; influence functions answer it with a one-step calculus approximation that is exact-ish for convex models and unreliable for deep ones, and unlearning is the same question run in reverse, where the only guarantee that really holds is to retrain without the data.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A customer is declined for a personal loan and appeals. The tools from F8 say which features pushed the score down: unstable salary, two late payments. The model-risk committee may ask something else: which past applicants shaped this decision? Perhaps 40 mislabelled defaults from a 2021 branch campaign are dragging down everyone who looks like her.

    That is data attribution: which training rows mattered, not which inputs. The cleanest definition is a thought experiment. Delete one row, retrain, see how the prediction moves, like removing one ingredient and retasting the dish. A big move means an influential row. It is also the costliest definition: one retraining per row.

    Now flip it. A customer exercises a right to erasure, or a record turns out to lack consent. The experiment becomes an instruction: produce the model you would have had without that row. That is machine unlearning.

    Hold onto thisAttribution measures the effect of removing data. Unlearning performs the removal. The gold standard for both is "retrain without it"; everything else here is an approximation, and the question is always how good.

    This is a frontier topic because of scale. A linear model on a few hundred thousand rows retrains in seconds, so just do it. A billion-parameter model trained for weeks on uncatalogued data cannot be retrained per request, so a calculus shortcut is all you have. Whether to trust it is open, and regulators and auditors are starting to ask.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    What "influence" means, precisely

    Let θ̂ be the trained parameters (the minimum of the training loss) and z one training row. Two questions:

    • Leave-one-out (LOO). Retrain without z and see how a test prediction changes. Exact, but one retraining per row.
    • Upweighting. Give z a tiny extra weight ε and ask how θ̂ moves per unit ε. A smooth version calculus can answer without retraining.

    Cook (1977) did this for linear regression; Koh and Liang (2017) carried it to modern ML. Since θ̂ is a minimum, the gradient is zero there; nudging one row's weight tilts the landscape, and θ̂ slides by an amount set by how curved it is (the Hessian H, the matrix of second derivatives, F1) and how hard the row pulls (its gradient). Think of a ball in a bowl: a steep bowl barely moves, a flat one rolls far.

    dθ^dε=−H−1∇ℓ(z,θ^)so the effect on a test loss is−∇ℓ(zt)TH−1∇ℓ(z)

    Read it as a similarity score. ∇ℓ(z) is the direction row z pushes the parameters; ∇ℓ(zt) is where the test point wants them. H−1 warps distance so flat directions (where the model moves easily) count more. Aligned gradients mean the row helps that test point; opposed means it hurts. Removing the row is ε = −1/n, which flips the sign.

    For ridge or linear regression the exact leave-one-out answer equals the influence estimate divided by (1 − h), where h is the row's leverage (0 to 1: how far out it sits in feature space). Typical rows have small h and the two agree. Like a person on the end of a seesaw, a far-out row has h near 1 and the shortcut badly understates what deleting it does. Simulation 1 shows this.

    • Influence is local and signed. A row can help one prediction and hurt another. "Most influential overall" is not "most influential for this customer".
    • It needs a smooth, convex-ish loss. The derivation assumes a unique minimum and invertible H. Ridge and regularised logistic regression, the bank scorecard workhorses, qualify. Neural networks do not, cleanly.
    • First order means small changes. One row in 100,000 is small. A whole branch's rows is not, and error grows (Simulation 2).

    Making it work on big models

    H−1 is out of reach: a 109-parameter model has 1018 Hessian entries. Each workaround gives something up.

    ApproachThe trickWhat it gives up
    Exact influence (Koh and Liang)Never form H; solve H−1v with conjugate gradients or a stochastic series (LiSSA) using Hessian-vector products.Many solves per query; sensitive to non-convexity, so a damping term is added to make H positive definite.
    EK-FAC / Kronecker approximationsApproximate H layer by layer as a product of two small matrices, so inversion is cheap. Used by Grosse et al.     TracIn (Pruthi et al.)No Hessian at all.     Datamodels, TRAK (Ilyas, Park et al.)Train many models on random subsets and fit a linear model from "which rows were in" to "what the model predicted".     The split for a bank: tabular models (logistic regression, small nets) are in range of exact tools, and you can test the answer by retraining. Large language models get only approximate tools, so the honest claim is "these rows are plausibly related to this output", not "removing them would change it".

    Grosse et al. found that influence became more abstract as models grew: answers were shaped by documents with similar themes or structure, not matching words. Interesting science, awkward audit evidence.

    What attribution is used for

    Four uses, in rising order of the trust each demands.

    • Debugging data. Sort training rows by how much they hurt a failing test case and inspect the top 20 for mislabels. Works well, because a human checks the result.
    • Explaining by example. "Customers most like you, and how they fared." Supporting colour for an appeal alongside feature-based reasons, not proof.
    • Provenance and credit. Which licensed documents or vendor feed shaped an output. The most demanding use: a causal claim about a model nobody can retrain.
    • Data selection. Pick or drop rows to improve a model. Recent evidence shows influence scores often track poorly with the real effect of dropping or reweighting rows in language-model fine-tuning.

    Machine unlearning: what is being asked for

    Deleting a row from a database is a lookup. A trained model is more like a cake: the customer's data is baked in with everyone else's, and no cell holds "customer 4417's contribution". Retraining is often impossible too, when the model is large, costly, or built by a vendor.

    Let D be the training set and F the forget set. Exact unlearning means the model you would get training on D minus F from scratch, or one with an identical outcome distribution ("retrain-equivalence"). Anything else is approximate and needs a definition of "close enough" and a way to check it. Four families:

    FamilyWhat it doesGuaranteeCost
    Retrain from scratchDrop the rows and train again.Exact, by definition.One full training run per batch of requests.
    Structured exact: sharding (SISA)Split data into S shards, train a model per shard (and checkpoint within it), combine by voting or averaging. A deletion retrains only the shard that held the row, from the last checkpoint before it.Exact for the ensemble, because the removed row never touched the other shards.Cheaper retraining, at some accuracy cost from each model seeing 1/S of the data. Works best for models that can be sharded.
    Influence-based updateTake one Newton-style step in parameter space that cancels the forget set's pull: θ' = θ + H−1Σ∇ℓ(z). With added noise it can carry a "certified removal" guarantee for convex models (Guo et al., 2020).Provable for convex models with bounded forget sets; none for deep nets.A Hessian solve; fast.
    Fine-tuning heuristicsGradient ascent on the forget set, pushing the model away from those examples; relabelling to random targets; preference-style objectives; editing a few layers; for language models, training the model to refuse or to produce a plausible replacement.None formal. Success is measured by tests.Cheap. Often damages other capabilities.
    • Closeness is checkable here. We can compute the retrained model and measure the gap. For a billion-parameter model we cannot.
    • "Forgotten" fails on two sides. Under-forgetting leaves the influence in place. Over-forgetting (gradient ascent) makes the model especially bad on those rows, which is a fingerprint: an attacker comparing behaviour on a person's records with a normal model can tell they were removed. A good result scores the forgotten rows as the retrained model would.
    • Utility matters. A method that forgets thoroughly but wrecks accuracy is no solution.

    Sharding: the arithmetic of exact unlearning

    Sharding is the one scheme with a clean guarantee, so check what it costs. One deletion retrains one shard, 1/S of the work. But requests arrive in batches: d requests hit random shards, and the expected number of distinct shards touched is

    S(1−(1−1S)d)

    16 shards, 5 requests: about 4.4 shards touched (28% of a full retrain). 16 shards, 200 requests: all 16, a full retrain anyway. Sharding pays off only when deletions are rare relative to shard count.

    How would you know it worked?

    If you can retrain, compare to the retrained model. If you cannot, you test behaviour, and those tests are weaker than they look.

    • Forget-set performance. Forgotten rows should score like rows the model never saw: not better, not far worse.
    • Membership inference (R11.3). Run the attack that guesses "was this row in training?". Success should fall to the level for unseen rows. The strongest practical test, though attacks keep improving.
    • Extraction and probing. Ask directly, paraphrase, switch language (Arabic against English), or prompt a continuation of a known passage.
    • Relearning. Fine-tune briefly on a little related data. If the knowledge returns in a few steps, it was hidden, not removed.
    • Utility on retained data. Check everything else did not degrade.

    A model can pass all of these and still carry the information. Many different weight settings behave identically on any finite test set, so retrain-equivalence cannot be verified from outside. Read "verified unlearning" as "passed these specific tests".

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reading influence on a deep net as a causal fact

      "These ten documents caused this answer." The score comes from convex-model theory and may not match what retraining without them does.

      Spot itAsk whether it was checked by retraining without the rows (even on a smaller model), and how well scores matched the real leave-out effect.
    • Ignoring leverage and group removal

      First-order estimates understate the effect of an outlying row (Simulation 1) or of deleting many rows at once (Simulation 2).

      Spot itAsk how many rows are removed relative to data size, and whether any sit far from the rest in feature space.
    • Counting suppression as unlearning

      The model refuses to answer about the person but still holds the knowledge; a small fine-tune or clever prompt brings it back.

      Spot itAsk for a relearning test and a membership-inference test, not just "it no longer says it".
    • Over-forgetting leaves a fingerprint

      Gradient-ascent methods make forgotten rows score unusually badly, so an observer can tell who was removed.

      Spot itCompare the forgotten rows' loss with a retrained model and with similar unseen rows. It should match, not be worse.
    • Collateral damage

      Unlearning one customer's pattern degrades accuracy for similar customers, or a whole language.

      Spot itRe-run validation by segment, including Arabic and English text and the fairness cuts.
    • Forgetting the copies

      The data leaves the weights but stays in feature stores, caches, backups, embeddings, RAG indexes, adapters, synthetic data and downstream models.

      Spot itTrace lineage: for any dataset, list every artefact it fed. If nobody can, that is the finding.
    • Unverifiable attestations

      "We certify this model has forgotten the request." Certified removal exists only for specific convex setups and noise levels.

      Spot itAsk what the guarantee is, under which assumptions, and who checked it independently.
    • Attribution leaking the data it explains

      Showing "the most similar past customers" to an applicant exposes other people's records.

      Spot itReview any example-based explanation under privacy rules before it leaves the bank.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What does the influence of a training row on a test prediction measure, and what does the exact version cost?

    How much the prediction (or loss) changes if the row is removed or slightly reweighted. The exact version is leave-one-out: one retraining per row, so n retrainings for n rows.

    2. Why does the influence formula contain the inverse Hessian, and why can't you compute it for a large network?

    The Hessian measures curvature. Where the loss is flat, parameters move far for a small push, so a row's gradient counts for more; the inverse captures that. With p parameters it has p2 entries, impossible to store at billions. So you use Hessian-vector products, Kronecker approximations or Hessian-free methods.

    3. In Simulation 1 with the far-out row on, why does the estimate disagree with true leave-one-out for that row?

    Its leverage is near 1. The fit is pulled hard towards it, so its residual is small, yet removing it changes the fit a lot. Exact effect = first-order estimate / (1 − leverage), so the estimate badly understates it as leverage nears 1.

    4. With 8 shards and 3 deletion requests in a batch, how many shards do you expect to retrain?

    8 × (1 − (7/8)3) = 8 × (1 − 0.670) ≈ 2.6, about a third of a full retrain. It is under 3 because two requests sometimes hit the same shard.

    5. An unlearned vendor model refuses questions about a deleted customer, but answers again after 20 fine-tuning steps on unrelated customer data. What does that tell you?

    The knowledge was suppressed, not removed. It was still in the weights, and the fine-tune undid the suppression. This is the relearning effect reported for heuristic LLM unlearning.

    6. Why is a model that scores much worse than normal on the forgotten rows not a successful unlearning?

    A retrained model would score those rows like any similar unseen rows. A much worse score marks them as rows the model was pushed away from, so a membership-inference attacker can tell who was removed. Success is matching the retrained model, not maximising forgetting.

    7. Name three places a customer's data may persist after unlearning from the weights.

    Feature stores or training snapshots, embeddings and RAG indexes, backups and logs, adapters and downstream or distilled models, synthetic datasets, vendor copies. Lineage tracking is the real control.
