    Privacy Basics: Why Removing Names Isn't Enough

    Part 1

    What it is

    In one sentenceA file is only anonymous if nobody can work out who a row is, even by matching it with other data. Deleting the name column does not do that.

    Say you prepare a customer file for a vendor. You drop name, Emirates ID, phone and account number. What is left: gender, nationality, date of birth, neighbourhood and a year of transactions. The ticket says "anonymous".

    It is not. Very few people share your date of birth, nationality and neighbourhood. Often nobody does. Those facts act like a fingerprint.

    This has happened with hospital records, film ratings and phone locations. Behaviour identifies people.

    Part 2

    The terms, and how they differ

    • Personal data: anything relating to a person who is identified or could reasonably be identified. A row with no name is still personal data if someone could work out who it is.
    • Pseudonymised: names swapped for a token ("customer 4471"). Like a coat-check ticket, the key still exists. Regulators treat it as personal data.
    • Anonymised: identification is not reasonably possible by anyone, including by linking to other data. This is a high bar.

    So removing names gives you pseudonymisation at best. Whether data is anonymous depends on what an attacker can link it to, not on what you deleted.

    How to measure it: k-anonymity

    Facts that identify without being a name, such as birth date, nationality and area, are called quasi-identifiers. A file is k-anonymous if every combination of them is shared by at least k people.

    k-anonymity is a trade, and it has limits:

    • Shared secrets: if all five people in a group share a sensitive fact (say, a restructured loan), knowing someone is in the group reveals it.
    • One release only: a later release, or a leaked list, can break it.
    • Stronger option: differential privacy adds noise so results barely change whether or not any one person is in the data. See the next lesson, P6.3.
    Part 3

    How to handle personal data in a model project

    1. Write the purpose in one sentence before choosing features. Say, "predict which customers will leave in 90 days".
    2. Minimise. Like packing for a weekend, take only what the trip needs. For each feature ask what it adds. Train with and without the most sensitive fields; if accuracy barely moves, drop them and record why.
    3. Check the purpose limit. Data given to open an account is not automatically free to train a marketing model. A new use needs a decision.
    4. Confirm the legal basis. Consent is weak in banking: a customer who must agree to open an account has not freely chosen. Contract, legal obligation or legitimate interest usually fit better. Ask the data protection officer (DPO).
    5. Set retention for training data and logs. The safest data is data you never collected.

    Models and prompts leak too

    • Models remember. Attacks can test whether a person was in the training set, and overfitted models leak most.
    • Prompts are disclosures. Pasting a customer complaint into an external chatbot sends personal data to a third party.
    • Proxies carry what you removed. A postcode can stand in for nationality. Dropping a column does not drop the information. To test, try predicting the removed field from the rest; if you can, it is still there.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    ApproachWhat it doesWeak spot
    Remove namesHides direct identifiersOther fields still identify
    PseudonymiseSwaps names for tokens, keeps joins possibleStill personal data; the key can leak
    k-anonymityMakes people hard to single outLoses detail; groups can share a secret
    Differential privacyAdds noise to published resultsCosts accuracy, hurts small groups
    • Strong at: minimisation costs almost nothing and removes risk at the source.
    • Weak at: no technique makes a rich file safe to share freely. Treat it as personal data when in doubt.

    In banking, two "safe" extracts can combine into an identifying one, so review the combination a vendor will hold, not each file alone.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • "We removed the names, so it is anonymous"

      Count the unique combinations of quasi-identifiers. Many one-of-a-kind rows means it is not anonymous.

    • Joining tables that were safe separately

      Ask what other data the recipient already holds, and review the combination.

    • Using consent as a blanket

      "The customer accepted the terms" does not cover every new purpose. Ask what purpose was stated and what basis the DPO recorded.

    • Sensitive fields coming back through proxies

      Merchant mix or postcode can stand in for what you removed.

    1. Why can a file with only gender, nationality and birth date still identify people?

    The combination is rare. Many people are alone in their group, so anyone with a named list holding the same fields can look them up.

    2. What does k = 5 guarantee, and what does it not?

    An attacker has at least five candidates for anyone. It does not stop all five sharing a sensitive fact, or a later release breaking it.

    3. What is the difference between pseudonymised and anonymised data?

    Pseudonymised data swaps names for tokens and can be re-linked, so it is still personal data. Anonymised data cannot reasonably be linked back to anyone.

    4. Dropping five sensitive fields from a churn model costs 0.01 AUC (an accuracy score). What do you do?

    Drop them. The cost is probably within noise while the exposure falls sharply. State the cost and record the choice.

    5. Why is consent a weak basis for bank models?

    It must be free and specific. Bank terms are bundled with services the customer needs and do not cover each new purpose.
