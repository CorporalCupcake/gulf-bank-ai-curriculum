    Privacy, Documentation and Oversight: Removing Names Is Not Anonymisation

    Why a dataset with no names can still point at one person, how to collect and keep only what a model needs, how to write down what a model is for and where it fails, and how to let customers and staff challenge what it decides.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePrivacy is protected mainly by collecting less and using data only for the stated purpose, because "anonymised" data is usually just data that is harder to link; and trust in a model is built by writing down its purpose and limits, and by giving a real person the power to overrule it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist prepares a customer file for a vendor who will build a churn model. She does what every checklist says: drops name, Emirates ID, phone number and account number. What is left is gender, nationality, date of birth, neighbourhood, employer sector and a year of transactions. "It's anonymous now," says the ticket.

    It is not. Think of how many customers share your date of birth, your nationality and your neighbourhood. In a bank with a few hundred thousand customers, often nobody does. Those three facts are a fingerprint. Anyone who holds a second list with the same fields and names attached (a leaked directory, a marketing list, a colleague who knows you) can look you up and read your whole transaction history.

    This is a very old result. In the 1990s a US state published "anonymised" hospital records. A researcher matched them to the public voter roll on postcode, birth date and sex, and found the governor's own record. Later work on Netflix ratings, phone locations and card transactions found the same thing: behaviour is identifying. A few purchases, with dates and shops, single out most people.

    Three ideas carry the rest of the lesson.

    • The safest data is data you never collected. Every field you hold can leak, be subpoenaed, be misused or be sold by mistake. Minimisation is a design rule that works before any cleverness.
    • Privacy is about purpose as much as secrecy. Data given for one reason (opening an account) is not free to use for any other (training a marketing model) just because the bank holds it.
    • Trust in a model needs two further things: a written record and a human exit. A model card says what the model was built for and where it breaks. Human oversight and a way to contest decisions mean a wrong output can be caught, fixed and learned from.
    Hold onto thisRemoving direct identifiers is pseudonymisation at best. Whether data is anonymous depends on what an attacker can link it to, not on what you deleted. When in doubt, treat it as personal data and use the controls that go with that.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Privacy basics: minimisation, consent, and the limits of anonymisation

    Personal data is anything that relates to an identified or identifiable person. The second word is where the trouble lives. A row with no name is still personal data if someone could reasonably work out who it is. Data protection laws in both the UAE and Saudi Arabia follow this broad idea, as does the EU's GDPR on which they are loosely modelled.

    Three working principles, shared by almost every regime:

    • Minimisation. Collect and keep only what the stated purpose needs. For an ML team this means asking of each feature: what does it add, and is that worth the exposure?
    • Purpose limitation. Use data for the purpose it was collected for, or a clearly compatible one. Training a new model is a new use and needs a decision, not an assumption.
    • Lawful basis. Consent is one basis, and a weak one in banking, because a customer who must agree to open an account has not freely chosen. Others usually include performing a contract, a legal obligation (AML and KYC retention are the classic cases), and the bank's legitimate interest. Which bases a given law allows, and with what conditions, is a legal question. Ask the data protection officer and compliance; don't guess.

    Pseudonymised is not anonymous

    Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with a token (customer 4471 instead of a name). It is valuable: it limits who can see what, and it lets you join tables. But the key exists somewhere, and the remaining fields can still identify. Regulators treat pseudonymised data as personal data.

    Anonymisation means that identification is no longer reasonably possible by anyone, using any means likely to be tried, including linking with other data. That is a high bar. The test is about the attacker's options, so it can change over time as more data leaks and tools improve.

    The classic measure is k-anonymity: every combination of the identifying-but-not-name fields (the quasi-identifiers) is shared by at least k people. If k = 5, an attacker who knows your birth date, nationality and area still faces at least five candidates. Simulation 2 shows what it costs to get there.

    The simulation shows the three honest limits of this family of defences.

    • It is a trade. Every step that protects people removes detail the model might have used. There is no setting that gives both.
    • k-anonymity does not stop attribute disclosure. If all seven people in a group have the flag, knowing someone is in the group tells you they have it. Refinements such as l-diversity and t-closeness address that; each has its own gaps.
    • It protects one release against known attackers. It says nothing about what happens when the next release, or a leaked list, arrives. The 2019 estimate by Rocher and colleagues found that 15 demographic attributes could correctly re-identify 99.98% of Americans in a typical dataset.

    Differential privacy is the stronger, mathematical alternative: you add calibrated noise so that the result of a query or training run is almost the same whether or not any one person is in the data. It gives a provable guarantee with a number (epsilon) attached, at a cost in accuracy. It is a topic of its own (A11.2); here the point is that it is the only family that talks honestly about "any other data an attacker might hold".

    Models and prompts are data stores too

    Removing identifiers from a file is not the end of the story for machine learning.

    • Models remember. A model trained on personal records can leak them. Membership inference tests whether a given person's record was in the training set; extraction attacks have pulled verbatim text, including names, emails and phone numbers, out of large language models. Overfit models leak most (R11.2).
    • Prompts are disclosures. A staff member who pastes a customer complaint into an external chatbot has sent personal data to a third party. Policy, redaction at the gateway and approved internal tools are the controls (A15.2).
    • Features can encode what you removed. Merchant categories can reveal health or religion. A postcode can stand in for nationality. Dropping a column does not drop the information (see fairness).
    • Where the data sits matters. The UAE and Saudi laws restrict transfers of personal data abroad in various ways, and the central banks add their own expectations for outsourcing and cloud. Details change; the data protection officer owns them.

    A practical minimisation routine for any new model:

    1. Write the purpose in one sentence before choosing features.
    2. For each candidate feature, record why it is needed and who approved its use.
    3. Run the model with and without the sensitive or high-exposure fields. If the gain is inside the noise (F2.1), drop them.
    4. Set retention: how long training data and logs live, and who can read them. Deleting the training set but keeping the features table is not deletion.
    5. Prefer aggregates and tokens to raw values (counts of cash withdrawals, not the list of ATMs visited).

    Model cards and documentation

    A model card is a short, standard document that travels with a model. Mitchell and colleagues proposed it in 2019 after noticing that models were being passed around with almost no description of what they were meant for or how they behaved on different groups. Its sibling, the datasheet for datasets, does the same for the data: where it came from, who is in it, what was left out.

    In a bank this is not a nicety. A validator (G3.2) cannot assess a model that nobody described. A new owner inherits a model in a year's time and needs to know what it was never meant to do. The card is how the intended use and known limits survive staff turnover.

    A card does not need to be a PDF. It can live next to the model in the registry as a structured file, and be partly generated from the pipeline (training data version, metrics, feature list). The parts that need a human, such as intended use and limitations, should have a named author and a review date.

    # model_card.yaml  (kept in the registry beside the model artefact)
name: retail-churn-90d
version: 3.2
owner: retail-analytics
intended_use: rank retail current-account customers by 90-day closure risk
              to prioritise retention calls. Not for credit or pricing decisions.
out_of_scope: [corporate accounts, customers under 6 months tenure]
training_data: {table: churn_features_v14, period: 2023-01..2025-12, label: "account closed within 90d"}
metrics: {auc: 0.77, ci95: [0.76, 0.78], recall_at_top_5pct: 0.31}
slices: {arabic_speaking_calls: {auc: 0.71}, new_to_bank: {auc: 0.68}}
limitations: weaker for new customers; drifts after pricing changes
sensitive_fields: none used; merchant detail excluded (purpose review 2026-03)
review_by: 2026-12-01

    Human oversight and contestability

    Two ideas that people run together.

    • Human oversight is about the bank's side: people who can watch the system, understand its limits, and stop or override it.
    • Contestability is about the customer's or employee's side: a route to ask why, to supply facts the system lacked, and to get a human re-decision.

    Data protection regimes in several places give individuals rights around decisions made solely by automated means, such as a right to object or to ask for human intervention. The EU's GDPR (Article 22) is the best known; the EU AI Act requires human oversight for high-risk systems (Article 14), and credit scoring of individuals is on its high-risk list. The UAE and Saudi laws each have their own provisions on automated processing and their own timelines for detailed rules. I have not verified the exact current wording of those provisions, so check the text and your compliance team before quoting one in a meeting.

    Having a human is not the same as having oversight

    Research on human-in-the-loop designs (see Green, 2022) finds that people often cannot do what the policy asks. Four failure patterns recur:

    • Automation bias. Reviewers agree with the machine, especially when busy. A reviewer who approves 98% of recommendations in four seconds each is a signature, not a check.
    • No information. The reviewer sees a score and a button. Without the underlying facts and the main reasons, there is nothing to evaluate.
    • No authority or incentive. Overriding is slower, needs a justification, and is blamed if wrong; following the model is safe.
    • No feedback. Overrides are not recorded, so the model never learns from the places where people knew better.

    Design for the real behaviour. Track override rate, agreement time and reversal-on-appeal rate. A rate of zero overrides means either a perfect model or a rubber stamp, and the second is far more likely. Deliberately insert known-wrong cases to test whether reviewers notice. Match the intensity of review to the stakes: a person on every marketing offer is wasteful, while a declined mortgage or an account closure deserves a real second look.

    What a contest route needs

    • Reasons the customer can use. "Debt-service ratio above policy" is actionable. A list of SHAP values is not. Counterfactual wording ("with AED 1,500 lower monthly commitments this would be approved") helps most, if it is true and checked for consistency (F8.1).
    • A way to correct the data. Many wrong decisions come from wrong inputs: an employer recorded incorrectly, a closed loan still showing.
    • A reviewer who can decide. With access to the facts, independence from the model's builders, and a deadline.
    • A learning loop. Reversals feed monitoring and data fixes (G3.3).

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Data sharing with vendors and group entities. Every extract sent out for modelling is a re-identification risk. Use minimised, tokenised extracts and contractual limits, not "we removed the names".
    • KYC and onboarding. Identity documents, face images and biometrics are the most sensitive data the bank holds. Document AI and eKYC models inherit those obligations (B3.1).
    • Internal copilots. Staff assistants that search customer files must respect who is allowed to see what (B7.2).
    • Credit decisions. The clearest contest cases: declined limits, repriced loans, closed accounts. Reasons and review routes are part of conduct expectations as well as privacy.
    • Fraud and AML. Blocking a card or filing a suspicious report involves human judgement and, for AML, strict confidentiality about the filing itself. Contestability here is limited by law; the design question is what can be explained and who reviews.
    • Call-centre analytics. Recordings in Arabic and English contain names, balances and health remarks. Redact before the transcript goes into any model or prompt.

    Where the field is now

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each of these is common in real projects. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "We removed the names, so it's anonymous"

      The file is shared with a vendor or posted to an internal data lake with open access.

      Spot itCount the unique combinations of the quasi-identifiers (Simulation 1). If many rows are one of a kind, it is not anonymous.
    • Rare values left in

      The one customer of an uncommon nationality in a small branch, or the single account holding AED 40 million.

      Spot itLook at the tails of every field, and the smallest cell in every cross-tab you plan to release.
    • Joining tables that were "safe" separately

      Two minimised extracts combine into an identifying one.

      Spot itAsk what other datasets the recipient already has, and review the combination, not each file.
    • Consent used as a blanket

      "The customer accepted the terms, so we can train on anything."

      Spot itAsk what the specific purpose was, whether it was stated at collection, and what basis the DPO has recorded.
    • Sensitive fields back in through proxies

      Merchant mix, device language or postcode stand in for what was removed.

      Spot itTrain a small model to predict the removed field from the remaining ones. If it succeeds, it is still there.
    • Customer text pasted into an external chatbot

      A well-meaning analyst summarises complaints with a public tool.

      Spot itCheck gateway logs for outbound traffic to public AI sites, and make an approved internal route easier than the shortcut.
    • A model card written once, after launch

      It describes the model as it was at approval and nobody has touched it since.

      Spot itCompare the card's version, training period and metrics with the production model. Check the review date.
    • Averages on the card, nothing by segment

      "AUC 0.84" with no breakdown by language, channel or tenure.

      Spot itLook for a slices table with intervals. If it is absent, ask for it.
    • The rubber-stamp reviewer

      A human is "in the loop" but agrees nearly every time.

      Spot itPull the override rate and median review time. Under 1% overrides and a few seconds per case is a warning.
    • A contest route nobody can find, or one that re-runs the same model

      Customers are told to "contact your branch", and the branch has no tool or authority.

      Spot itDo a mystery-customer test: file a contest and time how long it takes to reach someone who can change the outcome.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Taking names off a file does not make it anonymous: a date of birth, a nationality and a neighbourhood are enough to find most people. So the safest approach is to share and keep less in the first place, be clear what each dataset is for, and treat anything that could be linked back as personal data. For each model we should also keep a short document that says what it is for and where it fails, and make sure a customer who is declined can reach a person who is able to change the decision.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd treat pseudonymised extracts as personal data and test re-identification risk by counting unique quasi-identifier combinations before any release. I'd run an ablation to drop high-exposure features that add less than the confidence interval, keep a model card with intended and out-of-scope use, slice metrics with intervals and a review date, and measure human oversight with override rates, review times and seeded errors rather than assuming it works. Where stronger guarantees are needed, I'd look at differential privacy or a controlled environment.”
    “We've removed all the identifiers. Can we send it to the vendor?”

    Not on that basis alone. What remains may still identify people when combined with other data the vendor or a third party holds. I'd check how many records are unique on the remaining fields, remove or coarsen the rare ones, drop fields the vendor's task does not need, and put contractual limits and a retention date in place. Then the DPO should confirm the basis for sharing.

    “Customers agreed to our terms. Isn't that enough consent to train on their data?”

    Probably not by itself. Consent has to be specific and, in banking, is rarely truly free. The better question is what lawful basis the DPO has recorded for this purpose, and whether the use is compatible with why the data was collected. Training for fraud prevention is a different case from training for marketing, and I would not assume they are treated alike.

    “What is the difference between pseudonymisation and anonymisation?”

    Pseudonymisation swaps identifiers for tokens, and the bank (or someone) can reverse it or link through other fields; it is still personal data. Anonymisation means identification is not reasonably possible by anyone using likely means, which is a much harder standard and is tested against what an attacker could link to, not just what we deleted.

    “Is a model trained on customer data itself personal data?”

    It can be. Models can memorise and leak training records, and regulators such as the EDPB have said that it depends on testing how easily personal data can be extracted. I'd treat a model trained on sensitive data as sensitive until we have tested for memorisation, and reduce risk with less data, regularisation, and where needed differential privacy.

    “Isn't a model card just paperwork?”

    It is if nobody uses it. Used well, it is how the intended use and weaknesses survive staff changes, and what a validator starts from. The test of a good card is whether a new owner could decide, from it alone, if a proposed new use is in scope. I'd generate the factual parts from the pipeline and assign a named person for the judgement parts.

    “We have a human approving every decision. Isn't that oversight?”

    Only if the human has the information, time and authority to disagree. I'd look at the override rate, how long reviews take, and whether overrides are ever recorded and used. If reviewers agree in seconds 99% of the time, we have a stamp, not oversight. I would test it with a few seeded wrong cases.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A file has gender (2 values), nationality (9) and birth year (50). At most how many distinct combinations can there be, and why do 20,000 customers still leave some rows unique?

    2 × 9 × 50 = 900. Average group size is 22, but the groups are very uneven: a rare nationality born in a rare year in a small cell can have a single member. Add birth month, day or area and the combinations exceed the number of people, so most become unique.

    2. What does k = 5 guarantee, and what does it not?

    Each combination of quasi-identifiers appears at least 5 times, so an attacker who knows those fields has at least 5 candidates. It does not stop attribute disclosure (all 5 may share a sensitive value), does not hold against extra data in a later release, and does not cover fields you did not count as quasi-identifiers.

    3. A churn model uses 12 fields. Dropping the 5 most sensitive moves AUC from 0.779 to 0.769. How do you present the choice?

    The gain from those fields is about 0.01, probably within the confidence interval from a test set of a few thousand churners, while exposure rises sharply. Recommend dropping them, state the measured cost, and record that the choice was made on purpose.

    4. Why is "the customer consented" a weak foundation for bank ML projects?

    Consent must be specific, informed and freely given. Bank terms are bundled with services the customer needs, so it is rarely free; and a general acceptance does not cover each new purpose. Other bases (contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest where allowed) usually fit better, and the DPO decides which.

    5. Name four things a model card should contain that an AUC figure does not tell you.

    Intended and out-of-scope use; the data and label definition; performance by slice with intervals at the operating point; limitations and drift expectations; owner and review date. Any four.

    6. A reviewer overrides the model 0.4% of the time, in about 6 seconds a case. Is that good oversight? What would you do?

    It is more likely rubber-stamping than a perfect model. Check whether reviewers see the facts and reasons, whether overriding is hard or penalised, and test with seeded known-wrong cases. Fix the process, not just the people.

    7. Why is a list of SHAP values a poor reply to a declined customer?

    It describes the model's internals rather than what the customer can change or dispute, can be unstable or misleading, and shows no route to correcting wrong data. Give principal reasons in plain terms, ideally with an action ("reduce monthly commitments by AED X") and the route to request human review.
