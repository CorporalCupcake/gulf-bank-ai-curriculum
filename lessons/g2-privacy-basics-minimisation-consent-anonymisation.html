    Privacy, Documentation and Oversight: Removing Names Is Not Anonymisation

    Why a dataset with no names can still point at one person, how to collect and keep only what a model needs, how to write down what a model is for and where it fails, and how to let customers and staff challenge what it decides.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePrivacy is protected mainly by collecting less and using data only for the stated purpose, because "anonymised" data is usually just data that is harder to link; and trust in a model is built by writing down its purpose and limits, and by giving a real person the power to overrule it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist prepares a customer file for a vendor building a churn model. She drops name, Emirates ID, phone and account number. What is left: gender, nationality, date of birth, neighbourhood, employer sector and a year of transactions. "It's anonymous now," says the ticket.

    It is not. How many customers share your date of birth, nationality and neighbourhood? In a bank with a few hundred thousand customers, often nobody. Those three facts act like a fingerprint. Anyone with a second list holding the same fields plus names (a leaked directory, a marketing list) can look you up and read your transactions.

    This is old news. In the 1990s a US state published "anonymised" hospital records; a researcher matched them to the voter roll on postcode, birth date and sex and found the governor's record. Netflix ratings, phone locations and card transactions show the same: behaviour is identifying.

    Three ideas carry the lesson.

    • The safest data is data you never collected. A file you don't hold can't leak. Minimisation works before any cleverness.
    • Purpose matters as much as secrecy. Data given to open an account is not free for training a marketing model just because the bank holds it.
    • Trust needs a written record and a human exit. A model card says what the model is for and where it breaks. Oversight and contestability let a wrong output be caught and fixed.
    Hold onto thisRemoving direct identifiers is pseudonymisation at best. Anonymity depends on what an attacker can link the data to, not on what you deleted. When in doubt, treat it as personal data.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Privacy basics: minimisation, consent, and the limits of anonymisation

    Personal data is anything relating to an identified or identifiable person. A row with no name is still personal data if someone could reasonably work out who it is. UAE and Saudi data protection laws follow this broad idea, as does GDPR.

    Three principles, shared by almost every regime:

    • Minimisation. Like packing for a weekend, take only what the trip needs. For each feature ask: what does it add, and is that worth the exposure?
    • Purpose limitation. Use data for what it was collected for, or something clearly compatible. Training a new model is a new use and needs a decision.
    • Lawful basis. Consent is a weak basis in banking: a customer who must agree to open an account has not freely chosen. Others usually include contract, legal obligation (AML and KYC retention) and legitimate interest. Which bases a law allows is a legal question. Ask the data protection officer (DPO) and compliance.

    Pseudonymised is not anonymous

    Pseudonymisation swaps identifiers for a token (customer 4471 instead of a name). Like a coat-check ticket, the key still exists somewhere, and the other fields can still identify. It limits who sees what and lets you join tables, but regulators treat it as personal data.

    Anonymisation means identification is no longer reasonably possible by anyone, by any likely means, including linking to other data. That is a high bar, tested against the attacker's options, which grow as more data leaks.

    k-anonymity is the classic measure: every combination of the not-a-name-but-identifying fields (quasi-identifiers) is shared by at least k people. At k = 5, an attacker who knows your birth date, nationality and area still faces five candidates. Simulation 2 shows the cost.

    Three honest limits of this family of defences:

    • It is a trade. Every protective step removes detail the model might have used.
    • It doesn't stop attribute disclosure. If all seven people in a group have the flag, knowing someone is in the group tells you they have it. l-diversity and t-closeness address this, each with gaps.
    • It protects one release against known attackers. A later release or leaked list breaks it. Rocher and colleagues (2019) estimated 15 demographic attributes could correctly re-identify 99.98% of Americans in a typical dataset.

    Differential privacy is the stronger, mathematical alternative: calibrated noise makes a query or training run almost the same whether or not any one person is in the data. It gives a provable guarantee (a number, epsilon) at a cost in accuracy, and is the only family that speaks honestly about "any other data an attacker might hold" (A11.2).

    Models and prompts are data stores too

    • Models remember. Membership inference tests whether a person's record was in the training set; extraction attacks have pulled verbatim names, emails and phone numbers out of LLMs. Overfit models leak most (R11.2).
    • Prompts are disclosures. Pasting a customer complaint into an external chatbot sends personal data to a third party. Controls: policy, gateway redaction, approved internal tools (A15.2).
    • Features can encode what you removed. Merchant categories can reveal health or religion; a postcode can stand in for nationality. Dropping a column doesn't drop the information (see fairness).
    • Location matters. UAE and Saudi laws restrict transfers of personal data abroad, and central banks add outsourcing and cloud expectations. The DPO owns the details.

    Minimisation routine for any new model:

    1. Write the purpose in one sentence before choosing features.
    2. Record why each feature is needed and who approved it.
    3. Run with and without sensitive or high-exposure fields. If the gain is inside the noise (F2.1), drop them.
    4. Set retention for training data and logs, and who can read them. Deleting the training set but keeping the features table is not deletion.
    5. Prefer aggregates and tokens to raw values (count of cash withdrawals, not the list of ATMs).

    Model cards and documentation

    A model card is a short, standard document that travels with a model, like the leaflet in a medicine box: what it is for, how it behaves, who should not take it. Mitchell and colleagues proposed it in 2019. Its sibling, the datasheet for datasets, covers the data: source, who is in it, what was left out.

    A validator (G3.2) cannot assess an undescribed model, and a new owner next year needs to know what it was never meant to do. The card carries intended use and limits through staff turnover.

    SectionWhat goes in itWhat goes wrong when it is missing

    DataSource, period, filters, label definition, known gaps. A link to the datasheet and lineage.Nobody knows the label means "chargeback within 60 days", so a labelling change silently breaks the model.
    MethodModel type, key features, how thresholds were set, and what was tried and rejected.The next team repeats failed experiments; the validator cannot reproduce it.

    A card can be a structured file beside the model in the registry, partly generated from the pipeline (data version, metrics, features). Human-judgement parts, such as intended use and limitations, need a named author and review date.

    # model_card.yaml  (kept in the registry beside the model artefact)
name: retail-churn-90d
version: 3.2
owner: retail-analytics
intended_use: rank retail current-account customers by 90-day closure risk
              to prioritise retention calls. Not for credit or pricing decisions.
out_of_scope: [corporate accounts, customers under 6 months tenure]

    Human oversight and contestability

    Two ideas people run together:

    • Human oversight is the bank's side: people who can watch the system, know its limits, and stop or override it.
    • Contestability is the customer's side: a route to ask why, supply facts the system lacked, and get a human re-decision.

    Data protection regimes give individuals rights over solely automated decisions, such as objecting or asking for human intervention. GDPR Article 22 is best known; the EU AI Act (Article 14) requires human oversight for high-risk systems, and credit scoring is on its list. UAE and Saudi laws have their own provisions on automated processing; I have not verified their current wording, so check with compliance before quoting one.

    Having a human is not the same as having oversight

    Research (Green, 2022) finds people often cannot do what the policy asks. Four recurring failures:

    • Automation bias. Busy reviewers agree with the machine. Approving 98% of recommendations in four seconds each is a signature, not a check.
    • No information. A score and a button give nothing to evaluate. Reviewers need the facts and main reasons.
    • No authority or incentive. Overriding is slower, needs justifying and is blamed if wrong; following the model is safe.
    • No feedback. Unrecorded overrides teach the model nothing.

    Track override rate, agreement time and reversal-on-appeal rate. Zero overrides means a perfect model or a rubber stamp, and the second is far likelier. Insert known-wrong cases to see if reviewers notice. Scale review to the stakes: not every marketing offer, but every declined mortgage or account closure.

    What a contest route needs

    • Usable reasons. "Debt-service ratio above policy" is actionable; a list of SHAP values is not. Best is counterfactual: "with AED 1,500 lower monthly commitments this would be approved", if true and consistent (F8.1).
    • A way to correct data. Many wrong decisions come from wrong inputs: a mis-recorded employer, a closed loan still showing.
    • A reviewer who can decide. With the facts, independent of the builders, and a deadline.
    • A learning loop. Reversals feed monitoring and data fixes (G3.3).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "We removed the names, so it's anonymous"

      The file is shared with a vendor or posted to an internal data lake with open access.

      Spot itCount the unique combinations of the quasi-identifiers (Simulation 1). If many rows are one of a kind, it is not anonymous.
    • Rare values left in

      The one customer of an uncommon nationality in a small branch, or the single account holding AED 40 million.

      Spot itLook at the tails of every field, and the smallest cell in every cross-tab you plan to release.
    • Joining tables that were "safe" separately

      Two minimised extracts combine into an identifying one.

      Spot itAsk what other datasets the recipient already has, and review the combination, not each file.
    • Consent used as a blanket

      "The customer accepted the terms, so we can train on anything."

      Spot itAsk what the specific purpose was, whether it was stated at collection, and what basis the DPO has recorded.
    • Sensitive fields back in through proxies

      Merchant mix, device language or postcode stand in for what was removed.

      Spot itTrain a small model to predict the removed field from the remaining ones. If it succeeds, it is still there.
    • Customer text pasted into an external chatbot

      A well-meaning analyst summarises complaints with a public tool.

      Spot itCheck gateway logs for outbound traffic to public AI sites, and make an approved internal route easier than the shortcut.
    • A model card written once, after launch

      It describes the model as it was at approval and nobody has touched it since.

      Spot itCompare the card's version, training period and metrics with the production model. Check the review date.
    • Averages on the card, nothing by segment

      "AUC 0.84" with no breakdown by language, channel or tenure.

      Spot itLook for a slices table with intervals. If it is absent, ask for it.
    • The rubber-stamp reviewer

      A human is "in the loop" but agrees nearly every time.

      Spot itPull the override rate and median review time. Under 1% overrides and a few seconds per case is a warning.
    • A contest route nobody can find, or one that re-runs the same model

      Customers are told to "contact your branch", and the branch has no tool or authority.

      Spot itDo a mystery-customer test: file a contest and time how long it takes to reach someone who can change the outcome.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A file has gender (2 values), nationality (9) and birth year (50). At most how many distinct combinations can there be, and why do 20,000 customers still leave some rows unique?

    2 × 9 × 50 = 900. Average group size is 22, but groups are uneven: a rare nationality in a rare birth year can have one member. Add birth month, day or area and combinations exceed the number of people, so most become unique.

    2. What does k = 5 guarantee, and what does it not?

    Every quasi-identifier combination appears at least 5 times, so an attacker has at least 5 candidates. It does not stop attribute disclosure (all 5 may share a sensitive value), later releases with extra data, or fields you didn't count as quasi-identifiers.

    3. A churn model uses 12 fields. Dropping the 5 most sensitive moves AUC from 0.779 to 0.769. How do you present the choice?

    Those fields add about 0.01 AUC, probably inside the confidence interval for a test set of a few thousand churners, while exposure rises sharply. Recommend dropping them, state the measured cost, and record the choice.

    4. Why is "the customer consented" a weak foundation for bank ML projects?

    Consent must be specific, informed and free. Bank terms are bundled with services the customer needs, so it is rarely free, and general acceptance doesn't cover each new purpose. Contract, legal obligation or legitimate interest usually fit better; the DPO decides.

    5. Name four things a model card should contain that an AUC figure does not tell you.

    Intended and out-of-scope use; the data and label definition; performance by slice with intervals at the operating point; limitations and drift expectations; owner and review date. Any four.

    6. A reviewer overrides the model 0.4% of the time, in about 6 seconds a case. Is that good oversight? What would you do?

    Likelier a rubber stamp than a perfect model. Check whether reviewers see facts and reasons, whether overriding is hard or penalised, and seed known-wrong cases. Fix the process, not just the people.

    7. Why is a list of SHAP values a poor reply to a declined customer?

    It describes model internals, not what the customer can change or dispute, can be unstable, and offers no route to fix wrong data. Give plain principal reasons, ideally with an action ("reduce monthly commitments by AED X") and the route to human review.
