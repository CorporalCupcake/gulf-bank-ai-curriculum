    Privacy, Documentation and Oversight: Removing Names Is Not Anonymisation

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist prepares a customer file for a vendor. She drops name, Emirates ID, phone and account number. What is left: gender, nationality, date of birth, neighbourhood, employer sector and a year of transactions. "It's anonymous now," says the ticket.

    It is not. How many customers share your date of birth, nationality and neighbourhood? Often nobody. Those facts act like a fingerprint. Anyone with a second list holding the same fields plus names can look you up and read your transactions. The same has happened with hospital records, Netflix ratings and phone locations: behaviour is identifying.

    • The safest data is data you never collected. A file you don't hold can't leak.
    • Purpose matters as much as secrecy. Data given to open an account is not free for training a marketing model.
    • Trust needs a written record and a human exit.
    Hold onto thisRemoving names is pseudonymisation at best. Anonymity depends on what an attacker can link the data to, not on what you deleted. When in doubt, treat it as personal data.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Privacy basics: minimisation, consent, anonymisation

    Personal data is anything relating to an identified or identifiable person. A row with no name is still personal data if someone could reasonably work out who it is. UAE and Saudi laws follow this broad idea, as does GDPR.

    • Minimisation. Like packing for a weekend, take only what the trip needs. For each feature ask what it adds and whether that is worth the exposure.
    • Purpose limitation. Use data for what it was collected for. Training a new model is a new use and needs a decision.
    • Lawful basis. Consent is weak in banking: a customer who must agree to open an account has not freely chosen. Contract, legal obligation and legitimate interest usually fit better. Ask the data protection officer (DPO).

    Pseudonymised is not anonymous

    Pseudonymisation swaps identifiers for a token (customer 4471 instead of a name). Like a coat-check ticket, the key still exists, and the other fields can still identify. Regulators treat it as personal data. Anonymisation means identification is not reasonably possible by anyone, including by linking to other data. That is a high bar, and it rises as more data leaks.

    k-anonymity is the classic measure: every combination of identifying-but-not-a-name fields (quasi-identifiers) is shared by at least k people. At k = 5, an attacker who knows your birth date, nationality and area still faces five candidates. Simulation 2 shows the cost.

    Limits of this family of defences:

    • It is a trade. Every protective step removes detail the model might have used.
    • It doesn't stop attribute disclosure. If all seven people in a group have the flag, knowing someone is in the group reveals it.
    • It protects one release against known attackers. A later release or leaked list breaks it.

    Differential privacy is the stronger alternative: added noise makes results almost the same whether or not any one person is in the data, at a cost in accuracy (A11.2).

    Models and prompts are data stores too

    • Models remember. Attacks can test whether a person was in the training set, or pull names and emails out of LLMs. Overfit models leak most (R11.2).
    • Prompts are disclosures. Pasting a customer complaint into an external chatbot sends personal data to a third party (A15.2).
    • Features can encode what you removed. A postcode can stand in for nationality. Dropping a column doesn't drop the information (see fairness).

    A minimisation routine for any new model: write the purpose in one sentence before choosing features; record why each feature is needed; run with and without high-exposure fields and drop them if the gain is inside the noise (F2.1); set retention for training data and logs; prefer aggregates and tokens to raw values.

    Model cards and documentation

    A model card is a short document that travels with a model, like the leaflet in a medicine box: what it is for, how it behaves, who should not take it. Its sibling, the datasheet, covers the data: source, who is in it, what was left out. A validator (G3.2) cannot assess an undescribed model, and a new owner next year needs to know what it was never meant to do.

    Human oversight and contestability

    • Human oversight is the bank's side: people who can watch the system, know its limits, and stop or override it.
    • Contestability is the customer's side: a route to ask why, supply facts the system lacked, and get a human re-decision.

    Data protection regimes give individuals rights over solely automated decisions (GDPR Article 22; EU AI Act Article 14 for high-risk systems such as credit scoring). I have not verified the current UAE and Saudi wording, so check with compliance before quoting it.

    Having a human is not the same as having oversight

    • Automation bias. Busy reviewers agree with the machine. Approving 98% of recommendations in four seconds each is a signature, not a check.
    • No information or authority. A score and a button give nothing to evaluate, and overriding is slower and blamed if wrong.
    • No feedback. Unrecorded overrides teach the model nothing.

    Track override rate and review time. Zero overrides means a perfect model or a rubber stamp, and the second is far likelier. Scale review to the stakes: not every marketing offer, but every declined mortgage.

    What a contest route needs

    • Usable reasons. "Debt-service ratio above policy" is actionable; a list of SHAP values is not. Best is counterfactual: "with AED 1,500 lower monthly commitments this would be approved" (F8.1).
    • A way to correct data. Many wrong decisions come from wrong inputs, like a closed loan still showing.
    • A reviewer who can decide, independent of the builders, with a deadline, and a loop that feeds reversals back into monitoring (G3.3).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "We removed the names, so it's anonymous"

      The file goes to a vendor or an open data lake. Rare values stand out most.

      Spot itCount the unique combinations of quasi-identifiers (Simulation 1), and check the tails of every field. Many one-of-a-kind rows means not anonymous.
    • Joining tables that were "safe" separately

      Two minimised extracts combine into an identifying one.

      Spot itAsk what other datasets the recipient already has, and review the combination, not each file.
    • Consent used as a blanket

      "The customer accepted the terms, so we can train on anything."

      Spot itAsk what purpose was stated at collection and what basis the DPO has recorded.
    • Sensitive fields back in through proxies, or out through chatbots

      Merchant mix or postcode stands in for what was removed; or an analyst pastes complaints into a public AI tool.

      Spot itTrain a small model to predict the removed field from the rest; if it succeeds, it is still there. Check gateway logs for outbound traffic to public AI sites.
    • A model card written once, or averages with no segments

      The card describes the model as approved, shows "AUC 0.84" and nothing by language, channel or tenure.

      Spot itCompare the card's version and metrics with production, check the review date, and look for a slices table.
    • The rubber-stamp reviewer, or a contest route that goes nowhere

      A human is "in the loop" but agrees nearly every time, or customers are told to "contact your branch", which has no authority.

      Spot itUnder 1% overrides and a few seconds per case is a warning. Run a mystery-customer test and time how long it takes to reach someone who can change the outcome.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A file has gender, nationality and birth year only. Why can some of 20,000 customers still be unique, and what happens as you add birth month, day or area?

    Groups are uneven: a rare nationality in a rare birth year can have one member. Add more fields and the number of possible combinations passes the number of people, so most become unique.

    2. What does k = 5 guarantee, and what does it not?

    An attacker has at least 5 candidates for anyone. It does not stop attribute disclosure (all 5 may share a sensitive value) or later releases with extra data.

    3. A churn model uses 12 fields. Dropping the 5 most sensitive moves AUC from 0.779 to 0.769. How do you present the choice?

    About 0.01 AUC is probably inside the noise, while exposure rises sharply. Drop them, state the cost, record the choice.

    4. Why is "the customer consented" a weak foundation for bank ML projects?

    Consent must be free and specific. Bank terms are bundled with services the customer needs, and don't cover each new purpose. Contract, legal obligation or legitimate interest fit better.

    5. Name four things a model card should contain that an AUC figure does not tell you.

    Intended and out-of-scope use; the data and label definition; performance by slice with intervals; limitations and drift expectations; owner and review date. Any four.

    6. A reviewer overrides the model 0.4% of the time, in about 6 seconds a case. Is that good oversight? What would you do?

    Likelier a rubber stamp. Check whether reviewers see facts and reasons, whether overriding is penalised, and seed known-wrong cases. Fix the process.

    7. Why is a list of SHAP values a poor reply to a declined customer?

    It describes model internals, not what the customer can change or dispute. Give plain principal reasons, ideally with an action and the route to human review.
