    Loss Functions, Learning Rates and What Training Really Optimises

    A model optimises exactly what you wrote down, including its blind spots. Why squared error chases outliers, why a cash forecast should not aim for the average, why cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes, and how learning rate, schedule and batch size decide whether training works at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining is a search for the lowest point of the loss you chose, so the loss defines what "good" means (mean, median, a high quantile, a calibrated probability), and the learning rate, schedule and batch size are the dials that decide whether the search arrives, bounces, or blows up.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model learns whatever lowers the number you gave it, not what you meant. That number is the loss. Example: a treasury team trains an ATM cash forecast on squared error. It is right on average, yet short on about half of all days, and running short costs several times more than holding extra. Nobody said so, so the model never learned to lean high.

    Training is walking downhill in fog: loss is height, and the gradient (F1.3) is the slope under your feet. Two dials control the walk. The learning rate is the step size: too small and you crawl, too big and you leap across the valley. The batch is how many examples you use to feel the slope: small batches are noisy, big ones are truer but dearer.

    • Every loss is a hidden answer to "what number do you want?" Squared error: the mean. Absolute error: the median. Tilted absolute error: a chosen quantile. Cross-entropy: an honest probability.
    • The training loss and the reported metric can differ, and often should. Trouble starts when they disagree unnoticed (F7).
    • Most first-week "the model doesn't work" is an optimisation problem: learning rate, schedule or batch size, not the model.
    Hold onto thisBefore training, finish: "being wrong by AED X in this direction costs the bank Y". The loss is that sentence in mathematics. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to choose a loss.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Regression losses: mean, median, quantile

    The residual is actual minus predicted. Each loss turns that miss into a penalty, and each has a hidden answer to "what number do you want?"

    • Squared error (MSE) predicts the mean. Big misses count quadratically. Use it when outliers are real signal.
    • Absolute error (MAE) predicts the median. Every unit of miss costs the same. Use it when outliers are noise.
    • Huber behaves like squared error for ordinary misses and absolute error for outliers. Use it when a few bad rows should not steer everything.
    • Quantile (pinball) tilts the penalty so under- and over-prediction cost differently, and predicts a chosen quantile. Use it when misses are asymmetric, or you need an interval.

    In Simulation 1, one outlier drags the mean a long way and the median almost nowhere. If a huge withdrawal day is real and treasury must fund it, the mean carries that information. If it is a glitch, the median protects you. The outlier question is a business question.

    The quantile loss matters most in banking because costs are rarely symmetric. Like a baker: too few loaves loses sales, too many wastes dough. If a shortfall costs a and idle cash costs b, the best forecast is this quantile:

    τ=aa+b

    If a shortfall costs four times idle cash, τ = 0.8: forecast the level demand stays under 80% of the time, accepting a shortfall about 1 day in 5. Squared error aims near 0.5, which is wrong for this business by construction.

    Classification: cross-entropy and why it is the default

    Think of a forecaster who says "1% chance of rain" and gets soaked: confident wrongness should be punished severely. For yes/no problems (fraud, default) the model outputs a probability, and cross-entropy (log loss) charges for how surprised it was. On a real fraud, a model that said 50% takes a small penalty, one that said 1% a big one, one that said 0.001% a huge one. The penalty has no ceiling.

    Logistic regression (F2.5), neural classifiers and every language model's next-token loss (M1) are all cross-entropy. It also rewards honesty: it is lowest when the model reports the true probability, which is why calibration checks (F7.4) mean something.

    The push-to-correct stats are the real lesson. With cross-entropy, a confidently wrong model gets a big corrective push. Squared error on probabilities goes quiet exactly when the model is saturated and most wrong, so it barely learns it is wrong. That, not taste, is why cross-entropy is the default.

    Variants you will meet:

    • Class weights multiply the loss on rare positives. They inflate probabilities by a known factor (undone in F3.5), so the output's meaning changes.
    • Focal loss and label smoothing down-weight easy cases or soften the 0/1 target. They can help with extreme imbalance or over-confidence, but they distort calibration: check it.
    • Business losses weight a missed fraud by its AED exposure. Powerful, but it ties the model to today's cost estimates, so document them.

    Learning rate, momentum and schedules

    Each step moves the weights a little way downhill; the learning rate is the stride length. Too small and training crawls. Too big and each step overshoots the valley by more than the last, so the loss climbs and ends in NaN. Real losses are canyons: steep walls, gently sloping floor. The steep walls cap the stride, so the floor is crossed slowly.

    Remedies layered on the same step:

    • Momentum averages past gradients, like a rolling ball: steps along the canyon floor add up and the sideways zig-zag cancels.
    • Adam / AdamW give each parameter its own step size based on its recent gradients. AdamW is the transformer default.
    • Schedules change the rate over time: big steps early to cover ground, small steps late to settle rather than bounce.

    Schedules you will meet:

    • Cosine decay: smooth fall from peak to near zero.
    • Warmup: start near zero and ramp up. Early gradients are wild, and a big first step can wreck a transformer's weights beyond recovery.
    • Warmup–stable–decay (WSD): ramp up, hold flat, then decay quickly at the end. Lets you extend training later.

    When a run misbehaves, read the loss curve first, not the final metric.

    Batch size and gradient noise

    Think of polling: 16 people give a rough read, 1,000 a steadier one. Each step uses a batch, so the slope is right on average but noisy.

    • Noise sets a floor. Near the bottom the true slope is zero but the noisy one is not, so the weights jitter. To go lower, shrink the learning rate (a schedule) or grow the batch.
    • Bigger batches buy parallelism, not unlimited speed. Past a point, extra examples are mostly wasted.

    Change the batch size and you usually retune the rate: roughly scale it up with the batch, then confirm with a short sweep.

    • The learning rate is the most sensitive dial. Across a factor of ten you go from "too slow" to "fine" to "diverges". Sweep it on a log scale first (F4.9).
    • A decaying schedule is not cosmetic. A fixed rate leaves a noise floor; decay lets the model settle.
    • The loss curve is a diagnostic. Smooth and falling: healthy. Flat and high: rate too low or stuck. Spikes: rate too high or bad data. NaN: diverged. Validation rising while train falls: overfitting (F6.3).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Default squared error on an asymmetric problem

      A forecast right on average but short on half the days, where shortfalls are expensive.

      Spot itAsk what a miss costs in each direction, and which quantile the model aims at.
    • Outlier handling by accident

      One huge exposure drags a mean-based model; a median-based one quietly ignores rare events the business cares about.

      Spot itRefit without the top 1% of targets. If the answer moves a lot, the loss is deciding for you.
    • Training loss and reported metric disagree

      The loss falls while the metric stays flat or worsens.

      Spot itTrack both on validation data. If they diverge, change what you optimise.
    • Treating a weighted or smoothed loss's output as a probability

      Class weights, focal loss or label smoothing shift scores, then the number feeds a threshold or expected-loss formula.

      Spot itCompare mean predicted probability with the actual rate on a holdout. A gap means recalibrate (F7.4).
    • Learning rate copied from a different setup, or no schedule

      A recipe tuned at batch 256 reused at 4,096, or a constant rate that "plateaus" and gets blamed on exhausted data.

      Spot itLoss spikes or plateaus. Rerun a short log-spaced sweep of the rate and decay it over the last 20–30% of steps.
    • Judging runs by the final number, or NaNs and silent divergence

      Two models differ by 0.4 points and nobody looked at the curves; or the loss goes NaN.

      Spot itAsk for train and validation curves. For NaNs, log gradient norm, lower the peak rate, extend warmup, and check for bad rows (a -999 missing code).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Ten daily withdrawal figures: nine near AED 101,000 and one of 400,000. Which loss gives a forecast near 101,000, which one gets pulled up, and why?

    Absolute error predicts the median, which stays near 101. Squared error predicts the mean, which is pulled well up towards the 400. The squared penalty lets one big day dominate.

    2. A shortfall costs four times as much as idle cash. Which quantile should the forecast target, and what does that mean in practice?

    τ = a ÷ (a + b) = 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8. Forecast the level demand stays under 80% of the time, so you deliberately over-forecast and fall short on about 1 day in 5.

    3. A real fraud is scored at 1%. Why does cross-entropy train a classifier better than squared error here?

    Cross-entropy prices the confident mistake steeply and keeps a strong corrective push. Squared error gives a modest penalty and a push close to nothing, so the model barely learns it was wrong.

    4. You raise the learning rate and the loss suddenly shoots up and becomes NaN. What happened, and what do you try?

    The steps overshoot the valley by more each time, so training diverges. The steepest direction of the loss sets the limit. Lower the peak rate, add or extend warmup, and clip gradients.

    5. You quadruple the batch size. What do you do with the learning rate?

    Raise it, since the gradient is less noisy (more for plain SGD than for Adam-type optimisers). Then confirm with a short sweep.

    6. Training loss plateaus high with a constant learning rate. Name two cheap things to try before changing the model.

    Decay the learning rate (the plateau may be the noise floor), and sweep the peak rate on a log scale.

    7. When can you safely ignore the effect of class weights on probabilities?

    When only the ranking is used (for example, reviewing the top scores each day), because weights preserve order. Once the number meets a fixed threshold, an exposure or another model's score, correct or recalibrate it.
