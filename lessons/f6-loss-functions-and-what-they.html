    Loss Functions, Learning Rates and What Training Really Optimises

    A model optimises exactly what you wrote down, including its blind spots. Why squared error chases outliers, why a cash forecast should not aim for the average, why cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes, and how learning rate, schedule and batch size decide whether training works at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining is a search for the lowest point of the loss you chose, so the loss defines what "good" means (mean, median, a high quantile, a calibrated probability), and the learning rate, schedule and batch size are the dials that decide whether the search arrives, bounces, or blows up.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A treasury team asks for a forecast of tomorrow's cash demand across the ATM network. The data scientist trains a model to minimise squared error, the textbook default. The model is accurate on average. It is also under-forecast on about half of all days, and on those days the bank scrambles for emergency cash at a premium. Running short costs several times more than holding too much. Nobody told the model that, so it never learned to lean high.

    This is the central fact about training. A model does not learn what you meant. It learns whatever lowers the number you gave it. The number is the loss. If the loss rewards the average, you get the average. If it treats a wild outlier as a catastrophe, the model bends towards the outlier. If it says nothing about the cost of one type of error, the model is indifferent to it.

    Training then needs a way to reduce that number. Picture standing on a hillside in fog, the loss as height, wanting the valley. You feel the slope under your feet (the gradient, F1.3) and step downhill. How big a step? That is the learning rate. Too small and you crawl for days. Too big and you leap across the valley and land higher on the other side, again and again. And the slope you feel is not the true slope: it is measured from a small handful of examples (a batch), so it is noisy. Bigger batches give a truer slope at higher cost.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Every loss is a hidden answer to "what number do you want?" Squared error wants the mean. Absolute error wants the median. A tilted absolute error wants a chosen quantile. Cross-entropy wants an honest probability. Pick the loss by deciding which of these the business actually needs.
    • The loss you train on and the metric you report can differ, and often should. Training needs smooth, differentiable losses. Reporting needs numbers a risk committee understands (F7). Trouble starts when the two disagree about what is good and nobody noticed.
    • Optimisation is its own craft. The same loss, model and data can train beautifully or fail completely depending on learning rate, schedule and batch size. Most "the model doesn't work" in a first week is an optimisation problem, not an idea problem.
    Hold onto thisBefore training, finish the sentence: "being wrong by AED X in this direction costs the bank Y". The loss is that sentence in mathematics. If you cannot write the sentence, you are not ready to choose the loss.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Regression losses: mean, median, quantile

    Take the residual e = actual minus predicted. A loss assigns a penalty to e. The three workhorses:

    LossPenalty for error eWhat it rewardsUse when
    Squared error (MSE, L2)e²Predicting the conditional mean. Big errors count quadratically: ten times the error, a hundred times the penalty.Errors are roughly symmetric and outliers are real signal, not noise. Maths is smooth and easy.
    Absolute error (MAE, L1)|e|Predicting the conditional median. Each unit of error costs the same however large.Outliers are noise or should not dominate. Easier to explain: "off by AED 9,000 on average".
    Hubere²/2 up to δ, then δ(|e| − δ/2)Mean-like for ordinary errors, median-like for outliers.You want a smooth loss that does not let a few bad rows steer everything.
    Quantile (pinball)τ·e if e ≥ 0, else (τ − 1)·ePredicting the τ-quantile: the level actual values stay under τ of the time.Under- and over-prediction cost differently, or you need an interval, not a point.

    Why does squared error give the mean? Because the constant that minimises Σ(x − c)² is the average of the x. Differentiate with respect to c, set to zero, and the answer is the mean. For absolute error, the slope of the loss is +1 for every point below the guess and −1 for every point above, so it is flat in balance only when half the points sit on each side: the median. Simulation 1 shows the consequence. One outlier moves the mean by (outlier − typical) ÷ 10, and moves the median almost nothing.

    Neither is "right". If a large withdrawal day is a real day that treasury must fund, the mean carries real information. If it is a data glitch, the median protects you. The outlier question is a business question wearing a statistics costume. Ask where the outlier came from before choosing.

    The pinball loss is the one most worth knowing for banking, because costs are rarely symmetric. Suppose a shortfall costs a per AED million and idle cash costs b. The loss-minimising forecast is the quantile

    τ=aa+b

    If a shortfall costs four times as much as idle cash, a = 4b and τ = 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8: forecast the level that demand stays under 80% of the time. This is the newsvendor result from operations research, and it is exactly what a quantile loss learns. A model trained with squared error aims at τ = 0.5 (roughly) and is wrong for this business by construction.

    Classification: cross-entropy and why it is the default

    For yes/no problems (fraud or not, default or not) the model outputs a probability p that the answer is yes. The standard loss is cross-entropy, also called log loss. For a case whose true answer is yes it charges −ln p; for a no it charges −ln(1 − p).

    loss=−[ylnp+(1−y)ln(1−p)]

    y is 1 for a real fraud and 0 otherwise. Say the model gave a real fraud 50%: loss 0.69. Gave it 10%: loss 2.30. Gave it 1%: loss 4.61. Gave it 0.001%: loss 11.5. The penalty grows without bound as the model becomes confidently wrong.

    Where does it come from? It is the negative log of the probability the model assigned to what actually happened. Minimising it is the same as maximum likelihood, the principle of F1.6: choose the model under which the observed data were least surprising. That is why it connects to everything else: logistic regression (F2.5), neural network classifiers, and the next-token loss of every large language model (M1) are all cross-entropy.

    Cross-entropy is also a proper scoring rule: its expected value is lowest when the model reports the true probability, so a model trained on it is pushed to be honest, not just directionally right. That is why probabilities from a well-trained network are usually usable, and why the calibration checks of F7.4 mean something. Squared error on probabilities (the Brier score) is proper too, but it trains badly, as the next simulation shows.

    The gradient column is the real lesson. With a sigmoid output, cross-entropy's gradient on the score is simply (p − y): the model's error, nothing more. A wrong, confident prediction produces a large corrective signal. Squared error multiplies in the sigmoid's own slope, which is almost zero where the model is saturated, so a model that is confidently wrong barely learns it is wrong. This is why cross-entropy is the default, not because of taste.

    Variants you will meet, and what each changes:

    • Class weights multiply the loss on rare positives. They inflate the model's probabilities by a known factor, which F3.5 shows how to undo. The loss was changed, so the meaning of the output changed.
    • Focal loss shrinks the penalty on examples the model already gets right, so the flood of easy negatives stops dominating. Useful in extreme imbalance, but it distorts calibration.
    • Label smoothing replaces the hard 0/1 target by, say, 0.95/0.05. Without it, cross-entropy can only be driven to zero by pushing scores to infinity, which breeds over-confidence. With it, the model stops short. It often helps generalisation and can hurt probability quality for downstream use; check calibration.
    • Multi-class cross-entropy (softmax) is the same idea over many classes, such as the next word among 100,000 tokens.
    • Custom or business losses add the cost matrix straight into training: weight a missed fraud by its AED exposure, not a flat factor. Powerful and risky: it ties the model to today's cost estimates, so document them.

    One more link. The classification losses above are what the whole "alignment" stack of M1 and R3 builds on. Reinforcement learning from human feedback and its successors replace "match the next token" with "score well on a learned reward", and the same warning applies one level up: the model optimises the reward, including its flaws (reward hacking).

    Learning rate, momentum and schedules

    Gradient descent updates every parameter w by w ← w − η · gradient. The step size η is the learning rate. The simplest instructive case is a bowl-shaped loss ½·h·w², where h is the curvature, how steeply the bowl curves. Then each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by (1 − ηh):

    wt+1=(1−ηh)wt

    If η·h is between 0 and 1, you approach the minimum smoothly. Between 1 and 2 you overshoot and zig-zag, but still converge. Above 2 each step overshoots by more than the last and the loss explodes. So the largest safe learning rate is 2 ÷ h, set by the steepest direction. Real losses are bowls of wildly different curvature in different directions: a steep, narrow canyon with a gently sloping floor. The steepest direction caps the learning rate, and the gentle direction then crawls. This mismatch is the core difficulty of optimisation.

    The remedies are layered on the same step:

    • Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients, so steps along the consistent canyon floor add up and the sideways zig-zag cancels.
    • Adaptive methods (Adam, AdamW) also divide each parameter's step by a running estimate of its recent gradient size, giving rarely-updated parameters bigger steps. This largely removes the need to tune per-parameter scales. AdamW applies weight decay separately from the gradient, which behaves better than folding it into the loss; it is the default for transformers.
    • Gradient clipping caps the size of any one update, a seatbelt against the occasional enormous gradient.
    • Schedules change η over time. Large steps early cover ground; small steps late settle into the valley rather than bouncing around the bottom (Simulation 4 shows why).

    The common schedule shapes:

    ScheduleShapeWhy people use it
    ConstantOne fixed η.Simple. Settles at a noise floor, never lower.
    Step decayCut η by 10× at set points.Classic for image models. Easy to reason about.
    Cosine decaySmooth fall from peak to near zero along a half-cosine.Smooth, few knobs. Needs the total number of steps in advance.
    WarmupStart near zero, ramp up over the first few hundred or thousand steps.Early gradients are wild and, with Adam, the second-moment estimates are unreliable. Avoids early divergence in transformers.
    Warmup–stable–decay (WSD)Ramp up, hold flat for most of training, then decay quickly at the end.You can stop or extend training without committing to a length at the start, and branch cheap final-decay runs from a shared checkpoint.
    One-cycle / cyclicRise to a large peak, then fall.Fast training of smaller models; periodic restarts can escape bad regions.

    Warmup deserves a plain word. In a neat bowl it is unnecessary, and Simulation 4 will honestly show that: it only slows things. In a deep network at initialisation the loss surface is rough and the first gradients are large and unrepresentative, so a large first step can throw the weights somewhere from which training never recovers. Warmup buys safety at the price of a few slow steps. Practitioners who skip it on a transformer discover why.

    Here is how little code it takes, and how much hides behind the numbers (illustrative PyTorch):

    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR(
    opt, max_lr=3e-4, total_steps=n_steps, pct_start=0.05)   # 5% warmup, then cosine-like decay

for x, y in loader:
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)       # e.g. cross-entropy, Huber, a pinball loss
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # seatbelt
    opt.step(); sched.step(); opt.zero_grad()

    Every line is a decision: the loss on line 6, the peak rate and schedule on lines 1–3, the clip value on line 8. When a run misbehaves, the loss curve (not the final metric) is the first thing to read.

    Batch size and gradient noise

    The true gradient averages over all training rows, which is too expensive per step. So each step estimates it from a batch of B examples. The estimate is unbiased but noisy, and its variance falls as 1/B. Two consequences follow.

    • Noise sets a floor. Near the bottom, the true gradient shrinks to zero but the noisy one does not, so the weights jitter around the minimum. For a bowl, the expected loss at the floor is about η·σ² ÷ (4·B) for small η, where σ² is the per-example gradient variance: proportional to learning rate, inversely proportional to batch size. To go lower, shrink η (a schedule) or grow B.
    • Bigger batches buy parallelism, not unlimited speed. Early in training, when the gradient signal is strong relative to noise, doubling the batch nearly halves the number of steps. Past the critical batch size (McCandlish et al., 2018), the gradient is already accurate and extra examples per step are mostly wasted. The critical size is not fixed: it grows as the loss falls, which is why large-scale runs ramp batch size up over training.

    When you change the batch size you must usually change the learning rate. The two rules of thumb: for plain SGD, scale η linearly with B (Goyal et al., 2017, with warmup), which keeps the noise ratio η/B fixed. For Adam-type optimisers, theory and practice favour scaling with the square root of B. Both are rules of thumb and break at large batches, so treat them as starting points to test. Smaller batches also add noise that can act as mild regularisation (F6.3), which is partly why small-batch models sometimes generalise a little better.

    What the picture says, in order of importance.

    • The learning rate is the most sensitive dial. Over a factor of ten you move from "too slow to finish", through "fine", to "diverges". A learning-rate sweep on a log scale is the first experiment of any new model. See hyperparameter search in F4.9.
    • A decaying schedule is not cosmetic. A fixed rate leaves a noise floor above what the data allow. Decaying the rate lets the model settle. This is why the last part of a run, often the final 10–30%, delivers a disproportionate share of the gain.
    • Learning rate and batch size trade against each other. The floor depends on their ratio. That is the reason for the scaling rules above, and why a recipe tuned at one batch size does not transfer unchanged.
    • The loss curve is a diagnostic. Smooth and falling: healthy. Falling then flat at a high level: rate too low or stuck. Jagged with spikes: rate too high or bad data. Rising to NaN: diverged. Train loss and validation loss both low, then validation rising: that is overfitting, the subject of F6.3.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    Loss and optimiser sit under almost every model. These are the places they come back.

    F1.3 · F1.8 · Gradients and optimisationThe machinery under every step

    That lesson builds gradients and backpropagation. This one decides what to differentiate, and how big a step to take.

    F1.6 · F1.7 · Likelihood and entropyCross-entropy is maximum likelihood

    Squared error is the Gaussian likelihood in disguise; cross-entropy is the Bernoulli or categorical one. The loss is the model of the noise.

    F5.1 · Neural nets and backpropagationWhere the gradient comes from

    Backpropagation computes the slope; the loss supplies the thing to take the slope of; the learning rate scales the step.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataClass weights are a loss change

    Weighting the loss shifts the probabilities by a known factor. The threshold, not the weight, is usually the lever that matters.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Metrics and calibrationTraining loss versus reporting metric

    You train on a smooth loss and report precision, recall and calibration. Cross-entropy being proper is why calibration is achievable.

    F7.5 · Forecast and regression metricsMAE, RMSE and what they hide

    The metric side of the mean-versus-median choice: RMSE punishes large misses, MAE does not.

    F6.3 · Overfitting and regularisationWhen lower training loss stops helping

    Driving the training loss down is the goal of this lesson; overfitting is when that goal stops serving you. Weight decay, early stopping and batch noise are all regularisers.

    F4.1 · F4.9 · Generalisation and tuningLearning rate is the first hyperparameter

    Random search over log-spaced learning rates is the most valuable tuning you can do. Always judge on validation, never on training loss.

    F9.1 · Time series and forecastingQuantile forecasts for treasury

    Cash and liquidity planning is an asymmetric-cost problem; pinball loss turns it into a quantile forecast with an interval.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionGuaranteed-coverage intervals

    Quantile regression gives intervals with no guarantee. Conformal prediction wraps any model's output in an interval with a coverage guarantee.

    R3 · RLHF, DPO, verifiersOptimising a learned reward

    Post-training swaps the loss for a learned preference signal. The same truth holds: the model optimises the number, flaws included.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Liquidity and cash forecasting. Asymmetric cost. Pinball loss at the quantile the funding cost implies, with its coverage checked on past days (B5).
    • Credit and provisioning. Cross-entropy for probability of default, then calibration, because IFRS 9 feeds the probability straight into expected loss (B4). Exposure and loss-given-default models are regressions where outliers (a handful of huge losses) force the mean-versus-median decision.
    • Fraud and AML. Cross-entropy with class weights or a cost-weighted loss, a threshold from costs, and care about which probabilities downstream rules consume.
    • Fine-tuning and Arabic or English language models. Next-token cross-entropy, small learning rates (fine-tuning rates are typically one to two orders of magnitude below pretraining), warmup and short schedules. Too high a rate in fine-tuning destroys what the model already knew.
    • Experiment hygiene. When a team says a model "failed to train", the first checks are the loss curve, the learning rate and the data, in that order.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basics of loss functions are settled. The live work is on the optimisation side, where large language models made training recipes expensive to get wrong: which optimiser, which schedule, how batch size should grow, and how to transfer settings found on small runs to big ones.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026. Items marked "preprint" have not been verified beyond their abstracts; older items are cited from memory of the papers listed in Sources.

    1. 1964–78
      Robust and quantile losses

      Huber introduced the loss that bridges mean and median. Koenker and Bassett defined quantile regression, the basis of the pinball loss.

      Huber, Annals of Math. Statistics, 1964; Koenker & Bassett, Econometrica, 1978
    2. 2007
      Proper scoring rules

      Gneiting and Raftery set out which losses reward honest probabilities (log score, Brier) and which do not. The theory behind "train on cross-entropy, then check calibration".

      Gneiting & Raftery, JASA, 2007
    3. 2014–19
      Adam, then AdamW and cosine schedules

      Adam became the default optimiser. Loshchilov and Hutter showed that decoupling weight decay (AdamW) fixes a flaw in how Adam regularised, and introduced cosine annealing with restarts.

      Kingma & Ba, 2014; Loshchilov & Hutter, SGDR 2016 and AdamW (ICLR 2019)
    4. 2017–18
      Large batches, warmup and the critical batch size

      Goyal et al. trained ImageNet in an hour with the linear scaling rule and warmup. McCandlish et al. proposed measuring the gradient noise scale to predict the critical batch size.

      Goyal et al., 2017; McCandlish et al., 2018
    5. 2022
      Why the square-root rule for Adam

      Work on the stochastic-differential-equation view of adaptive optimisers derived square-root learning-rate scaling for Adam-type methods, in contrast to the linear rule for SGD.

      Malladi et al., NeurIPS 2022
    6. 2024
      WSD schedules beat the "fix the length first" constraint

      MiniCPM popularised warmup–stable–decay, and Hägele et al. showed that constant-rate training followed by a short cooldown matches cosine while letting you reuse checkpoints to explore different training lengths cheaply.

      Hu et al., MiniCPM, 2024; Hägele et al., 2024
    7. 2025–26
      Muon and matrix-aware optimisers meet WSD

      Muon, which orthogonalises the update for weight matrices, moved from a community optimiser to use in large-scale runs. In 2026, open model reports pair AdamW plus Muon with a WSD schedule, one reporting lower gradient volatility than plain AdamW over about 25 billion tokens. Other preprints study how Muon still benefits from a final decay, and argue warmup and decay can be derived rather than hand-tuned for such optimisers. Early, mostly preprints.

      Tucano 2 Cool, arXiv 2603.03543; AMUSE, arXiv 2605.22432; NeurIPS 2026 warm-up poster (see Sources)
    8. 2025–26
      Setting batch size and transferring hyperparameters

      Recent preprints study how to choose the pretraining batch size and propose recipes that transfer learning rate, weight decay and schedule across model width, depth, batch size and run length, so that tuning is done at small scale. The square-root rule is reported to be important for transfer across batch sizes. Active research, not settled practice.

      arXiv 2601.05034; arXiv 2512.22382 (preprints)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Default squared error on an asymmetric problem

      A cash or exposure forecast that is right on average but short on half the days, where shortfalls are expensive.

      Spot itAsk what a miss costs in each direction. If the answers differ, ask what quantile the model is aiming at and how often actual values exceeded the forecast.
    • Outlier handling by accident

      One huge exposure drags a mean-based model, or a median-based model quietly ignores the rare events the business cares most about.

      Spot itRefit without the top 1% of targets. If the answer moves a lot, the loss is deciding for you. Decide on purpose.
    • Training loss and reported metric disagree

      The loss goes down, the metric stays flat or worsens, because they reward different things.

      Spot itTrack both on validation data every epoch. If they diverge, change what you optimise or add early stopping on the metric.
    • Treating a weighted or smoothed loss's output as a probability

      Class weights, focal loss or label smoothing shift or squash scores, then the number goes into a 10% rule or an expected-loss formula.

      Spot itCompare mean predicted probability with the actual rate on an untouched holdout. A large gap means recalibrate (F7.4).
    • Learning rate copied from a different setup

      A recipe tuned at batch 256 reused at batch 4,096, or a pretraining rate used to fine-tune.

      Spot itLoss spikes early, or fine-tuning forgets prior abilities. Rerun a short log-spaced sweep (say 1e-5 to 1e-3) before trusting a result.
    • Judging a run by its final number only

      Two models differ by 0.4 points; nobody looked at the curves.

      Spot itAsk for train and validation loss curves. A still-falling curve means under-trained; a validation curve that turned up means overfit; spikes mean instability.
    • No schedule, then blaming the model

      A constant rate leaves a noise floor; the model "plateaus" and the team concludes the data are exhausted.

      Spot itTry a decay over the last 20–30% of steps. If the loss drops sharply, the plateau was the learning rate.
    • NaNs and silent divergence

      Loss becomes NaN, or the run looks fine but one layer blew up earlier.

      Spot itLog gradient norm. Clip it, lower the peak rate, extend warmup, and check for bad rows (a missing value coded as -999 or an enormous amount).
    • Comparing optimisers at their defaults

      "Optimiser A beats B" when each was given a single untuned learning rate.

      Spot itAsk whether each optimiser had its own tuned rate and schedule. Many published gaps shrink once both are tuned fairly.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A model learns whatever the scoring rule rewards, so we choose the rule from our own costs. For cash planning, running short costs us several times more than holding extra, so we train the forecast to aim high on purpose, at the level that balances those costs, rather than at the average. Then we test it on past days: did actual demand stay under the forecast the share of the time we intended?”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I pick the loss from the decision: pinball at the cost-implied quantile for asymmetric forecasts, Huber or MAE if outliers are noise, cross-entropy for probabilities followed by a calibration check on held-out data. For optimisation I'd use AdamW with a short warmup and a decaying schedule, sweep the peak learning rate on a log scale at the batch size I'll actually use, clip gradients, and judge by validation curves rather than final numbers. If I change batch size I retune the rate, starting from the square-root rule for Adam.”
    “Why not just use MSE for everything?”

    Because MSE answers one question: what is the conditional mean? That suits symmetric costs and outliers that carry real information. If costs are asymmetric it aims at the wrong target, and if outliers are noise it lets them steer the fit. It is a fine default, but I'd choose it knowingly, and for probabilities I'd use cross-entropy, which gives stronger learning signals and honest probabilities.

    “Our model's loss went to NaN overnight. What do you check?”

    In order: the data (an extreme or missing value), the learning rate (too high for this batch size or no warmup), and numerical issues (log of zero, overflow in mixed precision). I'd look at the gradient norm in the steps before it broke, add clipping, lower the peak rate, and restart from the last good checkpoint.

    “Why does the loss drop suddenly near the end of training?”

    That is the learning-rate decay. At a high rate the weights bounce around the bottom of the valley at a noise floor set by rate over batch size. Lowering the rate lets them settle. It is expected and is why schedules such as cosine or warmup–stable–decay end with a fast decay.

    “Can we double the batch size to train faster?”

    Often yes, up to the critical batch size, beyond which extra examples per step add little. You must retune the learning rate: scale it linearly for plain SGD, closer to the square root for Adam as a starting point. And check the result on validation, because very large batches can generalise a little worse.

    “The model is accurate but the probabilities look off. Is the loss to blame?”

    Possibly. Cross-entropy on its own is proper and tends to give usable probabilities, but class weights, focal loss, label smoothing, early stopping and resampling all distort them. I'd measure calibration on a representative holdout and apply the known correction or an isotonic or Platt recalibration before using any probability as a number.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Ten daily withdrawal figures: nine near AED 101,000 (sum 910 thousand) and one of 400 thousand. What do squared error and absolute error each predict, and why do they differ?

    Squared error predicts the mean: (910 + 400) ÷ 10 = 131. Absolute error predicts the median, between the 5th and 6th sorted values (101 and 103): 102. The squared penalty makes the 400 pull hard on the answer; the absolute penalty counts it as just one point above the middle.

    2. A shortfall costs AED 12,000 per million and idle cash AED 3,000 per million. What quantile should the forecast target?

    τ = a ÷ (a + b) = 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8. Forecast the level that demand stays under 80% of the time. With normal demand (mean 100, sd 15) that is about 100 + 0.84 × 15 ≈ 112.6.

    3. Cross-entropy loss on a real fraud when the model said 50%, then 1%?

    −ln 0.5 = 0.69; −ln 0.01 = 4.61. Squared error would give 0.25 and 0.98, a tame increase. That difference in how confident mistakes are priced, and in how strong the gradient is, is why cross-entropy trains classifiers better.

    4. A loss bowl has curvature h = 50. What is the largest stable learning rate, and what happens at 0.05?

    The limit is 2 ÷ h = 2 ÷ 50 = 0.04. At 0.05, each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by |1 − 0.05 × 50| = 1.5, so the error grows by half each step and training diverges. Real models have many curvatures; the steepest one sets the limit.

    5. You quadruple the batch size. What do you do with the learning rate?

    Rules of thumb: for plain SGD scale it by 4 (linear rule, keeping rate ÷ batch fixed), with warmup; for Adam-type optimisers scale by about 2 (square root). Then confirm with a short sweep, because both rules break at large batches.

    6. Training loss plateaus high with a constant learning rate. Name two cheap things to try before changing the model.

    Decay the learning rate (the plateau may be the noise floor, which falls with the rate or a bigger batch), and sweep the peak rate on a log scale since the current one may be too low or too high. Also check the data and whether validation loss is also flat.

    7. When can you safely ignore the effect of class weights on probabilities?

    When only the ranking is used (for example reviewing the top scores each day), because weights preserve the ordering. As soon as the number meets a fixed threshold, an exposure, or another model's score, correct or recalibrate it.
