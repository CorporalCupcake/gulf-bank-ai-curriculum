    Latent Reasoning and World Models

    Part 1

    What latent reasoning and world models are

    In one sentenceBoth ideas let a model think in its own internal numbers instead of in words or pixels: latent reasoning for solving problems, world models for predicting what happens next.

    "Latent" just means hidden: the long lists of numbers inside the network. Today's reasoning models can only think by writing. Each step of their chain of thought is one word (a token) picked from about 100,000 options, and the rest of what the network computed at that step is thrown away.

    Picture solving a maze. A written chain walks down one corridor at a time and must backtrack. A latent thought can look down several corridors at once.

    How it differs from other approaches

    • Written chain of thought: readable, one choice per step. This is the default today.
    • Coconut (Meta, 2024): the last hidden state goes back in as the next input. Several possibilities can stay open at once.
    • World model: not about words at all. It learns how a scene behaves, so a system can imagine outcomes before acting.
    Part 2

    How they work

    Coconut: thoughts that are not words

    1. The model reads the question and starts as usual.
    2. A marker token switches it into "latent mode".
    3. At each step the last hidden state is fed back in directly. No word is chosen. This repeats for a fixed number of thoughts.
    4. A second marker switches back to words, and the model writes the answer.

    On a graph-search task (ProsQA), one thought held several candidate next nodes at once, and the weights sharpened over later thoughts. That looks like breadth-first search. A written chain, forced to name one node, behaves more like depth-first search that must backtrack.

    Training is the catch. Models won't think in vectors on request, so Coconut uses a curriculum: start from ordinary written chains and swap steps for thoughts, one stage at a time. Each thought needs the one before it, so training runs in series, not in parallel. That makes it slower and harder to scale.

    World models: predict the summary, not the pixels

    A world model is an internal simulator to plan with. The obvious build predicts the next video frame pixel by pixel. But most of a frame is unpredictable detail (flickering leaves, ripples, sensor noise), and the model wastes effort on it. JEPA (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture) predicts a short summary of the next clip instead, so the encoder is free to drop what can't be predicted.

    Collapse is the danger. If everything maps to one summary, prediction is trivial and the loss is zero, so a near-zero loss is not good news by itself.

    Planning with it. V-JEPA 2 (2025) learned from over a million hours of unlabelled video, then needed under 62 hours of robot video to learn how actions change the scene. Given a goal image, it imagined candidate moves and picked the one ending closest to the goal.

    Genie (Google DeepMind) is the generative opposite: it renders playable worlds, and Genie 3 (2025) generates interactive 3D scenes from text in real time.

    Part 3

    How to read the results

    Coconut on school maths: about 34% against 43% for written chains
    On a small GPT-2-sized model, with a third of the tokens. Cheaper, but worse at careful arithmetic. Digit-by-digit work seems to need exact words.
    Coconut on graph search: about 97% against 78%
    Better where the task is searching several routes. With no reasoning at all, the same model scored about 16%, so the thoughts do help.
    Part 4

    Which to use, and when

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Written chain of thoughtYou need readable, auditable steps or exact arithmetic. The default today.Tokens and latency dominate and the task is routine.
    Latent thoughts (Coconut)Researching branching search, and unreadable steps are acceptable.You need production, an audit trail or precise sums.
    Tools and codeThe step is exact: sums, lookups, rules.The step is fuzzy judgement.
    JEPA-style world modelLots of unlabelled video or sensor data, plus a control task.You only have tabular data.

    Strong at:

    • Search-heavy reasoning: one thought can hold several candidates.
    • Cheap what-if testing: imagining outcomes costs far less than trying them for real.

    Weak at:

    • Auditing: continuous thoughts are not text, so a reviewer can't read them.
    • Exact arithmetic: written tokens are exact in a way vectors are not.
    • Shipping: results are on small models and benchmarks, with no frontier-scale evidence or vendor tooling.

    Usable today? Neither is production-ready. For reasoning, use a written reasoning model, give it tools for exact steps, and control cost with its thinking budget. Treat Coconut as a hint about where efficiency may come from. What would change that: ways to inspect latent steps, and results at frontier scale.

    In banking, credit and AML decisions need readable evidence, which is the opposite of latent thinking; the realistic uses of world models are simulators for stress tests and for testing agents.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating research results as products

      The Coconut numbers come from a GPT-2-sized model. They say little about frontier models or real documents.

    • Ignoring unreadable reasoning

      If a reviewer can't inspect the steps, a wrong step can't be audited and misbehaviour could hide in them. Output tests alone don't show what hidden steps did.

    • Trusting a near-zero JEPA loss

      It may be collapse: constant summaries, perfect loss, useless features. Check how much the summaries vary across a batch.

    • Believing the imagined best plan

      Compare predicted and real outcomes on held-out plans before trusting a planner.

    1. What does a normal reasoning model throw away at each step, and how does Coconut differ?

    It keeps one word and discards the rest of the hidden state. Coconut feeds the hidden state back directly, so nothing is reduced to a word.

    2. Why does Coconut need a curriculum, and why is training slow?

    Hidden states have no meaning as thoughts at first, so written steps are replaced gradually. Each thought depends on the last, so training runs in series.

    3. Why predict a summary instead of pixels, and what can go wrong?

    Unpredictable detail can be dropped, so capacity goes to what matters. The risk is collapse, where constant summaries give zero loss.

    4. A vendor offers a latent-reasoning assistant for credit memos. What do you ask?

    How the reasoning can be inspected, what evidence of faithfulness exists, and what independent benchmarks show. For credit, written chain of thought with tools for sums is the safer choice.

    5. You have 500 hours of unlabelled warehouse video and 10 hours of robot actions. Pixels or JEPA?

    JEPA. It learns from the unlabelled video, then the small action set trains the planner. Watch for collapse.
