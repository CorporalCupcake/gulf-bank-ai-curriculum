    Thinking Without Words: Latent Reasoning and World Models

    Two bets that the next step is not more text. Coconut lets a language model reason in its hidden states instead of in written tokens. JEPA and Genie learn how the world behaves by predicting it, not by describing it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceToday's reasoning models squeeze every thought through a written word, and these two research lines ask what happens if the thinking stays in the model's own numbers: you may gain search power and speed, and you certainly lose the ability to read what it was thinking.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture an analyst solving a hard case in her head, then asked to explain it. The explanation is a tidy list of sentences. The thinking was not. It was a feel for several possibilities at once, half-formed, and she discarded most of them before a single word appeared.

    A reasoning model today works the other way round. It can only think by writing. Each step of its "chain of thought" is a token: one word piece chosen from about 100,000 options. At each step it must commit to a single choice. Everything the network computed inside, thousands of numbers rich, is thrown away except that one choice, which is then fed back in as text.

    That has a real advantage: you can read the chain. It has a real cost: the model cannot hold two ideas open, cannot say "somewhere between these three", and spends many tokens on grammar. Coconut (Chain of Continuous Thought, Meta, 2024) skips the commitment. It takes the model's last hidden state, a vector of numbers, and feeds it straight back in as the next input. No word is chosen. The thought stays continuous.

    The second half of this lesson is a cousin of the same idea, aimed somewhere else. Language models learn about the world through descriptions of it. World models (JEPA, V-JEPA 2, Genie) learn from watching the world itself, and predict what happens next. JEPA does so in an abstract space and not in raw pixels, for the same reason Coconut stays out of words: the useful content is a compact internal state, and forcing it into a surface form wastes effort.

    That toy is the whole motivation for Coconut. Real problems with branching, such as planning a route through a graph or checking which of several accounts links to a flagged entity, reward a reasoner that can keep alternatives open and prune later. A written chain cannot do that without exploring one branch, failing, and writing out the retreat.

    Hold onto thisA token is a hard choice, a hidden state is a soft one. Latent reasoning and world models both bet that the next gain comes from letting the model work in its own continuous representation, and only translating to words or pixels when it must.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.5 · A token is a narrow doorway

    Recall from F5.3 and M1.1 how an autoregressive transformer generates. It computes a hidden state, a vector of a few thousand numbers, at the last position. A final layer turns it into a score for each word in the vocabulary. One word is picked (or sampled) and appended to the text. The hidden state itself is discarded. Only the word goes forward. (The earlier hidden states are kept as attention keys and values in the KV cache, M1.3, but the new position starts from the word's embedding.)

    Count what that costs in information. A vocabulary of 128,000 words carries at most log₂(128,000) ≈ 17 bits per step. A hidden state of 4,096 numbers at 16 bits each holds up to 65,536 bits in principle. Real hidden states do not use all of that, but the gap is several orders of magnitude. Chain-of-thought works despite the narrow doorway because the model learns to write what it needs to remember. It is also why "let's think step by step" costs tokens, money and latency.

    There is a second, subtler cost. A written step has to be grammatical, and training pressure rewards plausible-sounding text. Much of a chain of thought is connective tissue ("so", "therefore", "now we have"). Studies of chains of thought also find that many tokens carry little of the computation, while a few "fork" tokens carry most of it.

    R1.5 · The Coconut loop

    Coconut changes one thing. In a normal model the last hidden state h is projected to a word, and the word's embedding becomes the next input. In Coconut, during a "latent mode" the hidden state is fed back as the next input embedding directly. Special marker tokens (written <bos> and <eot> in the paper) open and close the latent stretch. Between them the model runs a fixed number of continuous thoughts, then switches back to language to write the answer.

    # Sketch of one forward chain (not runnable; shows the idea)
x = embed(question_tokens)             # normal text in
for t in range(n_latent_thoughts):
    h = transformer(x)[-1]             # last hidden state, shape (d_model,)
    x = append(x, h)                   # feed the vector back, no word chosen
answer = decode(transformer(append(x, embed("<eot>"))))   # back to words

    Because no word is chosen, the step is differentiable. You can train it end to end with ordinary back-propagation (F1.2): the loss on the final answer sends gradients back through every continuous thought. That is the source of its elegance, and of its main cost, which we meet next.

    What do the continuous thoughts contain? The paper's analysis on a graph-search task (ProsQA) found that a thought encodes several candidate next nodes at once, with weights that sharpen as the thoughts proceed. That resembles breadth-first search, and it is the behaviour Simulation 1 caricatured. A written chain, forced to name one node, behaves more like depth-first search that must backtrack.

    R1.5 · Training: replacing words with thoughts, one step at a time

    You cannot simply tell a model to think in vectors. Nothing in its pretraining gives hidden states that meaning. Coconut uses a curriculum. Start with ordinary chain-of-thought data: question, written steps, answer. At stage 1, replace the first written step with c continuous thoughts (the paper uses c = 2 on maths problems). At stage 2, replace the first two steps with 2c thoughts, and so on. The model gradually learns to carry in latent form what it used to write.

    The catch is sequential cost. To train, the loss needs the hidden state from each thought before the next can start, so a sample with n latent thoughts needs n + 1 forward passes in a row, where normal training needs one parallel pass over the whole text. Training is slower and harder to scale. Play with the trade below.

    What did the original experiments show? From the paper as I recall it (check the numbers before quoting): on a grade-school maths set (GSM8k) Coconut scored about 34% against 43% for ordinary chain-of-thought, using roughly a third of the tokens, and well above 16% for answering with no reasoning at all. On logic tasks that need search, it won clearly: about 97% against 78% on ProsQA, with fewer tokens. So the picture is not "latent beats written". It is cheaper where the steps are routine, better where search matters, worse where careful arithmetic is needed, on a small GPT-2-sized base model.

    Neighbouring ideas worth recognising. Pause tokens give the model extra compute slots with no content. Recurrent-depth models (Geiping et al., 2025) loop a block of layers many times at inference, spending more compute in latent space on hard inputs. Soft thinking and similar methods mix word embeddings by their probabilities instead of choosing one, with no training. They share one premise: give the model more internal steps than it has visible tokens.

    R1.6 · Predict the world, not the pixels

    Yann LeCun's argument (2022) starts from what children do. A baby learns that unsupported objects fall long before she can describe gravity, and from a tiny fraction of the data a language model reads. His proposal: learn a world model, an internal simulator that predicts what will happen, and use it to plan.

    The obvious way to build one is to predict the next video frame, pixel by pixel. The trouble is that most of a frame is unpredictable detail. Leaves flicker, water ripples, sensor noise hisses. A pixel predictor is graded on all of it, so it burns capacity on detail that is neither knowable nor useful, and blurs its answers into an average. JEPA, the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, avoids this. Two encoders turn a context (the video so far) and a target (the next clip, or a masked patch) into embeddings. A predictor tries to guess the target's embedding from the context's. The loss lives in embedding space:

    L=‖Pθ(Eθ(x),a)−sg(Eθ¯(y))‖1

    x is the observed context, y the target, E the encoder, P the predictor, a an optional action. "sg" means stop-gradient: the target encoder is a slowly updated copy of the first and gets no gradient. In words: the predictor is scored on how close its guessed embedding is to the real target's embedding. The encoder is free to drop whatever cannot be predicted, so unpredictable texture simply does not appear in the target.

    The danger is collapse. If the encoder maps everything to the same vector, predicting it is trivial and the loss is zero. JEPA-style methods stop this with the slowly updated target encoder plus stop-gradient (as in BYOL, F5.4) or with explicit regularisers that keep embeddings varied. This is the same trap that contrastive and self-supervised learning (F5.4) address, and training stability is the practical reason JEPA is harder than it sounds.

    The lineage: I-JEPA (images, 2023), V-JEPA (video, 2024), and V-JEPA 2 (June 2025). V-JEPA 2 was pretrained on over a million hours of internet video without labels. A second stage then fine-tuned an action-conditioned predictor on under 62 hours of unlabelled robot video, so that given a goal image the system could plan pick-and-place moves on robot arms in new labs without task-specific training. Plans are found by sampling candidate action sequences, imagining the outcome of each in embedding space, and picking the one whose imagined end state is closest to the goal's embedding. That is model-predictive control, and it is the next simulation.

    R1.6 · Planning with an imperfect model

    A world model is only useful if you can plan with it, and no learned model is perfect. This toy has the same shape as the V-JEPA 2 recipe. An agent starts at the origin and must end up at a goal 3 units away. It has six moves, each of length at most 1. The planner proposes N random six-move plans, predicts where each ends using its world model, and executes the one that looks closest. The world model adds random error to its predictions. The simulation reports how far the plan actually lands from the goal.

    Two lessons transfer. First, planning quality is bounded by model error: errors compound over the horizon, which is why systems replan after every few steps and use short horizons. Second, the planner is an optimiser, and optimisers exploit mistakes: the plan that scores best in imagination is partly the plan whose errors happened to be flattering. Reinforcement learning (F9.4) calls this model exploitation, and it is one reason world-model agents are hard to make reliable.

    R1.6 · Genie: world models that you can walk around in

    JEPA predicts in abstract space and cannot show you what it imagines. Genie from Google DeepMind takes the opposite end of the same idea: a generative world model. Genie 1 (2024) learned playable 2D worlds from unlabelled internet videos, inferring "latent actions" without being told what any button did. Genie 3 (August 2025) generates interactive 3D environments from a text prompt in real time, around 24 frames a second at 720p, and keeps them visually consistent for a few minutes. Its use case is training and testing agents inside a generated simulator, which links to the simulation testing idea in A4.3. (Details of Genie 3 here are from DeepMind's announcement as I remember it; check before quoting.)

    The two camps disagree on what a world model is for. The generative side (Genie, video generators) argues that to simulate you must render. The JEPA side argues that to plan you need only predict what matters, and that rendering every pixel is wasted effort. Both are live research programmes, and neither has shown a clear win for general reasoning yet.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Both topics sit on ideas you already have. They also change what you can say about auditability and cost.

    F5.3 · AttentionWhat the hidden state is

    Coconut feeds the last hidden state back as the next input. You need the transformer's data flow clear to see what is and is not thrown away at each step.

    M1.3 · Context windows and the KV cacheWhy long chains are expensive

    Every written reasoning token lengthens the cache. A short latent chain is one route to cheaper thinking on long tasks.

    A5.1 · Reasoning models and test-time computeThe written baseline

    Latent reasoning tries to buy the same extra thinking without paying in visible tokens. Compare results against these models, not against a bare LLM.

    R4.2 · Chain-of-thought faithfulnessCan we trust what the model wrote?

    If written chains are already imperfect evidence of the real computation, latent chains remove the evidence altogether. The faithfulness debate is the context for the safety worry.

    R5 · InterpretabilityReading hidden states with probes

    The research answer to unreadable thoughts is to probe the vectors. Probing, sparse autoencoders and representation engineering are the tools.

    R1.4 · Diffusion language modelsAnother break from left-to-right text

    Diffusion models refine whole sequences in parallel. Latent reasoning breaks a different assumption: that thinking must be spelled out.

    F5.4 · Self-supervised learningJEPA is self-supervised learning

    Same family as masked prediction and contrastive methods, with the same collapse problem and the same fixes.

    F9.4 · Bandits and reinforcement learningPlanning in a learned model

    Model-based RL, model error and exploitation are the foundation under Simulation 3.

    A8.1 · Foundation models for transaction dataThe banking cousin of JEPA ideas

    A model that predicts a customer's next transaction in embedding space is the same family of idea applied to your data, with no video involved.

    R3.2 · Process reward models and verifiersVerifiers need steps to check

    Step-level checking of reasoning assumes the steps are written down. Latent steps cannot be graded this way.

    Where it touches the bank:

    • Model risk and audit. A validator reviewing an AI-assisted credit memo or AML narrative wants to see the reasoning. A model that reasons in latent space cannot show it. Expect model risk teams to ask for external evidence (inputs, tools called, outputs) instead of an inner narrative (G3.2).
    • Cost and latency of reasoning agents. If latent chains mature, they would cut tokens for routine reasoning in copilots and agents. At present they are research results on small models, not products you can procure.
    • Search-like tasks. Link analysis, network tracing in AML and entity resolution all involve exploring branching structures. The Coconut finding on graph search is the most relevant to these, but only as a research signal.
    • Simulation and stress testing. A learned world model of a payment system or customer base that you can roll forward is a long-term aim. Today, practical gains come from simpler simulators and from synthetic scenarios checked by people.
    • Sequences of customer behaviour. Predicting the next event in embedding space rather than as an exact transaction is a plausible fit for JEPA-like methods, and a topic to watch in A8.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Both lines are research, not deployment. The open arguments: does latent reasoning scale beyond small models and toy logic, can it be made safe to monitor, and do JEPA-style world models give a better path to general intelligence than ever larger language and video models?

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are failures of the technology and of how it is talked about. Each comes with a quick check.

    • Unreadable reasoning

      Continuous thoughts are not text. You cannot audit a mistaken step, nor spot a model that reaches an answer for the wrong reason.

      Spot itAsk how a reviewer would inspect the intermediate reasoning. If the answer is "they would not", it fails the standard you would apply to a credit or AML decision.
    • Hidden misbehaviour

      Safety researchers worry that a model could pursue an unwanted goal in latent steps with no visible trace. Early 2026 work tries to detect this; it is not solved.

      Spot itBe wary of any claim that a latent-reasoning model is "safe because it passed tests on its outputs". Output tests do not show what the hidden steps did.
    • Toy-benchmark generalisation

      Strong results on graph puzzles and school maths with a GPT-2-sized model say little about frontier models or real documents.

      Spot itCheck model size, task type and whether the method was compared against a modern reasoning model, not only a plain baseline.
    • Training cost hidden behind inference savings

      Fewer output tokens at inference, but sequential training passes that parallelise poorly.

      Spot itAsk for training cost and wall-clock time alongside tokens per answer.
    • Representation collapse in JEPA

      The encoder learns to output near-constant vectors, so the loss looks perfect and the features are useless.

      Spot itInspect the spread of embeddings (their variance across a batch) and test with a downstream probe. A flat spread is the warning.
    • Compounding error and model exploitation in planning

      The plan looks excellent in imagination and fails on contact with reality (Simulation 3).

      Spot itCompare predicted outcomes against real ones on held-out plans. Short horizons and frequent replanning are the usual guard.
    • Demo-to-capability leaps

      A generated world that looks coherent is not a world that obeys physics or business rules. Consistency lasts minutes, not weeks.

      Spot itAsk for quantitative tests of rule-following, not video clips.
    • Funding as evidence

      A very large seed round shows investor belief, not that the approach beats language models.

      Spot itSeparate "funded" from "demonstrated". Ask what benchmark, which result, who replicated it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Today's reasoning models think by writing, which is slow and costs money but leaves a trail we can read. There is research on letting models think inside their own numbers instead. It could be faster, but it removes that trail, and for a bank the trail matters. It is a laboratory result on small models, so it changes nothing in our plans this year. We watch it, and we'd insist on being able to inspect reasoning before we ever relied on it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Coconut feeds the last hidden state back as the next input embedding, trained with a curriculum that replaces written reasoning steps with continuous thoughts. It beat written chain-of-thought on search-heavy logic tasks like ProsQA, because a continuous state can hold several frontier nodes at once, and it lost on arithmetic, on a small base model. The costs are sequential training passes and no readable trace. On world models, JEPA predicts target embeddings rather than pixels, avoids collapse with a slow target encoder and stop-gradient, and V-JEPA 2 plans by sampling action sequences in embedding space. I treat all of it as research signal, not deployable technology.”
    “Does thinking in latent space make models smarter?”

    Not in general. It makes some things cheaper and helps on tasks that need search over alternatives, where a vector can hold several candidates. It did worse on step-by-step arithmetic in the original paper. The honest summary is a trade: efficiency and breadth for transparency.

    “Why do we care that we can't read the reasoning, if the answers are right?”

    Because right on average is not the standard for a regulated decision. When a decision is challenged, we need to show why. Also, reading the reasoning is how people catch the model being right for the wrong reason, which fails on the next case. That said, written chains are not always faithful either (R4.2), so the real point is that we lose even an imperfect check.

    “What is a world model, and is it just a video generator?”

    A world model predicts how a situation changes, usually given an action, so you can plan by imagining outcomes. Generative ones (Genie) render the result as video. JEPA-style ones predict an abstract embedding and cannot render it. The camps disagree about whether rendering is necessary for planning.

    “Why not predict the next frame directly?”

    Because most pixels are unpredictable noise and the model is graded on them. It wastes capacity and produces blur. Predicting in embedding space lets the encoder drop what cannot be predicted. The risk is collapse, which JEPA avoids with a slowly updated target encoder and stop-gradient or regularisers.

    “Should we invest in this now?”

    No product investment. Read the papers, track the safety and interpretability work, and keep one person informed. The decisions to make now are on what we would require before accepting a model whose reasoning is hidden: inspectable inputs and tool calls, behavioural testing, and a human on consequential decisions.

    “What would change your mind?”

    Latent reasoning matching or beating written reasoning at frontier scale on varied tasks, together with probing tools that reliably show what the hidden steps did. For world models, a clear benchmark win over strong language-model baselines on planning that others reproduce.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. In a standard transformer, what is kept and what is discarded when a word is chosen? How does Coconut differ?

    The chosen word's embedding becomes the next input; the hidden state at the top is discarded (earlier states survive as keys and values). In Coconut's latent mode the last hidden state itself is fed back as the next input, so nothing is quantised to a word.

    2. With a 70% chance of ranking the right option first, 4 options and a depth of 6, what is the success chance for width 1 and width 2?

    Width 1: 0.7⁶ = 11.8%. Width 2: per step 0.7 + 0.3 × (1/3) = 0.8, and 0.8⁶ = 26.2%. Doubling the width more than doubles success here, because losses compound over depth.

    3. Why does Coconut need a curriculum and why is training sequential?

    Pretraining gives hidden states no meaning as "thoughts", so the model is weaned off written steps gradually, stage by stage. Each thought depends on the last, so a sample with n thoughts needs n + 1 forward passes in series; ordinary training does one parallel pass.

    4. Six steps of 25 tokens each, two thoughts per replaced step, stage 3. How many reasoning tokens are written, and how many sequential passes does thinking take?

    Written: 3 × 25 = 75 tokens. Passes: 75 + 3 × 2 = 81, against 150 for the full written chain. Half the steps remain readable.

    5. Why is predicting in embedding space better than predicting pixels, and what can go wrong?

    The encoder can discard unpredictable detail, so capacity goes to what matters and outputs are not blurred averages. The failure is collapse: constant embeddings make the loss zero. Slow target encoder with stop-gradient, or variance regularisation, prevents it.

    6. Your planner's imagined best plan fails in reality. Give two causes.

    Model error that compounds over the horizon, and exploitation: among many sampled plans, the one with the best predicted score is partly the one whose model error was favourable. Fixes include shorter horizons, replanning often, and checking predictions on held-out plans.

    7. A vendor offers a "latent reasoning" assistant for credit memos. What do you ask?

    How reasoning can be inspected; what evidence of faithfulness exists; model size and independent benchmark results on tasks like ours; how errors are caught; and what the human reviewer sees. If none of the reasoning is visible, the controls must come from inputs, tool calls, tests and human review.
