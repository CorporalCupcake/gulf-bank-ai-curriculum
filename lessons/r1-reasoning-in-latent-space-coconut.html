    Thinking Without Words: Latent Reasoning and World Models

    Two bets that the next step is not more text. Coconut lets a language model reason in its hidden states instead of in written tokens. JEPA and Genie learn how the world behaves by predicting it, not by describing it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceToday's reasoning models squeeze every thought through a written word, and these two research lines ask what happens if the thinking stays in the model's own numbers: you may gain search power and speed, and you certainly lose the ability to read what it was thinking.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture an analyst solving a hard case in her head. Her thinking is a feel for several possibilities at once. Her explanation is a tidy list of sentences, written after she dropped most possibilities.

    A reasoning model today can only think by writing. Each step of its chain of thought is one token, picked from about 100,000 options. The network computes thousands of numbers inside, then throws all but that one choice away and feeds the choice back as text.

    Writing has a plus (you can read the chain) and a cost (the model cannot hold two ideas open, and wastes tokens on grammar). Coconut (Chain of Continuous Thought, Meta, 2024) skips the commitment. It feeds the last hidden state, a vector of numbers, straight back in as the next input. No word is chosen.

    World models (JEPA, V-JEPA 2, Genie) apply a similar instinct elsewhere. They learn by watching the world and predicting what happens next, in an abstract space rather than raw pixels. Both bets say the same thing: keep the useful content in a compact internal state, and do not force it into a surface form.

    Think of a maze: a written chain walks down one corridor, hits a dead end and writes out the retreat. A latent thought can look down several corridors at once. Branching bank tasks, such as tracing which accounts link to a flagged entity, have this shape.

    Hold onto thisA token is a hard choice, a hidden state is a soft one. Both lines of research bet that models improve by working in their own continuous representation and translating to words or pixels only when they must.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.5 · A token is a narrow doorway

    A token is a narrow doorway, like squeezing a whole thought through a single word. The transformer computes a hidden state (a vector of a few thousand numbers) at the last position, turns it into a score per vocabulary word, picks one word and appends it. The hidden state is discarded. (Earlier states survive as keys and values in the KV cache, M1.3, but the new position starts from the word's embedding.)

    Size of the doorway: a 128,000-word vocabulary carries at most log₂(128,000) ≈ 17 bits per step. A 4,096-number hidden state at 16 bits each could hold up to 65,536 bits. Real states use far less, but the gap is orders of magnitude. Chain-of-thought works anyway because the model writes down what it must remember, at a cost in tokens, money and latency.

    Written steps must also be grammatical, so much of a chain is filler ("so", "therefore"). Studies find a few "fork" tokens carry most of the real computation.

    R1.5 · The Coconut loop

    Coconut changes one thing. Normally the last hidden state h becomes a word, and the word's embedding is the next input. In "latent mode" h is fed back directly as the next input. Marker tokens (<bos>, <eot>) open and close the latent stretch. The model runs a fixed number of continuous thoughts, then switches back to words for the answer.

    # Sketch of one forward chain (not runnable; shows the idea)
x = embed(question_tokens)             # normal text in
for t in range(n_latent_thoughts):
    h = transformer(x)[-1]             # last hidden state, shape (d_model,)
    x = append(x, h)                   # feed the vector back, no word chosen
answer = decode(transformer(append(x, embed("<eot>"))))   # back to words

    No word is chosen, so every step is differentiable: ordinary back-propagation (F1.2) sends the answer's loss back through every thought. That is the elegance, and also the cost (see training below).

    What a thought holds: on a graph-search task (ProsQA), a thought encoded several candidate next nodes at once, with weights that sharpen over successive thoughts. That is breadth-first search, as Simulation 1 caricatured. A written chain, forced to name one node, behaves like depth-first search that must backtrack.

    R1.5 · Training: replacing words with thoughts, one step at a time

    A model will not think in vectors on request, because pretraining gives hidden states no such meaning. Coconut uses a curriculum, like swapping training wheels out one at a time. Start from ordinary chain-of-thought data (question, written steps, answer). At stage 1, replace the first written step with c continuous thoughts (c = 2 on maths in the paper). At stage 2, replace two steps with 2c thoughts, and so on.

    The catch: each thought needs the previous hidden state, so a sample with n latent thoughts needs n + 1 forward passes in a row. Normal training needs one parallel pass. Training is slower and harder to scale.

    Results, from the paper as I recall it (check before quoting), on a small GPT-2-sized model: on grade-school maths (GSM8k) Coconut scored about 34% against 43% for written chain-of-thought, with roughly a third of the tokens (no reasoning at all: 16%). On search-heavy ProsQA it scored about 97% against 78%, with fewer tokens. So: cheaper where steps are routine, better where search matters, worse at careful arithmetic.

    Neighbours, sharing the premise of more internal steps than visible tokens: pause tokens (extra empty compute slots), recurrent-depth models (Geiping et al., 2025; loop a block of layers more times on hard inputs) and soft thinking (mix word embeddings by probability, no training).

    R1.6 · Predict the world, not the pixels

    LeCun's argument (2022): a baby learns that unsupported things fall long before she can describe gravity, from far less data than a language model reads. So learn a world model, an internal simulator that predicts what happens, and plan with it.

    The obvious build is to predict the next video frame pixel by pixel. But most of a frame is unpredictable detail (flickering leaves, ripples, sensor noise). Like a student marked on handwriting as well as content, the model wastes capacity and blurs its answers. JEPA (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture) fixes this. Two encoders embed a context (video so far) and a target (next clip or masked patch). A predictor guesses the target's embedding from the context's, so the loss lives in embedding space:

    L=‖Pθ(Eθ(x),a)−sg(Eθ¯(y))‖1

    x is the context, y the target, E the encoder, P the predictor, a an optional action. "sg" is stop-gradient: the target encoder is a slowly updated copy and gets no gradient. Reading it: the loss is the distance between guessed and real target embedding. 0 is perfect prediction, though see collapse below. The encoder may drop what cannot be predicted, so noise never enters the target.

    The danger is collapse: if the encoder maps everything to one vector, prediction is trivial and loss is zero. Fixes are a slowly updated target encoder with stop-gradient (as in BYOL, F5.4) or regularisers that keep embeddings varied. A loss near zero is therefore not good news by itself.

    Lineage: I-JEPA (images, 2023), V-JEPA (video, 2024), V-JEPA 2 (June 2025). V-JEPA 2 was pretrained on over a million hours of unlabelled video, then given under 62 hours of robot video to learn an action-conditioned predictor. Given a goal image, it planned pick-and-place moves on robot arms in new labs. The planner samples candidate action sequences, imagines each outcome in embedding space and picks the one ending closest to the goal. That is model-predictive control, the next simulation.

    R1.6 · Planning with an imperfect model

    Toy version of the V-JEPA 2 recipe. An agent starts at the origin and must reach a goal 3 units away using six moves of length at most 1. The planner proposes N random plans, predicts each endpoint with its world model, and executes the one that looks closest. The model adds random error. The simulation reports where the plan actually lands.

    Two lessons transfer. Planning quality is capped by model error: errors compound over the horizon, so systems use short horizons and replan often. And optimisers exploit mistakes: it is like picking the best-looking candidate from a thousand CVs, where some looked best by luck. Reinforcement learning (F9.4) calls this model exploitation.

    R1.6 · Genie: world models that you can walk around in

    Genie (Google DeepMind) is the generative opposite of JEPA: a world model that renders. Genie 1 (2024) learned playable 2D worlds from unlabelled video, inferring "latent actions". Genie 3 (August 2025) generates interactive 3D worlds from text in real time (about 24 fps, 720p) and stays consistent for a few minutes (from the announcement as I remember it; check before quoting). The use case is testing agents in a generated simulator (A4.3).

    The camps disagree. Generative side: to simulate, you must render. JEPA side: to plan, predict only what matters. Neither has shown a clear win for general reasoning.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with a quick check.

    • Unreadable reasoning

      Continuous thoughts are not text, so you cannot audit a wrong step or spot a right answer reached for the wrong reason. Model risk teams will ask for external evidence (inputs, tools called, outputs) instead (G3.2).

      Spot itAsk how a reviewer inspects the intermediate reasoning. "They would not" fails the standard for a credit or AML decision.
    • Hidden misbehaviour

      A model could pursue an unwanted goal in latent steps with no trace. Early 2026 work tries to detect this; it is unsolved.

      Spot itDistrust "safe because it passed output tests". Output tests do not show what the hidden steps did.
    • Toy-benchmark generalisation

      Strong graph-puzzle and school-maths results on a GPT-2-sized model say little about frontier models or real documents. Today these are research results, not products.

      Spot itCheck model size, task type, and comparison against a modern reasoning model.
    • Training cost hidden behind inference savings

      Fewer output tokens, but sequential training passes that parallelise poorly.

      Spot itAsk for training cost and wall-clock time beside tokens per answer.
    • Representation collapse in JEPA

      The encoder outputs near-constant vectors, so loss looks perfect and features are useless.

      Spot itCheck embedding variance across a batch and run a downstream probe. Flat spread is the warning.
    • Compounding error and model exploitation in planning

      The plan looks excellent in imagination and fails on contact with reality (Simulation 3).

      Spot itCompare predicted and real outcomes on held-out plans. Short horizons and frequent replanning are the guard.
    • Demo-to-capability leaps

      A coherent-looking generated world does not obey physics or business rules. Consistency lasts minutes, not weeks.

      Spot itAsk for quantitative rule-following tests, not video clips.
    • Funding as evidence

      A very large seed round (for example AMI Labs, reported at about $1.03 billion in March 2026) shows investor belief, not that the approach beats language models.

      Spot itSeparate "funded" from "demonstrated": which benchmark, which result, who replicated it.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening.

    1. In a standard transformer, what is kept and what is discarded when a word is chosen? How does Coconut differ?

    The word's embedding becomes the next input; the top hidden state is discarded (earlier states survive as keys and values). Coconut feeds the last hidden state back directly, so nothing is reduced to a word.

    2. With a 70% chance of ranking the right option first, 4 options and depth 6, what is the success chance for width 1 and width 2?

    Width 1: 0.7⁶ = 11.8%. Width 2: per step 0.7 + 0.3 × (1/3) = 0.8, and 0.8⁶ = 26.2%. Doubling width more than doubles success, because losses compound over depth.

    3. Why does Coconut need a curriculum, and why is training sequential?

    Pretraining gives hidden states no meaning as thoughts, so the model is weaned off written steps gradually. Each thought depends on the last, so n thoughts need n + 1 passes in series, against one parallel pass normally.

    4. Six steps of 25 tokens, two thoughts per replaced step, stage 3. How many tokens are written, and how many sequential passes?

    Written: 3 × 25 = 75. Passes: 75 + 3 × 2 = 81, against 150 for the full chain. Half the steps stay readable.

    5. Why is predicting in embedding space better than predicting pixels, and what can go wrong?

    The encoder can drop unpredictable detail, so capacity goes to what matters and outputs are not blurry averages. The failure is collapse (constant embeddings give zero loss). A slow target encoder with stop-gradient, or variance regularisation, prevents it.

    6. Your planner's imagined best plan fails in reality. Give two causes.

    Model error compounding over the horizon, and exploitation: the best-scoring plan among many is partly the one whose error was flattering. Fixes: shorter horizons, frequent replanning, checking predictions on held-out plans.

    7. A vendor offers a "latent reasoning" assistant for credit memos. What do you ask?

    How reasoning can be inspected; what evidence of faithfulness exists; model size and independent benchmarks on similar tasks; how errors are caught; what the reviewer sees. If no reasoning is visible, controls must come from inputs, tool calls, tests and human review.
