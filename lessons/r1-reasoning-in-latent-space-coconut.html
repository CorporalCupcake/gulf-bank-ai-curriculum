    Thinking Without Words: Latent Reasoning and World Models

    Coconut lets a model reason in hidden states instead of written tokens. JEPA and Genie learn how the world behaves by predicting it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceReasoning models squeeze every thought through a written word; these research lines ask what happens if thinking stays in the model's own numbers, where you may gain search power and speed but lose the ability to read the thinking.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A reasoning model today can only think by writing. Each step of its chain of thought is one token picked from about 100,000 options, and everything else the network computed is thrown away. The chain is readable, but the model cannot hold two ideas open at once.

    Coconut (Meta, 2024) skips the commitment and feeds the last hidden state straight back in as the next input. World models (JEPA, Genie) apply a similar instinct to video: learn how the world behaves in a compact internal space, not in raw pixels.

    Think of a maze: a written chain walks one corridor at a time, while a latent thought can look down several at once.

    Hold onto thisA token is a hard choice, a hidden state is a soft one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R1.5 · A token is a narrow doorway

    A word carries only about 17 bits per step, while the hidden state behind it holds far more, and that state is discarded once the word is picked. Chain-of-thought works anyway, at a cost in tokens, money and latency.

    R1.5 · The Coconut loop

    Coconut feeds the last hidden state back directly as the next input for a fixed number of continuous thoughts, then switches back to words for the answer. No word is chosen, so every step stays differentiable and ordinary training works. On a graph-search task, one thought held several candidate next nodes at once, like breadth-first search, while a written chain must name one node and backtrack.

    R1.5 · Training: replacing words with thoughts

    Models will not think in vectors on request, so Coconut uses a curriculum: start from ordinary written chains and replace steps with continuous thoughts one stage at a time,. The catch is that each thought needs the previous one, so training is sequential and harder to scale.

    On a small GPT-2-sized model (check before quoting), Coconut scored about 34% against 43% for written chains on school maths, with a third of the tokens. On graph search it scored about 97% against 78%. So it is cheaper where steps are routine, better where search matters and worse at careful arithmetic.

    R1.6 · Predict the world, not the pixels

    A baby learns that unsupported things fall from far less data than a language model reads, so LeCun argues for a world model: an internal simulator you plan with. Predicting pixels wastes capacity on unpredictable detail such as ripples and noise. JEPA instead predicts the embedding of the target from the embedding of the context, so the encoder can drop what cannot be predicted.

    The danger is collapse: if everything maps to one vector, prediction is trivial and loss is zero, so near-zero loss is not good news by itself. V-JEPA 2 (2025) pretrained on over a million hours of unlabelled video, then needed under 62 hours of robot video to plan pick-and-place moves from a goal image. It imagines candidate actions and picks the one ending closest to the goal, which is model-predictive control.

    R1.6 · Planning with an imperfect model

    In this toy, the agent proposes N random plans, predicts each endpoint with an imperfect world model and executes the best-looking one.

    Planning quality is capped by model error, which compounds over the horizon, so systems plan short and replan often. And optimisers exploit mistakes: like picking the best of a thousand CVs, the winner is partly lucky.

    R1.6 · Genie: world models that you can walk around in

    Genie (Google DeepMind) is the generative opposite of JEPA: a world model that renders playable worlds, with Genie 3 (2025) generating interactive 3D scenes from text in real time. Use it to test agents in a simulator (A4.3). Neither camp has shown a clear win for general reasoning.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Unreadable reasoning

      Continuous thoughts are not text, so you cannot audit a wrong step, and misbehaviour could hide in them. Risk teams will want external evidence instead (G3.2).

      Spot itAsk how a reviewer inspects the intermediate reasoning. "They would not" fails the bar for a credit or AML decision.
    • Toy-benchmark generalisation

      Results on a GPT-2-sized model say little about frontier models or real documents. These are research results, not products.

      Spot itCheck model size, task type and comparison with a modern reasoning model.
    • Training cost hidden behind inference savings

      Fewer output tokens, but sequential training passes that parallelise poorly.

      Spot itAsk for training cost beside tokens per answer.
    • Representation collapse in JEPA

      The encoder outputs near-constant vectors, so loss looks perfect and features are useless.

      Spot itCheck embedding variance across a batch.
    • Imagination that fails on contact with reality

      Plans look excellent in the model and fail outside it, and a coherent generated world does not obey physics or business rules.

      Spot itCompare predicted and real outcomes on held-out plans, and ask for rule-following tests, not video clips.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening.

    1. What is discarded when a standard transformer picks a word, and how does Coconut differ?

    The top hidden state is discarded and the word is fed back. Coconut feeds the hidden state back directly, so nothing is reduced to a word.

    2. Why does Coconut need a curriculum, and why is training slow?

    Pretraining gives hidden states no meaning as thoughts, so the model is weaned off written steps gradually. Each thought depends on the last, so training runs in series.

    3. Why predict in embedding space rather than pixels, and what can go wrong?

    The encoder can drop unpredictable detail, so capacity goes to what matters. The failure is collapse, where constant embeddings give zero loss.

    4. Your planner's imagined best plan fails in reality. Give two causes.

    Model error compounding over the horizon, and exploitation: the best-scoring plan is partly the one whose error flattered it.

    5. A vendor offers a "latent reasoning" assistant for credit memos. What do you ask?

    How reasoning can be inspected, what evidence of faithfulness exists, and what independent benchmarks show. If no reasoning is visible, controls must come from inputs, tool calls, tests and human review.
