    M3.9 · M3.10 Day one ★ About 40 minutes

    Garbage In, Confident Answer Out: Parsing Messy PDFs and Diagnosing RAG Failures

    Why a retrieval system is only as good as the text extracted from the page, what goes wrong with tables, stamps, scans and mixed Arabic and English, and how to tell in ten minutes whether a bad answer came from the retriever or the model.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA RAG system answers from the text it was given, so errors made while turning pages into text are invisible and permanent, and the only way to fix a failing system efficiently is to look at the evidence at each stage (parsed text, retrieved chunks, final answer) and ask which stage first went wrong.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks the internal assistant: "What is the overdraft limit in the Al Noor Trading facility letter?" The assistant answers, calmly and with a citation, AED 5,000,000. The real limit is AED 750,000. The 5,000,000 belongs to the Murabaha line one row above.

    Nothing was wrong with the language model. It read exactly what it was given. The facility letter was a PDF with a table. The extraction tool read the table down the columns instead of across the rows, so the text handed to the model said "Murabaha line, Overdraft, 5,000,000, 750,000", with the pairing lost. The model guessed the natural pairing. It guessed wrong.

    This is the main thing to understand about document RAG. A PDF is a drawing, not a text file. It records where to paint each glyph on a page. It does not record sentences, paragraphs, tables or reading order. Every RAG system starts by reconstructing those from the drawing, and that reconstruction is lossy in ways that look fine to the eye. A human sees the table. The pipeline sees a bag of positioned characters.

    The second half of the lesson is the mirror image. When an answer is wrong, there are at least four places it could have gone wrong: parsing, retrieval, the model's use of what it was given, or the test itself. Teams waste weeks tuning prompts when the right passage never reached the model, and weeks tuning embeddings when it did and the model ignored it. A short discipline stops that.

    Hold onto thisIn RAG, parsing is not preprocessing. It sets the ceiling on everything after it. Embeddings, rerankers and bigger models cannot recover a pairing that the text no longer contains.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Parsing messy PDFs and scans (M3.9)

    Born-digital or scanned: the first fork

    There are two very different kinds of PDF, and the first job is to tell them apart.

    • Born-digital. Exported from Word, a core banking system or a report generator. It has a text layer: characters with coordinates and fonts. Extraction is reconstruction of structure, and the characters themselves are usually correct.
    • Scanned. A photograph of paper wrapped in a PDF. There is no text at all, only pixels. You need OCR (optical character recognition) to read the characters before you can even start on structure. Characters can now be wrong.
    • Hybrid, the common nasty case. A scan that someone ran OCR over years ago, so it has a hidden text layer of unknown quality. Trade finance bundles, signed contracts and stamped letters are often this. The hidden layer can be confidently wrong and nothing flags it.

    A cheap triage step: for each page, count extractable characters and compare with the page's rendered content. Zero characters means scan. Characters that fail a dictionary and language check mean a bad hidden layer. Route accordingly rather than running one tool on everything.

    for page in pdf.pages:
    chars = page.extract_text() or ""
    if len(chars.strip()) < 20:
        route = "ocr"            # scanned: no text layer
    elif quality(chars) < 0.8:   # share of tokens that look like real words/numbers
        route = "ocr_and_compare" # old hidden OCR layer: do not trust it
    else:
        route = "layout_parse"   # born-digital: recover structure

    The stages of a modern parser

    Whatever the product, a good pipeline does roughly the same steps. Knowing them tells you where a failure comes from.

    1. Layout detection. Find regions on the page and label them: title, paragraph, table, figure, header, footer, footnote, stamp. A vision model does this on the page image.
    2. Reading order. Decide which region comes after which. Two-column pages, sidebars and footnotes break the naive "top to bottom, left to right" rule.
    3. Recognition per region. Text regions go to text extraction or OCR. Tables go to a table-structure model that outputs rows, columns and merged cells. Figures get captioned or skipped.
    4. Serialisation. Write the result as text a model can use: Markdown, HTML tables, or JSON with coordinates and page numbers. This choice matters for chunking and for citations.
    5. Cleaning. Strip repeated headers and footers, join hyphenated line breaks, normalise Unicode, drop page-number lines.

    Each step can fail quietly. Simulation 1 is a step 3 and 4 failure. A footnote swallowed into the next paragraph is a step 2 failure. "Strictly confidential" stamped on every page and embedded in every chunk is a step 5 failure that quietly drags all your chunks towards each other in embedding space.

    Tables: where most money is lost

    Bank documents are table-heavy: facility schedules, fee tariffs, financial statements, collateral lists, profit-rate sheets. Tables fail in predictable ways.

    • Linearised wrongly (Simulation 1): rows and columns interleaved.
    • Headers lost. A number with no column name is meaningless. In a financial statement "12,430" could be revenue, profit or a prior year.
    • Merged and spanning cells. A header "FY2025" that spans two columns, or a row label that spans several rows, breaks any extractor that assumes a regular grid.
    • Tables crossing pages. The header appears on page 4 and the rows continue on page 5 with no header. A page-by-page parser produces an orphan table.
    • Units and notes elsewhere. "AED thousands" in the title above the table, the footnote after it. Lose either and every number is off by 1,000.

    The practical fix is to serialise each table row with its headers and its context ("Facility letter, Al Noor Trading, page 4, Schedule 1. Facility: Overdraft; Limit (AED): 750,000; Tenor: 12 months"). It is wordier, but a retrieval hit on that row is self-explanatory, and the model never has to guess the pairing. For very large tables, keep the table whole in a structured store and let the model query it, instead of stuffing it into text. That is a design choice the agent lessons pick up (M4).

    Stamps, signatures, handwriting and scan noise

    • Stamps overlap text. OCR reads the stamp's letters into the paragraph underneath, or loses the paragraph. Round and angled stamps defeat the rectangular-line assumption of most OCR.
    • Signatures are not text and should not be OCR'd. Detect them as a region and record "signed by? yes/no, where" as metadata. For a KYC or trade document, the presence and position of a signature or stamp is often the fact you need, and no text-only pipeline captures it.
    • Handwriting (cheque amounts, handwritten corrections, form fields) is far less reliable than print. Treat extracted handwritten values as proposals needing a check.
    • Scan quality: skew, low resolution, fax artefacts, show-through from the other side of the page, shadows from phone photos. These raise the character error rate. Pre-processing (deskew, denoise, upscale) helps; so does asking for a better scan.

    Mixed Arabic and English

    Gulf bank documents are routinely bilingual: Arabic and English side by side, or Arabic body with English figures, company names and product codes. This creates its own failure family. The deeper OCR side is in Arabic OCR and document AI and the language models in Arabic LLMs. The parts that hit RAG parsing directly:

    • Right-to-left order. Arabic is read right to left, but many PDFs store glyphs in visual order (as painted), so naive extraction yields the letters reversed, or sentences with the words in the wrong order. The result looks like Arabic to a human who flips it, and like nonsense to an embedding model and a keyword index.
    • Joined letters. Arabic letters change shape by position (initial, medial, final, isolated). Some PDFs store these presentation forms as separate Unicode characters, so the same word has two spellings that never match. Unicode normalisation (NFKC) maps most presentation forms back to the base letters.
    • Digits. Arabic-Indic digits (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and Western digits (0123456789) both appear, sometimes in the same document. An amount indexed as "٧٥٠٬٠٠٠" is invisible to a query for "750,000".
    • Spelling variants. Hamza forms (أ إ آ ا), ta marbuta vs ha, ya vs alif maqsura, optional diacritics, and the decorative stretching letter (tatweel). Normalise for search, but keep the original text for display and citation.
    • Bidirectional runs. An English company name or a number inside an Arabic sentence can come out in the wrong place, and punctuation attaches to the wrong side.
    • Mixed-language embeddings and chunk size. Arabic splits into more tokens than English in many tokenisers (M1), so a "500-token" chunk covers less Arabic text, and an English query may not retrieve an Arabic passage as strongly as an Arabic one would.

    OCR errors compound

    Scans add a second, arithmetic problem. OCR quality is usually quoted as character error rate (CER): the share of characters wrong. A 1% CER sounds superb. But the facts you need are strings of characters that must all be right. If errors strike independently, the chance that a field of n characters survives is

    P(field intact)=(1−CER)n

    At CER 1%, an account number of 24 characters survives with probability 0.9924 = 79%. A 12-character amount survives 89% of the time. Ten such fields all intact: 0.88610 ≈ 30%. A single wrong digit in an amount is not "slightly worse text": it is a different fact.

    Which parser to use

    There are three families, and a serious bank system often mixes them.

    Vision-language model reads the page imageGive the page image to a multimodal model and ask for Markdown or JSON.Handles odd layouts, stamps, forms and mixed scripts without hand-built rules.     Two rules cover most of the design. First, never trust a single reading of a high-stakes number: extract it two ways (text layer and OCR, or two OCR engines) and flag disagreements for review. Second, keep provenance: store the page number and bounding box with every chunk, so that an answer can show the reader the exact region of the original page, and so that a reviewer can check the parse against the picture. Parsing is also where most of the real work in trade-finance document AI and reading cheques and scans lives.

    Chunking comes after parsing and inherits its structure. Split on the sections and tables the parser found, not on fixed character counts (chunking strategies). Search quality also depends on the index: exact numbers, clause numbers and Arabic spelling variants are why you want keyword matching alongside embeddings (BM25, hybrid search).

    Diagnosing failures: retrieval or generation? (M3.10)

    A RAG system is a chain: parse, chunk, index, retrieve, (rerank), assemble context, generate. A wrong answer means some link failed. The key move is to find the first link that failed, because everything after it is a consequence. A wrong chunk cannot be rescued by a better prompt, and a correct chunk ignored by the model cannot be rescued by a better embedding model.

    Work backwards from the answer, with a test set of real questions where you know where the answer lives (the gold passage and the gold answer). For each failed question, ask in order:

    1. Is the gold answer present in the parsed text at all? Search your own parsed corpus for it. If not, it is a parsing (or ingestion) failure: the page was skipped, the table was scrambled, the OCR misread it.
    2. Was the gold passage retrieved? Look at the top k chunks the model was given. If the passage was in the corpus but not in the top k, it is a retrieval failure. Sub-cases: the query used different words (rewriting helps: query rewriting), the chunk was cut badly, or an exact term needs keyword search.
    3. Was it retrieved but buried? It was in the top 20 but not the top 5 that the model got. That is a ranking or context-assembly failure. Distractors that look similar to the answer are especially harmful.
    4. Was it in the context, and the model still failed? Now it is a generation failure: the model ignored it, contradicted it, merged it with a similar passage, or used memory instead (long context and "lost in the middle"). Sub-cases: instructions allowed guessing, the passage was long, the question needed arithmetic or comparison.
    5. Was the answer actually right? Sometimes the grader or the gold answer is wrong, or the question is ambiguous. Check by hand before blaming the system.
    accuracy=r·g1+(1−r)·g0

    r is the retrieval hit rate: the share of questions where the gold passage is in the context given to the model. g1 is the share answered correctly when it is. g0 is the share answered correctly anyway when it is not (model memory, a lucky neighbour, or a correct "I don't know" if that counts). Measure r and g1 separately and you know where the next improvement is cheapest. Raising r by a point gains (g1 − g0) points; raising g1 by a point gains r points.

    Notice that the answer is not "always fix retrieval". When retrieval is already strong, the remaining errors are mostly generation faults, and more retrieval tuning buys little. The calculation also tells you the ceiling: if parsing drops the answer from the text for 15% of questions, then r cannot exceed 85% no matter how good the search is.

    To measure r you need gold passages, which means a labelled test set. Building it is the unglamorous work that makes everything else possible: 100 to 200 real questions from the business, each with the page that answers it, split between easy, hard, Arabic, English and table-based. Automated judges (an LLM scoring "was the answer supported by the context?") help scale, but they have their own errors and need spot checks (groundedness checks, human evaluation). Score the stages separately: retrieval with hit rate at k and rank of the gold passage; generation with faithfulness to the supplied context and with answer correctness.

    Case sorter: where did it first go wrong?

    Each case gives you what you would see in the trace. Pick the first failing stage. This is the exact routine for a bad-answer review meeting.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Parsing and diagnosis sit at the two ends of the RAG chain. Everything in the middle depends on one and is judged by the other.

    M3 · ChunkingChunks follow structure

    A parser that finds sections and tables lets you chunk at real boundaries. A parser that returns a flat string forces you to guess.

    M3 · Keyword search and hybridExact numbers and Arabic variants

    Clause numbers, amounts and names need exact matching, which only works if the digits and letters were normalised at parse time. See also hybrid search.

    M3 · Query rewritingA retrieval-side fix

    When the passage exists but the query words differ from it, rewriting is the remedy. Diagnosis tells you whether you are in that case.

    M1.5 · Context windowsGeneration faults with the right passage present

    Long or cluttered contexts and distractors explain many "retrieved but ignored" failures.

    M5 · EvaluationThe gold set and the judge

    Stage-wise diagnosis needs a labelled set and trustworthy graders. Reference-based metrics alone cannot tell you which stage failed.

    A4 · GroundednessIs the answer supported by the context?

    The generation-side check: separates "the model invented it" from "the context was wrong".

    A6 · Document-image search and agentic RAGSkip the parser, or let an agent retry

    Two frontier responses to parsing loss. See also agentic RAG, which retries retrieval when the first pass fails.

    A14 · Arabic OCRThe deep dive on Arabic scans

    Everything in this lesson about Arabic, in more depth and with the models behind it.

    B7 · Trade finance document AIThe live bank use case

    Letters of credit, invoices, bills of lading: stamps, signatures, tables and two languages in one bundle.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Staff copilots over policies and procedures (internal copilots). Policy PDFs with numbered clauses, tables of limits and version histories. A scrambled limits table gives wrong, confident answers about what staff may approve.
    • Credit files and facility letters. Mixed scans, signed pages, tables of covenants and collateral. Wrong digits are costly; provenance to the page image is a requirement.
    • KYC and onboarding. Emirates ID, passports, trade licences, Arabic and English fields. Extracting a name in both scripts and matching them is a parsing task before it is a screening task.
    • Regulatory circulars from CBUAE or SAMA, often bilingual PDFs. Retrieval must find the clause and show it, in the language the reader needs.
    • Customer complaints and contracts arriving as photos and scans from the branch.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Document parsing has moved from rule-based extraction towards vision-language models that read a page as an image, and RAG diagnosis has moved from ad hoc debugging towards stage-wise evaluation. The live arguments: do you still need a separate parser, how much does OCR quality really hurt downstream answers, and how do you evaluate a pipeline whose stages interact?

      How OCR errors reach the answer

      OHRBench studied how OCR noise cascades through RAG on 350 PDFs from six domains, separating semantic noise (wrong characters) from formatting noise (lost structure such as tables and formulas), and found current OCR falls short of building a high-quality knowledge base.       OmniDocBench covers nine page types with layout and attribute labels and compares both pipeline tools and end-to-end vision-language models. Its summary finding: pipeline tools do well on specific content types, while general vision-language models generalise better to unusual layouts.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the failures behind most bad document-RAG demos. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Scrambled or headerless tables

      The model pairs the wrong number with the wrong label, fluently.

      Spot itPick ten table-based questions and compare the parsed text for the table to the page image by eye.
    • Pages silently dropped

      A scanned appendix produced no text and nothing flagged it. The answer lives there.

      Spot itReport characters extracted per page at ingestion. Pages with near zero characters should raise an alert, not vanish.
    • Trusting a hidden OCR layer

      An old scan with a bad text layer extracts "cleanly" but wrongly.

      Spot itRe-OCR a sample and compare. Check the share of unknown words.
    • Arabic stored reversed or with unmatched forms and digits

      Searches in Arabic return nothing, or only some documents, and the index looks fine.

      Spot itRun a known Arabic phrase and a known amount through search, in both digit styles. Print raw parsed Arabic and read it.
    • Repeated headers and footers in every chunk

      "Strictly confidential, Page 3 of 40" appears in hundreds of chunks and distorts retrieval.

      Spot itLook for the most frequent lines across chunks.
    • Stamps and signatures read as text, or ignored when they were the fact

      Stamp letters corrupt a paragraph; "is it signed?" cannot be answered.

      Spot itAsk for a stamp or signature fact in a test question and see if any chunk carries it.
    • Tuning the wrong half

      Weeks on prompts while the passage was never retrieved, or on embeddings when the model ignored it.

      Spot itAsk for hit rate at k and answer accuracy given the gold passage, separately. If the team has only one number, that is the problem.
    • An evaluation set that is all easy, English prose

      Looks great, then fails on tables and Arabic scans in production.

      Spot itBreak the results down by document type, language and table versus text. The overall score hides the weak slice.
    • Grader and gold answer wrong

      The system is blamed for a mistake in the test.

      Spot itHand-check a sample of "failures" before changing anything.
    • No provenance

      An answer with no page reference cannot be checked by a banker or a validator.

      Spot itAsk: can I click an answer and see the highlighted region of the original page?
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The assistant can only answer from the text we extract from our documents, so reading the documents correctly is the first risk to manage, especially tables and scanned, bilingual files. When an answer is wrong we trace it stage by stage: was the information read correctly, was it found, did the model use it. That tells us whether to fix the reading, the search or the model, instead of guessing. Every answer will point back to the exact page so a person can check it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd triage pages into born-digital and scanned, use a layout-aware parser with table-structure recognition, serialise table rows with headers and context, normalise Arabic for search while keeping originals for display, and keep page and bounding-box provenance. High-stakes numbers get read two ways with disagreements reviewed. For evaluation I'd build a gold set of 150 or so real questions with gold passages, segmented by language and table versus prose, and report hit rate at k, faithfulness given the context, and answer correctness separately, so each failure is attributed to the first broken stage.”
    “Can't we just give the PDF to a multimodal model and skip all this?”

    For a few documents, often yes, and it can be the best reader of odd layouts. At bank scale there are open questions: cost and latency per page, whether the data may leave the bank's environment, silent omissions or invented digits, and that you still need retrieval over thousands of documents. A common design uses the model for hard pages and checks its numbers against a second reading. I'd benchmark it on our own Arabic and table-heavy documents before deciding.

    “The OCR is 99% accurate. Why do we still get wrong numbers?”

    99% of characters is not 99% of facts. A 12-character amount survives 89% of the time at that rate, and a document depending on ten such fields is fully right under a third of the time. Errors also cluster in poor scans. So add format and check-digit validation, cross-field checks (totals add up), dual reading, and human review for the low-confidence cases.

    “The assistant gave a wrong answer. How do you find out why?”

    I look at the trace in order. Is the answer in the parsed text? Was that passage among the chunks retrieved? Was it near the top of what the model saw? If it was in the context and the answer is still wrong, it is a generation fault; otherwise it belongs to retrieval or parsing. Then I check the test itself was right. Fixing starts at the first stage that failed.

    “Should we invest in a better embedding model or a better LLM?”

    Measure first. If retrieval hit rate is 70% and generation given the right passage is 90%, a retrieval gain is worth more. If hit rate is already 95%, remaining errors are mostly the model or the parsing ceiling. Without separate numbers we would be guessing, and we might also find that fixing table parsing beats both.

    “How do you handle Arabic and English in the same documents?”

    Check extraction order and digits first, normalise Unicode and digit forms for the search index, keep the original for display, and use hybrid search so exact terms and meaning both work. Evaluate Arabic and English questions separately and include cross-language ones, since an English query may retrieve an Arabic passage less strongly. The Arabic OCR specifics go in a separate review, and I'd say plainly where we haven't yet tested.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why is a PDF "a drawing, not a text file", and what is lost on extraction?

    It stores positioned glyphs to paint, not sentences, paragraphs, tables or reading order. Extraction has to rebuild those. Table row and column pairing, headers, reading order across columns, and the link between a footnote and its text are the usual casualties.

    2. A scanned page and a born-digital page look the same on screen. How do you tell them apart in code, and why does it matter?

    Count extractable characters per page. Near zero means a scan that needs OCR. Characters with many non-words means a poor hidden OCR layer. It matters because OCR introduces character errors and needs different tools; trusting a bad hidden layer produces wrong text that looks clean.

    3. Character error rate is 2%. A field has 15 characters. Probability it is read correctly? And a document with 8 such fields?

    0.9815 ≈ 0.739, so about 74%. Eight fields: 0.7398 ≈ 0.089, so under 9% of documents are fully correct (under independence). Real errors cluster, but the lesson stands: validate and review.

    4. Name three ways Arabic text can be extracted that makes an exact search fail even though it "looks right".

    Characters stored in visual (reversed) order; presentation-form characters rather than base letters; digits in Arabic-Indic form while the query uses Western digits. Also spelling variants (hamza forms, ta marbuta/ha) and diacritics or tatweel.

    5. Retrieval hit rate 60%, correct-given-passage 85%, correct-without-passage 10%. Accuracy? Which +10 points is worth more?

    0.6 × 0.85 + 0.4 × 0.10 = 0.51 + 0.04 = 55%. +10 points of retrieval gains 0.1 × (0.85 − 0.10) = 7.5 points. +10 points of generation gains 0.1 × 0.6 = 6 points. Retrieval first, but it is close; parsing losses would cap retrieval too.

    6. The right chunk was retrieved at rank 3 of 5 and the model still gave the wrong figure. Which stage failed and what do you check?

    Generation, or the context assembly. Check whether a similar distractor chunk contained a conflicting number, whether the instruction permitted guessing, whether the model needed to compute or compare, and whether the chunk itself was ambiguous (a table row without a header). If the chunk is ambiguous, the true fault is parsing.

    7. Why keep page numbers and bounding boxes with every chunk?

    So an answer can cite and highlight its source for a human check, and so reviewers can compare the parse with the original picture when debugging. It also supports audit and validation requirements for any assistant that influences credit or compliance work.
