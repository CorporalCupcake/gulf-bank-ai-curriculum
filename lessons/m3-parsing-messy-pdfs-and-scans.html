    M3.9 · M3.10 Day one ★ About 40 minutes

    Garbage In, Confident Answer Out: Parsing Messy PDFs and Diagnosing RAG Failures

    Why a retrieval system is only as good as the text extracted from the page, what goes wrong with tables, stamps, scans and mixed Arabic and English, and how to tell in ten minutes whether a bad answer came from the retriever or the model.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA RAG system answers only from the text it was given, so page-to-text errors are invisible and permanent; to fix a failing system, check the evidence at each stage (parsed text, retrieved chunks, final answer) and find which stage failed first.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks the internal assistant: "What is the overdraft limit in the Al Noor Trading facility letter?" It answers calmly, with a citation: AED 5,000,000. The real limit is AED 750,000. The 5,000,000 belongs to the Murabaha line one row above.

    The language model was fine. The extraction tool read the table down the columns, so the pairing was lost before the model saw it.

    A PDF is a drawing, not a text file. It says where to paint each glyph. It does not record sentences, tables or reading order, much like a photo of a spreadsheet.

    The second half of the lesson: a wrong answer can come from parsing, retrieval, the model, or the test itself. Check them in order.

    Hold onto thisParsing sets the ceiling on everything after it: embeddings, rerankers and bigger models cannot recover a pairing the text no longer contains.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Parsing messy PDFs and scans (M3.9)

    Born-digital or scanned: the first fork

    • Born-digital. Exported from Word or a banking system. It has a text layer, so characters are usually right and the job is rebuilding structure.
    • Scanned. Pixels only. You need OCR (optical character recognition) first, and characters can now be wrong.
    • Hybrid, the nasty case. An old scan with a hidden OCR layer of unknown quality. It can be confidently wrong and nothing flags it.

    Triage each page by counting extractable characters. Zero means scan. Text full of non-words means a bad hidden layer. Route each page to the right tool instead of running one tool on everything.

    The stages of a modern parser

    Like a factory line: the stage that failed tells you where to look.

    1. Layout detection. Label regions: paragraph, table, header, stamp.
    2. Reading order. Columns and footnotes break "top to bottom".
    3. Recognition. Extract or OCR text; send tables to a table model.
    4. Serialisation. Write Markdown or JSON with page numbers.
    5. Cleaning. Strip repeated headers and footers, normalise Unicode.

    Simulation 1 is a step 3 and 4 failure. "Strictly confidential" repeated in every chunk is step 5: it makes all chunks look alike to search.

    Tables: where most money is lost

    Facility schedules, tariffs and financial statements are all tables. They fail in three ways.

    • Linearised wrongly (Simulation 1): rows and columns interleaved.
    • Headers lost. "12,430" could be revenue, profit or last year's figure. Worst when a table crosses pages.
    • Units and notes elsewhere. "AED thousands" in the title. Lose it and every number is off by 1,000.

    The fix: serialise each row with its headers and context ("Facility letter, Al Noor Trading, page 4, Schedule 1. Facility: Overdraft; Limit (AED): 750,000"). A hit on that row explains itself.

    Stamps, signatures, handwriting and scan noise

    • Stamps overlap text, so OCR mixes their letters into the paragraph.
    • Signatures are not text. For KYC or trade documents the presence of a signature or stamp is often the fact you need, so detect it and store it as metadata.
    • Handwriting and poor scans are far less reliable than print. Treat the output as a proposal to check, or ask for a better scan.

    Mixed Arabic and English

    Gulf bank documents are routinely bilingual (deeper OCR in Arabic OCR and document AI, models in Arabic LLMs). The failures that hit RAG parsing:

    • Right-to-left order. Many PDFs store glyphs in visual order, so extraction yields reversed letters. A search index sees nonsense.
    • Joined letters and spelling variants. Arabic letters change shape by position, and some PDFs store each shape as a separate character, so one word gets two spellings that never match. Normalise (NFKC and similar) for search; keep the original for display and citation.
    • Digits. Arabic-Indic (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and Western (0123456789) digits can mix. An amount indexed as "٧٥٠٬٠٠٠" is invisible to a query for "750,000".

    OCR errors compound

    Character error rate (CER) is the share of characters read wrongly. 1% sounds superb, but a fact is a string where every character must be right, like a phone number. At 1%, a 24-character account number is fully correct only about 79% of the time, and a document with ten such fields only about 30%. A wrong digit in an amount is not "slightly worse text": it is a different fact.

    Which parser to use

    Three families; a serious bank system often mixes them.

    ApproachHow it worksStrengthsWeak spots
    Text-layer extractionRead the embedded characters and coordinates.Fast, cheap, exact on born-digital files.No help for scans. Weak on tables and visual-order Arabic.
    Layout-aware pipelineDetect regions, order them, OCR each, rebuild tables.Good structure, auditable stages, bounding boxes for citations, runs locally.Several models to maintain; early errors cascade.
    Vision-language model reads the page imageGive the page image to a multimodal model and ask for Markdown or JSON.Handles odd layouts, stamps and mixed scripts.Can hallucinate or skip content; cost per page; data residency; exact digits not guaranteed.

    Two rules. First, never trust a single reading of a high-stakes number: extract it two ways (text layer and OCR, or two engines) and flag disagreements. Second, keep provenance: store page number and bounding box with every chunk, so an answer can show its source region and a reviewer can check the parse against the picture. See also searching page images directly.

    Chunk after parsing, on the sections and tables the parser found (chunking strategies), and keep keyword matching beside embeddings for exact numbers (hybrid search).

    Diagnosing failures: retrieval or generation? (M3.10)

    A RAG system is a chain: parse, chunk, index, retrieve, rerank, assemble context, generate. Like a leaking pipe, you find the first break, because everything after it is a consequence. A better prompt cannot rescue a wrong chunk.

    Use a test set of real questions where you know the gold passage and gold answer. For each failure, ask in order:

    1. Is the gold answer in the parsed text? If not, it is a parsing failure: page skipped, table scrambled, OCR misread.
    2. Was the gold passage retrieved? In the corpus but not in the top k is a retrieval failure: different query words (query rewriting helps), a badly cut chunk, or an exact term needing keyword search.
    3. Retrieved but buried? In the top 20 but not the top 5 is a ranking failure.
    4. In the context, and the model still failed? A generation failure: ignored, merged with a look-alike passage, or answered from memory ("lost in the middle").
    5. Was the answer actually right? Sometimes the gold answer or grader is wrong. Check by hand first.

    Think of a student who needs the right textbook page and then must read it properly: they can fail either way. So measure two things separately. The retrieval hit rate (r) is how often the gold passage reached the model. g1 is how often the answer is right when it did, and g0 is how often it is right anyway when it did not (memory, luck, a correct "I don't know"). Measuring them separately shows where the next improvement is cheapest.

    The answer is not "always fix retrieval". When retrieval is strong, remaining errors are mostly generation faults. There is also a ceiling: if parsing drops the answer for 15% of questions, hit rate cannot exceed 85% however good the search.

    Measuring this needs a labelled set: 100 to 200 real questions, each with its answer page, split across easy, hard, Arabic, English and table-based. LLM judges help scale but need spot checks (groundedness checks).

    Case sorter: where did it first go wrong?

    Pick the first failing stage, as in a bad-answer review meeting.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each with its quickest check.

    • Scrambled or headerless tables

      The model pairs the wrong number with the wrong label.

      Spot itCompare parsed text with the page image for ten table questions.
    • Pages silently dropped, or stamps and signatures ignored

      A scanned appendix produced no text; or "is it signed?" cannot be answered.

      Spot itAlert on near-zero characters per page at ingestion.
    • Trusting a hidden OCR layer

      An old scan extracts "cleanly" but wrongly.

      Spot itRe-OCR a sample and compare; check the share of unknown words.
    • Arabic stored reversed or with unmatched forms and digits

      Arabic searches return nothing and the index looks fine.

      Spot itSearch a known phrase and amount in both digit styles. Print raw parsed Arabic and read it.
    • Tuning the wrong half

      Weeks on prompts while the passage was never retrieved.

      Spot itAsk for hit rate and accuracy-given-passage separately.
    • A flawed test set

      Easy English prose looks great, then fails on tables and Arabic scans; or the gold answer is wrong.

      Spot itBreak results down by document type and language, and hand-check a sample of "failures".
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Why is a PDF "a drawing, not a text file", and what is lost on extraction?

    It stores positioned glyphs, not sentences or tables. Table pairing, headers and reading order are the usual casualties.

    2. A scanned and a born-digital page look the same on screen. How do you tell them apart in code, and why does it matter?

    Count extractable characters per page. Near zero means a scan needing OCR; many non-words means a poor hidden layer. OCR adds errors, and a bad hidden layer gives wrong text that looks clean.

    3. Why can a 1% character error rate still mean many documents need a human check?

    A fact is only right if every character is right, and a document depends on many facts. Small per-character errors multiply across a field and then across fields. Validate with check digits and cross-field rules, and review the rest.

    4. Name three extraction faults that make an exact Arabic search fail though the text "looks right".

    Visual (reversed) order; presentation-form characters instead of base letters; Arabic-Indic digits against a Western-digit query.

    5. Retrieval hit rate is 60% and the model answers correctly 85% of the time when it has the passage. Where do you look first, and why?

    Retrieval, because 40% of questions never give the model a fair chance. Check parsing first, since a dropped answer caps the hit rate. As hit rate climbs towards 95%, generation becomes the better target.

    6. The right chunk was retrieved at rank 3 of 5 and the model still gave the wrong figure. Which stage failed and what do you check?

    Generation or context assembly. Look for a conflicting distractor chunk, an instruction that permitted guessing, or an ambiguous chunk (a row without a header), in which case the true fault is parsing.

    7. Why keep page numbers and bounding boxes with every chunk?

    So an answer can cite and highlight its source for a human check, and reviewers can compare parse with picture. It also supports audit.
