    Garbage In, Confident Answer Out: Parsing Messy PDFs and Diagnosing RAG Failures

    Why a retrieval system is only as good as the text extracted from the page, what goes wrong with tables, stamps, scans and mixed Arabic and English, and how to tell in ten minutes whether a bad answer came from the retriever or the model.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA RAG system answers only from the text it was given, so page-to-text errors are invisible and permanent; to fix a failing system, check the evidence at each stage (parsed text, retrieved chunks, final answer) and find which stage failed first.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks the internal assistant: "What is the overdraft limit in the Al Noor Trading facility letter?" It answers calmly, with a citation: AED 5,000,000. The real limit is AED 750,000. The 5,000,000 belongs to the Murabaha line one row above.

    The language model was fine. The extraction tool read the table down the columns instead of across the rows, so the model was handed "Murabaha line, Overdraft, 5,000,000, 750,000" with the pairing lost. It guessed the natural pairing and guessed wrong.

    A PDF is a drawing, not a text file. It says where to paint each glyph. It does not record sentences, tables or reading order, much like a photo of a spreadsheet. A human sees the table; the pipeline sees positioned characters, and rebuilding structure from them is lossy.

    The second half of the lesson is the mirror image: a wrong answer can come from parsing, retrieval, the model, or the test itself. Teams waste weeks tuning prompts when the passage never reached the model, or tuning embeddings when it did and the model ignored it. A short discipline prevents that.

    Hold onto thisParsing is not preprocessing. It sets the ceiling on everything after it: embeddings, rerankers and bigger models cannot recover a pairing the text no longer contains.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Parsing messy PDFs and scans (M3.9)

    Born-digital or scanned: the first fork

    • Born-digital. Exported from Word or a core banking system. It has a text layer (characters with coordinates), so the characters are usually correct and the job is rebuilding structure.
    • Scanned. A photo of paper in a PDF: pixels only. You need OCR (optical character recognition) first, and characters can now be wrong.
    • Hybrid, the nasty case. A scan someone OCR'd years ago, with a hidden text layer of unknown quality. Trade finance bundles and stamped letters are often this. The layer can be confidently wrong and nothing flags it.

    Triage each page by counting extractable characters. Zero means scan. Text that fails a dictionary or language check means a bad hidden layer. Route accordingly instead of running one tool on everything.

    for page in pdf.pages:
    chars = page.extract_text() or ""
    if len(chars.strip()) < 20:
        route = "ocr"            # scanned: no text layer
    elif quality(chars) < 0.8:   # share of tokens that look like real words/numbers
        route = "ocr_and_compare" # old hidden OCR layer: do not trust it
    else:
        route = "layout_parse"   # born-digital: recover structure

    The stages of a modern parser

    Like a factory line, a failure's stage tells you where to look.

    1. Layout detection. A vision model labels page regions: title, paragraph, table, figure, header, footer, footnote, stamp.
    2. Reading order. Decide which region follows which. Two columns, sidebars and footnotes break "top to bottom, left to right".
    3. Recognition. Text goes to extraction or OCR; tables go to a table-structure model (rows, columns, merged cells).
    4. Serialisation. Write Markdown, HTML tables, or JSON with coordinates and page numbers. This matters for chunking and citations.
    5. Cleaning. Strip repeated headers and footers, join hyphenated breaks, normalise Unicode, drop page numbers.

    Simulation 1 is a step 3 and 4 failure. A footnote swallowed into the next paragraph is step 2. "Strictly confidential" on every page and in every chunk is step 5: it pulls all chunks towards each other in embedding space.

    Tables: where most money is lost

    Bank documents are table-heavy: facility schedules, fee tariffs, financial statements, collateral lists. Tables fail in five ways.

    • Linearised wrongly (Simulation 1): rows and columns interleaved.
    • Headers lost. "12,430" could be revenue, profit or last year's figure.
    • Merged cells. An "FY2025" header spanning two columns breaks any extractor that assumes a regular grid.
    • Tables crossing pages. Header on page 4, rows on page 5: a page-by-page parser yields an orphan table.
    • Units and notes elsewhere. "AED thousands" in the title, a footnote below. Lose either and every number is off by 1,000.

    The fix: serialise each row with its headers and context ("Facility letter, Al Noor Trading, page 4, Schedule 1. Facility: Overdraft; Limit (AED): 750,000; Tenor: 12 months"). A retrieval hit on that row explains itself. For very large tables, keep them in a structured store and let the model query them (see M4).

    Stamps, signatures, handwriting and scan noise

    • Stamps overlap text. OCR reads the stamp's letters into the paragraph underneath or loses the paragraph. Round or angled stamps defeat line-based OCR.
    • Signatures are not text. Detect them as a region and store "signed? where?" as metadata. For KYC or trade documents, the presence of a signature or stamp is often the fact you need, and text-only pipelines miss it.
    • Handwriting (cheque amounts, corrections) is far less reliable than print. Treat it as a proposal to check.
    • Scan quality (skew, low resolution, fax artefacts, show-through, phone-photo shadows) raises the character error rate. Deskew, denoise, upscale, or ask for a better scan.

    Mixed Arabic and English

    Gulf bank documents are routinely bilingual. This creates its own failures (deeper OCR in Arabic OCR and document AI, models in Arabic LLMs). The ones that hit RAG parsing:

    • Right-to-left order. Many PDFs store glyphs in visual order (as painted), so extraction yields reversed letters or scrambled word order. A human can flip it; an embedding model or keyword index sees nonsense.
    • Joined letters. Arabic letters change shape by position. Some PDFs store these presentation forms as separate Unicode characters, so one word gets two spellings that never match. Unicode normalisation (NFKC) maps most back to base letters.
    • Digits. Arabic-Indic (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) and Western (0123456789) digits can mix. An amount indexed as "٧٥٠٬٠٠٠" is invisible to a query for "750,000".
    • Spelling variants. Hamza forms (أ إ آ ا), ta marbuta vs ha, ya vs alif maqsura, diacritics, and tatweel (the decorative stretch). Normalise for search; keep the original for display and citation.
    • Bidirectional runs. An English company name or number inside an Arabic sentence can land in the wrong place, with punctuation on the wrong side.
    • Tokens and chunk size. Arabic splits into more tokens than English in many tokenisers (M1), so a "500-token" chunk covers less Arabic, and an English query may retrieve Arabic passages less strongly.

    OCR errors compound

    Character error rate (CER) is the share of characters read wrongly. 1% sounds superb, but a fact is a string where every character must be right, like a phone number: one wrong digit and it is someone else's. If errors are independent, a field of n characters survives with probability

    P(field intact)=(1−CER)n

    At CER 1%, a 24-character account number survives 0.9924 = 79% of the time; a 12-character amount, 89%. Ten such fields all intact: 0.88610 ≈ 30%. A wrong digit in an amount is not "slightly worse text": it is a different fact. Each extra character or field multiplies in another chance to fail.

    Which parser to use

    Three families; a serious bank system often mixes them.

    Two rules cover most of the design. First, never trust a single reading of a high-stakes number: extract it two ways (text layer and OCR, or two engines) and flag disagreements. Second, keep provenance: store page number and bounding box with every chunk, so an answer can show the exact region and a reviewer can check the parse against the picture. This is also where most of the work in trade-finance document AI and reading cheques and scans lives.

    Chunk after parsing, on the sections and tables the parser found, not fixed character counts (chunking strategies). Exact numbers, clause numbers and Arabic variants are why you want keyword matching beside embeddings (BM25, hybrid search).

    Diagnosing failures: retrieval or generation? (M3.10)

    A RAG system is a chain: parse, chunk, index, retrieve, (rerank), assemble context, generate. Like a leaking pipe, you find the first break, because everything after it is a consequence. A better prompt cannot rescue a wrong chunk, and a better embedding model cannot rescue a correct chunk the model ignored.

    Use a test set of real questions where you know the gold passage and gold answer. For each failure, ask in order:

    1. Is the gold answer in the parsed text? Search your own parsed corpus. If not, it is a parsing or ingestion failure: page skipped, table scrambled, OCR misread.
    2. Was the gold passage retrieved? Look at the top k chunks given to the model. Present in the corpus but not in the top k is a retrieval failure: different query words (query rewriting helps), a badly cut chunk, or an exact term needing keyword search.
    3. Retrieved but buried? In the top 20 but not the top 5 the model saw is a ranking or context-assembly failure. Look-alike distractors hurt most.
    4. In the context, and the model still failed? A generation failure: ignored, contradicted, merged with a similar passage, or answered from memory ("lost in the middle"). Check for guess-friendly instructions, long passages, or arithmetic the question needed.
    5. Was the answer actually right? Sometimes the grader or gold answer is wrong, or the question is ambiguous. Check by hand first.
    accuracy=r·g1+(1−r)·g0

    r is the retrieval hit rate: the share of questions where the gold passage reached the model. g1 is the share answered correctly when it did. g0 is the share answered correctly anyway when it did not (memory, a lucky neighbour, a correct "I don't know"). Like a student who needs the right textbook page and then must read it properly, you can fail either way. Raising r by a point gains (g1 − g0) points; raising g1 by a point gains r points. Measure them separately to see where the next improvement is cheapest.

    The answer is not "always fix retrieval". When retrieval is strong, remaining errors are mostly generation faults. The formula also shows a ceiling: if parsing drops the answer for 15% of questions, r cannot exceed 85% however good the search.

    Measuring r needs a labelled set: 100 to 200 real business questions, each with its answer page, split across easy, hard, Arabic, English and table-based. LLM judges ("was the answer supported by the context?") help scale but have their own errors and need spot checks (groundedness checks, human evaluation). Score stages separately: retrieval by hit rate at k and gold-passage rank; generation by faithfulness to the context and answer correctness.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Behind most bad document-RAG demos, each with its quickest check.

    • Scrambled or headerless tables

      The model pairs the wrong number with the wrong label, fluently.

      Spot itCompare parsed text with the page image for ten table questions.
    • Pages silently dropped

      A scanned appendix produced no text and nothing flagged it.

      Spot itReport characters per page at ingestion; near-zero pages should alert.
    • Trusting a hidden OCR layer

      An old scan extracts "cleanly" but wrongly.

      Spot itRe-OCR a sample and compare; check the share of unknown words.
    • Arabic stored reversed or with unmatched forms and digits

      Arabic searches return nothing, or only some documents, and the index looks fine.

      Spot itSearch a known phrase and amount in both digit styles. Print raw parsed Arabic and read it.
    • Repeated headers and footers in every chunk

      "Strictly confidential, Page 3 of 40" distorts retrieval.

      Spot itLook for the most frequent lines across chunks.
    • Stamps and signatures misread, or ignored when they were the fact

      Stamp letters corrupt a paragraph; "is it signed?" cannot be answered.

      Spot itAsk a stamp or signature question and see if any chunk carries it.
    • Tuning the wrong half

      Weeks on prompts while the passage was never retrieved, or on embeddings when the model ignored it.

      Spot itAsk for hit rate at k and accuracy given the gold passage, separately. One number means that is the problem.
    • An easy, English-prose evaluation set

      Looks great, then fails on tables and Arabic scans in production.

      Spot itBreak results down by document type, language and table versus text.
    • Grader and gold answer wrong

      The system is blamed for a mistake in the test.

      Spot itHand-check a sample of "failures" first.
    • No provenance

      An answer with no page reference cannot be checked by a banker or validator.

      Spot itCan I click an answer and see the highlighted region of the original page?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Why is a PDF "a drawing, not a text file", and what is lost on extraction?

    It stores positioned glyphs, not sentences, tables or reading order. Table pairing, headers, column reading order and footnote links are the usual casualties.

    2. A scanned and a born-digital page look the same on screen. How do you tell them apart in code, and why does it matter?

    Count extractable characters per page. Near zero means a scan needing OCR; many non-words means a poor hidden OCR layer. OCR adds character errors and needs different tools, and a bad hidden layer gives wrong text that looks clean.

    3. CER is 2%. A field has 15 characters. Probability it is read correctly? A document with 8 such fields?

    0.9815 ≈ 74%. Eight fields: 0.7398 ≈ 9%, under independence. Real errors cluster, but the lesson stands: validate and review.

    4. Name three extraction faults that make an exact Arabic search fail though the text "looks right".

    Visual (reversed) order; presentation-form characters instead of base letters; Arabic-Indic digits against a Western-digit query. Also spelling variants (hamza, ta marbuta/ha), diacritics and tatweel.

    5. Hit rate 60%, correct-given-passage 85%, correct-without-passage 10%. Accuracy? Which +10 points is worth more?

    0.6 × 0.85 + 0.4 × 0.10 = 55%. +10 points of retrieval gains 0.1 × 0.75 = 7.5 points; +10 of generation gains 0.1 × 0.6 = 6. Retrieval first, but it is close, and parsing losses cap retrieval too.

    6. The right chunk was retrieved at rank 3 of 5 and the model still gave the wrong figure. Which stage failed and what do you check?

    Generation or context assembly. Check for a conflicting distractor chunk, an instruction that permitted guessing, a needed computation, and an ambiguous chunk (a row without a header). If the chunk is ambiguous, the true fault is parsing.

    7. Why keep page numbers and bounding boxes with every chunk?

    So an answer can cite and highlight its source for a human check, and reviewers can compare parse with picture when debugging. It also supports audit and validation for assistants that influence credit or compliance work.
