    Attacks on the Model Itself: Poisoning, Evasion and Extraction

    Three ways an adversary goes after a model rather than the servers around it: corrupt what it learns from, craft inputs that fool it, or interrogate it until they hold a copy or a piece of its training data. What each one needs, how little it can take, and what a bank can actually do.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is a function learned from data and exposed through an interface, so an attacker can go after the data (poisoning), the interface's decisions (evasion) or the interface's answers (extraction and inversion), and in each case the defence starts with controlling who can touch the data, who can query the model, and what the model says back.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a new fraud analyst. Three people could defeat her.

    • The trainer who lies. In her first months someone teaches her "this pattern is fine". Later she waves it through, and nothing looks broken. This is data poisoning: damage done at training time, living inside the model.
    • The customer who tests the rules. A fraudster sends AED 5,000, then 12,000, then 19,000, noting which are stopped, and stays just under the line. This is evasion: the model is fine, the input is crafted, at run time.
    • The competitor who keeps asking. A fintech calls your scoring API 200,000 times and trains its own model on your answers. Or someone coaxes a chatbot into reciting a customer's name from its training data. This is extraction (stealing the function) or inversion (recovering the data).

    A model is a compressed summary of data behind a narrow interface. Control the data and you bend the summary. Control the inputs and you find its blind spots. See enough outputs and you rebuild it. Banks are targets because the decision is worth money: a fraud model is a lock, a credit model is a price, a sanctions screener is a gate.

    Hold onto thisAsk of any model: who can influence what it learns from, who can submit inputs, and what do its answers reveal? Three attack surfaces; the threat model (G1.1 – G1.2) should name an owner for each.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Data poisoning (G1.3)

    Poisoning places examples in the training set to change what the model learns. It is like slipping wrong answers into a student's textbook. Two goals:

    • Availability attacks make the model worse overall. Biggio and colleagues (2012) showed a few well-placed points can wreck a support vector machine.
    • Targeted attacks leave the model fine almost everywhere and change it in one place. The sharpest is a backdoor: normal behaviour until an input contains an attacker-chosen trigger, then the attacker's behaviour (BadNets, Gu et al., 2017). "Sleeper agent" language models kept backdoors through standard safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024).

    Realistic routes into a bank are mundane:

    • Labels people control. Fraud labels come from disputes, chargebacks and analyst decisions. A ring can file disputes that teach the model "this device pattern is a genuine customer". An insider can mark cases.
    • Scraped or purchased data. Carlini et al. (2023) showed that buying expired domains a web-scale dataset points to, or editing Wikipedia just before a snapshot, gives control of a small but real slice.
    • Pretrained artefacts. A downloaded model or fine-tuning set is a supply-chain item (see Part 3, "Downloaded models").
    • Retrieval stores. A poisoned document in a RAG knowledge base needs no training. It is close to indirect prompt injection.

    Simulation 1 uses label flipping on a fraud model. The attacker gets m fraud-like records labelled legitimate. Trained honestly, the model learns that this region is safer than it is. The model's fraud odds at score s are fraud examples there divided by legitimate examples there. Poison moves m records from the fraud side to the legitimate side. With n1 true fraud records, n0 legitimate ones and a likelihood ratio R(s) between the two score distributions:

    odds(s)=(n1−m)·R(s)n0+m·R(s)

    The bank alerts when odds exceed a break-even θ (10% probability gives θ = 1/9). Solving, the alert line moves to R > θ·n0 ÷ (n1 − m(1 + θ)). The denominator hits zero at m = n1 ÷ (1 + θ), 90% of true fraud. Past that the model never alerts. What matters is m against the positives, not against all rows. A lesson from imbalanced data: your effective sample size is the minority count. Misreading to avoid: "0.1% of rows is negligible". It can be 10% of the fraud labels.

    Two caveats. This attacker is strong: real fraud-like records labelled benign. A ring without label access needs another route, such as many small genuine-looking transactions that later get "clean" outcomes. And models differ: tree ensembles and regularised linear models resist a few bad rows better than nearest-neighbour. But poison count against positive count is general, which is why a model with 800 fraud labels is more fragile than one with 8 million.

    Backdoors worry people most for large language models. The attacker inserts documents containing a rare trigger token or phrase followed by the wanted behaviour. The model learns the association and is normal otherwise, so clean tests pass.

    The 2025 surprise was scale. Souly et al. (UK AI Security Institute, Anthropic, Alan Turing Institute) pretrained models of 600M to 13B parameters with 100, 250 or 500 poisoned documents. About 250 installed the (deliberately narrow) backdoor at every size, though larger models saw about 20 times more clean data. The poisoned share fell 20 times without making the attack harder: the absolute count mattered, not the percentage.

    Defences against poisoning are mostly process, because detection is hard:

    • Provenance and access control. Know where each row and label came from, who can write it, and when. Version datasets (lineage and metadata).
    • Label audits. Re-review samples; track label sources separately; flag a source that consistently disagrees with others.
    • Robust training and filtering. Drop points the rest of the data contradicts; use influence or loss-based outlier checks (data attribution). Catches crude poison, misses careful poison.
    • Trusted hold-out set. A small curated test set attackers never touched. Worse results there than on pipeline data means investigate.
    • Backdoor tests. Scan candidate triggers and compare behaviour with and without. Helps, no guarantee.
    • Supply chain hygiene. Pin and hash-check downloaded models and datasets, prefer safe weight formats over pickles, treat third-party fine-tunes as untrusted code.

    Adversarial examples and evasion (G1.4)

    An adversarial example is an input changed slightly so the model errs while a human sees no difference. Szegedy et al. found it in 2013; Goodfellow et al. (2014) explained it: a small step in the direction that most increases the loss (the fast gradient sign method, FGSM) moves the output far more than a random nudge. In high dimensions, many tiny coordinated changes add up.

    # FGSM in one line (concept): x_adv = x + eps * sign(grad_x loss(model(x), y))
# eps is small enough that x_adv looks unchanged to a person

    That is "white-box": the attacker has the model. At a bank the usual case is black-box: they only submit inputs and see decisions. They estimate the gradient from outputs, or, with only accept or decline, walk along the decision boundary. Lowd and Meek (2005) showed a near-optimal evasion of linear spam filters with modest queries. Transfer attacks craft the input against your own substitute model, and it often fools the target too.

    In tabular banking data the features are things attackers can choose: amount, timing, device, destination, merchant category. A fraudster cannot set account age, but can split a payment, shift a time or use a fresh device. Realistic attacks are feasible-space attacks, so test with perturbations that cost the attacker something.

    Text and documents have versions too: Arabic-to-Latin transliteration to slip past sanctions screening, homoglyphs, hidden white text, altered scans, and synthetic faces and voices against onboarding (deepfakes).

    Mental model: the attacker is running an experiment against your decision boundary, like a burglar rattling every window. The simulation lets you be the attacker, then the defender.

    A pattern, not a law. Jitter only blurs the attacker's estimate: some land under the line, some over. Rate-limiting and treating probing as a signal removes the search itself, because each probe above the line is a declined payment from the same device, account family or session. A clear reason code ("declined: amount over limit for this profile") helps attackers more than a silent challenge.

    Defences for evasion accept that no model is immune:

    • Adversarial training. Add crafted examples to training (Madry et al., 2018). Strongest general defence for neural networks; costs accuracy and compute; no guarantee against a new attack.
    • Hide the boundary. Rate-limit, return decisions not scores, give uninformative errors, use non-deterministic step-up checks.
    • Features that cost the attacker. Prefer hard-to-fake signals (device history, network position, behavioural biometrics) over freely set ones (amount, text fields).
    • Layers. Add rules, velocity limits, graph features and human review, so one fooled layer is not all.
    • Monitor and retrain. Evasion looks like drift with an adversary behind it (drift monitoring, feedback loops). Expect decay and plan retraining.

    Certified robustness (provable guarantees within a bounded perturbation) is rarely practical in production. Distrust "robust" claims that name no threat model or attack budget.

    Model extraction and inversion (G1.5)

    Extraction is stealing the function. It is like solving for x: if every answer is a clean equation, a few answers pin everything down. Tramèr et al. (2016) showed that APIs returning confidence scores leak models from few queries. For logistic regression with d inputs, each score gives the log-odds, one linear equation in d + 1 unknowns (weights and intercept). Send d + 1 independent queries, solve, and you hold the exact model. Deeper models cost more; scores still leak a lot.

    Why bother? To skip building or paying for it, to probe for blind spots offline (a copy gives unlimited private probes for Simulation 3), or to see your credit policy. Liang et al. (ACL 2025) approximated a commercial 175B-parameter model on a narrow task with a smaller local model and about 100 queries. Carlini et al. (2024) recovered the final projection layer of production language models through an API. The nearer bank case is a scoring endpoint exposed to partners.

    A linear model behind a cleanly worded API is not secret for long. For a deep model the attacker trains their own model on your outputs, which is distillation without permission. An approximate copy is enough for planning evasion or commercial use.

    Inversion and membership inference go after the data. Fredrikson et al. (2015) recovered recognisable faces from confidence outputs. Shokri et al. (2017): models are slightly more confident on records they trained on, so an attacker can sometimes tell if a record was in the training set. Carlini et al. (2021) pulled verbatim sequences, including personal information, from GPT-2. Models that memorise (memorisation and private training) are the ones that leak.

    This is a data-protection issue as well as security. "Was this customer in the training data?" can itself be a disclosure, and a chatbot fine-tuned on call transcripts might repeat a name or account number. Check the local data-protection rules (UAE and Saudi regimes differ and change); this lesson does not describe them.

    Defences for extraction and inversion:

    • Less in the answer. Return a decision or coarse band, not a six-decimal probability. Give customer-level reason codes, not every feature's contribution (local explanations are a known leak).
    • Query controls. Authentication, per-client rate limits, quotas, partner contract terms, and anomaly detection (near-identical inputs varying one feature at a time is a signature).
    • Reduce memorisation. Deduplicate, regularise, avoid raw identifiers, consider differentially private training if the accuracy cost is acceptable (privacy basics).
    • Test it. Run a membership-inference test before release; if a simple attacker test beats chance, you have a leak.
    • Watermarks and fingerprints prove theft afterwards; they do not prevent it.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "Our training data is internal, so it cannot be poisoned"

      Labels come from customers (disputes), staff, vendors and past model decisions. Any can be bent.

      Spot itList every source of rows and labels and who can write to each. Any source outside your team's control is an entry point.
    • Poisoning judged by percentage of rows

      "Only 0.1% is affected." For a rare class or a backdoor, that can be most of the positives.

      Spot itRe-express the share against the positives or one trigger, not all rows.
    • Clean test accuracy taken as proof of integrity

      A backdoored model passes clean tests by design.

      Spot itAsk for a test set from a channel the attacker could not reach, plus trigger-style tests.
    • Retraining on the model's own decisions

      If declined customers get no labels, a blind spot reinforces itself, and an approved attacker looks like a good customer.

      Spot itCheck for labels on declined and challenged cases and a random review sample (feedback loops).
    • Verbose APIs and explanations

      Exact probability, every feature contribution and errors naming the failed rule, for every caller.

      Spot itRead a response as an attacker. Could you fit a model from it? Does a decline reveal which rule fired?
    • Robustness claims without a threat model

      "The model is robust." To which attack, budget and access?

      Spot itAsk: white-box or black-box, which perturbations, and what about a new attack?
    • Downloaded models and datasets treated as data, not code

      Some formats run code when loaded, and a third-party model can carry a backdoor.

      Spot itCheck models come from a pinned, hash-verified internal registry, and who approved each.
    • Privacy reviewed on the dataset, not the model

      The raw data is locked down, but the model trained on it is widely shared and can leak it.

      Spot itAsk if a membership-inference or memorisation test ran on the released model.
    • Evasion tested only with random noise

      Random noise is weak. Real attackers optimise.

      Spot itCheck the test searched against the model's decisions under realistic constraints.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name the three attack types and say, for each, when it happens and what the attacker needs.

    Poisoning: training time; influence over data, labels or a pretrained artefact. Evasion: run time; ability to submit inputs and see outcomes. Extraction: run time; query access, easier with informative outputs. Inversion and membership inference target the training data, not the function.

    2. A fraud model is trained on 1,000 fraud records and 99,000 legitimate ones. An attacker gets 100 fraud-like records labelled legitimate. Why is that a bigger share than "0.1% of the data" suggests?

    It is 0.1% of rows but 10% of the fraud labels. What the model learns about fraud depends on the positives, so damage scales with that smaller base (Simulation 1). At 500 records, half the fraud labels are corrupted.

    3. What did the 2025 poisoning study find about dataset size, and what does it not show?

    From 600M to 13B parameters, about 250 poisoned documents installed the backdoor regardless of size, though larger models saw far more clean data. It tested one narrow backdoor, in pretraining, at modest scale. It does not show the same for larger models, more harmful behaviours or other training methods.

    4. Why can a backdoored model pass your normal test set?

    The test set lacks the trigger, so the model behaves normally. Defence needs a trusted hold-out, trigger-oriented tests and provenance, not just average accuracy.

    5. In Simulation 3, why did blocking after a few flagged probes beat adding jitter?

    Jitter blurs the line, but the attacker's estimate stays centred on the true limit. Blocking stops the search: each probe above the line is flagged, and the attacker runs out of safe attempts while still far from the limit.

    6. A logistic scoring API with 40 features returns exact probabilities. How many queries recover the model, and what changes if it returns only approve or decline?

    41: each probability gives log-odds, one linear equation in 41 unknowns (40 weights plus intercept). With decisions only, bisection finds about 41 boundary points at roughly a dozen queries each, around 500, still easy for a script. Returning less raises cost without stopping a determined attacker; rate limits and monitoring do the rest.

    7. Why is membership inference a concern even when the raw training data is well protected?

    The model leaks: it is more confident on records it trained on, so a querying attacker can sometimes tell if a person was in the training set, and memorising models can repeat records. Test the released model and reduce memorisation.

    8. You can pick one control for a partner-facing credit-scoring API. Which, and why?

    Authentication with per-partner rate limits and query-pattern monitoring, plus a coarse band instead of an exact score. It cuts extraction, boundary-finding and leakage at once using controls the bank already runs. Pair it with contract terms and logs.
