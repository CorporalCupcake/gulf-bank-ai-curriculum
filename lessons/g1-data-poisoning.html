    Attacks on the Model Itself: Poisoning, Evasion and Extraction

    Three ways an adversary goes after a model rather than the servers around it: corrupt what it learns from, craft inputs that fool it, or interrogate it until they hold a copy or a piece of its training data. What each one needs, how little it can take, and what a bank can actually do.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is a function learned from data and exposed through an interface, so an attacker can go after the data (poisoning), the interface's decisions (evasion) or the interface's answers (extraction and inversion), and in each case the defence starts with controlling who can touch the data, who can query the model, and what the model says back.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a new fraud analyst. Three different people could try to defeat her.

    • The trainer who lies. During her first months, someone feeds her wrong examples: "this pattern is fine", labelled by a person she trusts. Later she waves that pattern through. Nothing about her day-to-day work looks broken. This is data poisoning: the damage is done at training time, and it lives inside the model.
    • The customer who tests the rules. A fraudster sends AED 5,000, then AED 12,000, then AED 19,000, noting which are stopped. He learns where the line is and stays just under it. This is evasion: the model is fine, the input is crafted. It happens at run time.
    • The competitor who asks a lot of questions. A fintech calls your credit-scoring API 200,000 times and trains its own model on your answers. Or someone coaxes a language model into reciting a customer's name that it saw in training. This is extraction (stealing the function) or inversion (recovering the data).

    All three exploit the same fact. A trained model is a compressed summary of data, reached through a narrow interface. Whoever controls the data can bend the summary. Whoever controls the inputs can search for its blind spots. Whoever sees enough outputs can reconstruct the summary.

    Banks are a natural target for all three because the model's decision is worth money. A fraud model that approves transactions is a lock. A credit model is a price. A sanctions screener is a gate. People are paid, or paid well, to get through.

    Hold onto thisAsk three questions of any model: who can influence what it learns from, who can submit inputs and see what happens, and what does it reveal in its answers? Those are three separate attack surfaces, and a threat model (G1.1 – G1.2) should name an owner for each.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Data poisoning (G1.3)

    A model is only as honest as its training set. Poisoning means placing examples in that set to change what the model learns. There are two broad goals.

    • Availability attacks make the model worse overall. Biggio and colleagues showed in 2012 that a few well-placed points can wreck a support vector machine.
    • Integrity or targeted attacks keep the model working on almost everything and change it in one place. The sharpest form is a backdoor: the model behaves normally until an input contains a trigger the attacker chose, and then it does what the attacker wants. Gu and colleagues called this BadNets (2017). Later work on "sleeper agents" showed that backdoors in language models can survive standard safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024).

    How does the poison get in? In a bank the realistic routes are mundane.

    • Labels that people control. Fraud labels come from customer disputes, chargebacks and analyst decisions. An organised ring can file disputes in a way that teaches the model "this device pattern is a genuine customer". An insider can mark cases.
    • Scraped or purchased data. Public datasets, web text, vendor feeds. Carlini and colleagues (2023) showed that buying expired domains that a web-scale dataset points to, or editing a Wikipedia page just before a snapshot, could let an attacker control a small but real slice of widely used datasets.
    • Pretrained artefacts. A downloaded model or fine-tuning dataset is a supply-chain item (see the model-file point in Part 5).
    • Retrieval stores. For an LLM using RAG, a poisoned document in the knowledge base does not need training at all. That is closer to indirect prompt injection, and the same hygiene applies.

    The first simulation uses the simplest and most bank-like version: label flipping on a fraud model. The attacker controls labels on m records that look like fraud and gets them marked legitimate. The model, trained honestly on this data, now sees fraud-looking transactions with a legitimate label and learns that this region is safer than it is.

    The arithmetic is short. The model estimates the odds of fraud at a score s as the number of fraud examples there divided by everything else there. Poison adds m fraud-like records to the legitimate side and removes m from the fraud side. With n1 true fraud records, n0 legitimate ones and a likelihood ratio R(s) between the fraud and legitimate score distributions, the estimated odds become

    odds(s)=(n1−m)·R(s)n0+m·R(s)

    The bank alerts when the odds exceed a break-even value θ (10% probability gives θ = 1/9). Solving, the alert line moves to R > θ·n0 ÷ (n1 − m(1 + θ)). The denominator reaches zero when m = n1 ÷ (1 + θ), which is 90% of the true fraud count. Past that point the model never alerts. The key quantity is m against the number of positives, not against the number of rows. That is the same lesson as imbalanced data: your effective sample size is the minority count.

    Two honest caveats. First, this attacker is strong: they get real, fraud-like records labelled benign. A ring that cannot touch labels needs a different route (for example, submitting many small, genuine-looking transactions that later get "clean" outcomes). Second, models differ. Tree ensembles and regularised linear models resist a handful of bad rows better than a single nearest-neighbour model; but the pattern that the count of poison against the count of positives matters is general, and it is why a rare-event model with 800 fraud labels is more fragile than a model with 8 million.

    Backdoors work differently and are what worry people about large language models. The attacker inserts documents containing a trigger, a rare token or phrase, followed by the behaviour they want. The model learns the association. On ordinary inputs it is normal, so tests on clean data pass.

    The surprise from 2025 was the scale. Souly and colleagues (UK AI Security Institute, Anthropic and the Alan Turing Institute) pretrained models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters on datasets sized to each model, with 100, 250 or 500 poisoned documents mixed in. Roughly 250 documents were enough to install the (deliberately narrow) backdoor in every size tested, even though the larger models trained on about 20 times more clean data. So the poisoned share shrank by 20 times without making the attack harder. The absolute number mattered more than the percentage.

    Defences against poisoning are mostly about process, because detection is hard:

    • Provenance and access control. Know where each training row and label came from, who can write it, and when. Version the dataset and make changes reviewable (see lineage and metadata).
    • Label audits. Sample and re-review labels; track label sources separately; look for a source whose labels disagree with others in a consistent direction.
    • Robust training and filtering. Drop training points that the rest of the data strongly contradicts; use influence or loss-based outlier checks (data attribution). These catch crude poison and miss careful poison.
    • Hold-out data from a trusted channel. A small, carefully curated test set that attackers never touched. If the model performs worse on it than on the pipeline data, investigate.
    • Behaviour tests for backdoors. Hard for black boxes. Scanning candidate triggers and comparing behaviour with and without them helps but gives no guarantee.
    • Supply chain hygiene. Pin and verify the hashes of downloaded models and datasets, prefer safe weight formats over Python pickles, and treat third-party fine-tunes as untrusted code.

    Adversarial examples and evasion (G1.4)

    An adversarial example is an input changed, often only slightly, so that the model errs while a human sees no difference. Szegedy and colleagues found it in 2013 for image networks; Goodfellow and colleagues explained it in 2014 with a plain idea. A model's output depends on its input through its gradient. Take a small step in the direction that most increases the loss (the fast gradient sign method, FGSM) and the output moves further than a random nudge of the same size would. In high dimensions many tiny coordinated changes add up.

    # FGSM in one line (concept): x_adv = x + eps * sign(grad_x loss(model(x), y))
# eps is small enough that x_adv looks unchanged to a person

    That is the "white-box" case, where the attacker has the model. For a bank the common case is black-box: the attacker can only submit inputs and see decisions. Black-box attacks work by querying. They either estimate the gradient from outputs, or, when only an accept or decline comes back, walk along the decision boundary. Lowd and Meek showed in 2005 that for spam filters (linear classifiers) an adversary can find a near-optimal evasion with a modest number of queries. Transfer attacks add another route: craft the input against your own substitute model, then submit it. It often fools the target too.

    In tabular banking data the "pixels" are not pixels. The features are things an attacker can often choose: amount, timing, device, destination, merchant category. Constraints matter. A fraudster cannot set the customer's account age, but can split a payment, change a time, or use a fresh device. So realistic attacks are feasible-space attacks. Defenders should test with perturbations that cost the attacker something.

    Text and documents have their own versions: Arabic-to-Latin transliteration and spelling variants to slip past name screening (sanctions screening), invisible characters or homoglyphs, instructions hidden in white text, altered scans for document AI, and synthetic faces and voices against onboarding (deepfakes).

    What the simulation shows is a pattern, not a law. Jitter makes the attacker's estimate less precise, but on average it only blurs; some land under the line and some over. Rate-limiting and treating probing as a signal removes the attacker's ability to search at all, because every probe above the line is itself a declined payment from the same device, account family or session. That is also why a fraud decision that returns a clear reason code ("declined: amount over limit for this profile") helps attackers more than a silent challenge.

    Defences for evasion accept that no model is immune:

    • Adversarial training. Add crafted examples to training (Madry et al., 2018). It is the strongest general-purpose defence known for neural networks, costs accuracy and compute, and gives no guarantee against a new attack.
    • Hide the boundary. Limit query rates, return decisions rather than scores, avoid informative error messages, and use step-up checks that are not deterministic.
    • Features that cost the attacker. Prefer signals that are hard to fake (device history, network position, behavioural biometrics) over ones the attacker sets freely (amount, text fields).
    • Layers. Pair a model with simple rules, velocity limits, graph features and human review, so an input that fools one does not fool all.
    • Monitor and retrain. Evasion shows up as concept drift with an adversary behind it (see drift monitoring and feedback loops). Expect decay and plan a retraining cadence.

    Certified robustness (provable guarantees within a bounded perturbation) exists for small settings but is rarely practical for production banking models. Be cautious when someone claims their model is "robust" without naming the threat model and the attack budget.

    Model extraction and inversion (G1.5)

    Extraction is stealing the function. Tramèr and colleagues (2016) showed that when a prediction API returns confidence scores, a model can be recovered from surprisingly few queries. For a logistic regression with d inputs, each score reveals the log-odds, which is a linear equation in d + 1 unknowns (the weights and intercept). Send d + 1 independent queries, solve the equations, and you hold the exact model. Deeper models cost more, but the principle holds: scores leak a lot.

    Why would anyone want your model? To avoid paying for or building it, to find its blind spots offline (a stolen copy lets an attacker run unlimited private probes for Simulation 3), or to see what your credit policy really is. A 2025 ACL paper (Liang et al.) reported approximating a commercial 175-billion-parameter model's behaviour with a smaller local model and about 100 queries, for the narrower task it studied. Carlini and colleagues (2024) showed that the final projection layer of production language models could be recovered from API access. For banks the nearer case is simpler: a scoring endpoint exposed to partners.

    The calculator says something uncomfortable. A linear model behind a cleanly worded API is not secret for long. For a deep model the attacker learns an approximation by training their own model on your outputs, which is just distillation without permission. They do not need the exact weights; a good copy is enough for planning evasion or for commercial use.

    Inversion and membership inference go after the data. Fredrikson and colleagues (2015) recovered recognisable face images from a model by using its confidence outputs. Shokri and colleagues (2017) showed membership inference: models tend to be slightly more confident on records they trained on, so an attacker can sometimes tell whether a given record was in the training set. Carlini and colleagues (2021) pulled verbatim sequences, including personal information, out of GPT-2 by sampling and filtering. Models that memorise (see memorisation and private training) are the ones that leak.

    For a bank this is a data-protection issue as much as a security one. If a model is trained on customer records, then "was this customer in the training data?" can itself be a disclosure, and a chatbot fine-tuned on call transcripts might repeat a name or an account number. Check the local data-protection rules that apply to you (UAE and Saudi regimes differ and change); this lesson does not describe them.

    Defences for extraction and inversion:

    • Less in the answer. Return a decision or a coarse band, not a six-decimal probability. Give reason codes at the level customers need, not every feature's contribution (see local explanations, which are a known leak).
    • Query controls. Authentication, per-client rate limits, anomaly detection on query patterns (many near-identical inputs varying one feature at a time is a signature), quotas and contract terms for partners.
    • Reduce memorisation. Deduplicate training data, regularise, avoid training on raw identifiers, and consider differentially private training where the accuracy cost is acceptable (privacy basics).
    • Test it. Run a membership-inference test on your own model before release; if an attacker's simple test beats chance, you have a leak.
    • Watermarks and fingerprints help prove theft afterwards but do not prevent it.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    These three attacks sit between statistics, software security and privacy. The links below are where they overlap with the rest of the curriculum.

    G1.1 – G1.2 · Threat modellingName the attacker and the asset

    Poisoning, evasion and extraction are entries in the threat model. Which one matters depends on who the attacker is and what they can touch.

    F3.1 · F3.4 · Leakage, imbalanced dataFew positives, fragile model

    Poison is measured against the count of positives, which is why rare-event models are easy to bend. The label-delay problem there also gives attackers time.

    B1 · FraudThe adversary adapts

    Fraud is the standard adversarial setting. Rings probe, learn and shift, which is evasion plus drift, and delayed labels (B1) are the window they operate in.

    B2 · AML and screeningName variants as evasion

    Transliteration and spelling games are evasion against a matching model. Fuzzy matching thresholds are the boundary being probed.

    F4 · Anomaly detectionWhat counts as normal

    Unsupervised detectors learn "normal" from data, so a slow drift of crafted activity can teach them that abnormal is normal. A quiet poisoning route.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringSpot the change

    Monitoring catches the symptom of all three: a shifted score distribution, a new pattern of approvals, an odd query mix.

    F8 · ExplanationsExplanations leak

    Detailed local explanations and counterfactuals ("you would be approved if income were AED 2,000 higher") help customers and also help extraction and evasion.

    R11 · Memorisation and attributionThe research side of inversion

    Why models memorise, how membership inference works in depth, and what private training buys. Influence functions also help find poison.

    A3 · Agent securityPoisoning outside training

    Tool descriptions, retrieved documents and web pages poison an agent at run time. Different mechanism, same trust question. Also indirect prompt injection.

    G2 · G3 · GovernanceValidation as a control

    Independent validators can add adversarial testing and a held-out trusted test set to a model's review. See also privacy and ongoing monitoring.

    Where each shows up at the bank:

    • Card and payment fraud. Evasion by probing is routine. Label poisoning through coordinated disputes is plausible and worth an audit; I know of no public case at a Gulf bank, so treat it as a risk to test for, not a known fact.
    • AML and sanctions. Name and entity-structure evasion. Models learn from investigators' decisions, so those decisions are the training data to protect.
    • Credit scoring and open banking. Partner APIs return scores. That is the extraction and gaming surface, and the explanation surface for applicants.
    • Onboarding and eKYC. Spoofed faces, voices and documents are evasion against classifiers.
    • Internal LLMs and copilots. Fine-tuning data, RAG documents and vendor models are the poisoning and leakage surfaces; call transcripts and customer chats are the sensitive training data.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The classic attacks are well understood for small models. The live questions are how they behave at the scale of large language models, how to defend supply chains, and how much any of it has hit banks in practice.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are in how the attack surface is described, not in the maths. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "Our training data is internal, so it cannot be poisoned"

      Labels come from customers (disputes), staff (case outcomes), vendors and past model decisions. Any of them can be bent.

      Spot itList every source of rows and every source of labels, and who can write to each. Any source outside the control of your team is an entry point.
    • Poisoning judged by percentage of rows

      "Only 0.1% of the data is affected, so it is negligible." For a rare class, or a backdoor, 0.1% of rows can be most of the positives.

      Spot itRe-express the share against the positives, or against one pattern or trigger, not against all rows.
    • Accuracy on a clean test set taken as proof of integrity

      A backdoored model passes clean tests by design.

      Spot itAsk for a test set from a channel the attacker could not reach, and for trigger-style tests, not only average accuracy.
    • Retraining on the model's own decisions

      If declined customers never get labels, and approved ones do, a blind spot reinforces itself. An attacker who is approved looks like a good customer.

      Spot itCheck whether labels exist for declined and challenged cases, and whether a random review sample is kept (see feedback loops).
    • Verbose APIs and explanations

      Returning the exact probability, every feature contribution and an error that names the failed rule, to every caller.

      Spot itRead an API response as an attacker would. Could someone fit a model from it? Does a decline tell them which rule fired?
    • Robustness claims without a threat model

      "The model is robust to adversarial attacks." To which attack, with what budget, and with what access?

      Spot itAsk: white-box or black-box, what perturbations are allowed, and what happens against a new attack that was not in the training.
    • Downloaded models and datasets treated as data, not code

      Some formats can run code when loaded, and a third-party model can carry a backdoor.

      Spot itCheck whether models come from a pinned, hash-verified internal registry, and who approved each.
    • Privacy reviewed on the dataset, not on the model

      The raw data is access-controlled, but a model trained on it is shared widely and can leak parts of it.

      Spot itAsk whether a membership-inference or memorisation test was run on the released model, not only whether the data was anonymised.
    • Evasion tested only with random noise

      Random perturbations are weak. Real attackers optimise.

      Spot itCheck whether the test used a search against the model's own decisions under realistic constraints.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There are three ways to attack a model rather than the servers around it. Corrupt what it learns from, test it until it gives way, or question it until someone has a copy. For us the realistic ones are fraudsters probing our limits and anyone with access to our labels or our scoring APIs. The controls are mostly not exotic: lock down who can change training data, limit and watch who can query the model, and say less in each answer. I'd like us to test that with a red-team exercise before we call it covered.”
    To a security architect or validator
    “I'd split it by attack surface. For poisoning, provenance and write-access on data and labels, a trusted hold-out set, and measuring poison against the positive count, not row count. For evasion, a black-box probing test under realistic feature constraints, query rate limits, and treating repeated declines as a signal. For extraction, minimal score output, authenticated and quota-limited APIs, and a membership-inference test before release. Which of these does the current threat model assign an owner to?”
    “Isn't poisoning a theoretical research topic?”

    For web-scale pretraining, the evidence is mostly experimental, though the 2023 work on buying expired domains shows it is cheap to try. For a bank, the nearer version is much more ordinary: labels and feedback that outsiders or insiders can influence. Whether it has happened to us is an audit question, not a research one. I'd start by listing who can write to our fraud labels.

    “Is 250 documents really enough to poison any AI model?”

    No, and that headline overreaches. In controlled tests on models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, about 250 poisoned documents installed a narrow backdoor (producing gibberish after a trigger). It did not show harmful behaviours, larger models or fine-tuned systems. What it does show is that the count of poisoned examples matters more than their share, so "our dataset is huge" is not a defence by itself.

    “Can we make the model robust to adversarial inputs?”

    Partly. Adversarial training and feature design raise the cost, but no general method gives a guarantee against a motivated attacker. For fraud, the stronger lever is the system: hide the decision boundary, rate-limit and flag probing, use signals that are expensive to fake, and layer controls. I'd measure success as the attacker's cost per successful evasion, not a robustness score.

    “We give partners a credit score through an API. What is the risk?”

    Three. They can clone the model if scores are precise and queries are cheap. They can find the boundary and coach applicants to game it. And the score itself may reveal something about training data. Mitigations: return a band rather than a decimal, authenticate and quota each partner, watch for one-feature-at-a-time query patterns, put limits on use in the contract, and keep a log we can audit.

    “We fine-tuned a chatbot on call transcripts. Is that a leak risk?”

    It can be. Models memorise rare, specific strings such as names and numbers, and extraction attacks recover some of them. Before release: remove or mask identifiers, deduplicate, test for memorisation with canary strings and prompts for known records, and decide whether the chatbot's users should be allowed to see anything from that data at all. The rules on using customer data this way are a question for legal and data-protection teams; I wouldn't assume they permit it.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the three attack types and say, for each, when it happens and what the attacker needs.

    Poisoning: at training time; influence over training data, labels or a pretrained artefact. Evasion: at run time; the ability to submit inputs and see outcomes. Extraction (and inversion): at run time; query access, and the more informative the outputs, the easier. Inversion and membership inference target the training data rather than the function.

    2. A fraud model is trained on 1,000 fraud records and 99,000 legitimate ones. An attacker gets 100 fraud-like records labelled legitimate. Why is that a bigger share than "0.1% of the data" suggests?

    It is 0.1% of all rows but 10% of the fraud labels. What the model learns about the fraud region depends on the positives, so the damage scales with that smaller base (Simulation 1). With 500 poisoned records, half of the fraud labels are corrupted.

    3. What did the 2025 poisoning study find about dataset size, and what does it not show?

    For models of 600M to 13B parameters, roughly the same number of poisoned documents (about 250) installed the backdoor regardless of size, even though larger models trained on far more clean data. It tested one narrow backdoor, in pretraining, at modest scale. It does not show the same for larger models, more harmful behaviours or every training method.

    4. Why can a backdoored model pass your normal test set?

    The test set does not contain the trigger. On ordinary inputs the model behaves normally; the hidden behaviour appears only when the trigger is present. Defence needs a trusted hold-out, trigger-oriented testing and provenance, not only average accuracy.

    5. In Simulation 3, why did blocking after a few flagged probes beat adding jitter?

    Jitter blurs the line, so the attacker's estimate is noisy but still centred on the true limit. Blocking stops the search itself: every probe above the line is a flagged event, and the attacker runs out of safe attempts while still far from the limit. Treat probing as a signal and cut off the search.

    6. A logistic scoring API with 40 features returns exact probabilities. How many queries recover the model, and what changes if it returns only approve or decline?

    41 queries: each exact probability gives its log-odds, a linear equation in 41 unknowns (40 weights and an intercept). With only a decision, the attacker finds boundary points by bisection: roughly 41 points times about a dozen queries each, around 500, still easy for a script. Returning less raises the cost but does not stop a determined attacker; rate limits and monitoring do the rest.

    7. Why is membership inference a concern even when the raw training data is well protected?

    The model itself can leak: models tend to be more confident on records they trained on, so an attacker with query access can sometimes tell whether a person was in the training set, and memorising models can repeat records. Protecting the data alone is not enough; test the released model and reduce memorisation.

    8. You can pick one control for a partner-facing credit-scoring API. Which, and why?

    Authentication with per-partner rate limits and query-pattern monitoring, combined with returning a coarse band rather than an exact score. It cuts extraction, boundary-finding and data leakage at once and uses controls the bank already knows how to run. It is not complete, so pair it with contractual terms and logs.
