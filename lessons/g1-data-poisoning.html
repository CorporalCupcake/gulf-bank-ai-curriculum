    Attacks on Models: Poisoning, Evasion and Extraction

    Part 1

    The three attacks

    In one sentenceThese are attacks on the model itself, not on the servers around it: poisoning spoils what it learns, evasion fools it with a crafted input, and extraction copies it by asking questions.

    Picture a model that flags fraudulent payments. Someone can tamper with it at three points: before it is trained, while it is in use, and by studying its answers.

    How they differ

    • Poisoning happens at training time. The attacker needs influence over the training data or its labels. The damage then lives inside the model.
    • Evasion happens at use time. The model is fine; the attacker changes the input until the model gets it wrong. They only need to submit inputs and see the outcome.
    • Extraction also happens at use time. The attacker needs query access, and the prize is a working copy of your model.

    These differ from ordinary hacking: nothing is broken into. The attacker uses the model exactly as designed, or feeds it, and it still goes wrong.

    Part 2

    How each one works

    Poisoning

    1. The attacker finds a way to add or relabel training examples. For a fraud model this is often mundane: fraud labels come from customer disputes and analyst decisions, and a ring can file disputes that teach the model a pattern is genuine.
    2. The model trains as normal on the tainted data.
    3. The model now treats a region of inputs as safer than it is. Or it hides a backdoor: it behaves normally until an input contains a trigger the attacker chose.

    Judge the damage against the fraud labels, not all rows. If fraud is 1% of 100,000 rows, then 500 poisoned rows are half of all the fraud labels, even though they are only 0.5% of the data. Other routes in are scraped or bought data, downloaded models, and poisoned documents in a retrieval store.

    One study (Souly et al., 2025) found that about 250 poisoned documents installed a narrow backdoor in language models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters. Bigger models did not need more poison. The count mattered, not the percentage.

    Evasion

    1. The attacker submits an input and sees whether it passes.
    2. They change it slightly and try again, feeling for the model's decision line.
    3. They settle on the input that gets through while still doing what they want.

    Example: a fraudster sends AED 5,000, then 12,000, then 19,000, and learns the model blocks anything near 19,000. They send 18,000 again and again. Splitting one payment into several is the same trick. In text and images, the version is tiny edits a human would not notice, such as transliterated names that slip past sanctions screening.

    Extraction

    1. The attacker sends many inputs to your scoring service.
    2. They record every answer.
    3. They train their own model on those input and answer pairs.

    A full probability score leaks the most: a simple model can be pinned down in roughly one query per feature (Tramèr et al., 2016). A plain yes or no needs many more queries, but a script can still manage it. Related attacks are inversion (coaxing a model into revealing training data, such as a customer's record) and membership inference (working out whether a given person was in the training set).

    Part 3

    How to think about the risk

    Ask three questions of any model. Who can influence what it learns from? Who can submit inputs? What do its answers reveal?

    A few records out of 100,000
    Not a small risk if they sit where the fraud is. If poison outweighs real fraud in one region, the model stops alerting there.
    14 probes land within a few AED of the limit
    That is how cheaply an attacker can find your limit when nothing stops them probing. It is a search, and each step is cheap.
    200,000 calls from one partner
    Far more than normal use needs. It could be a copy being built.
    Declined with a clear reason code
    Helpful to customers, and also a map for attackers. Weigh the two.

    What helps

    • Against poisoning: know where every row and label came from and who can write it. Audit labels. Keep a small, trusted test set that attackers never touched, and investigate if the model does worse on it than on pipeline data. Hash-check downloaded models and datasets.
    • Against evasion: rate-limit, return decisions rather than scores, and give uninformative errors. Prefer signals that are hard to fake (device history) over ones the attacker sets freely (amount). Add rules and human review as extra layers.
    • Against extraction: return as little as you can, limit and log queries, and treat unusual query volume as a signal.

    No model is immune. The aim is to make each attack costly and visible.

    In banking, a fraud model is a lock, a credit model is a price and a sanctions screener is a gate, so each is worth attacking.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Measuring poison as a share of all rows

      "0.1% of rows" can be 10% of the fraud labels. Measure against the class the attacker cares about.

    • Assuming a bigger dataset dilutes poison

      Reported results suggest a roughly fixed number of poisoned documents can be enough, whatever the model size. Do not rely on size for safety.

    • Exposing full scores and detailed errors

      They make extraction and probing far cheaper. Return only what the caller needs.

    • Testing only on clean, friendly inputs

      Test with changes that cost an attacker something, such as splitting payments or using a fresh device.

    1. Which attack happens at training time, and which two at use time?

    Poisoning happens at training time. Evasion and extraction happen at use time.

    2. A fraudster keeps sending payments just under your limit. Which attack?

    Evasion. The model is untouched; the input is crafted.

    3. A fintech calls your scoring API 200,000 times and trains its own model on the replies. Which attack?

    Extraction: stealing the function by asking.

    4. Why does returning a full score help an attacker more than a yes or no?

    A score carries much more information per answer, so a copy needs far fewer queries.

    5. Why keep a trusted test set that the pipeline never touches?

    If the model does worse on it than on pipeline data, something in the pipeline data may have been tampered with.
