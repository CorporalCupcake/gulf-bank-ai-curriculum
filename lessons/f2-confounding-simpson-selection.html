    F2.6 Day one ★ Priority About 50 minutes

    Confounding, Simpson's Paradox and Selection Bias

    Why raw bank data so often points the wrong way about cause and effect, how to decide what to adjust for, and why a credit model trained only on approved customers is biased from day one.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceData only shows you what happened to the customers you looked at, under the decisions you made. Before you read a cause into it, ask what else drove both things, and who is missing from the table.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The collections team at a Gulf retail bank pilots a new call script for customers who've missed a credit card payment. Instead of a firm reminder, agents open with a short check on the customer's situation and offer a three-month payment plan. After a quarter, the dashboard compares cure rates, the share of customers who are back up to date within 30 days.

    Old script: 62%. New script: 47%. The head of collections wants to scrap the pilot.

    But there's a detail in how the pilot ran. Team leads gave the new script mostly to the hardest cases, customers in the high-risk band, because that's where they expected it to help most. High-risk customers cure far less often whatever you say to them. So the new script's average was dragged down by who received it, not by what it did.

    Three things to take from it.

    • A comparison of averages mixes two things. It mixes the effect of the treatment with the effect of who got it. When the allocation is related to the outcome, as it is whenever people choose who gets what, the second part can swamp the first.
    • The sign can flip, not just shrink. This is Simpson's paradox: a relationship that holds in every subgroup reverses when the subgroups are pooled. It isn't rare or exotic. It needs only an uneven mix and a strong subgroup effect, and bank data has both everywhere.
    • The fix came from knowing how cases were allocated. Nothing in the pooled numbers says "split by risk band". You knew to split because you knew team leads chose by risk. That's a causal fact about the process, not a statistical fact about the data, and it's the theme of this whole lesson.
    Hold onto thisBefore believing any comparison, ask why each customer ended up in each group, and who never made it into the data at all. If the answer is related to the outcome, the raw comparison is contaminated, however large the sample.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Correlation, causation and the question you're really asking

    Most business questions in a bank are causal, even when they're phrased as "what does the data say". Did the payment-plan script cause more cures? Would these customers have stayed if we hadn't sent the retention offer? Will raising limits make customers spend more, or default more?

    The cleanest way to state such a question is with potential outcomes. Each customer has two outcomes: Y(1), what happens if they get the new script, and Y(0), what happens if they don't. The effect for that customer is the difference. The problem is that you only ever see one of the two. Every causal method is a way of filling in the missing one.

    The naive approach fills it in with other customers: compare those who got the treatment with those who didn't. Here's what that comparison actually measures:

    E[Y∣T=1]−E[Y∣T=0]what the dashboard shows=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣T=1]the effect+E[Y(0)∣T=1]−E[Y(0)∣T=0]selection bias

    T = 1 means the customer got the treatment. The last term asks: how differently would the treated customers have done without the treatment, compared with the untreated ones? In the pilot, the new-script customers would have cured far less anyway, because most were high-risk. Under the old script they would have cured 42% of the time (0.3 × 70% + 0.7 × 30%), against 62% for the old script's own customers. That −20 point term buried a +5 point effect, and the dashboard showed 5 − 20 = −15.

    When two things move together in bank data, there are only five explanations, and you should run through all of them before believing the first:

    • X causes Y. The script changed behaviour.
    • Y causes X. Reverse causation. Customers who are about to close their accounts call the contact centre more, so "call volume predicts churn" doesn't mean calls cause churn.
    • Something causes both. Confounding. Income drives both credit limit and spending.
    • Selection. The rows in your table were chosen by a process tied to both. You only see loans that were approved, alerts that were reviewed, customers who answered the survey.
    • Chance. Especially after slicing the data 40 ways (F2.4).

    Randomisation makes the selection-bias term zero by construction: a coin flip can't know who was going to cure anyway. That's why a properly run experiment (F3.6) beats any amount of clever adjustment. Everything below is about what to do when you can't randomise, and how to tell when an analysis has quietly gone wrong.

    Causal diagrams: forks, chains and colliders

    A causal diagram, or DAG (directed acyclic graph), draws each variable as a node and each direct cause as an arrow. It's a written-down assumption about how the world works. It doesn't come from the data. That's the point: it makes your assumptions visible, so a colleague can argue with them.

    Every diagram, however large, is built from three junctions. Learn these and you can read any of them.

    JunctionBank exampleAre X and Y associated through it?What adjusting for the middle does
    Fork
    X ← Z → Y    		Income (Z) raises both credit limit (X) and monthly spend (Y).Yes, even if limit has no effect on spend at all.Blocks the spurious association. This is what "controlling for a confounder" means.
    Chain
    X → M → Y    		A budgeting nudge (X) increases use of the budgeting tool (M), which improves on-time payment (Y).Yes, and that association is part of the real effect.Blocks the effect flowing through M. You're left with only the direct part.

    X → C ← Y    		A fee increase (X) and low satisfaction (Y) both make customers call the contact centre (C).No. The path is closed on its own.Opens a false association. Among callers, fee and satisfaction become linked even with no effect at all.

    Forks and chains behave the same way: information flows through them, and conditioning on the middle stops it. Colliders behave in reverse: information is blocked, and conditioning on the middle lets it through. That reversal is behind most of the surprises in this lesson.

    Why does conditioning on a collider create a link? Take only the customers who called. If a caller wasn't hit by the fee, something else must have made them call, so they're more likely to be unhappy for another reason. Knowing one cause is absent makes the other more likely. That's "explaining away", and it manufactures a correlation out of nothing.

    The formal rule for reading paths through a whole diagram is called d-separation. You won't need it by name, but you'll need its conclusion, the back-door criterion: to estimate the effect of X on Y from observational data, adjust for a set of variables that

    1. blocks every path from X to Y that starts with an arrow into X (the back doors, which carry confounding), and
    2. contains nothing caused by X (no mediators, no colliders downstream, nothing measured after the treatment that the treatment could have changed).

    What to adjust for, and what never to

    The habit to break is "add every variable we have, to be safe". Adjustment is not a safety measure. Each variable you add either removes bias, adds it, or does nothing to bias and just changes the noise. Which one depends on where it sits in the diagram, and no amount of data or model fit tells you.

    • Good controls: common causes of treatment and outcome, measured before the treatment. Income, tenure, prior arrears, risk band at the time of allocation. A noisy proxy for a confounder helps partly but leaves some bias behind.
    • Harmless and often helpful: things that predict the outcome but don't affect the treatment, such as the customer's region when scripts were assigned at random. They don't fix bias, but they shrink standard errors.
    • Bad: mediators, if you want the total effect. Adjusting for "number of products held after the offer" when evaluating a retention offer removes the part of the offer's effect that works by selling more products.
    • Bad: colliders and their descendants. Including any filter that keeps only certain rows: "customers who responded", "cases that were reviewed", "loans that were approved".
    • Bad: anything measured after treatment that the treatment could change. Call duration, whether the customer engaged on the call, balance after the offer. These are usually mediators or colliders in disguise, and they're the most common mistake in practice.
    • Usually bad: pure causes of the treatment only. A variable that drives who gets treated but has no other path to the outcome, like which agent picked up the call. Adjusting for it adds noise and can amplify any bias that remains.

    A related trap is the "Table 2 fallacy": reading every coefficient in a multivariable regression as a causal effect. A model built to estimate the effect of X can correctly include confounder Z, but Z's own coefficient is then not Z's effect, because X sits on Z's causal path to Y and has been held fixed.

    The kitchen-sink result is worth remembering. The model with every variable has the best fit, the most "complete" feature set, and a coefficient that's wrong in sign. R² rewards predicting Y. It has no idea whether the coefficient on X means anything. For prediction, colliders and mediators are often excellent features. For causal questions they're poison. Same data, different question, different correct model.

    One more consequence. Two analysts with the same data and different diagrams will get different answers, and both will be "statistically significant". The argument has to be about the diagram, and that's an argument about the business process, which is something a bank's operations people often know better than its data scientists.

    Simpson's paradox, properly

    The paradox is just arithmetic. A pooled rate is a weighted average of band rates, and each script is averaged with its own weights:

    rB, pooled=(1−sB)·rB, low+sB·rB, high

    sB is the share of high-risk customers given script B. With the defaults: 0.3 × 75% + 0.7 × 35% = 47%. For script A: 0.8 × 70% + 0.2 × 30% = 62%. B wins in both bands but loses the average, because 70% of its weight sits on the band where everyone does badly.

    The fix is to average both scripts over the same mix of customers. That's the adjustment formula, also called standardisation:

    P(cure∣do(script))=∑bP(cure∣script,b)P(b)

    "do(script)" means giving the script to everyone, as opposed to looking at those who happened to get it. Take each band's cure rate under the script, and weight by how common the band is in the whole population, not in that script's caseload. This is exactly the back-door adjustment from the previous section, written for one confounder.

    Now the uncomfortable part: the numbers alone can't tell you whether to pool or to split. Suppose the "band" were instead "customer engaged on the call: yes or no", and the new script makes customers more likely to engage. Engagement is then a mediator, and splitting by it would hide part of the script's effect. The pooled number would be the right one. Exactly the same table, opposite conclusion. What decides it is whether the grouping variable was fixed before the treatment (a confounder: split) or affected by it (a mediator or collider: don't).

    The textbook case is UC Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions. Overall, men were admitted at a noticeably higher rate than women. Department by department, there was no such bias, and if anything a small one the other way. Women had applied mainly to departments that rejected most applicants of either sex. The bank version is easy to imagine: approval rates by nationality or gender that look unequal overall, and look different again once you split by product, because groups apply for different products with different approval rates. Which comparison answers the fairness question depends on whether product choice is itself shaped by something you'd consider unfair, such as how products are marketed. That is a judgement about the causal story, not something the data can settle.

    Watch outSplitting fixes confounding only if you split on the right thing, finely enough. A risk band of "high" might still contain a range of risk, with the new script's customers at the worse end of it. Residual confounding inside coarse bands is common, and it's one reason adjusted observational estimates are less trustworthy than they look.

    Selection bias and survivorship

    Confounding is about who got the treatment. Selection bias is about who made it into your table at all. If the process that chose the rows depends on the outcome, or on something that causes it, then every statistic on those rows is distorted, including ones that have nothing to do with treatments.

    • Survey response. Satisfaction surveys after a contact-centre call are answered most by the very pleased and the very angry. The average misses the indifferent middle.
    • Survivorship in tenure analysis. "Customers with more than five years' tenure have high balances" can simply mean low-balance customers left. Analysing only current customers, or only accounts still in the warehouse after old closed accounts were purged, builds this in.
    • Loans that reached the end. A study of "completed" murabaha or personal finance contracts can miss early defaults that were written off and archived. Profitability looks better than it was.
    • Investigated cases. Fraud and AML labels exist mostly where someone looked. The labelled data describes the old rules' blind spots by leaving them out.

    The classic illustration is Abraham Wald's analysis of bomber damage in the Second World War. Planes that came back had bullet holes clustered in the wings and fuselage. The instinct was to armour those areas. Wald's point was that the returning planes were the survivors. Hits to the engines didn't show up because the planes hit there didn't return. Armour the places with no holes.

    In diagram terms, selection is conditioning on a collider. Write "in the data" as a node S. If both X and Y (or causes of Y) affect S, then filtering to S = 1 opens the path X → S ← Y. That's why selection bias can create associations, hide them, or reverse them, and why it can't be fixed by adding more variables to the model. Heckman's 1979 selection model treats it as an omitted-variable problem and corrects it, but only if you can find a variable that affects selection and has no other route to the outcome, which is rarely credible in practice.

    Reject inference: the bank's own selection bias

    This is the selection problem every lender has, and it sits underneath every application scorecard. The bank approves applicants above a cut-off. It only observes repayment for the ones it approved. The next scorecard is trained on those approved loans, then applied to every applicant, including the kind it never saw repay. That's the reject inference problem.

    It's actually two problems, and they need different answers.

    • Extrapolation. Below the old cut-off there's little or no outcome data, so the model's shape there is a guess. This matters as soon as anyone asks "what if we lowered the cut-off?" or "can we lend to thin-file customers?"
    • Missing not at random. If the old approvals used information the new model doesn't have, then even at the same score, the approved applicants are systematically better than the rejected ones. Underwriter judgement, policy overrides, a salary certificate, an employer list, a detail in the Al Etihad Credit Bureau (AECB) or SIMAH report that isn't a model feature, a relationship manager's knowledge: all of these select on things the model can't see.
    P(default∣x,approved)=P(default∣x)?

    Is the default rate among approved applicants with a given profile the same as among all applicants with that profile? Only if approval depended on x alone. Here x is everything the new model uses. If the old decision used only variables in x (plus pure chance), the approved-only data is unbiased wherever it exists, and the remaining risk is extrapolation. If the old decision used anything outside x, the approved-only data is biased everywhere, and most of all near the cut-off. Hand and Henley made this point in 1993, and it still decides whether reject inference is worth doing.

    What the simulation shows is the mechanism behind decades of argument in credit scoring. Near the cut-off, the old underwriters approved only the low-score applicants who looked good on the hidden factor. Their outcomes are excellent, so the model concludes that low scores aren't very risky. The curve flattens. Reweighting by score doesn't help, because within each score it still only sees the cherry-picked approvals. Weighting can fix selection on things you can see, never on things you can't.

    ApproachWhat it doesIts limit

    A useful recent finding: correcting the evaluation can matter more than correcting the training. If your test set is also approved-only, you'll pick the wrong model and misjudge every cut-off decision. More in Part 4.

    Feedback loops: when your decisions create your labels

    Reject inference is one case of a general pattern. In a bank, the model's decisions decide which outcomes get observed, and sometimes change the outcomes themselves. Then the next model is trained on data the last model shaped.

    • Fraud. Confirmed fraud labels come from alerts that analysts reviewed, plus disputes customers raise. Fraud the rules never flagged and no customer noticed, such as some first-party and mule activity, never gets a label. Retrain on that, and the new model learns the old model's view of fraud. This is the selective labels problem. Over generations, the net can get narrower without anyone noticing, because the measured recall looks fine.
    • Collections. Suppose last year the team called high-risk customers intensively, and the calls worked. In the data, those customers now look like they pay better than they would have alone. A "probability to pay" model learns they're lower risk, so next year they get fewer calls, and they pay less. The treatment contaminated the label. The fix is to model the outcome under each action (uplift, A10.4), record which action was taken, and keep a small random holdout that gets the default treatment.
    • Credit limits. A limit increase changes utilisation, which feeds the behaviour score, which drives the next limit decision.
    • AML. Suspicious activity reports exist only for alerts that were investigated. Typologies learned from past cases describe what the old scenarios could see.

    Researchers call predictions that change their own targets performative. The practical lessons are simple. Log every score, decision and treatment at decision time. Keep a small amount of randomisation, such as reviewing a random sample of unflagged transactions, or giving a random 2–5% of accounts the standard strategy. Without randomness in past decisions, methods for evaluating a new policy from old logs (off-policy evaluation, R8.4) have nothing to work with.

    Berkson's paradox: colliders in the wild

    In 1946 Joseph Berkson pointed out that two diseases can look associated among hospital patients even when they're unrelated in the population, because either one can put you in hospital. Studying only hospital patients conditions on a collider.

    Banks are full of hospitals. Reviewed alerts, approved loans, customers who complained, accounts referred to collections: each is a group selected because something was high. Inside it, the causes of selection become negatively related. Among approved applicants, low income goes with an excellent credit history, because the ones with low income and a poor history were declined. Among reviewed fraud alerts, a high transaction velocity tends to come with a clean device, because cases with neither never reached the queue.

    The practical damage comes when someone analyses the queue and draws conclusions about the signals. "Velocity and device risk are negatively correlated in our cases, so they're substitutes, and we can drop one." Or a model trained only on reviewed cases learns that high velocity predicts a clean device. Both are artefacts of the rule that built the queue. The same goes for any analysis restricted to approved loans: relationships between application variables inside the approved book are not the relationships in the applicant population.

    Fairness: proxies, labels and the causal view

    Fairness in credit is a causal question in disguise, and the ideas above are the tools for it.

    Dropping the column doesn't drop the information. Removing nationality or gender from a model's inputs, "fairness through unawareness", rarely works, because other variables carry the same signal. In Gulf data the proxies for nationality are strong and numerous: name, preferred language, employer and employer category, visa or residency type, residential area, and above all remittance corridors, since the countries a customer sends money to reveal a great deal. For gender, title fields, product holdings and spending categories can act as proxies. A model can reconstruct a removed attribute from these with high accuracy, and will, if it's predictive.

    Labels carry history. Outcomes only exist for applicants who were approved, so if a group was approved less often in the past, the model has seen less of it, and reject inference can amplify that (a 2025 University of Edinburgh study is looking at exactly this). And a label can be a proxy for the thing you care about. A widely used US healthcare algorithm predicted health costs as a stand-in for health need; because less was historically spent on Black patients with the same need, the algorithm under-referred them. The bank analogue is training on "was referred to collections" or "had a SAR filed" as if it were "was a credit loss" or "was laundering money".

    The causal view. Draw the paths from a protected attribute to the decision, and decide which are acceptable. A path through verified income and repayment history is generally accepted. A path through a name, or through a branch's past habit of declining certain applicants, isn't. Counterfactual fairness formalises this: would the decision change if the customer's protected attribute were different, with everything it legitimately causes adjusted accordingly? You won't usually compute that exactly, but it's the right question to ask of a feature list.

    The regulatory context. In February 2026 the Central Bank of the UAE issued a guidance note on consumer protection and the responsible use of AI and machine learning. Legal summaries describe it as formally non-binding but clear in its expectations: AI systems shouldn't produce discriminatory or unfair outcomes, directly or indirectly; banks should test periodically for bias; and consumers can ask for human review of AI-driven decisions and challenge them. In Saudi Arabia, SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles include fairness as a core principle, though they're voluntary. Whether a variable such as nationality may be used in a given credit decision is a question for your bank's compliance and policy teams. Your job is to show precisely what the model does with the variable and with its proxies, so that the decision is an informed one. The full treatment is in G2.1, G2.2 and A10.2.

    How it connects

    This lesson is the bridge between statistics that describe and decisions that change things. These are the places where it comes back.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rule and base ratesConditioning is the whole game

    Simpson's paradox is conditional probability with uneven weights. Collider bias is what Bayes' rule does when you condition on a common effect.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodThe fit is only as good as the sample

    Logistic regression on approved loans maximises the likelihood of the wrong population. MLE is consistent for whatever process generated your rows.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, calibrationMetrics measured on a selected sample

    Fraud precision and recall are computed on labels that exist only where someone looked. Calibration of a scorecard can't be checked below the cut-off.

    F2.4 · Forking pathsThe fifth explanation

    Before anything causal, rule out chance. Splitting by many bands until one shows a reversal is its own error.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsCoefficients are not effects by default

    A coefficient is an effect only if the model's adjustment set is right for that variable. Different variables need different models.

    F3.1 · LeakagePost-treatment variables leak

    A feature measured after the decision is a leakage problem in prediction and a mediator or collider problem in causal analysis. Same root cause.

    F3.6 · A/B testingThe way out

    Randomisation removes confounding and selection by design. When you can randomise, do; this lesson is for when you can't, and for checking that you really did.

    F8.5 · Limits of post-hoc explanationSHAP explains the model, not the world

    A large SHAP value for "number of products" says the model leans on it, not that selling products reduces default.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceThe full toolkit

    Propensity scores, matching, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity, which is natural for scorecard cut-offs.

    B4.2, B4.5 · Scorecards and reject inferenceThe applied version

    How reject inference is done in practice, how validators challenge it, and swap-set analysis for new cut-offs.

    A10.4, B6.2 · Uplift for collections and retentionPredict the effect, not the outcome

    Target the customers whose behaviour the action changes, not those most likely to pay or stay anyway.

    R8.2, R8.4 · Double ML, off-policy evaluationCausal ML at the frontier

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core ideas are old and settled: Simpson in 1951, Berkson in 1946, Heckman in 1979, Pearl's diagrams in the 1990s. The live work is in three places: machine learning methods for causal effects, new attempts at the reject inference problem, and whether large language models can reason causally.

    1. 1975
      The Berkeley admissions case

      Aggregate admission rates favoured men; department by department they didn't. The standard real-world Simpson's paradox, and an early lesson that a fairness question depends on the comparison chosen.

      Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell, Science, 1975
    2. 1993
      "Can reject inference ever work?"

      Argued that common industry methods rest on untestable assumptions, and that reliable reject inference needs outcome information on rejects, for example by approving some of them.

      Hand & Henley, IMA Journal of Mathematics Applied in Business & Industry, 1993

      Causal diagrams for empirical research

      Pearl's back-door criterion turned "what should I control for?" into a question you can answer by reading a graph.

      Pearl, Biometrika, 1995

      Augmentation barely changes ranking

      On a dataset where rejected applicants' outcomes were known, the common reject inference method did little for scorecard ranking, though it helped in setting the cut-off.

      Crook & Banasik, Journal of Banking & Finance, 2004
    5. 2017
      The selective labels problem

      When past decisions determine which outcomes are observed, evaluating a new model needs care. The paper used differences between individual decision-makers to evaluate without counterfactual guesses.

      Lakkaraju et al., KDD 2017

      Double machine learning and causal forests

      Use flexible ML to model confounding, then estimate the effect with valid confidence intervals (double/debiased ML), or estimate how the effect varies across customers (causal forests). These are now the default tools for observational effects with many controls.

      Chernozhukov et al., Econometrics Journal, 2018; Wager & Athey, JASA, 2018

      A proxy label caused large-scale bias

      A healthcare algorithm used cost as a proxy for need and under-referred Black patients. The model was accurate for the label it was given. The label was the problem.

      Obermeyer et al., Science, 2019

      Performative prediction

      A formal framework for predictions that change the outcomes they predict, and conditions under which repeated retraining settles down.

      Perdomo et al., ICML 2020
    9. 2021
      Uplift for collections calls, in production

      A digital lender ran a randomised test of calling late borrowers, then built an uplift model. Calling the top 10% by predicted revenue uplift captured over half the incremental revenue of calling everyone, and only the top half was profitable to call at all.

      Tala engineering blog, January 2021

      LLMs and causality: impressive and brittle

      GPT-4-based methods scored 97% on a pairwise causal-direction benchmark and 92% on counterfactual questions, mostly by drawing on knowledge in text.       Kıcıman et al., TMLR; Jin et al., ICLR 2024; Jin et al. (CLadder), NeurIPS 2023

      Good and bad controls, catalogued

      A widely used guide that sorts candidate control variables by their position in the diagram, including cases where adding a pre-treatment variable makes things worse.

      Cinelli, Forney & Pearl, Sociological Methods & Research, 2024
    12. 2025
      Correct the evaluation, not just the training

      Proposed a bias-aware self-labelling method for training and a Bayesian evaluation that includes rejects. Using data containing an unbiased sample of applicants accepted without scoring, correcting evaluation improved profitability far more than correcting training.

      Kozodoi et al., European Journal of Operational Research, 2025
    13. 2025
      Double ML for loan limits and pricing

      Preprints from lending platforms apply double ML to credit limits, interest rates and terms as continuous treatments, with monotonicity constraints so that a higher limit never lowers predicted risk; one reports an online A/B test. Another finds that limit increases raise spending mainly at the top of the distribution.

      Zhao et al., arXiv 2508.02183; Leung, Li & Wu, arXiv 2509.03063
    14. 2025
      Reject inference keeps evolving

      New semi-supervised methods label only the rejects that look most like approved applicants, and report a trade-off between overall AUC and performance on rejects. A separate study asks whether reject inference amplifies bias against groups that were rejected more often.

      Ribeiro & Raimundo, arXiv 2510.12967; University of Edinburgh Credit Research Centre, 2025
    15. 2026
      LLMs collapse down the causal ladder, and bend under pressure

      A 5,000-case benchmark found models answering interventional questions with associational evidence ("rung collapse"), and abandoning correct causal judgements when the user pushed back.

      Geng et al., CausalT5k, arXiv 2602.08939
    16. 2026
      UAE central bank guidance on AI

      The CBUAE guidance note on consumer protection and responsible AI sets expectations against discriminatory or unfair outcomes, for periodic bias testing, and for human review of AI-driven decisions.

      CBUAE, February 2026, as summarised by Pinsent Masons and Hadef & Partners

    LLMs proposing causal diagramsDraft a DAG from variable descriptions for experts to correct.Proposed
    The current consensus, in one lineDesign beats adjustment. Randomise where you can, log decisions so you can learn from them later, and when you must adjust, write down the causal diagram first. Use LLMs to draft diagrams and list possible confounders, never to certify a causal claim.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes you'll meet in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest way to catch it.

    • Comparing treated and untreated customers who were chosen differently

      "Customers who received the retention offer churned 40% less." They were picked because they were engaged.

      Spot itAsk how customers were chosen for the treatment. If a person or a model chose, ask for the comparison within the selection criteria, or for a holdout.
    • Adjusting for a post-treatment variable

      Evaluating a collections script "controlling for call duration" or "for whether the customer engaged". The script changes both.

      Spot itFor every control, ask: was this measured before the treatment, and could the treatment have changed it? Anything after is suspect.
    • Kitchen-sink regression read as causal

      Forty features, one coefficient quoted as "the effect of the limit increase", chosen because the model had the best fit.

      Spot itAsk for the causal diagram, or at least the reason each control is there. Fit statistics are not an answer.
    • Reading every coefficient as an effect

      The Table 2 fallacy. "Tenure has a negative coefficient, so long tenure reduces default" from a model built for something else.

      Spot itAsk which single effect the model was designed to estimate. The others are not interpretable as effects without their own adjustment sets.
    • Splitting or pooling by habit

      Reporting the pooled number because it's simpler, or splitting by a variable affected by the treatment because "more detail is better".

      Spot itAsk whether the grouping was fixed before the treatment. If yes, split and standardise; if the treatment can change it, don't split on it.
    • A scorecard validated only on approved loans

      A new model "beats the old one by 4 Gini points" on a test set of approved applications, then is used to lower the cut-off.

      Spot itAsk what the old approval process used beyond the scorecard: overrides, policy rules, judgement. Ask what evidence exists about performance below the cut-off.
    • Reject inference that adds no information

      Rejects labelled by the approved-only model, then everything retrained. The documentation says "reject inference applied".

      Spot itAsk where information about the rejects came from: bureau data, random approvals, or only the model itself. If only the model, nothing was corrected.
    • Retraining on outcomes your actions caused

      A collections or fraud model retrained on last year's data, where last year's model chose who got called or reviewed.

      Spot itAsk whether the treatment each case received is in the data, and whether any cases got a random or default action.
    • Analysing a queue as if it were the population

      "Among our AML alerts, cash intensity and cross-border activity are negatively correlated, so one rule is redundant."

      Spot itAsk how cases got into the dataset. If a rule or score selected them, relationships among selection variables are distorted.
    • "We removed nationality, so the model is fair"

      Remittance corridors, employer, name and language preference remain.

      Spot itAsk whether anyone tried to predict the removed attribute from the remaining features. If it's predictable, the model can use it.
    • AI-generated causal analysis

      Typical signs: "after controlling for all available variables", a causal verb ("drives", "leads to") attached to an observational correlation, or a confident reversal of its own conclusion when you push back. LLMs are good at listing plausible confounders and poor at deciding, from data alone, which way causation runs.

      Spot itAsk it to draw the diagram and name each control's role. Then ask "what if the grouping was affected by the treatment?" If the answer flips without new evidence, don't trust either version.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The new script looks worse only because we gave it the hardest cases. Compare like with like, customers in the same risk band, and it's about five points better in every band. Before we decide, I'd run it properly for six weeks: randomly assign the scripts within each band. Then the comparison is clean, and we'll know the real gain in cures and in dirhams recovered.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start with a causal diagram of how treatment was assigned, then pick the adjustment set by the back-door criterion: pre-treatment confounders, no mediators, no colliders or selection filters. I'd standardise, or use double ML if there are many controls, and check overlap. For the scorecard, the key question is whether old approvals used information outside the new model's features. If they did, the approved-only data is missing not at random, so I'd want bureau outcomes on rejects or a small random-approval sample, and I'd correct the evaluation as well as the training.”
    “We controlled for everything we have. Isn't that enough?”

    No. Controlling for more isn't always safer. Controlling for a confounder removes bias, but controlling for something the treatment affects, or for a selection filter, adds bias. And no set of controls fixes a confounder we didn't measure. I'd want to see why each control is there, and a sensitivity analysis showing how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to change the conclusion.

    “Our model was trained on two million loans. Surely selection bias averages out?”

    It doesn't. Sample size shrinks noise, not bias. Two million approved loans tell us very precisely how approved applicants behave. They tell us nothing directly about applicants the old process declined, and if that process used information the model doesn't have, they mislead us about applicants at the same score too.

    “Why not just use reject inference? Every bank does.”

    We should document it, but most standard methods label rejects using the approved-only model, so they don't add information. They're useful for making the cut-off analysis consistent, much less for fixing the bias. Real information comes from bureau performance of rejected applicants elsewhere or from a small, governed sample of random approvals. Even a modest programme, run steadily, makes every future scorecard more trustworthy.

    “The collections model says these customers will pay anyway. Should we stop calling them?”

    Not on that evidence. If they were called heavily last year, the model may be learning the effect of the calls. What we need is the difference between calling and not calling for each customer: an uplift estimate, ideally from a randomised holdout. Some customers pay anyway; others pay because we call. Only uplift tells them apart.

    “Can't we ask an LLM to tell us what causes churn?”

    It's useful for brainstorming: drafting a causal diagram, listing confounders we might have missed, spotting post-treatment variables. Recent benchmarks show LLMs confusing correlation with intervention and changing their answers under pushback. So they help with the thinking, but the answer has to come from an experiment or a defensible design, with the assumptions written down.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the weighted-average formula from Part 2.

    1. Old script: 90% of its customers are low-risk (cure rate 70%) and 10% high-risk (30%). New script: 50/50, with cure rates of 72% and 34%. What are the pooled cure rates, and which script is better?

    Old: 0.9 × 70% + 0.1 × 30% = 66%. New: 0.5 × 72% + 0.5 × 34% = 53%. Pooled, the new script looks 13 points worse. Within bands it's 2 points better (low) and 4 points better (high). Standardised to a common 70/30 mix: old 0.7 × 70 + 0.3 × 30 = 58%; new 0.7 × 72 + 0.3 × 34 = 60.6%. The new script is better, provided risk band was fixed before the script was assigned.

    2. You want the total effect of a salary-transfer cashback offer on 12-month balances. Candidate controls: income at application, number of products held three months after the offer, and "responded to the follow-up survey". Which do you include?

    Income at application only. It's a pre-treatment common cause. Products held after the offer is a mediator: the offer may work partly by cross-selling, so controlling for it removes part of the effect. Survey response is likely a collider, influenced by both the offer and the customer's experience, so conditioning on it can open a false path.

    3. Among approved personal-finance customers, applicants with lower declared salaries have markedly better credit-bureau histories. A colleague concludes that low earners manage credit better. What's going on?

    Berkson's paradox. Approval required enough salary or a strong bureau history. Low earners with weak histories were declined, so among those approved, low salary goes with strong history. The approved book is a collider-filtered sample. Check the relationship in the full applicant population, where it's probably weak or positive.

    4. Old approvals used only the old scorecard, whose inputs are all in the new model. Is reject inference needed?

    The approved data is then missing at random given the model's inputs, so within the approved region it's unbiased. The remaining risk is extrapolation: the model's shape below the old cut-off is unverified. If you'll keep a similar cut-off, the bias is small. If you plan to lower it, you still need outcome information there. And check the assumption: were there really no overrides, policy declines or manual referrals? There usually are.

    5. A fraud model is retrained every quarter on confirmed fraud, which comes from analyst-reviewed alerts and customer disputes. What bias builds up, and what's the cheapest fix?

    Selective labels. Fraud that's never flagged and never disputed is never labelled, so each model learns the previous model's view of fraud, and measured recall overstates true recall. The cheapest fix is to review a small random sample of unflagged transactions every period, record it as random, and use it both as extra training data and to estimate true recall.

    6. A data scientist removes nationality from a credit model. A logistic regression on the remaining features predicts nationality group with an AUC of 0.93. What does this tell you?

    The model still has access to nationality through proxies, likely remittance corridors, employer, name or language. Removing the column hasn't removed the information. You need to look at outcomes by group, find which features carry the signal, and decide with compliance which paths are acceptable, rather than assuming the model is neutral.

    7. True or false: if a regression with an extra control has a higher R² and a smaller standard error on the treatment coefficient, the extra control should stay in.

    False. Fit and precision say nothing about bias. A mediator or collider can raise R² and tighten the interval around a wrong number, as the kitchen-sink diagram in Simulation 2 shows. Whether a control belongs depends on its causal position, not on fit.
