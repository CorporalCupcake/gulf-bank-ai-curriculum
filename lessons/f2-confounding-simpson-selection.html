    Confounding, Simpson's Paradox and Selection Bias

    Part 1

    What confounding is

    In one sentenceA confounder is a third thing that pushes both the "cause" and the outcome, so a comparison looks like an effect when part of it is not.

    Say a hospital finds that patients on the new treatment recover less often than patients on the old one. Before blaming the treatment, ask who got it. Doctors gave the new treatment mostly to severe cases, and severe cases recover less often whatever you give them.

    Severity is the confounder. It sits behind both the treatment choice and the recovery.

    How it differs from similar ideas

    • Confounding means a hidden third factor muddles the comparison.
    • Simpson's paradox is what confounding can look like in numbers: the trend flips when you split the data into groups.
    • Selection bias is about who is missing from the table, not a hidden factor behind the groups.
    • Random noise is just luck. More data shrinks it, but not the three above.
    Part 2

    Simpson's paradox in numbers

    In one sentenceSimpson's paradox is when a pattern holds in every group but reverses when you pool the groups.

    Take 1,000 patients on each treatment. The old treatment mostly went to mild cases, the new one mostly to severe cases.

    Recovery rateOld treatmentNew treatment
    Mild cases70% (800 patients)80% (200 patients)
    Severe cases40% (200 patients)50% (800 patients)
    Everyone pooled64%56%

    The pooled rate is an average that mixes two things: how well each treatment works, and what kind of patient it received.

    The fair comparison gives both treatments the same mix of patients. Doing that is called adjusting (or standardising) for severity.

    Part 3

    Selection bias

    In one sentenceSelection bias is when the rows in your table were picked by something linked to the outcome, so they are not a fair picture of everyone.
    • Survey responders: unhappy customers may answer more often than happy ones.
    • Survivorship: in the Second World War, analysts looked at bullet holes on returning bombers. Wald's insight was to armour the places with no holes, because planes hit there never came back.
    • Turned-away cases: a model trained only on people who were accepted has never seen the rejected ones, yet is asked to score them.

    Adding more variables does not fix this. Only information about the missing rows does, such as a small random test that lets a few rejected cases through.

    Part 4

    What to do about it

    1. Ask why. For each person, why did they land in this group, and who never made it into the data?
    2. List the common causes. Things that came before the treatment and push both treatment and outcome, like severity or past grades.
    3. Compare like with like. Split by those factors, or put them in a regression, and read the effect within groups.
    4. Leave out the wrong variables. Do not adjust for things the treatment itself changes (a result of the treatment) or for things caused by both treatment and outcome.
    5. Randomise when you can. A coin flip makes the groups alike on everything, including what you never measured (see the A/B tests lesson).

    What to adjust for

    • A common cause (severity drives treatment and recovery): adjust. It blocks a false link.
    • A middle step (study app, then hours studied, then score): do not adjust. It hides part of the real effect.
    • A shared result (coffee and short sleep both send people to a sleep clinic): do not adjust. Looking only at clinic patients creates a false link between coffee and sleep.

    The model's fit cannot tell you which is which. That takes knowledge of how the world works, and drawing the arrows helps you argue about it.

    Part 5

    Which approach to use

    ApproachFixesCatch
    Randomised testConfounding, seen or unseenNot always possible or ethical
    Split by groupSimpson's paradox from a known factorOnly if the grouping came before the treatment
    Regression adjustmentMeasured confoundersUnmeasured ones remain
    Extra data on missing rowsSelection biasCosts something to collect
    • Strong at: adjusting and splitting are cheap and use data you already have.
    • Weak at: they only handle what you measured.

    In banking, a retention offer sent to customers who looked likely to leave, or a credit model that sees repayment only for approved applicants, both carry these biases.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting the pooled number

      A pooled average mixes the effect with who received it. Always look at the groups too.

    • Adjusting for everything

      Adding every variable can create a false link or hide a real effect. Even the best-fitting model can have the wrong sign.

    • Splitting by something the treatment changed

      If the treatment moved people between groups, splitting hides part of the real effect.

    • Thinking more data helps

      A million biased rows are still biased. Size only shrinks noise.

    1. What is a confounder?

    A third factor that influences both the treatment and the outcome, so the comparison mixes its effect with the treatment's.

    2. In the table, why does the old treatment win when pooled?

    It received mostly mild cases, who recover more often. The new treatment is better within both groups.

    3. What is the fair way to compare?

    Give both treatments the same patient mix, by adjusting for severity or randomising.

    4. Bombers return with holes in the wings. Where do you add armour?

    Where there are no holes, such as the engines, because planes hit there did not return.

    5. Why can't a model trained on approved loans safely score rejected applicants?

    It has never seen how they would have repaid, so its predictions for them are guesses.
