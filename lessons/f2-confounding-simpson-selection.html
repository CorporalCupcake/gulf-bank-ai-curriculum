    F2.6 Day one ★ Priority About 50 minutes

    Confounding, Simpson's Paradox and Selection Bias

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A new call script looks worse than the old one, yet it is better. In a collections pilot, the old reminder gets 62% of missed-payment customers back up to date within 30 days and the new, gentler script only 47%. But team leads gave the new script mostly to high-risk customers, who cure less whatever you say.

    This is Simpson's paradox: a pattern that holds in every subgroup reverses when you pool them, because an average mixes what the treatment did with who received it.

    Hold onto thisBefore believing any comparison, ask why each customer landed in each group and who never made it into the data.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Correlation versus causation

    Most bank questions are causal, but you never see the same customer with and without treatment. A naive comparison adds a selection term (how differently the treated would have done anyway) to the real effect. Randomisation removes it (F3.6); the rest of this lesson is for when you cannot randomise.

    Causal diagrams: forks, chains and colliders

    A causal diagram draws variables as dots and causes as arrows, so your assumptions can be argued with. In a fork (income drives both credit limit and spend), adjusting blocks a spurious link. In a chain (nudge, then tool use, then payment), adjusting blocks part of the real effect. In a collider (fee rise and unhappiness both make customers call), adjusting creates a false link.

    What to adjust for, and what never to

    Adjust for common causes measured before treatment, such as income or prior arrears. Never adjust for mediators, colliders or anything the treatment changes, such as call duration or "approved". Model fit cannot tell you which is which, and reading every coefficient as causal is the "Table 2 fallacy".

    The best-fitting model can have the wrong sign. R² measures prediction, not whether a coefficient means anything.

    Simpson's paradox, properly

    A pooled rate is a weighted average of the band rates, and each script has its own weights. The fix is standardisation: compare both over the same mix. Split only if the grouping was fixed before treatment. If treatment changed it, splitting hides part of the real effect.

    Selection bias and survivorship

    Selection bias is about who made it into your table: survey responders, long-tenure survivors and investigated fraud cases are all chosen by something tied to the outcome. Wald's bombers show the logic: armour where there are no holes, because planes hit there never returned. Adding variables cannot fix this, only information about the missing rows can.

    Reject inference: the bank's own selection bias

    The bank sees repayment only for approved applicants, then applies the scorecard to everyone. Fixing this is called reject inference. Below the old cut-off the model is guessing, and if underwriters used information it lacks, the approved look better than the rejected at the same score.

    Reweighting cannot fix what you cannot see. Bureau outcomes on rejects help partly, and randomised approvals below the cut-off are the only clean fix, at a real cost.

    Feedback loops: when your decisions create your labels

    Your model's decisions decide which outcomes get observed, so the next model trains on data the last one shaped, as with fraud nobody flagged. Log every decision and keep some randomness, such as reviewing a random sample of unflagged transactions.

    Berkson's paradox: colliders in the wild

    Inside any selected group, such as reviewed alerts or approved loans, the causes of selection turn negatively related. Among approved applicants, low income can go with an excellent credit history because the others were declined.

    Concluding from the queue that two signals are redundant is an artefact of the rule that built it.

    Fairness: proxies, labels and the causal view

    Dropping a column does not drop the information: remittance corridors, employer, name and language carry nationality, and labels carry history. Draw the paths from the protected attribute to the decision and decide which are acceptable, with compliance. See G2.1, G2.2 and A10.2.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Comparing groups that were chosen differently

      "The retention offer cut churn 40%", when the engaged were picked.

      Spot itAsk how customers were chosen, and for a holdout.
    • Kitchen-sink regression read as causal

      One coefficient from forty features is quoted as "the effect", often by AI tools.

      Spot itAsk why each control is there.
    • A scorecard validated only on approved loans

      A model "beats the old" on approved applications, then is used to lower the cut-off.

      Spot itAsk where information on rejects came from.
    • Treating decision-shaped data as the population

      A fraud model is retrained on outcomes last year's model chose.

      Spot itAsk whether any cases got a random action.
    • "We removed nationality, so the model is fair"

      Remittance corridors, employer and name remain.

      Spot itTry to predict the removed attribute from the rest.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. The new script has a lower pooled cure rate but a higher rate in both risk bands, and got mostly high-risk customers. Which would you back?

    The new one, provided risk band was fixed before assignment.

    2. For a cashback offer's effect on balances, do you control for income at application, products held after the offer, or survey response?

    Only income at application, since the other two are a mediator and a likely collider.

    3. Approved customers with lower salaries have better bureau histories. Why?

    Berkson's paradox: low earners with weak histories were declined, so check the full applicant population.

    4. A fraud model is retrained on confirmed fraud from reviewed alerts. What bias builds up?

    Selective labels, fixed cheaply by reviewing a random sample of unflagged transactions.

    5. Does an extra control that raises R² belong in a causal model?

    No, because fit says nothing about bias and a collider can tighten the interval around a wrong number.
