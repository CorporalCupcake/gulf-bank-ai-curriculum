    Causal Effects Without a Randomised Test: Synthetic Control, Modern Difference-in-Differences and Causal Discovery

    What to do when the bank rolled something out and nobody randomised it: build the comparison group the data never gave you, avoid the quiet bias in staggered rollouts, and see what an algorithm (or an LLM) can and cannot say about the arrows between variables.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceWithout randomisation every causal estimate is only as good as the comparison group you can defend: difference-in-differences borrows the trend of untouched units, modern versions stop the staggered rollout from contaminating that borrowing, synthetic control builds the comparison from a weighted blend of other units, and causal discovery can narrow the possible cause-and-effect structures but never settle them without expert knowledge.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank launches a savings feature in its Dubai branches only. By June, new accounts per 1,000 customers are up 14%. Did the feature cause it? You can't see what Dubai would have done without it. That unobserved world is the counterfactual, and every causal method is a way of guessing it.

    Two cheap guesses fail. "Before versus after" blames the feature for everything else that changed (salaries, Ramadan, a market rally). "Dubai versus Abu Dhabi" blames it for every permanent difference between the cities.

    Better: see how Abu Dhabi changed over the same months. It is like judging a diet by comparing your weight change with a friend's over the same holiday season: the season hits you both, so only the extra change is yours. That is difference-in-differences (DiD): the treated group's change minus the comparison group's change. It removes anything constant per group and anything hitting both equally. The price is one assumption: absent the feature, Dubai would have moved in parallel with Abu Dhabi. Three extensions:

    • Staggered rollouts. Programmes reach branches at different dates. The textbook regression (two-way fixed effects) can then be wrong, even in sign, when effects grow over time. Newer estimators never use already-treated units as comparisons.
    • Synthetic control. With one treated unit and no single good comparison, build a weighted blend that tracked it before launch. The post-launch gap is your estimate.
    • Causal discovery. When you don't know which variables cause which, algorithms can rule structures out, rarely in a single answer. An LLM can propose a graph that looks sensible and is wrong.
    Hold onto thisWithout randomisation, each method trades it for assumptions. The skill is not running the estimator (one line of library code) but stating the assumption plainly, showing evidence for it, and reporting how wrong the answer would be if it fails.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Difference-in-differences and the parallel-trends assumption

    Take two groups and two periods. Let Ȳ be an average outcome.

    DiD=(Y¯T,post−Y¯T,pre)−(Y¯C,post−Y¯C,pre)

    T is treated, C comparison. The first bracket is the treated group's change; the second is what everything else would produce. Under parallel trends (absent treatment, T would have changed as much as C), the difference is the average effect on the treated. The regression y ~ treated + post + treated:post gives the same number as the interaction coefficient. Read it as: "8" means 8 more accounts per 1,000 than the comparison trend predicts.

    Parallel trends does not say levels match (Dubai starts higher) or that nothing trends. Only the gap must have been stable absent treatment. You can't prove it about a world you can't see; you can only look for evidence against it in the pre-launch periods.

    The standard check is an event study: plot the treated-minus-comparison gap per period relative to just before launch. Flat before and moving after is reassuring. Already drifting means DiD credits the drift to the treatment. Simulation 1 shows how much.

    Three practical points.

    • Parallel trends depends on the scale. Parallel in AED may be non-parallel in percentage growth; high- and low-balance segments rarely move together in levels. Pick levels or logs according to which "same shock, same proportional response" story is believable, and say so.
    • A flat pre-trend is necessary, not sufficient. It can't catch something starting at launch, like a competitor's Dubai promotion. Rambachan and Roth (2023) "honest DiD" asks how big a post-launch violation, relative to the pre-launch wobble, would erase your conclusion, and reports intervals valid under that much violation.
    • Pre-trend tests are weak and can distort. Passing one with six noisy months proves nothing, and screening on a pass biases what survives (Roth, 2022). Plot the event study and reason about size.

    Cluster standard errors at the level treatment was assigned (branch, segment), since outcomes within a branch correlate over time. Be sceptical with few clusters: two cities means two clusters, and no formula rescues that.

    # 2x2 DiD as a regression, clustered by branch
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
m = smf.ols("accounts ~ treated + post + treated:post", data=df).fit(
        cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch_id"]})
print(m.params["treated:post"], m.conf_int().loc["treated:post"].values)

    Staggered rollouts and why the old regression breaks

    Banks rarely switch things on everywhere at once: an onboarding flow reaches branch clusters in waves, a pricing change hits one segment per quarter. The habit is a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression: outcome on a unit effect, a time effect and a 0/1 "treated yet?" dummy. With one launch date it is just DiD. With several, it hides a problem.

    Picture grading a late starter against an early starter who is still improving: their progress is subtracted from his as if it were background. TWFE is a weighted average of many two-group comparisons (Goodman-Bacon, 2021). Some are clean (late-treated vs never-treated). Some use already-treated units as the comparison. If the early cohort's effect is still growing, that growth is subtracted from the late cohort's estimate as if it were a trend. The result is biased down, and some comparisons get negative weights, so the estimate can be negative when every unit's true effect is positive (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille, 2020; Sun and Abraham, 2021).

    If the effect is identical for every unit and date, none of this bites. That is rare: a feature builds up as customers learn it.

    Modern estimators all avoid forbidden comparisons:

    • Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021). Estimate an effect ATT(g,t) for each cohort g (first treated at date g) and date t, using never-treated or not-yet-treated controls, then aggregate by time since treatment (event study), cohort, or overall. This is Simulation 2's "cohort vs never-treated".
    • Sun and Abraham (2021). A regression with relative-time dummies interacted with cohort, averaged with cohort-share weights.
    • Imputation estimators (Borusyak, Jaravel and Spiess, 2024; Gardner). Fit unit and time effects only on untreated observations, predict each treated observation's untreated outcome, average the differences. Efficient and easy to explain.
    • de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille. Built from units that switch status, and handle treatment turning on and off (a limit raised then cut).

    You don't need a winner. Know the trigger (staggered timing plus effects that change with exposure time), the symptom (TWFE and the event study disagree) and the remedy (compare only with untreated units; make the event-study plot the main exhibit). Roth, Sant'Anna, Bilinski and Poe (2023) review the field; Baker et al. give a practical guide.

    These fix the estimator, not the assumption. You still need:

    • parallel trends (conditional on what you adjust for);
    • no anticipation (behaviour didn't change before launch, common when a feature is announced early);
    • no spill-over (comparison branches unaffected, which fails if customers move between branches or one call centre serves both).

    Synthetic control and synthetic difference-in-differences

    With one treated unit (an emirate, a product line, a segment), picking one comparison feels arbitrary and averaging all others never resembled it. Like building a "fantasy team" that mimics the star player's past form, synthetic control (Abadie and Gardeazabal, 2003; Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller, 2010) builds the comparison as a weighted average of untreated "donor" units, with weights chosen so the blend tracked the treated unit's pre-launch path as closely as possible.

    minw∑t≤T0(Y1t−∑j=2J+1wjYjt)2subject towj≥0,∑jwj=1

    Unit 1 is treated, units 2 to J+1 are donors, T0 is the last pre-launch period. Weights are non-negative and sum to one, so the synthetic unit is a blend, never an extrapolation. After launch, the effect at t is the treated outcome minus the synthetic one. Weights are usually sparse, so you can say "our synthetic Sharjah is 45% Ajman-like, 30% ...".

    Two ideas make it credible.

    • Fit before launch is the diagnostic. If the blend can't reproduce the pre-launch path, the post-launch gap means nothing. This is a far tougher check than "the pre-lines looked similar".
    • Inference by placebo. One treated unit gives no sampling-based standard errors, so pretend each donor was treated, run the same procedure, and see how big a gap arises by chance. If the real gap (scaled by its pre-fit error, since a poor fit makes big gaps easily) beats nearly all placebos, chance is unlikely. With 10 donors the best possible p-value is 1 in 11, about 0.09. That is honest, not weak.

    Extensions:

    • Augmented synthetic control (Ben-Michael, Feller and Rothstein, 2021) adds a regression correction when the pre-fit is imperfect.
    • Synthetic difference-in-differences (Arkhangelsky et al., 2021) weights donor units and pre-launch periods, then runs a DiD. It allows a constant level gap between treated and synthetic, which plain synthetic control does not, and works whether treated units are many (DiD's home) or few (synthetic control's).
    • Staggered and multi-unit versions exist. Treat them as active research.

    Use synthetic control for few treated units, a long pre-period and many donors: a regional pricing change, one product's repricing, a rule applied to one business line. For many treated units, DiD with a modern estimator is better. Always report the weights, pre-fit error, placebo distribution, and what happens if you drop the top donor.

    Causal discovery: learning structure from data

    Everything above assumed you knew what causes what. Causal discovery asks which arrows are consistent with the data at all. Three families:

    • Constraint-based (PC, FCI). Test conditional independences, drop edges between variables that are independent given some set, then orient what the pattern allows. The signature is the collider A → B ← C: A and C are independent, but become dependent once you condition on B.
    • Score-based (GES). Search over graphs and score fit with a complexity penalty.
    • Functional-form-based. Assume more about the mechanism (for example linear with non-Gaussian noise, LiNGAM) and arrow direction becomes identifiable. NOTEARS recasts the search as smooth optimisation under an acyclicity constraint.

    The limit is fundamental. A chain A → B → C, its reverse and the fork A ← B → C all say only "A and C are independent given B". They form a Markov equivalence class, and no observational data separates them. Only a collider can be oriented. Even with the usual assumptions (Markov and faithfulness: independences come from the graph, not from cancelling paths; no hidden common causes unless you use FCI; enough data) you get a class of graphs, not one graph.

    Real data is harsher: dozens of variables, measurement error, hidden confounders, feedback loops. Benchmarks also flatter methods: in many generators variance rises along the causal order, which algorithms can exploit without learning causation (Reisach, Seiler and Weichwald, 2021). Treat a discovered graph as a hypothesis generator: candidate arrows to test with an experiment, a natural experiment or domain review.

    Language models that propose causal graphs

    An LLM has read a lot of text about how the world works. Give it variable names ("missed payments", "salary credit") and it often proposes arrows an expert would agree with. Kıcıman and colleagues (2023) found accuracy well above data-only methods on pairwise cause-effect benchmarks. So the model can act as a cheap prior-knowledge source for causal discovery, like a junior expert who has read every textbook.

    Sensible bank uses:

    • Draft a graph for review. Ask for a diagram of what drives early arrears, then have credit experts correct it. You get a fast start and a record of assumptions, not an answer.
    • Suggest confounders and mediators for a regression, including consequences of treatment that must not be controlled for.
    • Prune a search. Forbid arrows that break time order or common sense (a balance cannot cause age), as the expert-knowledge button did in Simulation 4.

    Risks:

    • It rewards fluent, plausible text, so it recalls textbook links even where this bank's data disagrees (it will say income causes default whatever your population shows).
    • Answers vary with prompt wording and run.
    • It cannot see unmeasured variables.
    • Accepting its graph is the "confidently wrong" case in Simulation 4: it narrows the equivalence class using knowledge nobody verified. LLMs do well on famous relationships and worse on unfamiliar or counter-intuitive ones.

    Safe workflow: the model proposes with reasons, humans challenge each arrow, anything that matters is checked against data or an experiment, and the final graph is versioned like any model document.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A comparison group treated differently by design

      The rollout went to the busiest branches first, so the untreated ones are small and rural, with different trends.

      Spot itAsk how rollout order was decided. If it followed performance or expected benefit, expect non-parallel trends and study the event plot.
    • Declaring parallel trends from a passed pre-test

      "The pre-trend test was not significant, so the assumption holds." With few periods the test has almost no power.

      Spot itAsk for the event-study plot with confidence bands and how large a pre-trend the data could hide.
    • Two-way fixed effects on a staggered rollout with growing effects

      The coefficient is small or wrongly signed while the event study shows a clear rise.

      Spot itCompare it with a Callaway-Sant'Anna or imputation estimate. A big gap means contamination. Ask which units serve as controls.
    • Anticipation and early announcement

      Customers reacted at the announcement, before the official start. The "pre" period already contains the effect, shrinking the estimate.

      Spot itLook for movement just before launch and find out when staff and customers first heard.
    • Spill-over into the comparison group

      Customers move between branches, or one team serves both. The comparison is partly treated.

      Spot itAsk how separate the units are. Compare with a more distant group.
    • Synthetic control with a poor pre-fit

      A large post-launch gap, but the synthetic line never matched before launch. The gap is a fitting artefact.

      Spot itCheck pre-launch fit first, then the post/pre error ratio against the placebos, as in Simulation 3.
    • Donor pool or fit window chosen after seeing the result

      Donors dropped until the answer looks right, or the window that gives the biggest gap.

      Spot itAsk whether donors and window were fixed in advance, and for the leave-one-donor-out results.
    • Standard errors that treat customers as independent

      Treatment was assigned to branches, so customer-level errors are far too small.

      Spot itAsk what level errors are clustered at and how many clusters. Below about 20, ask for a bootstrap or randomisation-based interval.
    • Treating a discovered graph as a finding

      A tidy diagram of arrows gets read as the bank's causal structure.

      Spot itAsk which assumptions were used (no hidden confounders, faithfulness, linearity), how stable the graph is across bootstrap resamples, and which arrows come only from prior knowledge.
    • LLM-proposed graphs accepted unchecked

      The graph matches the textbook, so it is trusted. It may recall general knowledge that fails in this customer base.

      Spot itRe-run with different wording and see whether arrows flip. Ask for a reason per arrow. Test the arrows that matter against data or an experiment.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Treated branches averaged 40 accounts per 1,000 before and 52 after. Comparison branches averaged 45 before and 49 after. What is the DiD, and what does it assume?

    (52 − 40) − (49 − 45) = 12 − 4 = 8 per 1,000. It assumes the treated branches would have risen by the same 4 without the feature (parallel trends), with no anticipation and no spill-over.

    2. The pre-launch gap between treated and comparison grows by 0.3 each month, and the post-launch period averages 6 months after the pre-period mean. Roughly how much does DiD overstate the effect?

    The drift continues, so bias is about 0.3 × 6 = 1.8 units. Simulation 1's rule: bias = hidden trend × distance between the average pre and post months.

    3. Why can a two-way fixed effects regression give a negative coefficient when every treated unit benefited?

    Some comparisons use already-treated units as controls. If their effect is still growing, the later group's gain is measured against a rising baseline and looks smaller. Some comparisons also get negative weights, so the sum can drop below zero (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille; Goodman-Bacon).

    4. Name the three conditions beyond the estimator that a modern staggered-DiD analysis still needs.

    Parallel trends (conditional on any covariates), no anticipation, and no spill-over. Clustering at the level of treatment assignment is also needed for honest uncertainty.

    5. A synthetic control matches the treated cluster poorly before launch (pre-fit error 5, against noise of about 1) but shows a post-launch gap of 8. Is that strong evidence of an effect?

    No. Judge the gap against the pre-fit error: the post/pre ratio is under 2, and typical placebo ratios could exceed that. Find a better donor pool or pre-fit (or use augmented synthetic control or synthetic DiD), then repeat the placebo test.

    6. With 12 units in total (one treated, 11 donors), what is the smallest p-value the placebo test can give?

    If the treated unit ranks first of 12, p = 1/12 ≈ 0.083. You can't go lower without more donors, however large the effect.

    7. Which of A → B → C, A ← B → C and A → B ← C can observational data distinguish, and why?

    Only the collider A → B ← C: A and C are independent, but dependent given B. The chain and fork imply the same pattern (A and C dependent, independent given B), so they share one Markov equivalence class. Time order or an intervention separates them.

    8. A colleague uses an LLM to produce a causal graph of arrears drivers, and the graph matches the textbook. Why is that not enough?

    It shows the model recalls general knowledge, not that those relationships hold in our customers or that it knows about unlisted variables. Answers also vary with wording. Each decision-driving arrow needs an expert's reason and a check against data or an experiment.
