    Synthetic Control and Difference-in-Differences

    Part 1

    What these methods do

    In one sentenceDifference-in-differences and synthetic control estimate what would have happened without a change, by watching groups that did not get it, so you can measure an effect without running a randomised test.

    Say a bank launches a savings feature in Dubai only, and new accounts rise from 100 to 130 a week. Did the feature cause the 30? Perhaps. But Abu Dhabi, with no launch, also rose from 80 to 90 over the same months (a seasonal bump, say).

    What you cannot see is Dubai without the feature. That unseen world is the counterfactual, and both methods are ways of guessing it from the groups that stayed untouched.

    Dubai changed by +30. Abu Dhabi changed by +10. The extra +20 is the effect of the launch.

    Part 2

    How they differ from simpler comparisons

    • Before versus after blames the feature for everything else that changed, such as the season.
    • Dubai versus Abu Dhabi blames the feature for every permanent difference between the cities.
    • Difference-in-differences (DiD) uses each group as its own baseline, so only the extra change counts.
    • Synthetic control is for when no single group is a good comparison. It builds one by blending several.
    • A randomised test is better still, because chance does the matching. These methods are for when you could not randomise.
    Part 3

    How they work

    Difference-in-differences

    1. Pick a treated group (Dubai) and one or more untouched groups (Abu Dhabi).
    2. Measure each before and after the launch.
    3. Work out each group's change.
    4. Subtract the untouched group's change from the treated group's change.

    This cancels anything fixed per group (Dubai is simply bigger) and anything that hits both groups equally (Ramadan, interest-rate moves). Its one assumption is parallel trends: without the launch, the gap between the groups would have stayed steady. The levels need not match. Only the gap must be stable.

    You cannot prove that about a world you cannot see. The standard check is an event study: plot the gap for each period. A flat gap before launch is reassuring. A gap already drifting means DiD credits the drift to the feature.

    Staggered rollouts

    When branches get the feature at different dates, the habitual tool (a regression with fixed effects for branch and month, called two-way fixed effects) can go wrong. It quietly uses branches that already have the feature as comparisons. If their effect is still growing, that growth is subtracted as if it were background, and the answer can be too small or even the wrong sign.

    Modern estimators, such as Callaway and Sant'Anna, avoid this. They compare each launch cohort only with branches that never got it, or have not got it yet, then average the results.

    Synthetic control

    1. Choose a pool of untreated "donor" units that could have followed a similar path.
    2. Find weights, never negative and adding to 100%, so the blend matches the treated unit before launch.
    3. After launch, the gap between the real unit and the blend is the estimate.

    The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so the blend is an average of donors, never an extrapolation beyond them. You can say "our synthetic Sharjah is 45% Ajman-like and 30% Fujairah-like", which is easy to check.

    Part 4

    How to read the results

    Event-study gap flat, then jumps at launch. Good sign: the groups were moving together until the change.
    Gap already sloping upward before launch. Parallel trends is in doubt. DiD will overstate the effect.
    Synthetic control matches the past closely. The post-launch gap can be trusted more. If the blend cannot reproduce the past, the gap means nothing.
    Placebo test. Pretend each donor was the treated one and see how big a gap appears by chance. If your real gap tops the placebos, it is unusual. With 10 donors the smallest possible p-value is about 0.09, which is honest, not weak.

    Always report the weights, the pre-launch fit and the placebo results. Group results by the level at which treatment was assigned (branch, segment), because outcomes within a branch move together over time.

    Two cautions. A flat pre-trend is necessary, not sufficient: a competitor promotion starting at the same date looks exactly like your effect. And the scale matters: parallel in dirhams may not be parallel in percentage growth, so state which you chose.

    Part 5

    Which to use

    MethodUse whenNeedsFails when
    Before versus afterNothing else changedNothingSeason or trend moves
    Difference-in-differencesTreated and untreated groups, before and afterParallel trendsGroups were already diverging
    Modern DiD (staggered)Branches launch at different datesParallel trends, no early reactionSpill-over between branches
    Synthetic controlOne treated unit, many donors, long historyA good blend of donorsTreated unit is unlike every donor
    Randomised testYou can randomiseControl over who gets itNot possible or not ethical

    Strong at:

    • Cheap, credible answers from records you already have.
    • One checkable assumption, shown in a plot that non-specialists can read.
    • Something else starting at launch, or customers reacting at the announcement.
    • Few periods or few groups, where checks have almost no power.
    Show the formula

    DiD effect = (Dubai after − Dubai before) − (Abu Dhabi after − Abu Dhabi before)

    In words: the treated group's change, minus the change the untouched group had anyway. Here (130 − 100) − (90 − 80) = 20.

    In banking, these methods suit branch, product and pricing rollouts that were never randomised, such as a feature launched in one emirate or a fee change at one segment.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Skipping the pre-trend plot

      Without it you have no evidence for the one assumption DiD needs.

    • Trusting the old regression on staggered launches

      It can use already-treated branches as comparisons and give a biased or wrongly signed answer.

    • Forcing a poor synthetic match

      If the blend cannot track the past, the post-launch gap is noise. Say so rather than report it.

    • Ignoring spill-over

      If customers move between treated and comparison branches, the comparison is contaminated.

    1. Treated group goes 50 to 80; comparison goes 40 to 55. What is the DiD effect?

    (80 − 50) − (55 − 40) = 15.

    2. What is parallel trends?

    The assumption that without the change, the gap between treated and comparison groups would have stayed steady.

    3. Why can't you prove it?

    It is about the world without the launch, which you never see. You can only look for evidence against it, such as drift before launch.

    4. When do you prefer synthetic control to DiD?

    When there is one treated unit and no single good comparison, but many donors that together can mimic it.

    5. Why must synthetic control weights be non-negative and sum to one?

    So the blend is an average of real donors and never extrapolates beyond them, which keeps it honest and easy to explain.
