    Causal Effects Without a Randomised Test: Synthetic Control, Modern Difference-in-Differences and Causal Discovery

    What to do when the bank rolled something out and nobody randomised it: build the comparison group the data never gave you, avoid the quiet bias in staggered rollouts, and see what an algorithm (or an LLM) can and cannot say about the arrows between variables.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceWithout randomisation every causal estimate is only as good as the comparison group you can defend: difference-in-differences borrows the trend of untouched units, modern versions stop the staggered rollout from contaminating that borrowing, synthetic control builds the comparison from a weighted blend of other units, and causal discovery can narrow the possible cause-and-effect structures but never settle them without expert knowledge.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    In March a bank launches a new savings feature in its Dubai branches only. By June, new savings accounts per 1,000 customers are up 14%. The product head wants to know: did the feature cause that? You cannot ask the Dubai customers what they would have done without it. That unobserved world is the counterfactual, and every causal method is a way of guessing it.

    The cheap guesses are bad. "Before versus after" blames the feature for everything that changed in the spring: salaries, Ramadan, a market rally. "Dubai versus Abu Dhabi" blames it for every permanent difference between the two cities. Neither is a fair comparison.

    There is a better idea, and it is simple. Look at how Abu Dhabi changed over the same months. Whatever happened to both cities (the season, the economy) shows up in Abu Dhabi too. Only the Dubai-specific extra change is a candidate for the feature's effect. That is difference-in-differences (DiD): the change in the treated group, minus the change in the comparison group. It removes anything that is constant per group and anything that hits both groups equally.

    It costs one big assumption: absent the feature, Dubai would have moved in parallel with Abu Dhabi. This lesson is mostly about that assumption, and about three ways to extend or stress-test the idea:

    • Staggered rollouts. Real programmes reach branches, segments or products at different dates. The textbook regression for that situation (two-way fixed effects) can give a wrong answer, even a wrong sign, when effects grow over time. Newer estimators fix this by never using already-treated units as comparisons.
    • Synthetic control. When you have one treated unit (one region, one product, one segment) and many possible comparisons, none of which is a good match alone, you build a weighted blend that tracked the treated unit before launch. If the blend then drifts away after launch, that gap is your estimate.
    • Causal discovery. Sometimes the question is earlier: you do not know which variables cause which. Algorithms can test patterns of dependence to rule structures out. They rarely rule in a single answer, and a language model can propose a graph that looks sensible while being wrong.
    Hold onto thisAll of these methods trade randomisation for assumptions. The skill is not running the estimator (a library does that in one line) but stating the assumption in plain words, showing the evidence for it, and reporting how wrong the answer would be if it fails.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Difference-in-differences and the parallel-trends assumption

    Take two groups and two periods. Let Ȳ be an average outcome.

    DiD=(Y¯T,post−Y¯T,pre)−(Y¯C,post−Y¯C,pre)

    T is the treated group, C the comparison group. The first bracket is the treated group's change; the second is the change you would expect from everything else. Under parallel trends (absent treatment, T's average would have changed by the same amount as C's), the difference is the average effect on the treated. The same number comes out of the regression y ~ treated + post + treated:post, where the interaction coefficient is the DiD.

    Notice what parallel trends does not say. Levels may differ (Dubai starts higher). Both groups may trend. Only the gap between them must have been stable, in the absence of treatment. It is an assumption about a world you cannot see, so you can never prove it. You can only look for evidence against it, using the periods before launch.

    The standard check is an event study: plot the treated-minus-comparison gap for each period, relative to the gap just before launch. If the gap was flat before launch and moves after, that is reassuring. If it was already drifting, DiD attributes the drift to the treatment. Simulation 1 shows exactly how much.

    Three practical points follow.

    • Parallel trends depends on the scale. Parallel in AED may be non-parallel in percentage growth. A high-balance segment and a low-balance segment rarely move in parallel in levels. Choose the scale (levels, logs) for which a story of "same shock, same proportional response" is believable, and say so.
    • A flat pre-trend is necessary, not sufficient. It cannot catch something that begins at launch: a competitor's promotion in Dubai that month, a rule change that affects only Dubai. The more units and the longer the pre-period, the more a coincidence of that sort becomes unlikely, but never impossible. Rambachan and Roth (2023) turned this into a tool, often called "honest DiD": instead of testing for a pre-trend and then pretending there is none, you ask how big a post-launch violation would need to be, relative to what you saw before launch, to erase your conclusion, and report intervals that stay valid under that much violation.
    • Pre-trend tests have weak power and can distort. A test that fails to reject "no pre-trend" with six noisy months is not evidence of parallelism. Roth (2022) showed that screening studies on a passed pre-test also biases what survives. Prefer plotting the event study and reasoning about size.

    Standard errors also need care: outcomes within a branch are correlated over time, so cluster at the level at which treatment was assigned (the branch, the segment), and be sceptical when there are few clusters. With two cities there are two clusters; no formula rescues that.

    # 2x2 DiD as a regression, clustered by branch
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
m = smf.ols("accounts ~ treated + post + treated:post", data=df).fit(
        cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch_id"]})
print(m.params["treated:post"], m.conf_int().loc["treated:post"].values)

    Staggered rollouts and why the old regression breaks

    Banks seldom switch something on everywhere at once. A new onboarding flow reaches branch clusters in waves; a pricing change hits one customer segment per quarter; a fraud rule goes to retail, then business banking. The habit is to run a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression: an outcome on a unit effect, a time effect and a 0/1 "treated yet?" dummy. With one launch date this is just DiD. With several, it hides a problem.

    The TWFE coefficient is a weighted average of many small two-group, two-period comparisons (Goodman-Bacon, 2021). Some are good: late-treated units compared with never-treated units, or early-treated compared with not-yet-treated. But some use already-treated units as the comparison group: the late cohort is compared with the early cohort, which is by then in its treated state. If the early cohort's effect is still growing, that growth is subtracted from the late cohort's estimate as though it were a background trend. The result is biased downwards, and with enough heterogeneity some good comparisons receive negative weights, so the estimate can be negative when every unit's true effect is positive (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille, 2020; Sun and Abraham, 2021).

    If the effect is the same for every unit at every date, none of this bites. That is rarely true: a new feature builds up as customers learn it.

    The modern family all avoid forbidden comparisons, in slightly different ways:

    • Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021). Estimate a separate effect for each cohort g (units first treated at date g) and each date t, written ATT(g,t), using never-treated or not-yet-treated units as controls. Then aggregate those building blocks however you need: by time since treatment (an event study), by cohort, or overall. This is what Simulation 2's "cohort vs never-treated" does.
    • Sun and Abraham (2021). Keep a regression, but interact relative-time dummies with cohort, so each cohort's dynamics are free, then average with cohort-share weights.
    • Imputation estimators (Borusyak, Jaravel and Spiess, 2024; Gardner's two-stage). Fit the unit and time effects only on untreated observations, predict each treated observation's untreated outcome, and average the differences. Efficient, and easy to explain: "we predicted what each treated branch-month would have looked like and compared".
    • de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille. Estimators built from the units that switch treatment status, also handling treatment that turns on and off, which matters for things like a limit that is raised and later cut.

    You do not need to choose a winner for a meeting. You need to know the trigger for the problem (staggered timing plus effects that change with exposure time), the symptom (TWFE and an event study disagree) and the remedy (an estimator that compares only with untreated units, and an event-study plot as the main exhibit). Roth, Sant'Anna, Bilinski and Poe (2023) review the whole literature; Baker et al. give a practical guide.

    An important remaining caution: these methods fix the estimator, not the assumption. They still need parallel trends (conditional on whatever you adjust for), no anticipation (customers did not change behaviour before the official launch, which is common when a feature is announced early), and no spill-over (the comparison branches were not affected by the treated ones, which fails if customers move between branches or the same call centre serves both).

    Synthetic control and synthetic difference-in-differences

    Sometimes you have one treated unit: one emirate, one product line, one customer segment, one subsidiary. Choosing a single comparison unit feels arbitrary, and averaging all the others gives a group that never resembled the treated unit. The synthetic control method (Abadie and Gardeazabal, 2003; Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller, 2010) builds a comparison as a weighted average of the untreated "donor" units, with weights chosen so that the blend tracked the treated unit's pre-launch path as closely as possible.

    minw∑t≤T0(Y1t−∑j=2J+1wjYjt)2subject towj≥0,∑jwj=1

    Unit 1 is the treated unit, units 2 to J+1 are donors, T0 is the last pre-launch period. The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so the synthetic unit is a blend, never an extrapolation. After launch, the estimated effect at date t is the treated unit's outcome minus the synthetic unit's. The weights are usually sparse: a handful of donors get most of the weight, which makes the result inspectable ("our synthetic Sharjah is 45% Ajman-like, 30% ...").

    Two ideas make it credible. First, fit before launch is the diagnostic: if the blend cannot reproduce the treated path before launch, the post-launch gap means nothing. This also gives synthetic control a cousin of the parallel-trends check that is much more demanding than "the pre-lines looked similar". Second, inference by placebo: with one treated unit there are no standard errors from a sample. So pretend, in turn, that each donor was the treated unit, run the same procedure, and see how large a post-launch gap arises by chance. If the real unit's gap (scaled by its pre-launch fit error, because a poorly fitted unit makes a big gap easily) is bigger than nearly all placebos, the effect is unlikely to be chance. With 10 donors, the best p-value you can reach is 1 in 11, about 0.09. That is honest, not weak.

    Where it goes beyond the original recipe:

    • Augmented synthetic control (Ben-Michael, Feller and Rothstein, 2021) adds a regression correction when the pre-fit is imperfect, so you do not need an exact blend.
    • Synthetic difference-in-differences (Arkhangelsky, Athey, Hirshberg, Imbens and Wager, 2021) is a hybrid. It weights donor units as synthetic control does and weights pre-launch periods, then runs a DiD with those weights, allowing a constant level gap between treated and synthetic (which DiD allows and plain synthetic control does not). It works across the setting where DiD has many treated units and synthetic control has few, and is designed to behave sensibly on both.
    • Staggered and multi-unit versions exist, including recent work on inference when several units are treated at different dates. Treat these as active research, not settled practice.

    Synthetic control suits small numbers of treated units with a long pre-period and many donors: a regional pricing change, one product's repricing, one regulator-driven change applied to one business line. It suits large numbers of treated units less well, where DiD with a modern estimator is the better tool. Always report the weights, the pre-fit error, the placebo distribution, and what happens if you drop the top donor.

    Causal discovery: learning structure from data

    (In brief.) Everything above assumed you knew what caused what and wanted the size. Causal discovery asks the earlier question: given data on many variables, which arrows are consistent with it? Three families:

    • Constraint-based (PC, FCI; Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines). Test conditional independences, remove edges between variables that are independent given some other set, then orient what the pattern of tests allows. The key signature is the collider A → B ← C: A and C are independent, but become dependent once you condition on B.
    • Score-based (GES; Chickering, 2002). Search over graphs and score each by how well it fits the data, with a penalty for complexity.
    • Functional-form-based. Assume more about the mechanism, for example linear with non-Gaussian noise (LiNGAM; Shimizu et al., 2006) or additive noise, and the direction of an arrow becomes identifiable from the data's shape. The continuous-optimisation approach NOTEARS (Zheng et al., 2018) recasts the search as smooth optimisation under an acyclicity constraint.

    The limit is fundamental. Different graphs imply the same independences. A chain A → B → C, the reverse chain, and the fork A ← B → C all say "A and C are independent given B" and nothing else; they form a Markov equivalence class, and no amount of observational data separates them. Only a collider is orientable from this pattern. Add the usual assumptions (the Markov and faithfulness conditions: independences in the data come from the graph, not from cancelling paths; no hidden common causes unless you use FCI; enough data for the tests) and you get a class of graphs, not a graph.

    Real data is harsher than this toy: dozens of variables, mixed types, measurement error, hidden confounders, feedback loops, time. Benchmarks on synthetic graphs are also known to flatter methods, for instance because in many generators the variance of variables rises along the causal order, which algorithms can exploit without learning causation (Reisach, Seiler and Weichwald, 2021, "Beware of the simulated DAG"). A discovered graph is best treated as a hypothesis generator: a set of candidate arrows to test with an experiment, a natural experiment (the methods above) or domain review.

    Language models that propose causal graphs

    Language models know a great deal about how the world works from text. Given variable names ("credit utilisation", "missed payments", "salary credit", "customer age"), they can propose which variable causes which, and often agree with what a domain expert would say. Kıcıman and colleagues (2023) tested this on pairwise cause-effect benchmarks and found accuracy well above what data-only methods achieved on the same pairs, which opened a line of work: use the model as a source of prior knowledge to constrain or seed causal discovery. The model plays the part of the expert, cheaply and at scale.

    The uses that make sense in a bank:

    • Draft a graph for review. Ask for a causal diagram of a process (what drives early arrears, what influences alert volume), then have credit or fraud experts correct it. The value is a faster start and a record of assumptions, not an answer.
    • Suggest confounders and mediators to include or avoid in a regression, such as variables that are consequences of treatment and should not be controlled for.
    • Prune a search. Forbid arrows that violate time order or common sense (a customer's age cannot be caused by their balance), shrinking the space discovery must explore, as the expert-knowledge button did in Simulation 4.

    The risks are specific. A model is rewarded for fluent, plausible text; it will recall textbook relationships even where this bank's data disagrees. It can leak from names: if "default" and "income" appear, it will say income causes default whether or not the data supports an arrow in your population. Answers vary across prompt wording and run. It cannot see unmeasured variables. And a graph accepted because an LLM drew it is exactly the "confidently wrong" case in Simulation 4: it narrows the equivalence class using knowledge nobody verified. Studies keep finding that language models are strong on well-known relationships and weaker on unfamiliar or counter-intuitive ones, and that they can reproduce the surface patterns of causal language without reliable reasoning (see Part 4). The safe workflow: model proposes with reasons, humans challenge each arrow, anything that matters is checked against data or an experiment, and the final graph is versioned like any model document.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These methods sit between the randomised test and plain correlation. Where they come from and where they lead:

    F9 · Causal inferenceThe foundations this lesson builds on

    Potential outcomes, confounding, adjustment sets and the basic DiD idea. This lesson is the edge: when the simple versions fail and what replaces them.

    F2.6 · Confounding and selectionWhy the comparison group matters

    Branches get a rollout because they are large, urban or willing. That selection is why "treated minus untreated" fails and why parallel trends must be argued.

    R8.3 – R8.4 · Modern A/B testingWhen you can randomise

    If you can randomise, do: one clean assignment beats every assumption here. DiD and synthetic control are what you do when you could not, or when the unit is too big to randomise.

    R8.1 – R8.2 · Conformal predictionDistribution-free intervals

    Conformal methods give prediction intervals without a model of the noise. Placebo inference in synthetic control is the same spirit: use the data's own variation as the reference.

    F2.1 · F2.2 · Intervals and bootstrapFew clusters, wide intervals

    Cluster-robust and bootstrap intervals matter hugely here because treatment is assigned to branches or segments, not to customers.

    F9 · Time seriesCounterfactual forecasting

    A forecast of what would have happened is a counterfactual. Synthetic control and structural time-series methods differ in where the counterfactual comes from: peers versus the unit's own past.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeTime discipline for treatment dates

    Anticipation and announcement dates are leakage in causal form. Define the treatment date by when customers could first act, not by the project plan.

    F3.6 · Experiments and write-upsReporting an observational result

    An effect estimate from DiD needs a different write-up from an A/B test: assumptions first, event-study plot, sensitivity.

    A4 · Statistics for evalsUncertainty with small counts

    The same discipline of intervals and honest comparisons applies when you evaluate agents and assistants.

    R4 · Faithfulness of reasoningWhen a model's explanation is not its cause

    A language model giving causal-sounding reasons is not the same as causal knowledge. The faithfulness literature explains why to check.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Product and channel rollouts. A feature, a fee change or a call-centre script launched in waves across branches or segments: staggered DiD with an event study. See churn and retention for the outcome side and why retention programmes are so easily over-credited.
    • One-off changes to one unit. A new fraud rule on one channel, a pricing change in one market, a policy change applied to one business line only: synthetic control with placebo inference.
    • Regulatory or market shocks. Events that hit some segments and not others (for instance a rule that applies to one customer class). Do not assume the comparison group is untouched: check for spill-over and anticipation, and state any uncertainty about what a regulation actually covers.
    • Did the model help? Post-hoc evaluation of an already deployed credit or collections model when no holdout was kept. A synthetic comparison is far weaker than the holdout you should have kept (see baselines), but better than a before-and-after chart.
    • Root-cause exploration. Discovery methods and LLM-drafted graphs for data-quality incidents, alert-volume spikes or arrears drivers, as sources of hypotheses to test, never as a finding.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The quiet revolution of the last few years is that the default tool for panel data was found to be unreliable under common conditions, and a family of fixes became standard in economics, public health and tech. The causal-discovery side moves faster and is less settled, with language models pushing a wave of benchmarks.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in vendor decks, internal analyses and AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Choosing a comparison group that was treated differently by design

      "Branches without the feature" are the small, rural, low-footfall ones, because the rollout went to the busiest first. Their trends differ for reasons unrelated to the feature.

      Spot itAsk how rollout order was decided. If it followed performance or expected benefit, expect non-parallel trends and look hard at the event study.
    • Declaring parallel trends from a pass on a pre-test

      "The pre-trend test was not significant, so the assumption holds." With few periods that test barely has power.

      Spot itAsk to see the event-study plot with confidence bands, and how large a pre-trend the data could hide.
    • Two-way fixed effects on a staggered rollout, effects growing

      The coefficient is small or even the wrong sign while the event study shows a clear rise.

      Spot itCompare the TWFE coefficient with a Callaway-Sant'Anna or imputation estimate. A big gap means contamination. Ask which units serve as controls.
    • Anticipation and early announcement

      Customers changed behaviour when the feature was announced, before the official start date. The "pre" period already contains the effect, shrinking the estimate.

      Spot itLook for movement in the periods just before the launch date, and find out when staff and customers first heard.
    • Spill-over into the comparison group

      Customers move to or from the treated branch, or one team serves both. The comparison is partly treated.

      Spot itAsk how separate the units are. Compare the comparison group's trend with a more distant group.
    • Synthetic control with a poor pre-fit

      A large post-launch gap, but the synthetic line never matched before launch. The gap is a fitting artefact.

      Spot itLook at the pre-launch fit first. Compare the treated unit's post/pre error ratio with the placebo distribution, as in Simulation 3.
    • Choosing the donor pool or fit window after seeing the result

      Dropping donors until the answer looks right, or picking the fitting window that gives the biggest gap.

      Spot itAsk whether the donor list and window were fixed in advance and for the effect of leaving out each donor in turn.
    • Standard errors treating customers as independent

      Treatment is assigned to branches. Customer-level standard errors are far too small.

      Spot itAsk at what level errors are clustered and how many clusters there are. Below about 20, ask for a bootstrap or randomisation-based interval.
    • Treating a discovered graph as a finding

      A causal-discovery run outputs a tidy diagram of arrows. People read it as the bank's causal structure.

      Spot itAsk which assumptions were used (no hidden confounders, faithfulness, linearity), how stable the graph is across bootstrap resamples, and which arrows are only oriented because of prior knowledge.
    • LLM-proposed graphs accepted without checks

      The model's graph agrees with the textbook, so it is trusted. It may be recalling general knowledge that does not hold in this customer base, or not know about a measured proxy.

      Spot itRe-run with differently worded prompts and see if arrows flip. Ask for the reason per arrow. Test the arrows that matter against data or an experiment.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We didn't run a test, so we compared the branches that got the feature with similar branches that hadn't, looking at how each moved over the same months. That strips out the season and the market. The result is about six extra accounts per thousand customers, but it holds only if the two groups would otherwise have moved together. The months before launch support that, and I'll show a version that allows for some drift. If you can hold back a few branches for the next phase, the answer becomes a lot more solid.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Rollout was staggered, so I wouldn't use a two-way fixed-effects coefficient, since it can use already-treated branches as controls and bias the estimate when effects build over time. I'd estimate cohort-by-date effects against not-yet-treated or never-treated branches, aggregate to an event study, cluster at branch level, and add a sensitivity analysis on parallel trends. For a single treated unit I'd use synthetic control with pre-fit reported and placebo inference. Anything LLM-drafted in the causal graph goes through expert review first.”
    “Why not just compare before and after?”

    Because everything else changed too: seasons, salaries, the market. Before and after attributes all of it to the feature. In Simulation 1 it read about 4 when the true effect was 6, because a seasonal dip happened to cancel part of the trend. It could just as easily have been too high. The comparison group gives us a record of what happened to everyone else over the same months.

    “How do you know the two groups would have moved together?”

    I can't know; it is an assumption about the world that didn't happen. I can show that they moved together in the months before, that nothing else changed in one group at launch, and how large a divergence would have to be to overturn the conclusion. If none of that is convincing, I'd say the estimate is a hypothesis, not a result.

    “Our analyst ran a fixed-effects regression with a treatment dummy. What's wrong with that?”

    Nothing, if there was a single launch date or the effect is the same everywhere and at every date. With a staggered rollout and effects that grow, it compares later-treated units with already-treated ones and can understate or even flip the sign. I'd rerun with an estimator that only uses untreated units as comparisons, and compare the two. If they agree, no harm done.

    “We changed the card fee in one segment only. Can we measure the impact?”

    Plausibly, with a synthetic control built from similar segments: weights chosen to match the segment's pre-change path, then the gap afterwards compared with what happens when each other segment is pretended to be treated. The pre-change fit must be good, and I'd want a long pre-period and a donor pool not affected by the fee. With ten donors, the lowest p-value is around 0.09, so I'd report the size of the effect and how it ranks, not just a significance label.

    “Can we ask the LLM to draw the causal diagram for us?”

    As a first draft for the experts to correct, yes: it is quick, and it exposes assumptions. As a finding, no. Models recall textbook relationships whether or not they hold in our customers, can change answers with the wording, and cannot see unmeasured variables. I'd have the model give reasons for each arrow, have credit or fraud experts accept or reject each one, and test the ones that drive decisions against data or an experiment.

    “Can an algorithm just find what causes defaults in our data?”

    It can narrow the possibilities, not settle them. Observational data usually leaves several graphs equally consistent, for instance A causes B causes C versus B causing both. It also assumes no hidden confounders, which is unrealistic in lending. I'd use discovery to suggest candidates, then confirm with domain knowledge and either an experiment or a quasi-experiment.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Treated branches averaged 40 accounts per 1,000 before and 52 after. Comparison branches averaged 45 before and 49 after. What is the DiD, and what does it assume?

    (52 − 40) − (49 − 45) = 12 − 4 = 8 accounts per 1,000. It assumes that without the feature, the treated branches would have risen by the same 4 as the comparison branches (parallel trends), with no anticipation and no spill-over.

    2. The pre-launch gap between treated and comparison grows by 0.3 each month, and the post-launch period averages 6 months after the pre-period mean. Roughly how much does DiD overstate the effect?

    The gap's drift continues, and the average post month sits about 6 months beyond the average pre month, so the bias is about 0.3 × 6 = 1.8 units. This is Simulation 1's rule: bias = hidden trend × distance between the average pre and post months.

    3. Why can a two-way fixed effects regression give a negative coefficient when every treated unit benefited?

    Some of the comparisons it averages use already-treated units as controls. If their effect is still growing, the later group's gain is measured against a rising baseline and looks smaller, and the weights on some comparisons are negative. With large and growing effects the weighted sum can go below zero (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille; Goodman-Bacon).

    4. Name the three conditions beyond the estimator that a modern staggered-DiD analysis still needs.

    Parallel trends (conditional on any covariates used), no anticipation (the pre-period is untouched by the programme), and no spill-over between treated and comparison units. Clustering at the level of treatment assignment is also needed for honest uncertainty.

    5. A synthetic control matches the treated cluster poorly before launch (pre-fit error 5, against noise of about 1) but shows a post-launch gap of 8. Is that strong evidence of an effect?

    No. A gap has to be judged relative to the pre-fit error: here the post/pre ratio is under 2 and typical placebo ratios could exceed that. Either find a better donor pool or a better pre-fit (or use augmented synthetic control or synthetic DiD), then repeat the placebo test.

    6. With 12 units in total (one treated, 11 donors), what is the smallest p-value the placebo test can give?

    If the treated unit ranks first of 12 by the post/pre error ratio, the p-value is 1/12 ≈ 0.083. You cannot get below that without more donors, however large the effect.

    7. Which of A → B → C, A ← B → C and A → B ← C can observational data distinguish, and why?

    Only the collider A → B ← C is distinguishable from the other two: A and C are independent, but dependent given B. The chain and the fork imply the same thing (A and C dependent, independent given B) so they sit in one Markov equivalence class. Time order or an intervention is needed to separate them.

    8. A colleague uses an LLM to produce a causal graph of arrears drivers, and the graph matches the textbook. Why is that not enough?

    Matching the textbook shows the model recalls general knowledge, not that those relationships hold in our customers, nor that the model knows about variables we did not list. Answers also vary with the wording. Each arrow that drives a decision needs an expert's reason and a check against data or an experiment.
