    Time Series and Graphs: Forecasting What Comes Next, and Seeing Who Is Connected to Whom

    Why a series with a calendar needs different handling from a table of customers, how to beat the naive forecast honestly and say how wrong you might be, and how fraud rings, mule networks and ownership chains become something a model can read.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTime series and graphs are the two cases where rows are not independent: in a series each value leans on the ones before it, in a graph each account leans on its neighbours, so you must split, validate and explain with that dependence in mind, and you gain a great deal of signal when you use it properly.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most ML assumes each row stands alone: shuffle the rows and nothing changes. Two kinds of banking data break that.

    Time series. Cash taken from a Dubai Mall ATM today resembles yesterday's and last week's, and jumps on salary day. Shuffle the days and the information is gone. Forecasting uses the past to say something about days that have not happened, and says how unsure it is.

    Graphs. Two accounts sharing a phone, a device and a flow of money are not independent customers, much as friends of a known shoplifter deserve a second look. A company 60% owned by a holding company that is 50% owned by an individual is partly that person's, though their name is not on the record.

    Both are about dependence: in a series, today leans on the past; in a graph, a node leans on its neighbours. Use it and you gain strong signal (a seasonal pattern that halves forecast error, a fraud ring no single-account feature sees). Ignore it and validation lies, because test data is not really separate from training data.

    Hold onto thisBefore modelling, ask what the dependence is. Series: what repeats (weekly, monthly, Ramadan, Eid) and how far ahead must we see? Graph: what counts as a link (shared device, money moved, shared director) and which way does suspicion flow? These decide the method more than the algorithm does.

    Part 2 covers forecasting first, with two simulations (racing methods on ATM cash demand; how uncertainty widens with the horizon), then graphs, with a simulation that spreads suspicion through a network. Parts 3 and 4 cover failure modes and self-check.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    What makes a time series different

    Think of a daily ATM withdrawal series as a recipe with four ingredients:

    • Level and trend. Where it sits and drifts: customer growth, inflation, a new branch.
    • Seasonality. Fixed-period repeats: weekly (Thursday and Friday peaks), monthly (salary day), yearly (summer travel).
    • Calendar events. Uneven dates: public holidays, pre-Eid days. Ramadan and Eid follow the Islamic calendar, about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year. A "month of year" feature learns Ramadan as one month, then misses by ten days next year; use Hijri-calendar features. Weekends differ too: Friday–Saturday in Saudi Arabia, Saturday–Sunday in the UAE (since 2022), so regional models need a calendar per country.
    • Noise. What is left. It sets a floor under every forecast.

    Autocorrelation means today's value correlates with yesterday's, last week's, last month's. It makes forecasting possible, and breaks the usual validation tricks. A stationary series behaves the same over time; most real ones do not (trends, shifting volatility, breaks such as a new payment rail). Classical methods difference the series (model the change, not the level); modern ones get the right features and frequent re-fits. The recipe in a line:

    yt=trend(t)+weekday effect+calendar effects+εt

    The additive form. If swings grow with the level, use a multiplicative form or a log transform. Forecasting: estimate each piece from history, extend it, add them up.

    Simple methods stay competitive because of the noise floor. Once a method captures trend, weekly pattern and known events, what is left is mostly randomness, and extra complexity adds only variance. Ask: how much better than seasonal naive? MASE answers it: the model's mean absolute error divided by the naive method's error on training data. 1.0 means no better than naive; 0.7 means 30% better; above 1.0 means worse (F7: forecast metrics).

    Run the race below on an invented ATM: gentle upward trend, a weekday pattern peaking Thursday and Friday, a salary-day bump on two days of every 28, plus noise you control. Each method sees 84 days and forecasts the next 28.

    • Seasonal naive is a serious baseline. It already has the weekly shape. It loses mainly because it copies one noisy week and cannot extend a trend.
    • Gains come from encoding calendar knowledge, not a cleverer algorithm. The payday term explains the two big spikes. In practice this is a holiday and salary-day table, built once and reused.
    • You cannot beat the floor. An error below the noise level means something leaked from the future (F3.1).

    Banks forecast thousands of series (ATM by ATM, branch by branch), and totals must add up. Hierarchical reconciliation adjusts forecasts across levels so ATM forecasts sum to the regional one. Finance teams ask for it often.

    Validating a forecast, and how uncertainty grows with the horizon

    Never shuffle. A random split puts next Tuesday in training and last Monday in test; correlated neighbours leak the answer. Use rolling origin: train to a date, forecast the next block, move forward, repeat (F4.5). You see how error varies across periods, including Ramadan and summer. Fit all preprocessing, including holiday-effect estimates, on training data only. Match the real use: if treasury needs a 14-day forecast every morning, test exactly that.

    Report an interval, not a single number. "AED 12.4m" invites false precision; "AED 12.4m, 80% likely between 11.1m and 13.7m" lets treasury size its buffer. Intervals widen with the horizon because errors accumulate, at a speed set by how persistent the series is. The simplest model shows this.

    A balance drifts back to its usual level but gets daily shocks. In an AR(1), each day's deviation is a fraction φ of yesterday's plus a fresh shock. If today is d0 above usual, then h days ahead:

    forecast=φhd0,variance of error=σ21−φ2h1−φ2

    σ is the size of a daily shock. The forecast decays towards the usual level at rate φ per day. Error variance starts at σ² and levels off at σ²/(1 − φ²). At φ = 1 (a random walk) there is no level to return to, the forecast stays at today's value, and variance grows without limit: hσ². An 80% interval is the forecast ± 1.28 error standard deviations.

    • Horizon matters as much as method. One-day and 30-day error are different problems. State the horizon beside every accuracy figure.
    • Persistent series (balances, deposits) are forecastable far ahead with growing doubt. Mean-reverting series (a daily transaction spike) are forecastable only briefly, then collapse to the average.
    • Random walks defeat clever models. Exchange rates behave this way: the best forecast is today's value. Demand out-of-sample evidence from anyone promising more. Conformal prediction (R8) gives intervals with fewer assumptions.
    • Structural breaks are a first-class risk. A new payment rail, pricing change or regulatory limit changes the process itself, and backtests across a break mislead. Watch for drift (F7.7) and re-fit on a schedule.

    Graphs: turning relationships into data

    A graph has nodes (accounts, customers, devices, companies, merchants) and edges (sent money to, shares a phone with, is director of, owns 40% of). Edges can be directed, weighted, typed and timestamped. Banks already hold this in tables; the graph view helps with questions about structure rather than single rows:

    • Fraud rings. Accounts opened within days, sharing a device or address, cycling money before cashing out.
    • Mule networks. A collector receives from many unrelated senders and forwards within hours. Each account looks ordinary; the shape (many in, one out) gives it away.
    • Ownership chains. Who ultimately controls a company through layers of holdings and jurisdictions.
    • Contagion risk. Who depends on whom for payment or supply, and what happens if one fails.

    Decide what an edge is first. "Shares a device" and "sent over AED 5,000 in 30 days" give different graphs from the same data. A family's shared device is a fair edge; a public Wi-Fi hotspot or corporate payment hub links thousands of strangers and floods the graph with false connections.

    Classic graph analytics: components, hubs, flows and ownership

    Plain computation answers a lot before any machine learning, and is often what banks run.

    • Connected components. Groups of accounts reachable from each other. 40 accounts sharing a few devices is worth a look. 2 million is not a ring: your edge definition is too loose.
    • Degree and centrality. Link count and how central a node is. A collector mule has high in-degree and a low end-of-day balance. A merchant or payroll account also has high degree, so degree is a lead, not a verdict.
    • Motifs. Small recurring shapes: cycle A→B→C→A, fan-in, fan-out, fast pass-through chains. Layering looks like these, with timing.
    • Community detection. Finds densely linked groups; splits a giant component into candidate rings.
    • Random-walk scores. PageRank and its personalised version (below) measure how much suspicion flows from starting nodes to every other node.

    Ownership chains show structure no row-level feature captures. A person's effective stake is the product of percentages along each path, summed over paths:

    effective stake=∑paths∏links on the pathownership share

    Example: the person owns 60% of Holdco A, A owns 50% of Holdco B, B owns 80% of Operating Co C, and the person holds 5% of C directly.

    Path from the person to CCalculationEffective stake
    Through A, then B0.60 × 0.50 × 0.8024%
    Direct0.055%
    Total24% + 5%29%

    Each path alone is under 25%; the total is over it. Beneficial-ownership rules use thresholds like this (25% is common; check the bank's policy and local rule, plus control-based tests that apply regardless of percentage). Looking only at the first link, or each path alone, misses the person. Cycles (A owns B owns A) need care to avoid infinite counting, and nominee structures hide control. This powers B3: customer due diligence.

    Graph machine learning: propagating suspicion, then learning to

    Guilt by association means neighbours of a confirmed bad node are more likely bad. A personalised random walk makes this precise: a walker starts on a confirmed fraud account and at each step either jumps back to a confirmed account (probability α) or follows a random link (1 − α). Time spent on each account is its score. Accounts close to fraud, via many routes, score high; distant ones near zero.

    r=αs+(1−α)PTr

    s spreads weight equally over confirmed fraud accounts. P moves the walker to each neighbour with equal chance. r is every account's score, found by repeating the right-hand side until it stops changing. Small α spreads suspicion far; large α keeps it near the seeds. Splitting by link count stops hubs spreading suspicion: a busy merchant passes only a sliver to each customer.

    The simulation shows the power and the danger. The power: one confirmed account yields a ranked review list no single-account model would produce. The danger: the answer depends on which links you include and how far suspicion spreads. And real truth labels come from investigators, late, and only for accounts someone looked at (F3.5, F2.6).

    To go beyond hand-built walks, learn from the graph:

    • Graph features into a standard model. Per account: link count, confirmed-bad neighbours, component size, in a cycle or not, the personalised score, neighbours' average features. Feed to gradient boosting (F4.4). Cheap, explainable, often the strongest practical choice.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Like asking your friends for opinions before deciding. Each node collects its neighbours' vectors, averages them, mixes with its own and transforms. After k layers it summarises its k-hop neighbourhood. It is the attention idea (F5.5) restricted to links.
    hv(k+1)=σ(W1hv(k)+W2mean{hu(k):u∈neighbours of v})

    hv is the vector for account v at layer k; W1, W2 are learned matrices; σ is a non-linearity (GraphSAGE form). Two or three layers let an account "know" accounts two or three links away. Training predicts fraud labels from the final vectors (F5.1).

    • Transactions are edges. Edge-classification predicts whether a payment is part of a laundering chain, which often fits better than labelling accounts.
    • Time must be respected. Use only edges that existed before the prediction date. "Confirmed-fraud neighbours" computed today quietly includes labels confirmed next month. This is the point-in-time rule (F3.1) and the commonest way graph models look better than they are.
    • Hubs. Neighbourhoods explode through hubs, so systems sample a fixed number of neighbours and treat high-degree nodes (payroll, merchants) specially.
    • Splitting. A random split puts test accounts' labelled neighbours in training. Split by time or by connected group (F3.5, F4.5).
    • Embeddings. node2vec learns a vector per node by predicting which nodes co-occur on random walks. Use it for clustering, similarity search or features (F5.7).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Random train and test split on a time series

      Next week's rows train the model tested on last week. Error looks superb, then collapses live.

      Spot itIs every test date later than every training date? Ask for a rolling-origin result.
    • No baseline

      "MAPE is 8%" means nothing if seasonal naive scores 8.5%.

      Spot itAsk for naive and seasonal naive on the same windows, and a MASE or skill score.
    • One test window

      A 28-day test with one holiday and one lucky week decides the winner. Simulation 1 shows close methods swapping places.

      Spot itHow many windows were tested, and how does the ranking vary?
    • A single number with no horizon or interval

      "We forecast liquidity to 3% error", with no stated horizon or uncertainty.

      Spot itHow many days ahead, what is the 80% or 95% range, and does coverage hold in backtests?
    • Features that use the future

      A centred rolling average, a holiday flag built after the event, or a lag not yet available at forecast time.

      Spot itFor each feature: would I have known this at 08:00 on the forecast date? Error below the noise floor is a red flag.
    • Moving holidays treated as fixed

      Month-of-year used for Ramadan and Eid, so the peak lands on the wrong dates each year. Or one national calendar for all countries.

      Spot itPlot residuals around Eid and Ramadan per year. Which calendar and weekend does the model use?
    • Edges that merge everything

      A public IP, corporate account or popular merchant joins unrelated customers, and the "ring" is a million-node component.

      Spot itCheck component sizes and top-degree nodes. Cap or remove hubs and re-check.
    • Graph leakage through time and labels

      Neighbour features use edges or fraud labels that arrived after the prediction date. AUC looks superb.

      Spot itRebuild features as of each prediction date. A sharp fall means it was leaking.
    • Guilt by association applied as proof

      An account is frozen because a neighbour was flagged, with no independent evidence. Family, landlords and merchants get caught.

      Spot itTreat graph scores as leads. Check alerts across customer groups for concentration on a nationality or household type.
    • A GNN where a table would do

      A complex model whose gain over boosted trees on a few graph features is within noise, and far harder to explain to a validator.

      Spot itAsk for the boosted-trees-plus-graph-features baseline on the same time-ordered test, with intervals.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. Why is a random 80/20 split wrong for a daily cash-demand series, and what would you do instead?

    Neighbouring days are correlated, so training contains days next to every test day and the model effectively interpolates answers. Use rolling origin at the production horizon, with preprocessing fitted on training data only.

    2. A balance is 6 units above its usual level and follows an AR(1) with φ = 0.9 and shock size 1. What is the 10-day-ahead forecast and 80% interval half-width?

    Forecast = 6 × 0.910 ≈ 2.09. Variance = (1 − 0.920) ÷ (1 − 0.81) ≈ 4.62, so standard deviation ≈ 2.15 and half-width = 1.28 × 2.15 ≈ 2.76. Interval: about −0.7 to 4.8.

    3. φ is 0.95. How many days until half of today's gap is forecast to close?

    0.95h = 0.5, so h = ln 0.5 ÷ ln 0.95 ≈ 13.5 days. At φ = 0.5 it is one day; at φ = 1 it never closes.

    4. Your model's mean absolute error is AED 30,000; seasonal naive's is AED 40,000. What are the improvement and MASE, and what else must you check?

    Error is 75% of seasonal naive's: a 25% improvement (MASE 0.75 if scaled by seasonal naive error; conventional MASE uses the naive in-sample error, so state which). Also check several windows, the production horizon, and the gap to the noise floor.

    5. A person owns 40% of A; A owns 50% of B; B owns 70% of C; they also hold 8% of C directly. What is their effective stake in C, and would a 25% threshold catch it?

    Chain: 0.40 × 0.50 × 0.70 = 14%. Direct: 8%. Total 22%, under 25%. Ownership alone would not trigger it, but control tests (voting rights, board appointments) still apply.

    6. In Simulation 3 you lower α from 0.3 to 0.05 and suspicion on innocent accounts rises. Why, and what does that say about choosing α?

    α is the chance the walker restarts at a seed. Lower α means longer walks, so suspicion crosses weak bridges (the landlord, a shared device) into ordinary accounts. High α stays local: fewer false leads, but distant members are missed. Choose α on a time-ordered set with known outcomes and your review capacity in mind, like any threshold.

    7. A graph model reaches AUC 0.97 for mule accounts. What do you ask before believing it?

    Were features and labels built as of each prediction date? Was the split by time and connected group? What happens with hubs capped? How does it compare with boosted trees on simple graph features, with an interval? How many confirmed positives are in the test set?
