    Time Series and Graphs: Forecasting What Comes Next, and Seeing Who Is Connected to Whom

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most ML assumes each row stands alone. Two kinds of banking data break that. In a time series, cash taken from an ATM today resembles yesterday's and jumps on salary day. In a graph, two accounts sharing a device and a flow of money are not independent customers.

    Use the dependence and you gain strong signal. Ignore it and validation lies, because test data is not really separate from training data.

    Hold onto thisBefore modelling, ask what the dependence is. Series: what repeats, and how far ahead must we see? Graph: what counts as a link, and which way does suspicion flow?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What makes a time series different

    Think of a daily series as level and trend, seasonality (weekly peaks, salary day), calendar events and noise, added together. Noise sets a floor under every forecast. Ramadan and Eid move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, so use Hijri-calendar features and one calendar per country (weekends differ between Saudi Arabia and the UAE).

    Forecasting means estimating each piece from history, extending it, and adding the pieces back up. The enabling fact is autocorrelation: today correlates with yesterday and with the same weekday last week. That is what makes forecasting possible, and also what breaks ordinary validation, because neighbouring rows are near-copies of each other.

    Two practical points. If swings grow as volumes grow, model percentage changes (or take logs) rather than raw amounts, otherwise the weekly pattern looks different every year. And series drift and break: a new payment rail or a pricing change alters the process itself, so plan to re-fit on a schedule rather than once.

    The forecasting toolbox, and why simple baselines win

    Climb this ladder only when a rung beats the one below.

    MethodWhat it does
    Seasonal naiveRepeat the value from one season ago. The baseline every model must beat.
    Exponential smoothing, ARIMAClassic per-series models. Smoothing is a weighted average that favours recent days; ARIMA mixes past values and past shocks. Cheap, give intervals, but weak with many holidays and need one fit per series.
    Regression on calendar featuresTrend, weekday, salary-day and holiday flags. Transparent, and the features must be knowable for future dates.
    Gradient boosting on lagsOne global model over thousands of ATMs or branches, fed lagged values, calendar flags and series identity. Learns shared patterns, but cannot extrapolate a trend by itself, so detrend first or add a trend term.
    Deep and foundation modelsSequence networks, or pretrained models that forecast with no training on your data (R9). Often only match tuned simple models, and are harder to audit.

    Simple methods stay competitive because once trend, weekly pattern and known events are captured, the rest is mostly randomness. The knobs that matter are the season length (7 days, 12 months), how much history to use (old regimes can hurt) and how you handle holidays. Ask how much better than seasonal naive? MASE answers it: 1.0 means no better, 0.7 means 30% better (F7: forecast metrics).

    Gains come from encoding calendar knowledge, not a cleverer algorithm: a holiday and salary-day table, built once and reused across every series. You also cannot beat the noise floor. An error below it means something leaked from the future.

    Validating a forecast, and how uncertainty grows

    Never shuffle. A random split puts next Tuesday in training and last Monday in test, so neighbours leak the answer. Use rolling origin: train to a date, forecast the next block, move forward, repeat. Test at the real horizon: if treasury needs 14 days ahead every morning, score exactly that, over several windows including Ramadan and year-end.

    Report an interval, not a single number: "AED 12.4m, 80% likely between 11.1m and 13.7m" lets treasury size its buffer. A balance that drifts back to its usual level has a band that stops widening; a random walk fans out for ever. Intervals are only trustworthy if they are checked: an 80% band should contain about 80% of actual outcomes in backtests. Too few means overconfidence.

    Horizon matters as much as method: one-day and 30-day error are different problems, so state the horizon beside every accuracy figure. Random walks such as exchange rates defeat clever models, because the best forecast is today's value. A structural break makes backtests across it mislead (F7.7).

    Graphs: turning relationships into data

    A graph has nodes (accounts, devices, companies) and edges (sent money to, shares a phone with, owns 40% of). It reveals structure that single rows hide: fraud rings sharing devices, mule networks where a collector receives from many senders and forwards within hours, and ownership chains. Decide what an edge is first: a public Wi-Fi hotspot links thousands of strangers. Also decide whether edges carry weights (amount, count, recency) and direction (who paid whom), because a mule pattern is directional and old edges should fade.

    Classic graph analytics: components, hubs and ownership

    • Connected components. Groups of reachable accounts. 40 accounts is worth a look; 2 million means your edges are too loose.
    • Degree. Link count. A collector mule has high in-degree, but so does a merchant, so degree is a lead, not a verdict.
    • Motifs. Cycles, fan-in, fan-out and fast pass-through chains look like layering.

    Ownership chains. A person's effective stake multiplies along each path and adds across paths, so two stakes each under 25% can total over it. Thresholds like this drive B3: customer due diligence.

    Graph machine learning: propagating suspicion

    Guilt by association means neighbours of a confirmed bad node are more likely bad. A personalised random walk starts on a confirmed fraud account, follows random links and sometimes jumps back. Time spent on each account is its suspicion score. A low restart probability (α) spreads suspicion far; a high α keeps it close.

    One confirmed account yields a ranked review list, but the answer depends on which links you include and how far suspicion spreads.

    • Graph features into a standard model (link count, confirmed-bad neighbours, walk score) is cheap, explainable and often the strongest practical choice.
    • Graph neural networks let each account blend in its neighbours' information: each layer averages in one more hop, so after two or three layers an account knows about accounts two or three links away. They can learn patterns you did not hand-code, but need more labelled data, more engineering and are harder to explain.
    • Cap hubs. Payroll and merchant accounts have thousands of neighbours and swamp everything. Sample or down-weight them.
    • Respect time. Use only edges and labels that existed before the prediction date, and split by time or connected group.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Time-series methods are strong at:

    • Capacity and liquidity planning (ATM cash, call-centre volume, branch footfall), because repeating weekly, salary-day and holiday patterns are most of the signal.
    • Many series at once. A global boosted model shares what it learns across thousands of ATMs, so a new or short series borrows strength from similar ones.
    • Honest uncertainty. Intervals let treasury hold a buffer sized to the risk, not to a single guess.

    Time-series methods are weak at:

    • Random-walk series such as exchange rates and share prices, where nothing beats today's value.
    • Long horizons and structural breaks. Past patterns stop applying after a new rail, a regulation or a pandemic, and backtests across the break mislead.
    • Very short or very sparse series, where there is too little history to separate pattern from noise. Pool them or fall back on a group average.

    Graph methods are strong at:

    • Collusion that rows cannot show. Rings, mule collectors and shell chains are defined by connections, so only a graph sees them.
    • Few labels. Propagation from a handful of confirmed cases gives a ranked review list before you have enough data to train a classifier.
    • Ownership and control, where percentages must be multiplied along chains and added across paths.

    Graph methods are weak at:

    • Sloppy edges. One public IP or merchant hub merges unrelated customers.
    • Guilt as proof. Neighbours include family, landlords and shared workplaces, so scores are leads for an investigator, not grounds to freeze an account.
    • Leakage and cost. Edges and labels must be rebuilt as of each prediction date, and large graphs need real engineering.
    OptionPick it whenAvoid when

    Exponential smoothing / ARIMAA handful of series, clear seasonality, you need quick intervals and easy explanation.Thousands of series, many moving holidays, or rich extra inputs.

    In practice. For forecasting, start with seasonal naive, then calendar regression, then boosting only if it wins across several rolling windows. Hundreds of daily observations per series is plenty for the simple models; global models like thousands of series. Training takes minutes on a laptop, and the real effort goes into the holiday table and the backtest set-up. Banks use this for ATM cash, deposit and liquidity forecasts, call volumes and expected transaction volumes for capacity.

    For graphs, build the graph from the cleanest links you have (shared device, direct transfer), compute simple features, and feed them to the model you already trust. Try a walk score with moderate α for ranking alerts. Move to a GNN only if those features plateau. Banks use this for mule and ring detection in fraud and AML, and for beneficial-ownership checks in due diligence.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Random split on a time series

      Error looks superb, then collapses live.

      Spot itIs every test date later than every training date?
    • No baseline, or one lucky window

      "MAPE is 8%" means nothing if seasonal naive scores 8.5%.

      Spot itAsk for seasonal naive on several windows.
    • Features that use the future

      A centred rolling average, or graph neighbours labelled after the prediction date.

      Spot itWould I have known this on the forecast date? Error below the noise floor is a red flag.
    • Edges that merge everything, and guilt as proof

      A public IP joins unrelated customers into a huge "ring", and freezing accounts because a neighbour was flagged catches family and landlords.

      Spot itCheck component sizes and top-degree nodes. Treat graph scores as leads.
    • A single number with no horizon or interval

      "3% error" says nothing without the days ahead or the likely range.

      Spot itHow many days ahead, and what is the 80% range? Does it cover about 80% in backtests?
    • Moving holidays treated as fixed

      Month-of-year stands in for Ramadan and Eid, or one calendar serves every country.

      Spot itPlot residuals around Eid for each year.
    • Graph leakage

      Neighbour features use labels that arrived after the prediction date, so the model looks better than it is.

      Spot itRebuild features as of each prediction date and split by time and connected group.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is a random 80/20 split wrong for a daily cash-demand series?

    Neighbouring days are correlated, so the model effectively interpolates answers. Use rolling origin at the production horizon.

    2. A balance is well above its usual level today. How does the forecast look over the next month?

    If persistent, it fades back slowly; if weakly persistent, it snaps back within days. In a random walk it never fades.

    3. Your model beats seasonal naive by 25% on one window. What else do you check?

    Whether the gain holds across several windows, including Ramadan, at the production horizon. One window may be luck.

    4. Lowering α in Simulation 3 puts more suspicion on innocent accounts. Why?

    Longer walks cross weak bridges such as the landlord. High α stays local: fewer false leads, but distant members are missed.

    5. A graph model reaches AUC 0.97 for mule accounts. What do you ask first?

    Were features and labels built as of each prediction date, and was the split by time and connected group? Compare with boosted trees on simple graph features.

    6. You must forecast cash for 3,000 ATMs, most with two years of history. Would you fit ARIMA per ATM or one global boosted model, and why?

    Start with seasonal naive as the bar. Then I would favour a global boosted model: it shares calendar effects across ATMs, handles new ATMs and needs one pipeline, not 3,000 fits. I would still check it against ARIMA or calendar regression on rolling windows, and keep whichever wins at the production horizon.

    7. You have 30 confirmed mule accounts and a new graph. Would you go straight to a graph neural network or start with graph features and a walk score? Why?

    Start with graph features and a walk score. With 30 labels a GNN is likely to overfit, while simple features are cheap, explainable to investigators and a strong baseline. Move to a GNN only if they plateau and you have more labels.
