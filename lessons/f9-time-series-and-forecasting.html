    Time Series and Graphs: Forecasting What Comes Next, and Seeing Who Is Connected to Whom

    Why a series with a calendar needs different handling from a table of customers, how to beat the naive forecast honestly and say how wrong you might be, and how fraud rings, mule networks and ownership chains become something a model can read.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTime series and graphs are the two cases where rows are not independent: in a series each value leans on the ones before it, in a graph each account leans on its neighbours, so you must split, validate and explain with that dependence in mind, and you gain a great deal of signal when you use it properly.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most of the machine learning you have met so far assumes a table in which each row stands alone. Shuffle the rows and nothing changes. Two kinds of banking data break that assumption, and both are everywhere.

    Time series. The cash taken from a Dubai Mall ATM today looks like yesterday's, and like the same weekday last week, and jumps on salary day. The order carries information. If you shuffle the days you destroy it. Forecasting means using the past to say something about days that have not happened, and honestly saying how unsure you are.

    Graphs. Two accounts that share a phone, a device and a flow of money are not two independent customers. One confirmed mule account makes its neighbours suspicious. A company owned 60% by a holding company that is owned 50% by an individual is partly that individual's, even though their name appears nowhere on the company record. The links carry information that no single row contains.

    Both topics are about dependence. In a series, today depends on the past. In a graph, a node depends on its neighbours. Handle the dependence and you get strong signal: a seasonal pattern that cuts forecast error by half, or a ring of fraud accounts that no single-account feature could see. Ignore it and your validation lies, because the test set is not really separate from the training set.

    Hold onto thisBefore modelling, ask what the dependence is. For a series: what repeats (weekly, monthly, around Ramadan and Eid) and how far ahead must we see? For a graph: what counts as a link (shared device, money moved, shared director), and which direction does suspicion flow? Those two questions decide the method far more than the choice of algorithm.

    Here is the plan. Part 2 takes forecasting first, with two simulations: one that races simple methods on ATM cash demand, and one that shows why forecast uncertainty widens with the horizon. It then turns to graphs, with a simulation in which you mark a few accounts as fraud and watch suspicion spread through a network. Parts 3 to 7 connect it to the rest of the curriculum, to the live research, to the failure modes, and to what to say in a meeting.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    What makes a time series different

    Start by taking a series apart. A daily series of ATM withdrawals is usually a sum of a few simple things:

    • Level and trend. Where the series sits and where it is drifting. Customer growth, inflation, a new branch.
    • Seasonality. Patterns that repeat on a fixed period. Weekly (Thursday and Friday peaks), monthly (salary day), yearly (summer travel).
    • Calendar events. Dates that do not repeat evenly. Public holidays, the days before Eid, a national day sale. In the Gulf these matter a lot, and some move: Ramadan and Eid follow the Islamic calendar, which shifts about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year. A model that only knows "month of year" will learn Ramadan as a particular month, then be wrong by ten days the next year. Give it Hijri-calendar features instead. The weekend also differs: Friday–Saturday in Saudi Arabia, Saturday–Sunday in the UAE (which moved in 2022), so a regional model needs the right calendar per country.
    • Noise. What is left. It sets a floor under every forecast: no method can predict the part that is genuinely random.

    The second key idea is autocorrelation: today's value correlates with yesterday's, last week's, last month's. It is why forecasting is possible at all, and why the usual validation tricks break (Part 2, validation, below). A series whose statistical behaviour does not change over time is called stationary. Most real series are not: they trend, their volatility changes, they have structural breaks (a new payment rail launches and card volumes shift). Classical methods handle this by differencing (modelling the change rather than the level) or by modelling trend and seasonality explicitly. Modern methods handle it by being given the right features and being re-fitted often.

    Think of the pieces as a recipe you can write in a line:

    yt=trend(t)+weekday effect+calendar effects+εt

    The additive form. When swings grow with the level (a bigger branch has bigger swings), a multiplicative form, or a log transform that turns it into an additive one, fits better. Forecasting is then: estimate each piece from history, extend each into the future, add them up.

    The reason simple methods stay competitive is the noise floor. Once a method captures the trend, the weekly pattern and the known events, what remains is mostly randomness, and no model can forecast randomness. Extra complexity then buys nothing and adds variance. The honest question is always: how much better than seasonal naive? The usual way to say this is a scaled error such as MASE, the model's mean absolute error divided by the error of the naive method on the training data, so that 1.0 means "no better than naive" and 0.7 means "30% better" (F7: forecast metrics builds this out properly).

    The next simulation lets you run the race. The data is an invented ATM's daily cash demand with a known recipe: a gentle upward trend, a weekday pattern peaking Thursday and Friday, and a salary-day bump on two days of every 28, plus random noise you control. Each method sees only the first 84 days and forecasts the next 28.

    Three lessons sit in that simulation.

    • Seasonal naive is a serious baseline. It already captures the weekly shape. It is beaten mainly because it copies one noisy week and cannot extend a trend.
    • The gain comes from encoding what you know about the calendar, not from a cleverer algorithm. The payday term lifts the error towards the noise floor because it explains the two big spikes in the test window. In real work this is the holiday and salary-day table, built once and reused.
    • You cannot go below the floor. Anyone who reports an error below the noise level has leaked something from the future (F3.1).

    A last practical point. Banks forecast thousands of series at once, ATM by ATM, branch by branch, product by product, and the totals must add up: the sum of the ATM forecasts should equal the regional forecast. Hierarchical reconciliation methods adjust forecasts across levels so they are coherent. It is a standard topic in the forecasting literature and, in practice, a frequent request from finance teams who dislike numbers that do not add.

    Validating a forecast, and how uncertainty grows with the horizon

    Never shuffle. A random split puts next Tuesday in training and last Monday in test. Because neighbouring days are correlated, the model effectively sees the answers. The honest design is rolling origin evaluation: train up to a date, forecast the next block, move the date forward, repeat (F4.5). That gives several test blocks, so you see how error varies between periods, including through Ramadan and summer. Fit all preprocessing, including scaling and any holiday-effect estimates, on the training window only. And match the test to the real use: if the treasury team needs a 14-day cash forecast issued every morning, evaluate exactly that, not a one-step-ahead score.

    The second honest practice is to report an interval, not a single number. A forecast of AED 12.4m with no range invites false precision. A statement such as "AED 12.4m, 80% likely between 11.1m and 13.7m" lets a treasury desk decide how much buffer to hold. Intervals should widen the further ahead you look, because errors accumulate. How fast they widen depends on how persistent the series is, and that is worth seeing once with the simplest possible model.

    Take a balance that tends to return to its usual level but is pushed around by daily shocks. The standard model is an AR(1): each day's deviation from the usual level is a fraction φ of yesterday's, plus a fresh shock. If today is d0 above the usual level, then h days ahead:

    forecast=φhd0,variance of error=σ21−φ2h1−φ2

    σ is the size of a daily shock. The forecast decays towards the usual level at rate φ per day. The error variance grows from σ² on day one and levels off at σ²/(1 − φ²). When φ is exactly 1 (a random walk) there is no usual level to return to, the forecast stays at today's value, and the variance grows without limit: hσ². An 80% interval is the forecast ± 1.28 standard deviations of the error.

    What to take from this:

    • Horizon matters as much as method. One-day-ahead error and 30-day-ahead error are different problems with different answers. Always state the horizon next to any accuracy figure.
    • Persistent series are forecastable far ahead but with growing doubt (balances, deposits, gradual trends). Mean-reverting series are forecastable only near-term and then collapse to the average (a daily spike in transactions).
    • Random walks defeat clever models. Exchange rates and many prices behave this way. The best forecast is today's value, and anyone who promises more should show out-of-sample evidence. See conformal prediction (R8) for intervals that make fewer assumptions.
    • Treat structural breaks as a first-class risk. A new instant-payment rail, a pricing change or a regulatory limit changes the process itself. Backtests that straddle a break flatter or punish the model unfairly. Watch for drift (F7.7) and re-fit on a schedule.

    Graphs: turning relationships into data

    A graph has nodes (things: accounts, customers, devices, companies, merchants) and edges (relationships: sent money to, shares a phone with, is a director of, owns 40% of). Edges can be directed, weighted and typed, and can carry timestamps. Banks already hold the raw material in relational tables. The graph view is a different way to query and model it, and it matters because some questions are about structure rather than about single rows:

    • Fraud rings. Several accounts open within days, sharing a device or an address, moving money among themselves in cycles before cashing out.
    • Mule networks. A collector account that receives from many unrelated senders and forwards money on within hours. Individually each account looks ordinary. The pattern only shows in the shape: many in, one out.
    • Ownership chains. Who ultimately owns or controls a corporate customer, through layers of holding companies, in several jurisdictions.
    • Counterparty and contagion risk. Who depends on whom for payment or supply, and what happens if one fails.

    Always start by deciding what an edge is. "Shares a device" and "sent more than AED 5,000 in the last 30 days" give different graphs from the same data, and the choice drives everything downstream. A device shared by a family is a legitimate edge; a public Wi-Fi hotspot or a corporate payment hub is a hub that links thousands of unrelated people and floods the graph with false connections.

    Classic graph analytics: components, hubs, flows and ownership

    Before any machine learning, a lot is answered by direct computation, and these are often what a bank actually runs.

    • Connected components. The groups of accounts reachable from each other. A component of 40 accounts sharing a handful of devices is worth a look. A component of 2 million is not a ring, it means your edge definition is too loose and has merged everything through a hub.
    • Degree and centrality. How many links a node has, and how central it is. A collector mule has a high in-degree and a low balance at day end. A very high-degree node can equally be a legitimate merchant or payroll account, so degree alone is a lead, not a verdict.
    • Motifs. Small recurring shapes: a cycle A→B→C→A, a fan-in, a fan-out, a chain that passes money through quickly. Layering in money laundering looks like these shapes, with timing.
    • Community detection. Algorithms that find densely linked groups. Useful for segmenting a customer network or splitting a giant component into candidate rings.
    • Random-walk scores. PageRank, and its personalised version below, which measures how much suspicion or influence flows from some starting nodes to every other node.

    Ownership chains are a clean example of structure that no row-level feature captures. The effective stake a person holds in a company is the product of the percentages along each path from person to company, and you add the products over different paths:

    effective stake=∑paths∏links on the pathownership share

    Example below: the person owns 60% of Holdco A. A owns 50% of Holdco B. B owns 80% of Operating Co C. The person also holds 5% of C directly.

    Path from the person to CCalculationEffective stake
    Through A, then B0.60 × 0.50 × 0.8024%
    Direct0.055%
    Total24% + 5%29%

    Each path alone is under 25% and the total is over it. Beneficial-ownership rules in many jurisdictions use thresholds of this kind (25% is a common figure; check the bank's policy and the current rule for the exact local threshold and the control-based tests that apply regardless of percentage). A system that only looks at the first link, or at each path alone, misses the person. The same traversal handles cycles (A owns B owns A), which need care to avoid counting forever, and nominee structures where the percentage is not the whole story and control is exercised through other means. This is the engine of the checks in B3: customer due diligence.

    Graph machine learning: propagating suspicion, then learning to

    The most useful graph idea for fraud is guilt by association: the neighbours of a confirmed bad node are more likely to be bad. A clean way to make that precise is a personalised random walk. Imagine a walker who stands on a confirmed fraud account, and at every step either jumps back to a confirmed fraud account (with probability α) or follows a random link to a neighbour (with probability 1 − α). The share of time the walker spends on each account is its score. Accounts that are close to confirmed fraud, and linked to it through many routes, score high. Accounts a long way off score near zero.

    r=αs+(1−α)PTr

    s puts equal weight on the confirmed fraud accounts. P says, for each account, that the walker moves to each neighbour with equal chance (one over the account's number of links). r is the resulting score for every account. You solve it by repeating the right-hand side until the scores stop changing. A small α lets suspicion spread far; a large α keeps it close to the seeds. Dividing by the number of links is what stops a hub from being treated as strongly connected to everyone: a busy merchant passes only a small slice of suspicion to each customer.

    The simulation shows both the power and the danger of graph methods. The power: from a single confirmed account, you get a ranked list of other accounts to review, which no single-account model would have produced. The dangers are the ones you just pressed: the answer depends on which links you include and how far you let suspicion spread. Real graphs add a third: nobody gives you the truth labels shown in the picture. They come from investigators, late, and only for accounts someone looked at (F3.5, F2.6).

    A step up from hand-built walks is to learn from the graph. Two families dominate.

    • Graph features into a standard model. Compute, for each account, things such as number of links, number of confirmed-bad neighbours, size of its component, whether it sits in a cycle, the personalised score above, and the average of its neighbours' features. Feed them to gradient boosting (F4.4). This is cheap, explainable and often the strongest practical approach.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Each node starts with its own feature vector. In every layer, a node collects its neighbours' vectors, averages or sums them, mixes them with its own, and applies a learned transformation. After k layers, a node's representation summarises its k-hop neighbourhood. This is the same "lookup what is relevant" idea as attention (F5.5), restricted to the graph's links.
    hv(k+1)=σ(W1hv(k)+W2mean{hu(k):u∈neighbours of v})

    hv is the vector describing account v at layer k. W1 and W2 are learned matrices, and σ is a non-linearity. This is the GraphSAGE form. Stack two or three layers and an account "knows" about accounts two or three links away. Training is as usual: predict fraud labels from the final vectors, and backpropagate (F5.1).

    Things that matter in practice:

    • Transactions are edges, not nodes. In payments the object of interest is often the transfer. Edge-classification variants predict whether an edge is suspicious, which fits "this payment is part of a laundering chain" better than labelling accounts.
    • Time must be respected. When you predict on a given day, only edges that existed before it may be used. A feature such as "number of confirmed-fraud neighbours" computed today will quietly include labels that were only confirmed next month. This is the point-in-time rule (F3.1) applied to graphs, and it is the most common way graph models look better than they are.
    • Scale and hubs. Neighbourhoods explode through hubs; practical systems sample a fixed number of neighbours per node. They also treat high-degree nodes specially, because a payroll or merchant account touches everyone.
    • Splitting is tricky. Neighbouring nodes share information, so a random split of accounts lets test accounts' neighbours (with their labels) sit in training. Split by time, or by connected group, as in F3.5 and F4.5.
    • Embeddings. Methods such as node2vec learn a vector for each node by training a model to predict which nodes appear together on random walks. These vectors can be used for clustering, similarity search, or as features, and tie into self-supervised learning (F5.7).

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Liquidity and cash. Forecasting deposit outflows, intraday liquidity needs and regulatory ratios feeds treasury and asset-liability management (B5). Horizon and interval width are the whole conversation.
    • ATM and branch operations. Cash demand per ATM, replenishment routes, staffing. Calendar effects, including Ramadan, Eid and salary days, dominate. A few thousand series, daily, with a cost for both running out of cash and tying up too much.
    • Contact centre and digital. Call volumes, chat, app logins and payment peaks by half hour for staffing and capacity.
    • Fraud and scams. Mule networks and rings on instant-payment rails (A9), synthetic identities that share attributes (A9), and card fraud with shared merchants and devices (B1).
    • AML and KYC. Network views of transaction monitoring alerts (B2), and corporate ownership traversal for beneficial owners (B3).
    • Credit and risk. Macroeconomic scenarios and portfolio loss forecasts are time series; supply-chain and group-exposure links between corporate borrowers are graphs.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Forecasting is a mature field whose live argument is whether pretrained models have changed the picture. Graph machine learning has strong research and uneven production evidence, and the argument there is whether neural networks add much over well-built graph features.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Random train and test split on a time series

      Next week's rows train the model that is tested on last week. Error looks superb, then collapses live.

      Spot itAsk whether every test date is later than every training date. Ask for a rolling-origin result.
    • No baseline

      "MAPE is 8%." Against a seasonal naive forecast that scores 8.5%, the model adds nothing.

      Spot itAsk for naive and seasonal naive on the same windows. Look for MASE or a skill score.
    • One test window

      A 28-day test that includes one holiday and one lucky week decides the winner. Simulation 1 shows close methods swapping places.

      Spot itAsk how many windows were tested and how the ranking varies across them.
    • A single number with no horizon or interval

      "We forecast liquidity to 3% error", with no statement of how far ahead or how uncertain.

      Spot itAsk: how many days ahead, and what is the 80% or 95% range? Does coverage hold in backtests?
    • Features that use the future

      A rolling average centred on the day, a holiday flag built from data known only after the event, or a lag that is not yet available at forecast time.

      Spot itFor each feature ask: would I have known this at 08:00 on the forecast date? An error below the noise floor is a red flag.
    • Moving holidays treated as fixed

      Month-of-year used for Ramadan and Eid, so the peak lands on the wrong dates each year. Or one national calendar used for all countries.

      Spot itPlot residuals around Eid and Ramadan in each year. Ask which calendar and which weekend the model uses.
    • Edges that merge everything

      A shared public IP, a corporate account or a popular merchant joins unrelated customers. The "ring" is a component of a million nodes.

      Spot itLook at the component size distribution and the top-degree nodes. Cap or remove hubs and check the result.
    • Graph leakage through time and labels

      Neighbour features computed with edges or fraud labels that arrived after the prediction date. AUC looks superb.

      Spot itRebuild the features as of each prediction date. If performance falls sharply, the graph was leaking.
    • Guilt by association applied as proof

      A customer is declined or an account frozen because a neighbour was flagged, with no independent evidence. Family members, landlords and merchants get caught.

      Spot itTreat graph scores as leads for review. Check fairness across customer groups and whether alerts concentrate on a nationality or a household type.
    • A GNN where a table would do

      A complex model whose gain over boosted trees on a few graph features is within noise, but which is far harder to explain to a validator.

      Spot itAsk for the boosted-trees-plus-graph-features baseline, scored on the same time-ordered test with intervals.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our ATM cash demand is mostly calendar: weekly peaks, salary days, and the weeks around Eid. A forecast that simply repeats last week is our benchmark. The new model knows the calendar, and cuts the average miss from about AED 36,000 to AED 24,000 per machine per day. I'd show you that with a range, because even a perfect model has noise it cannot predict, and on the days before Eid I'd hold a larger buffer. For the fraud network work, we find accounts linked to a confirmed bad one through shared devices and payments. That produces a ranked list for investigators, not a decision to freeze anyone.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For forecasting I'd benchmark against seasonal naive, then regression with calendar and Hijri-event features and a global gradient-boosted model, evaluated by rolling origin at the production horizon, reporting MASE and interval coverage. Pretrained models would be an extra baseline. For graphs I'd define edges deliberately, cap hubs, build features as of each prediction date, and compare boosted trees with graph features against a GNN on a time-ordered split, splitting by connected group so neighbours do not leak. Outputs are leads with reason codes, and I'd audit alert rates across customer groups.”
    “Why not just use a deep learning model for the cash forecast?”

    We can test it, but the evidence says calendar-aware regression and boosted trees are very hard to beat on this kind of series, and the remaining error is mostly noise. I'd put the deep or pretrained model into the same rolling backtest against seasonal naive and the tuned baseline. If it wins by more than the variation between windows, we use it; if not, the simpler model is cheaper and easier to explain to a validator.

    “How accurate will the liquidity forecast be?”

    It depends on the horizon. Next-day is tight; 30 days out the band is several times wider, because uncertainty accumulates. I'd state the accuracy by horizon with a backtested interval, for example “80% of the time the outcome lies within this range”, and check that the range holds up historically. A single accuracy percentage with no horizon is not a useful promise.

    “Ramadan moved and the forecast missed the peak. What went wrong?”

    Almost certainly the model learned the peak as a calendar month. The Islamic calendar shifts about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, so a month-based feature is wrong by roughly ten days. The fix is Hijri-calendar features: days to Eid, Ramadan week, the pre-Eid cash-up period. And we should backtest across several years so at least two or three Ramadans are in the test.

    “Can graph analytics find money laundering?”

    It finds structures that are often associated with it: layered cycles, fan-in and fan-out, shared devices across unrelated customers. It produces leads, and confirmed cases are few and labelled late, so results need intervals and an investigator in the loop. Public benchmarks are mostly synthetic, so I would not quote their numbers as what we'd get here. A first step is usually simple graph queries and features feeding the existing model, then a GNN only if it beats that.

    “Isn't it unfair to flag someone because of who they are connected to?”

    It can be. Family, landlords and workplaces are legitimate links, and the same person may sit in several of them. So the score should only prioritise review, never trigger action alone, and should show which links drove it. We should check outcomes across customer groups, and take extra care with link types that proxy for protected characteristics. Our privacy and fairness requirements (G2) set the limits.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. Why is a random 80/20 split wrong for a daily cash-demand series, and what would you do instead?

    Neighbouring days are correlated, so a random split puts days adjacent to each test day into training, and the model effectively interpolates the answers. Use a rolling-origin split: train up to a date, forecast the next block at the production horizon, move forward, repeat, and fit preprocessing on training data only.

    2. A balance is 6 units above its usual level and follows an AR(1) with φ = 0.9 and shock size 1. What is the forecast for 10 days ahead, and what is the 80% interval half-width?

    Forecast = 6 × 0.910 = 6 × 0.3487 ≈ 2.09. Error variance = (1 − 0.920) ÷ (1 − 0.81) = (1 − 0.1216) ÷ 0.19 ≈ 4.62, so the standard deviation is about 2.15 and the 80% half-width is 1.28 × 2.15 ≈ 2.76. The interval is roughly −0.7 to 4.8.

    3. φ is 0.95. How many days until half of today's gap is forecast to close?

    Solve 0.95h = 0.5: h = ln 0.5 ÷ ln 0.95 = −0.693 ÷ −0.0513 ≈ 13.5 days. At φ = 0.5 it is one day, and at φ = 1 it never closes.

    4. Your model's mean absolute error is AED 30,000. Seasonal naive, over the same period, has AED 40,000. What are the relative improvement and the MASE, and what must you also check?

    The model's error is 75% of seasonal naive's, an improvement of 25% (a MASE of 0.75 if the scale is the seasonal naive error on the same data; the conventional MASE uses the naive in-sample error, so state which you used). Also check the error across several windows, at the production horizon, and compare it with the noise floor to see how much headroom exists.

    5. Someone's ownership chain: a person owns 40% of A; A owns 50% of B; B owns 70% of C; the person also holds 8% of C directly. What is their effective stake in C, and would a 25% threshold catch it?

    Through the chain: 0.40 × 0.50 × 0.70 = 14%. Direct: 8%. Total = 22%, below a 25% ownership threshold. Ownership alone would not trigger it, but control tests (voting rights, board appointment, other arrangements) still apply, and a customer with this structure may justify enhanced review for other reasons.

    6. In Simulation 3 you lower α from 0.3 to 0.05 and the share of suspicion on innocent accounts rises. Why, and what does that say about choosing α?

    α is the chance the walker restarts at a seed. Lower α means longer walks, so suspicion travels further, across the weak bridges (the landlord, the shared device) into the ordinary part of the network. A high α keeps scores local, which gives fewer false leads but misses distant members. Choose α by testing on a time-ordered set with known outcomes and a review capacity in mind, as with any threshold.

    7. A graph model reaches AUC 0.97 for mule accounts. What do you ask before believing it?

    Were the neighbour features and labels built as of each prediction date, or from today's graph? Were train and test split by time and by connected group? What happens to performance if hub accounts are capped? How does it compare with boosted trees on simple graph features, with an interval? And how many confirmed positives sit in the test set?
