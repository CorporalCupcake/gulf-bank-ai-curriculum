    Time Series and Graphs: Forecasting What Comes Next, and Seeing Who Is Connected to Whom

    Why a series with a calendar needs different handling from a table of customers, how to beat the naive forecast honestly and say how wrong you might be, and how fraud rings, mule networks and ownership chains become something a model can read.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTime series and graphs are the two cases where rows are not independent: in a series each value leans on the ones before it, in a graph each account leans on its neighbours, so you must split, validate and explain with that dependence in mind, and you gain a great deal of signal when you use it properly.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Most ML assumes each row stands alone: shuffle the rows and nothing changes. Two kinds of banking data break that.

    Time series. Cash taken from a Dubai Mall ATM today resembles yesterday's and last week's, and jumps on salary day. Shuffle the days and the information is gone.

    Graphs. Two accounts sharing a device and a flow of money are not independent customers, much as friends of a known shoplifter deserve a second look.

    Use the dependence and you gain strong signal: a seasonal pattern that halves forecast error, a fraud ring no single-account feature sees. Ignore it and validation lies, because test data is not really separate from training data.

    Hold onto thisBefore modelling, ask what the dependence is. Series: what repeats (weekly, monthly, Ramadan, Eid) and how far ahead must we see? Graph: what counts as a link (shared device, money moved, shared director) and which way does suspicion flow? These decide the method more than the algorithm does.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What makes a time series different

    Think of a daily ATM withdrawal series as four ingredients added together:

    • Level and trend. Where it sits and drifts: customer growth, inflation, a new branch.
    • Seasonality. Fixed repeats: weekly (Thursday and Friday peaks), monthly (salary day), yearly (summer travel).
    • Calendar events. Holidays and pre-Eid days. Ramadan and Eid move about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year, so a "month of year" feature misses next year. Use Hijri-calendar features and one calendar per country (weekends differ between Saudi Arabia and the UAE).
    • Noise. What is left. It sets a floor under every forecast.

    Forecasting means estimating each piece from history, extending it, and adding them back up. Autocorrelation, today correlating with yesterday, makes this possible and breaks the usual validation tricks. Real series also drift and break (a new payment rail), so re-fit often.

    The forecasting toolbox, and why simple baselines win so often

    Climb this ladder only when a rung beats the one below on honest tests.

    MethodWhat it doesWatch out
    Naive and seasonal naiveLast value, or the value one season ago (same weekday last week). The baseline every model must beat.Copies last week's noise and one-offs.
    Exponential smoothing, ARIMAClassic methods: a weighted average of the past, or a mix of past values and shocks.Weak with many holidays.

    Deep and pretrained modelsIncluding foundation models that forecast with no training on your data (R9).Often only match tuned simple models; harder to audit.

    Simple methods stay competitive because once trend, weekly pattern and known events are captured, what is left is mostly randomness. Ask: how much better than seasonal naive? MASE answers it. 1.0 means no better than naive, 0.7 means 30% better, above 1.0 means worse (F7: forecast metrics).

    • Gains come from encoding calendar knowledge, not a cleverer algorithm. In practice, a holiday and salary-day table built once and reused.
    • You cannot beat the floor. An error below the noise level means something leaked from the future (F3.1).

    Validating a forecast, and how uncertainty grows with the horizon

    Never shuffle. A random split puts next Tuesday in training and last Monday in test; correlated neighbours leak the answer. Use rolling origin: train to a date, forecast the next block, move forward, repeat (F4.5). Test at the real horizon: if treasury needs 14 days every morning, test exactly that.

    Report an interval, not a single number. "AED 12.4m, 80% likely between 11.1m and 13.7m" lets treasury size its buffer; "AED 12.4m" invites false precision. Intervals widen with the horizon. A balance that drifts back to its usual level has a band that stops widening; a random walk fans out for ever.

    • Horizon matters as much as method. One-day and 30-day error are different problems. State the horizon beside every accuracy figure.
    • Random walks defeat clever models. Exchange rates behave this way: the best forecast is today's value. Demand out-of-sample evidence from anyone promising more.
    • Structural breaks such as a new payment rail change the process itself, and backtests across a break mislead. Watch for drift (F7.7) and re-fit on a schedule.

    Graphs: turning relationships into data

    A graph has nodes (accounts, devices, companies) and edges (sent money to, shares a phone with, owns 40% of). It answers questions about structure rather than single rows:

    • Fraud rings. Accounts sharing a device or address and cycling money before cashing out.
    • Mule networks. A collector receives from many unrelated senders and forwards within hours. Each account looks ordinary; the shape gives it away.
    • Ownership chains. Who ultimately controls a company through layers of holdings.

    Decide what an edge is first, because "shares a device" and "sent over AED 5,000" give different graphs. A public Wi-Fi hotspot or payment hub links thousands of strangers and floods the graph with false connections.

    Classic graph analytics: components, hubs, flows and ownership

    • Connected components. Groups of accounts reachable from each other. 40 accounts sharing devices is worth a look; 2 million is not a ring, your edges are too loose.
    • Degree. Link count. A collector mule has high in-degree and a low end-of-day balance. A merchant or payroll account also has high degree, so degree is a lead, not a verdict.
    • Motifs. Cycles, fan-in, fan-out and fast pass-through chains look like layering.

    Ownership chains show structure no row-level feature captures. A person's effective stake is the percentages multiplied along each path, then added across paths. Example: the person owns 60% of A, A owns 50% of B, B owns 80% of C, and the person holds 5% of C directly.

    Path from the person to CCalculationEffective stake
    Through A, then B0.60 × 0.50 × 0.8024%
    Direct0.055%
    Total24% + 5%29%

    Each path alone is under 25%; the total is over it, so looking at one path misses the person. Thresholds like this drive B3: customer due diligence.

    Graph machine learning: propagating suspicion, then learning to

    Guilt by association means neighbours of a confirmed bad node are more likely bad. A personalised random walk makes this concrete: a walker starts on a confirmed fraud account and keeps following random links, sometimes jumping back to a confirmed account. Time spent on each account is its suspicion score. Accounts close to fraud, via many routes, score high. A low restart probability (α) spreads suspicion far; a high α keeps it near the confirmed accounts.

    One confirmed account yields a ranked review list no single-account model would produce. The danger: the answer depends on which links you include and how far suspicion spreads. Real labels come late from investigators (F3.5, F2.6).

    • Graph features into a standard model. Link count, confirmed-bad neighbours, component size, the walk score, fed to gradient boosting (F4.4). Cheap, explainable, often the strongest practical choice.
    • Graph neural networks (GNNs). Like asking your friends before deciding: each account blends its neighbours' information with its own, so after a few layers it knows about accounts two or three links away.
    • Time must be respected. Use only edges and labels that existed before the prediction date. "Confirmed-fraud neighbours" computed today includes labels confirmed next month (F3.1). It is the commonest way graph models look better than they are.
    • Split by time or by connected group, not at random, or test accounts' labelled neighbours sit in training (F3.5, F4.5).
    • Cap hubs. Payroll and merchant accounts make neighbourhoods explode.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check.

    • Random train and test split on a time series

      Error looks superb, then collapses live.

      Spot itIs every test date later than every training date?
    • No baseline, or one lucky test window

      "MAPE is 8%" means nothing if seasonal naive scores 8.5%. One window can crown the wrong winner (Simulation 1).

      Spot itAsk for seasonal naive on the same, several windows.
    • A single number with no horizon or interval

      "3% error" with no stated horizon or uncertainty.

      Spot itHow many days ahead, and what is the 80% range?
    • Features that use the future

      A centred rolling average, or a lag not yet available at forecast time.

      Spot itWould I have known this at 08:00 on the forecast date? Error below the noise floor is a red flag.
    • Moving holidays treated as fixed

      Month-of-year used for Ramadan and Eid, or one calendar for all countries.

      Spot itPlot residuals around Eid per year.
    • Edges that merge everything

      A public IP or popular merchant joins unrelated customers into a million-node "ring".

      Spot itCheck component sizes and top-degree nodes.
    • Graph leakage, and guilt treated as proof

      Neighbour features use labels that arrived after the prediction date. Or an account is frozen because a neighbour was flagged, catching family and landlords.

      Spot itRebuild features as of each prediction date. Treat graph scores as leads, not proof.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Why is a random 80/20 split wrong for a daily cash-demand series, and what would you do instead?

    Neighbouring days are correlated, so the model effectively interpolates answers. Use rolling origin at the production horizon.

    2. A balance is well above its usual level today. How should the forecast and its interval look over the next month if the series is highly persistent, and how if it is weakly persistent?

    Highly persistent: the forecast fades back slowly and the band keeps widening. Weakly persistent: it snaps back within days and today's value tells you little beyond that. In a random walk the forecast never fades.

    3. Why should every accuracy figure come with a horizon?

    Error grows as you look further ahead, so a one-day and a 30-day forecast are different problems. A figure with no horizon cannot be compared or trusted.

    4. Your model beats seasonal naive by 25% on one test window. What else must you check before trusting it?

    Whether the gain holds across several windows, including Ramadan, at the production horizon, and how close the error is to the noise floor. One window may be luck.

    5. Someone holds 24% of a company through a chain of holdings and 5% directly. Why might they count as a beneficial owner under a 25% rule, and what else matters?

    Stakes multiply along a path and add across paths, giving 29%, though neither path is over 25% alone. Control tests such as voting rights apply regardless of percentage.

    6. In Simulation 3 you lower α from 0.3 to 0.05 and suspicion on innocent accounts rises. Why, and what does that say about choosing α?

    Lower α means longer walks, so suspicion crosses weak bridges (the landlord) into ordinary accounts. High α stays local: fewer false leads, but distant members are missed. Choose α like any threshold, with review capacity in mind.

    7. A graph model reaches AUC 0.97 for mule accounts. What do you ask before believing it?

    Were features and labels built as of each prediction date? Was the split by time and connected group? How does it compare with boosted trees on simple graph features? How many positives are in the test set?
