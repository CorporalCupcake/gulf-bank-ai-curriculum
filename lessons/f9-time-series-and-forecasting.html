    Time Series Forecasting

    Part 1

    What a time series is

    In one sentenceA time series is a list of numbers recorded in order, such as daily sales, and forecasting means predicting the next ones by finding what repeats in the past.

    Our running example is a café that records cups sold each day. A café sells roughly the same on each Tuesday, sells more on payday, and slowly grows over the year. Forecasting means finding those patterns and extending them.

    The noise is the part nobody can predict. It sets a floor under every forecast: even a perfect model misses by about the size of the noise.

    How it differs from ordinary prediction

    • Ordinary models (house prices, spam) treat each row as separate. Shuffle the rows and nothing changes.
    • A time series has an order. Today looks like yesterday, and like the same weekday last week. This is called autocorrelation: a series correlating with its own past.
    • Why that matters: it is what makes forecasting possible, and it is also what breaks ordinary testing, as Part 3 shows.
    Part 2

    How forecasting works

    1. Plot it first. Look for trend, a weekly or yearly cycle, holidays and sudden jumps.
    2. Set a baseline. The simplest forecast is "same as the same day last week". This is the seasonal naive forecast.
    3. Fit a method that captures the pieces: trend, the repeating pattern and known events like paydays.
    4. Test it honestly on a period it has not seen, at the horizon you need (see Part 3).
    5. Forecast with a range, not a single number.
    6. Re-fit regularly. The pattern changes over time: a new competitor or a price change alters the series itself.

    A baseline that is hard to beat. Once trend, weekday pattern and known events are captured, what is left is mostly noise. That is why simple methods stay competitive, and why every fancy model must beat the baseline before you use it.

    The main methods

    • Seasonal naive: repeat the value from one season ago. Free, and the baseline.
    • Exponential smoothing and ARIMA: classic methods fitted to one series. Smoothing is an average that weights recent days more. ARIMA mixes past values with past surprises. Cheap and they give ranges, but you fit one per series.
    • Regression on calendar features: trend, weekday, payday and holiday flags as inputs. Easy to read. The inputs must be known for future dates.
    • Gradient boosting on lags: one model across thousands of series, fed yesterday's value, last week's value and calendar flags. It shares patterns between series. It cannot continue a trend by itself, so remove the trend first.
    • Deep and foundation models: neural networks, or pretrained models that forecast with no training on your data. They often only match tuned simple models and are harder to explain.

    Holidays that move. Ramadan and Eid shift about 11 days earlier each Gregorian year. Use Hijri-calendar features, and one holiday calendar per country, since weekends differ between Saudi Arabia and the UAE.

    Growing swings. If the ups and downs grow as sales grow, model percentage changes (or take logs) instead of raw cups.

    Part 3

    How to test and read a forecast

    Never shuffle. A random split puts next Tuesday in the training data and last Monday in the test data. Neighbouring days are near-copies, so the model has effectively seen the answer.

    Use a rolling origin instead: train up to a date, forecast the next block, then move forward and repeat.

    Test at the real horizon. If the business needs 14 days ahead every morning, score exactly that, over several windows including holidays and year-end.

    How to read a forecast

    • Compare with the baseline. A score called MASE divides your error by the seasonal naive error. 1.0 means no better than the baseline; 0.7 means 30% better.
    • Read the range. "About 1,240 cups, 80% likely between 1,110 and 1,370" lets the café decide how much milk to order. A bank treasury does the same to size its cash buffer.
    • Check the range is honest. An 80% range should contain about 80% of real outcomes in past tests. Fewer means the model is overconfident.
    • State the horizon. Tomorrow's error and next month's error are different problems. Quote the horizon beside every accuracy figure.
    • Be suspicious of too good. An error well below the noise floor means something from the future leaked in.

    How far ahead can you see? If the series is pulled back to its usual level (queue length, say), the range stops widening after a while. If it has no such pull, like an exchange rate, the range fans out for ever and nothing beats "tomorrow equals today".

    Show the formula

    MASE = your average miss ÷ the average miss of the seasonal naive forecast, both measured on the same test days. Below 1 beats the baseline.

    Part 4

    A short note on graphs

    The same lesson, that rows are not independent, applies to graphs: nodes (accounts, devices) joined by edges (sent money to, shares a phone with). Two accounts on one device are not unrelated customers.

    • What it reveals: fraud rings, mule accounts that receive from many senders and forward within hours, and ownership chains.
    • Simple tools first: group sizes, link counts and "how many confirmed-bad neighbours". Feed these as columns into a normal model such as boosting. This is cheap, explainable and often enough.
    • Graph neural networks let each node blend in its neighbours' information. They need more labelled data and are harder to explain.
    • Beware: decide what an edge means first, since a public Wi-Fi hotspot links thousands of strangers. A high score is a lead for an investigator, not proof.
    Part 5

    Which one to use

    MethodPick it whenAvoid when
    Seasonal naiveAlways, as the baseline. Often enough for stable weekly seriesYou expect trend or holiday spikes
    Smoothing / ARIMAA handful of series, clear seasonality, you want quick rangesThousands of series or many moving holidays
    Calendar regressionCalendar effects dominate and you want to read each oneEffects change by branch or interact
    Boosting on lagsHundreds of related series with useful extra inputsOne short series, or a strong trend you have not removed
    Deep / foundationVery many series, little time, or no history at allYou must audit each forecast, or a simple model is already near the noise floor

    Forecasting is strong at:

    • Planning capacity: cash in ATMs, call-centre volume, branch footfall, because repeating patterns are most of the signal.
    • Many series at once: a shared model lets a new series borrow from similar ones.
    • Honest uncertainty: ranges size a buffer to the risk.

    It is weak at:

    • Random walks such as share prices, where nothing beats today's value.
    • Long horizons and sudden breaks. A new payment rail, a regulation or a pandemic makes past patterns stop applying.
    • Very short series, where there is too little history to separate pattern from noise. Pool them or use a group average.

    In banking, forecasting drives ATM cash planning and liquidity buffers, and graph features help find mule networks and hidden ownership.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Shuffling the data before testing

      Neighbouring days leak the answer, so scores look great and the live forecast disappoints. Always split by time.

    • Skipping the baseline

      Without "same as last week" for comparison, you cannot tell whether the model adds anything.

    • Giving one number, not a range

      A single figure hides the risk. Report a range and check it against past outcomes.

    • Fitting once and forgetting

      Series drift and break. Re-fit on a schedule and watch the error.

    1. Why is a time series different from ordinary rows of data?

    The order matters: today resembles yesterday and last week. That helps forecasting but breaks random train/test splits.

    2. What is the seasonal naive baseline?

    Predict the value from one season ago, such as the same weekday last week. Any model must beat it to be worth using.

    3. How do you test a forecast properly?

    Rolling origin: train on the past, forecast the next block, move forward and repeat, at the horizon the business needs.

    4. Your forecast error is far below the noise floor. Good news?

    No. Something from the future has probably leaked into the inputs.

    5. An 80% range contains only 55% of real outcomes. What does that mean?

    The model is overconfident. Its ranges are too narrow and need widening or recalibrating.
