    Probability, Base Rates and the Shape of Data

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Like a smoke alarm that also rings for 1% of toast, a fraud model that catches 99% of fraud and flags 1% of genuine customers mostly rings for toast. In 10,000 customers with 0.2% fraudsters, it catches the 20 fraudsters but also flags about 100 genuine people. When it fires, it is right about 1 time in 6. This is base-rate neglect, the commonest reasoning error in fraud, AML and credit.

    The second half is the same idea for averages. An average card payment might be AED 650, yet most are far smaller and a few are enormous. A probability needs a base rate, and an average needs a shape.

    Hold onto thisBefore trusting a probability, ask "out of how many, and how common was it to start with?" Before trusting an average, ask "what is the shape, and how much of the total comes from the top 1%?"
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Conditional probability, and why direction matters

    Detection rate asks, of the fraudsters, how many get flagged? Hit rate asks, of the flagged, how many are fraudsters? In Simulation 1 they were 99% and about 17%.

    Vendors quote the first and the fraud desk lives with the second, so mixing them up (the prosecutor's fallacy) is costly.

    Bayes' rule as updating

    Think of Bayes' rule as updating: new belief = how diagnostic the evidence is × where you started. The multiplier is the likelihood ratio: 1 means the clue tells you nothing, 10 means ten times likelier under fraud, below 1 pulls risk down.

    Weak evidence cannot overturn a strong starting point.

    The channel sets the starting line, and evidence that lowers risk counts as much as red flags.

    When the base rate moves: prior shift

    A model's probabilities silently assume the base rate it was trained on, like a thermometer calibrated for one room. When the base rate moves (a new channel, Eid, a balanced training sample) the clues stay good but every probability is wrong: prior shift.

    If only the frequency changed, rescale; if the fraud pattern changed, retrain.

    Where this connectsPlatt scaling's intercept in F7 is exactly this adjustment. See calibration in F7 and why rare events fool almost everyone.

    Many clues at once: naive Bayes and its flaw

    Naive Bayes multiplies the evidence from many clues, assuming they are independent, which is nearly always false: a new phone brings a new IP, SIM and app install, one fact counted four times.

    The result is overconfident probabilities; logistic regression fixes it by sharing credit between correlated clues.

    Counts and yes-or-no events: binomial, Poisson, overdispersion

    Ask first: is it yes/no, a count or an amount? Binomial counts yeses among independent trials (defaults among 1,000 contracts); Poisson counts rare independent events (complaints per day) and ties its spread to its average.

    Bank counts arrive in bursts, so they are usually overdispersed and need a negative binomial, or a Poisson alarm cries wolf.

    The normal distribution and the central limit theorem

    The central limit theorem: averages of many independent values are roughly bell-shaped whatever the individual shape. So the normal suits averages, not individual amounts or losses, and it fails when values move together or tails are very heavy.

    Heavy tails: log-normal, Pareto, and when the mean misleads

    Amounts and losses are positive and skewed: many small values, a few huge ones. Log-normal arises when factors multiply and puts the mean well above the median; Pareto has a tail so heavy the variance, or even the mean, can be infinite.

    In practice the next big loss can exceed everything seen so far combined.

    For heavy tails the median and percentiles are stable and the mean is fragile, so report the median, 99th percentile and top-1% share alongside it.

    Expected loss, variance and capital

    Expected loss is the chance of default, times the share lost, times the amount exposed. It is a priced-in cost of doing business that adds up whatever the correlations.

    Borrowers share exposure to oil and property, so in a bad year many default at once; banks hold capital for that unexpected tail.

    Expected loss is about the average, capital is about the tail, and the tail is about correlation.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each has a fast check.

    • Quoting detection rate as the hit rate

      "Detects 95% of mules" says nothing about how many alerts are real, so ask how many of every 100 alerts were confirmed.

    • Scored on one base rate, deployed on another

      Average predicted probabilities over live traffic and check they sit near the actual fraud rate.

    • Double-counting correlated evidence

      Rules that almost always fire together (new device, IP, SIM) are one piece of evidence, not three.

    • Averages on skewed data

      Ask for the median, 99th percentile and top-1% share, since a mean that moves a lot when one case is dropped is no planning number.

    • Expected loss right, capital wrong

      Provisions can be sound while portfolio correlation is assumed zero, so ask what correlation the capital numbers use.

    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A model detects 95% of mules and flags 3% of genuine accounts; mules are 1 in 1,000. Is most of what it flags mule?

    No, only about 3% of alerts are mules, because genuine accounts are 1,000 times more numerous.

    2. A model trained on a 50/50 sample says 80% fraud; the true rate is 0.5%. Take it at face value?

    No, every score is inflated, though the ranking stays useful.

    3. Daily complaints vary far more than Poisson allows. What does that tell you?

    Complaint days cluster, so use a negative binomial or a Poisson alarm fires far too often.

    4. Fraud losses look Pareto with a heavy tail. What should you report?

    The median, high percentiles and top-case share, because the mean jumps with each giant case.

    5. True or false: higher borrower correlation raises expected loss.

    False, it fattens the tail and so raises capital, not expected loss.
