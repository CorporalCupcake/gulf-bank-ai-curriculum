    Probability, Base Rates and the Shape of Data

    Why a 99% accurate fraud model can be wrong most of the times it fires, how evidence should move a belief, and why the shape of a loss distribution matters more than its average.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA probability means nothing until you know how common the thing was to begin with, and an average means nothing until you know the shape of the data it came from.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A vendor pitches a fraud model to your bank. "It catches 99% of fraud, and only 1% of genuine customers get flagged." That sounds like a model that's right 99% of the time. So when it flags someone, surely they're almost certainly a fraudster?

    No. Count people instead of percentages. Take 10,000 customers, of whom 0.2% are fraudsters. That's 20 fraudsters and 9,980 genuine customers. The model catches about all 20. It also flags 1% of the 9,980 genuine customers, which is about 100 people. So 120 people get flagged, and only 20 of them are fraud. When the model fires, it's right about 1 time in 6.

    Nothing is wrong with the model. The problem is that the 1% false alarm rate applies to a group 500 times larger than the fraud group. Rarity wins. This is base-rate neglect, and it's the most common reasoning error in fraud, AML, credit and medicine.

    The second half of this lesson is the same idea in a different form. A bank's average card transaction might be AED 650. But most transactions are far smaller than that, and a handful are enormous. The average is dragged up by the rare giants, just as the alert queue is dragged down by the common genuine customers. In both cases, a single summary number hides the thing that decides what happens.

    • A probability needs a base rate. "99% accurate" tells you about the model. "1 in 6 alerts is fraud" tells you about the model in this population, which is what the fraud desk lives with.
    • An average needs a shape. For bell-shaped data the average is a fine summary. For transaction amounts, fraud losses and operational losses, the average can be unstable, misleading or, in extreme cases, not even exist.
    Hold onto thisBefore trusting any probability, ask "out of how many, and how common was it to start with?" Before trusting any average, ask "what does the distribution look like, and how much of the total comes from the top 1%?"
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Conditional probability, and why direction matters

    A conditional probability restricts attention to a subgroup. P(flag | fraud), read "the probability of a flag given fraud", asks: among fraudsters, what share get flagged? P(fraud | flag) asks: among the flagged, what share are fraudsters? Same two events, completely different questions, and in Simulation 1 they were 99% and about 17%.

    P(A∣B)=P(AandB)P(B)

    Of all the cases where B happened, what share also had A? The denominator is the group you're restricting to. Swap A and B and the denominator changes, which is why the answer changes.

    Mixing these up has a name in courtrooms: the prosecutor's fallacy. "Only 1 in 1,000 innocent people would match this profile, so there's a 1 in 1,000 chance he's innocent." Wrong: if a million people could have done it, about 1,000 innocent people match, and the suspect is one of 1,001. In a bank it sounds like: "Only 2% of genuine customers trigger this rule, so a customer who triggers it is 98% likely to be fraud." That inverts the conditional.

    Vendors and model reports almost always quote P(flag | fraud) (sensitivity, recall, detection rate), because it's a property of the model and doesn't depend on your customers. The fraud desk experiences P(fraud | flag). Bayes' rule is how you get from one to the other.

    Bayes' rule as updating, in odds

    The textbook form of Bayes' rule is a fraction with a sum underneath, and almost nobody can do it in their head. The odds form is far more useful, and it's how experienced risk people actually think:

    P(fraud∣E)P(genuine∣E)posterior odds=P(E∣fraud)P(E∣genuine)likelihood ratio×P(fraud)P(genuine)prior odds

    Odds after seeing evidence E = how much more often E shows up in fraud than in genuine activity × the odds before you saw it.

    Run the vendor's model through it. A base rate of 0.2% is odds of 1 to 499. A flag is 99 times more likely for a fraudster than for a genuine customer (0.99 ÷ 0.01), so the likelihood ratio is 99. Posterior odds: 99 to 499, which is 99 ÷ 598 ≈ 16.6%. No tree diagram needed.

    This form separates the two things that matter. The likelihood ratio is a property of the evidence: how diagnostic it is. The prior is a property of the population. A model can't fix a bad prior, and a strong prior can't be overturned by weak evidence.

    Take logarithms and multiplication becomes addition. In log-odds, each piece of evidence adds a fixed amount: ln(likelihood ratio). A new device adds the same push whether you started at 0.05% or 5%. That's the mental model behind every logistic regression and every points-based scorecard: start at the prior, add evidence, convert back to a probability at the end.

    Two things to take from it. First, the channel sets the starting line. The same three clues give about 9% on a shop-till payment and about 66% on an online card payment, because the prior differs by a factor of 20. Second, evidence that lowers risk is just as real. A successful passkey check has a likelihood ratio below 1 and pulls the bar back down. Good fraud strategy is as much about gathering exonerating evidence cheaply as about spotting red flags.

    When the base rate moves: prior shift

    A model's probabilities silently assume the base rate it was trained on. When that rate changes, the likelihood ratios may be fine, but every probability is off. This is prior shift, also called label shift, and in banking it's the norm, not the exception:

    • Channel. A model trained on all card payments, then used only for online payments, where fraud is several times more common.
    • Season. Fraud and spending both rise around Ramadan, Eid, White Friday and the Dubai Shopping Festival, but not in proportion.
    • Attack waves. A new scam script on instant payments can double the fraud rate in a fortnight.
    • Resampling. Fraud models are often trained on a balanced sample. The model then believes fraud is 50% common, which is a prior shift you created yourself.

    If the likelihoods are stable and only the base rate has changed, the correction is exact and takes one line. Convert the model's probability to odds, multiply by the ratio of new to old prior odds, and convert back:

    oddsnew=oddsmodel×πnew/(1−πnew)πtrain/(1−πtrain)

    π is the fraud rate. Trained on a 50/50 sample and deployed where fraud is 0.2%: a score of 90% (odds 9) becomes 9 × (1/499) ÷ 1 ≈ 0.018, so about 1.8%. In log-odds it's a constant shift of the intercept.

    The catch is that you often don't know the new base rate, because fraud labels arrive weeks late. There's a neat answer: estimate it from the model's own outputs on the new, unlabelled data. The classic method (Saerens and colleagues, 2002) alternates between adjusting the probabilities for a guessed prior and re-estimating the prior from the adjusted probabilities, an EM algorithm. It works well if the model was calibrated to begin with. If the fraud pattern itself changes, not just its frequency, no prior correction will save you, and you need retraining. That distinction, frequency versus pattern, is the first question to ask when a fraud model degrades.

    Where this connectsThe F7 lesson shows the same correction from the other side: Platt scaling's intercept is exactly this prior adjustment, and an undersampled model is a prior shift by design. See calibration in F7 and, for how precision falls with the base rate, why rare events fool almost everyone.

    Many clues at once: naive Bayes and its flaw

    With several clues, the full Bayes' rule needs the likelihood of the whole combination: how often fraud shows a new device and a new network and a large amount together. With twenty binary clues there are about a million combinations, far too many to estimate. Naive Bayes makes one simplifying assumption: given the class, the clues are independent. Then the joint likelihood ratio is just the product of the individual ones, and in log-odds you simply add them.

    logoddspost=logoddsprior+∑i=1klogLRi

    Each clue contributes its own log-likelihood ratio. In credit scoring this quantity has a name: the weight of evidence, and a classic scorecard is very close to this sum.

    The assumption is almost always false. A new phone usually comes with a new IP address, a new SIM and a new app install, so those clues are really one fact seen four times. Naive Bayes counts it four times and becomes overconfident: its probabilities pile up near 0 and 1. In Simulation 2, multiplying "new device" (×8) by "new network" (×5) gives ×40, when the joint evidence is only worth about ×12.

    Surprisingly, naive Bayes often ranks cases well even when its probabilities are badly off, because double-counting tends to push everything in the right direction. That's why it survives as a fast baseline for text such as sorting customer complaints in Arabic and English into categories. Logistic regression is the grown-up version: same additive log-odds structure, but it learns the weights jointly, so correlated clues share credit instead of each claiming it in full. Rules engines that add points per triggered rule have the same flaw as naive Bayes, and few people notice.

    Counts and yes-or-no events: binomial, Poisson, overdispersion

    The second topic is about the shape of data. A distribution is a description of which values are possible and how often each occurs. Three questions identify most of the shapes you'll need: is it a yes-or-no outcome, a count, or an amount? Can it be negative? Is it bounded?

    • Bernoulli: a single yes-or-no event with probability p. Will this customer default in the next 12 months? Its mean is p and its variance is p(1 − p).
    • Binomial: the number of yeses among n independent Bernoulli trials. Defaults among 1,000 personal finance contracts, each with PD 2%: mean 20, standard deviation √(1,000 × 0.02 × 0.98) ≈ 4.4. The word that matters is independent. Hold onto it.
    • Poisson: counts of events in a fixed window when events are rare and independent. Complaints per day, card disputes per hour, failed logins per minute. It has one parameter, and its variance equals its mean. If the contact centre averages 20 complaints a day, Poisson says the standard deviation is √20 ≈ 4.5.

    That "variance equals mean" property is a testable claim, and in bank data it usually fails. Real complaint counts bunch up: an app outage, a fee change or a delayed salary run produces bursts. When the observed variance is bigger than the mean, the data are overdispersed. The usual fix is the negative binomial, which is a Poisson whose rate itself varies from day to day.

    The difference is not academic. Suppose complaints average 20 a day. A Poisson model says a day with 35 or more happens about 0.15% of the time, roughly once every two and a half years of working days, so it looks like an emergency. If the true variance is 80 rather than 20, a negative binomial says it happens about 7% of the time, more than once a month. An anomaly detector built on Poisson will cry wolf constantly.

    The normal distribution and the central limit theorem

    The central limit theorem says that if you add up or average many independent values with a finite variance, the total is approximately normal, whatever the shape of the individual values. That's why the bell curve is everywhere: measurement error, averages, sums of many small effects.

    X¯≈N(μ,σ2n)

    The average of n values is close to normal, centred on the true mean μ, with a standard deviation (the standard error) of σ ÷ √n. Quadruple the sample and you halve the uncertainty.

    Two conditions do all the work, and both fail in banking. Independence fails when defaults move together in a recession, as the portfolio simulation below shows. Finite variance fails for the heaviest-tailed loss data, where the theorem doesn't apply at all. And "approximately normal" arrives slowly when the data are skewed: averages of transaction amounts can need thousands of values before the bell shape appears. Normal is a good model for averages of well-behaved data. It's a poor model for individual amounts, losses or anything with a hard floor at zero.

    Heavy tails: log-normal, Pareto, and when the mean misleads

    Transaction amounts, fraud losses, operational losses, account balances and corporate exposures are all positive and strongly skewed: lots of small values and a long tail of very large ones. Two shapes cover most of it.

    • Log-normal: the logarithm of the amount is normal. It arises when many factors multiply together: price × quantity × currency × seasonal uplift. The median is eμ, and the mean is bigger by a factor of eσ²/2. With σ = 1.2 and a median of AED 400, the mean is about AED 820 and the 99th percentile about AED 6,500. All moments exist, but the variance can be enormous.
    • Pareto (a power law): the chance of exceeding an amount falls off as amount−α. The tail index α decides everything. With α ≤ 2 the variance is infinite. With α ≤ 1 even the mean is infinite: the sample average just keeps growing as you collect more data. With α ≈ 1.16 you get the "80/20 rule": the top 20% hold 80% of the total.

    "Infinite mean" sounds like a mathematical curiosity, but its practical meaning is concrete: the next big loss can be larger than everything you've seen so far put together, and your historical average is mostly a record of how lucky you've been. Operational loss data in banks is the classic example. A decade of small losses and then one rogue trader or one cyber incident.

    What the simulation should leave you with:

    • The median and percentiles are stable; the mean is the fragile one. For heavy-tailed data, report median, 90th, 99th percentile and the share of total from the top 1%. Report the mean too, but never alone.
    • Mean minus two standard deviations can be negative for amounts that can't be. If an analysis gives a "normal range" for transaction amounts that starts below zero, the shape was ignored.
    • The method follows the shape. Model log amounts, not raw amounts. Use quantile or Tweedie losses rather than squared error for skewed targets. Fit tails with extreme value theory rather than extrapolating a bell curve. Squared error chases the mean and so chases the giants; absolute error targets the median, which is one reason the forecast metrics you choose matter.

    Expected loss, variance and capital

    The expected value is the probability-weighted average outcome. For a single loan, the loss is zero with probability 1 − PD and LGD × EAD with probability PD. So its expected value is the most important formula in retail credit:

    EL=PD×LGD×EAD

    PD, probability of default within the horizon. LGD, loss given default: the share of the exposure you don't recover after collateral and collections. EAD, exposure at default: how much is owed when it happens, including undrawn limits that tend to get drawn first. PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 150,000 gives an expected loss of AED 1,350 per contract.

    Expected loss is a cost of doing business. It's priced into the profit rate on a murabaha or the interest on a loan, and under IFRS 9 it's held as a provision. Expectation is also linear: the expected loss of a portfolio is the sum of the expected losses of its contracts, whatever the correlations between them.

    Variance is different. It measures how far actual losses swing around the expectation, and for a portfolio it depends heavily on whether defaults move together. With n loans, default probability p and a pairwise default correlation ρD, the variance of the number of defaults is:

    Var(defaults)=np(1−p)[1+(n−1)ρD]

    The bracket is the overdispersion factor again. With 1,000 loans, a default correlation of just 0.01 makes the variance about 11 times bigger than the independent binomial. Small correlations, multiplied across a large book, dominate.

    Correlation comes from shared exposure to the economy: oil prices, a property cycle, a rise in the policy rate, an employer laying off staff. In a bad year, many borrowers default together. Banks hold capital against this unexpected part: the gap between a very bad year's loss and the expected loss. Basel's internal-ratings approach sets it at the loss that is exceeded only once in 1,000 years, using a one-factor model (Vasicek's) in which each borrower's fortunes depend partly on a single economic factor. The weight on that factor is the asset correlation, and Basel fixes it by asset class: 4% for credit cards, 15% for residential mortgages, 12% to 24% for corporates.

    This is the single most important picture in credit risk. Expected loss is about the average; capital is about the tail; and the tail is about correlation. A model that predicts PDs perfectly but treats defaults as independent will get provisions right and capital badly wrong. That's roughly what happened with mortgage-backed securities before 2008: individual default probabilities were estimated reasonably, and the correlation between them was not.

    How it connects

    Base rates and distribution shapes sit underneath most of the curriculum. These are the links you'll use most.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, thresholds, calibrationPrecision is Bayes' rule

    Precision is P(fraud | flag). Its dependence on the fraud rate, and the cost-based threshold p*, are this lesson's ideas put to work. Calibration is whether the posterior is honest.

    F1.6 · Maximum likelihoodEvery loss function is a distribution

    Log loss is the Bernoulli likelihood. Squared error assumes normal noise. Poisson and Tweedie losses assume those shapes. Choosing a loss is choosing a distribution.

    F4.2 · Logistic regressionAdditive log-odds, learnt properly

    Same structure as naive Bayes, but correlated features share credit. The intercept carries the prior, which is why prior shift is an intercept fix.

    F2.1 · Standard errorsThe CLT is why error bars work

    Confidence intervals rest on the central limit theorem, and they get unreliable with heavy-tailed data and small samples.

    F2.5 · Generalised linear modelsPick the shape, then regress

    Logistic for yes-or-no, Poisson or negative binomial for counts, gamma or Tweedie for amounts. The GLM family is this lesson's list of distributions.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataResampling is a prior shift

    Undersampling genuine cases changes the prior the model learns. Correct for it with the odds formula, or recalibrate.

    F7.5 · Forecast metricsMean or median, pick deliberately

    Squared error is minimised by the mean, absolute error by the median, quantile loss by a percentile. With heavy tails they give very different forecasts.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringFrequency drift versus pattern drift

    A changed base rate can be fixed with an intercept. A changed fraud pattern needs retraining. Monitor them separately.

    B4.1 · PD, LGD, EADExpected loss, in depth

    How each component is modelled, why LGD is bimodal (most recover nearly everything or very little), and why EAD includes undrawn limits.

    B4.2 · Scorecards, WOE and IVWeight of evidence is a log-likelihood ratio

    A scorecard adds points per attribute. Each attribute's weight of evidence is exactly the log-LR from Simulation 2.

    B4.3 – B4.4 · IFRS 9 and stress testingExpectation for provisions, tails for stress

    IFRS 9 provisions are probability-weighted expected losses across scenarios. Stress tests ask what happens in the tail.

    B5.2 · VaR and expected shortfallWhy regulators moved past VaR

    A percentile says nothing about how bad things get beyond it. Expected shortfall averages the tail, which matters most when tails are heavy.

    B2.4 · AML alert triageBase rates even lower than fraud

    Genuine money laundering among alerts is rarer still, so false positive rates above 90% are normal. The base-rate maths explains why, and what would change it.

    R9.2 · Tabular foundation modelsA network trained to do Bayes

    TabPFN is trained on millions of synthetic datasets to approximate Bayesian prediction. Part 4 has the detail.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The probability theory here is settled. The live work is in three places: how humans and AI systems reason with base rates, how to adapt models when base rates move, and how to model and regulate tails.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in vendor pitches, internal analyses and AI-generated reports. Each comes with the fastest check.

    • Quoting detection rate as if it were the hit rate

      “Detects 95% of mule accounts” is P(flag | mule). The investigation team cares about P(mule | flag), which may be under 5%.

      Spot itAsk: out of every 100 alerts, how many were confirmed? Then ask for the base rate.
    • A model scored on one base rate, deployed on another

      A model built on online payments and switched on for shop-till payments, or validated in a quiet month and run through Eid.

      Spot itCompare the fraud rate in the validation data with the live population, segment by segment. If they differ, ask whether the probabilities were adjusted.
    • Reading a balanced-sample probability as real

      A model trained on 50/50 data says 90%. With a true rate of 0.2%, that's about 1.8%.

      Spot itAverage the model's predicted probabilities over a month of live traffic. It should be close to the actual fraud rate. If it's 30 times higher, the prior is wrong.
    • Double-counting correlated evidence

      A rules engine adds points for new device, new IP and new SIM, and a single phone change triples the score.

      Spot itCheck how often the triggering rules fire together. Rules that almost always co-occur are one piece of evidence.
    • Average loss from a heavy-tailed history

      “Average fraud loss per case is AED 9,000.” The median is AED 1,200, and three cases make up half the total.

      Spot itAsk for the median, the 99th percentile and the share of total from the top 1%. If the mean moves a lot when you drop the largest case, the mean is not a planning number.
    • Normal assumptions on amounts and counts

      An AML rule flags amounts more than three standard deviations above a customer's mean. For log-normal amounts that flags far more than 0.1% of genuine activity, and for some customers almost nothing.

      Spot itLook for “mean ± k standard deviations” on positive, skewed data. Ask whether logs or percentiles were used instead.
    • Poisson where the data are overdispersed

      Complaint or alert-volume forecasts with intervals far too narrow, and spike alarms that fire every week.

      Spot itCompare the variance of daily counts with the mean. If the ratio is well above 1, Poisson is the wrong shape.
    • Getting expected loss right and capital wrong

      A PD model is validated, provisions look sound, and the portfolio's correlation is assumed to be zero or borrowed from somewhere else.

      Spot itAsk how concentrated the book is by employer, sector, emirate and collateral type, and what correlation the capital or stress numbers assume.
    • AI-generated analysis that skips the base rate

      Typical signs: “fraud risk is 3× higher for this segment” with no base rate, a relative risk on a tiny count, or a “normal range” for amounts that starts below zero.

      Spot itRewrite one headline claim as natural frequencies: out of 10,000 customers, how many? If the text can't support it, the claim is hollow.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A model that's 99% accurate can still be wrong most of the times it raises an alarm, because fraud is rare. Out of 10,000 customers, about 20 are fraudsters. The model catches them, but also flags about 100 genuine customers. So 1 alert in 6 is real. To improve that, cutting false alarms is worth more than catching the last few frauds, and so is cheap evidence that clears customers quickly, like a passkey check.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I think in log-odds: the prior from the segment's base rate, plus a log-likelihood ratio per piece of evidence. That keeps the model's discrimination separate from the population's prior, so when the fraud rate shifts by channel or season I can correct the intercept, or estimate the new prior with EM if labels are delayed. For losses, I check the tail before choosing a method: log scale or Tweedie for amounts, negative binomial when counts are overdispersed, percentiles and tail shares instead of means, and for portfolios I keep expected loss and correlation-driven tail risk separate.”
    “The vendor says the model is 99% accurate. Should we buy it?”

    Accuracy doesn't tell us what we'll experience. I'd ask for the detection rate and false positive rate separately, then apply our own fraud rate. At 0.2% fraud, 99% detection and 1% false positives means about 1 alert in 6 is fraud. Whether that's acceptable depends on the cost of review and our team's capacity, which the F7 threshold analysis handles.

    “Fraud has doubled since we trained the model. Do we need to rebuild it?”

    Not necessarily. If it's the same kind of fraud, just more of it, the ranking is still fine and the probabilities are too low by about a factor of two in odds. That's a one-number intercept correction. If fraudsters have changed tactics, the likelihoods have changed too, and that needs retraining. I'd check which it is by comparing the model's score distribution for confirmed fraud now against at training time.

    “Why can't we budget fraud losses on last year's average?”

    Because fraud losses are heavy-tailed. A few cases make up much of the total, so the average depends heavily on whether last year happened to include a big one. I'd budget on the median year plus a tail scenario, and look at the 95th or 99th percentile of annual loss from a model or a longer history, not the single-year mean.

    “If correlation doesn't change expected loss, why do we care about it?”

    Because capital is about the bad years, not the average year. Correlation makes defaults cluster, which stretches the tail of the loss distribution. With the same expected loss, the 1-in-1,000-year loss can be several times higher. That's what capital has to cover, and why concentration by sector or employer matters so much.

    “Is naive Bayes ever the right choice?”

    As a fast baseline, yes, especially for text: sorting complaints or emails into categories. It often ranks well. But its probabilities are overconfident because it treats correlated features as independent, so I wouldn't use its outputs as probabilities without calibration, and for tabular risk models logistic regression or gradient boosting is the better default.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. Counting people is usually the fastest route.

    1. One in 1,000 new accounts is a money mule. A model detects 95% of mules and flags 3% of genuine accounts. Out of 100,000 new accounts, how many are flagged, and what share are mules?

    Mules: 100, of which 95 are flagged. Genuine: 99,900, of which 3% = 2,997 are flagged. Total flagged 3,092. Share that are mules: 95 ÷ 3,092 ≈ 3.1%. Roughly 1 alert in 33.

    2. The prior is 1%. Two independent pieces of evidence have likelihood ratios of 20 and 3. What's the posterior?

    Prior odds 1 : 99. Multiply by 20 × 3 = 60: odds 60 : 99. Posterior = 60 ÷ 159 ≈ 37.7%. If the two clues were correlated, the true answer would be lower.

    3. A model trained on a 50/50 sample outputs 0.8. The true fraud rate is 0.5%. What's the corrected probability?

    Model odds 0.8 ÷ 0.2 = 4. Multiply by the new prior odds (0.005 ÷ 0.995 ≈ 0.00503) and divide by the training prior odds (1): 4 × 0.00503 ≈ 0.0201. Probability ≈ 0.0201 ÷ 1.0201 ≈ 2.0%.

    4. Daily complaints average 30, but their variance across days is 150. Is Poisson a good model, and what does the gap tell you?

    No. Poisson implies a variance of 30, so the data are five times overdispersed. Something makes complaint days cluster: outages, campaigns, salary-day issues. Use a negative binomial, or better, model the drivers. A Poisson-based spike alarm would fire far too often.

    5. Fraud losses look Pareto with tail index α = 1.4. Does the mean exist? The variance? What should you report?

    The mean exists (α > 1); the variance is infinite (α ≤ 2). The sample mean will converge, but slowly and with big jumps, and standard errors computed the usual way are meaningless. Report the median, high percentiles, the share of total from the largest cases, and use extreme value methods for the tail.

    6. 2,000 auto finance contracts: PD 3%, LGD 40%, EAD AED 80,000 each. What's the expected loss?

    Per contract: 0.03 × 0.40 × 80,000 = AED 960. For the book: 2,000 × 960 = AED 1.92 million. That's the same whatever the correlation between borrowers.

    7. True or false: raising the correlation between borrowers raises a portfolio's expected loss.

    False. Expectation is linear, so expected loss is the sum of individual expected losses regardless of correlation. Correlation raises the variance and the tail: the bad-year loss and therefore the capital.
