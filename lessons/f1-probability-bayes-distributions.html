    Probability, Base Rates and Distributions

    Part 1

    What probability is, and why base rates matter

    In one sentenceA probability is a number from 0 to 1 for how likely something is, and it only makes sense when you know how common the thing was to start with: the base rate.

    Running example: a test catches 90% of people who have a condition, and wrongly flags 10% of healthy people. Only 1 person in 100 has the condition. You test positive. How likely is it that you have it?

    Most people say about 90%. The picture says otherwise: 90 true positives are swamped by 990 false alarms, so only about 8% of positives are real. The test is good. The condition is just rare.

    Before trusting any probability, ask: out of how many, and how common was it to start with?

    Part 2

    Two probabilities people confuse

    • Detection rate (90%): of people who have it, how many test positive? This is what test makers quote.
    • Hit rate (8%): of people who test positive, how many have it? This is what the person holding the result needs.

    They answer different questions and can be very far apart. Mixing them up is a common and costly error.

    How to work it out

    1. Imagine a round number of cases, such as 10,000.
    2. Split them by the base rate (100 have it, 9,900 do not).
    3. Apply the test's rates to each group (90 and 990 flagged).
    4. The share of flagged cases that are real is your answer: 90 ÷ 1,080.

    This is Bayes' rule without the algebra. It is a way of updating a starting belief (the prior, here 1%) after seeing evidence (the positive test).

    How to read it

    • Rare thing, good test: most positives are still false alarms.
    • Common thing, same test: at a base rate of 1 in 2, the same test makes almost every positive real (90 of 95).
    • Prior shift: a model's probabilities quietly assume the base rate it was trained on. If the real rate changes, the model's clues are still good but its probabilities are wrong, and need rescaling.
    Show the formula

    P(have it | positive) = P(positive | have it) × P(have it) ÷ P(positive). In words: chance of a real case given a positive equals how often real cases test positive, times how common real cases are, divided by how often anyone tests positive. With our numbers: 0.9 × 0.01 ÷ 0.108 ≈ 0.083.

    Part 3

    The shape of data

    The second half of the idea is for averages. A probability needs a base rate. An average needs a shape. Ask first: is it a yes/no, a count, or an amount?

    • Yes/no events (does this customer default?) follow a binomial pattern: how many yeses among many independent tries.
    • Counts (emails per hour) follow a Poisson pattern when events are rare and independent. Real counts often arrive in bursts, so Poisson-based alarms cry wolf.
    • Amounts (order sizes, incomes, losses) are positive and skewed: many small values and a few huge ones.
    • Normal (bell): averages of many independent values come out bell-shaped whatever the individual shape. This is the central limit theorem. So the bell suits averages, not individual amounts or losses.
    • Pareto (very heavy tail): the tail is so heavy that the average may never settle. The next big loss can exceed everything seen so far combined.

    With skewed data, report the median (the middle value) and a high percentile, such as the 99th, alongside the mean. They are stable while the mean is fragile. Tell, too, how much of the total comes from the top 1%.

    Part 4

    Which distribution to use

    Data looks likeStart withWatch out for
    Yes/no outcomesBinomialOutcomes that move together, such as defaults in a recession
    Counts of rare eventsPoissonBursts: use a negative binomial if spread exceeds the average
    Averages of many valuesNormalValues that move together, or very heavy tails
    Amounts, many small and a few hugeLog-normalMean far above median
    Extreme lossesPareto (heavy tail)Averages that never settle
    • Strong at: turning "how likely?" into numbers you can compare and update.
    • Weak at: hiding assumptions. Every distribution assumes something, such as independence, that real data often breaks.

    In banking, a fraud model that flags "95% of fraud" says nothing about how many alerts are real, and transaction amounts and fraud losses are heavy-tailed, so quote medians and high percentiles, not just averages.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting the detection rate as the hit rate

      "Catches 95% of cases" does not say how many positives are real. Ask how many of every 100 alerts were confirmed.

    • Scored on one base rate, used on another

      Average the model's predicted probabilities on live data and compare with the real rate. If they differ, rescale.

    • Using the average on skewed data

      A few huge values drag the mean up. Report the median and a high percentile too.

    1. A test is 99% accurate but the condition affects 1 in 10,000. A positive result: likely real or likely false?

    Likely false. Among 10,000 people about 1 has it and about 100 healthy people are flagged, so most positives are false alarms.

    2. What is the difference between detection rate and hit rate?

    Detection rate is the share of real cases flagged. Hit rate is the share of flagged cases that are real.

    3. Order sizes have a mean of AED 650 and a median of AED 200. What does that tell you?

    The data is skewed: most orders are small and a few large ones pull the mean up. The median describes a typical order better.

    4. A model trained on a 50/50 sample is used where only 1% are positive. What goes wrong?

    Its probabilities are far too high, because it assumes the wrong base rate. The clues are fine, so rescale rather than retrain.
