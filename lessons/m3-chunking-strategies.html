    Chunking and Vector Search

    Part 1

    What chunking and vector search are

    In one sentenceChunking cuts documents into passages small enough to search and show to a language model, and vector search finds the nearest passages to a question quickly, without comparing against every one.

    Our running example is a staff copilot that answers questions from a 400-page credit policy manual. An officer asks: "Can we lend to a self-employed customer with 11 months of trading history?" Page 212 says 24 months are required. The next line says 12 months is acceptable for customers of more than five years, with Credit Committee approval.

    Why both matter

    • Chunking decides what a "passage" is. It is a bet on what a question will need.
    • Vector search decides how fast and how faithfully the nearest passages come back. It is also a bet: it gives up a little accuracy for a lot of speed.
    • Neither failure shows in the answer. A missed exception gives a fluent, confident, wrong reply. So test retrieval itself, before the model speaks.

    Vectors and cosine similarity are explained in the previous lesson. Here a vector is simply the list of numbers an embedding model gives each chunk.

    Part 2

    Chunking

    A chunk is the unit of retrieval: one vector, one search hit, one passage handed to the model. Measure size in tokens (the word-pieces a model reads), not characters. Cut the manual every 500 characters ignoring sentence ends, and the rule lands in one chunk and the exception in the next. Search finds the first, and the model answers "No".

    Ways to cut

    1. Fixed-size window. Every N tokens, optionally overlapping. Simple, but cuts mid-sentence.
    2. Recursive. Split on paragraphs, then sentences, then words, only as fine as needed. The usual default.
    3. Structure-aware. Split on headings, clauses and tables, and keep the heading path with each chunk. Best for policies and contracts, but needs good document parsing first.
    4. Parent-child. Search small chunks, but return the larger section that contains the match.
    5. Contextualised. Add a short description ("Retail Credit Policy, 4.2, self-employed") to each chunk before embedding. This helps chunks like "Exception: 12 months is acceptable", which mean nothing alone, but costs a model call per chunk.

    How to choose size and overlap

    • Size. Think of a filing folder. One holding five topics matches none well, and one holding a single page lacks the answer. Start at 200 to 800 tokens, then size to the shortest self-contained unit, such as a FAQ pair or a regulation article.
    • Overlap. Repeat the last 10 to 20% of each chunk at the start of the next, so a fact on a boundary survives. The cost is a bigger index and duplicate hits. It helps fixed windows a lot and structure-aware splitting little.
    • Metadata. Store document, section path, date, version, language and who may see it. Without dates, old and new policy versions are both retrieved and the model blends them.

    The tension: small chunks match sharply but lose surrounding facts, and large chunks keep context but dilute the match. Structure-aware and parent-child chunking aim at both.

    Part 3

    Vector search

    Exact search compares the question with every stored vector. That is fine up to roughly a million vectors. With five million chunks and hundreds of staff searching at once it is too slow, so production systems build an index, like a library catalogue. It jumps to the right neighbourhood and compares only a few thousand vectors.

    This is approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search. The jump is a guess: sometimes the true best match sits in a neighbourhood that was never visited. You accept finding, say, 95 of the true top 100 in return for a hundredfold speed-up. A vector database stores the vectors, metadata and index together.

    Three ideas, often combined

    1. IVF (group first). Sort the library into rooms and search the few most promising. It misses a neighbour shelved in a skipped room, usually one just across a boundary. Searching more rooms recovers it at higher cost.
    2. HNSW (a map you walk). Like finding a house by asking each neighbour who lives closer. The search hops to whichever linked vector is closer to the question, a few dozen steps instead of millions of comparisons. A wider search beam gives higher recall but is slower. It is the usual default, but the graph lives in memory and deletions are awkward.
    3. PQ (product quantisation, compress). Store each vector as a short string of codes, shrinking it about 64-fold: five million 1,024-number vectors drop from about 20 GB to about 0.3 GB. The cost is accuracy, so the fix is re-ranking: shortlist about 100 candidates from the compressed index, then re-score only those with exact distances.

    How to read the numbers

    • ANN recall is the share of the true top results the index returns. 0.95 means 95 of the true best 100.
    • Retrieval recall@k is the share of real questions where a correct passage is in the top k (5, say). 0.8 means 8 in 10 questions get it.
    • They are different numbers. "ANN recall is 99%" only says the index copies exact search faithfully. If exact search itself ranks the right passage low, a perfect index returns the wrong chunk efficiently.
    • How much recall you need depends on the use. A system that re-ranks 20 chunks survives missing the 7th best. Sanctions screening, which must find the matching record, does not.

    Filters

    Real queries are "nearest to this vector, among documents this user may see, in Arabic or English, effective after January 2026". Filtered search is where approximate indexes are most fragile. Search first and filter afterwards, and a narrow filter leaves almost nothing. Filter first and a graph can fall apart, since most links lead to excluded chunks. Test recall with your real filters, and enforce access control in retrieval, never in the prompt.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ChoiceStrong atWeak at
    Fixed windows with overlapSimple, any textSplits rules from exceptions
    Structure-awarePolicies, contracts, regulationsNeeds good parsing

    IVFCheap, easy to tuneMisses boundary neighbours
    HNSWHigh recall, low latencyUses memory, awkward deletes
    PQ plus re-rankingTiny memoryLoses accuracy without re-ranking
    • Under a few hundred thousand chunks, plain exact search is simple and often fast enough.
    • Evaluate chunking first. Take 50 to 200 real questions with known source passages and measure recall@5 under each candidate chunking, before comparing embedding models or prompts.
    • Plan for change. A withdrawn policy must vanish promptly. A new embedding model means re-embedding every chunk and rebuilding the index.

    In banking, a credit-policy copilot that misses one exception clause can give a confidently wrong lending answer, so test retrieval on known rule-and-exception pairs.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • A rule separated from its exception

      The rule chunk is retrieved, the exception chunk is not. Check ten clauses with known exceptions to see whether both come back together.

    • Chunks that make no sense alone

      "As described above, the Bank may, subject to Section 7..." has no subject. A fee table split without its header has no meaning. Read twenty random chunks; if you cannot tell what they are about, neither can the model.

    • Old versions still indexed

      Retrieval returns last year's limit and this year's. Add effective dates, and test that a withdrawn document disappears.

    • Measuring only the index

      "Recall is 98%" compares the index with exact search. Also measure retrieval recall@k on questions with known answers. Never mix vectors from different embedding models.

    1. What does overlap buy you, and what does it cost?

    It protects facts that straddle a boundary, because the end of one chunk repeats at the start of the next. It costs a larger index and duplicate hits.

    2. Why does enlarging chunks not always improve answers?

    Bigger chunks hold whole rules, but their vector blends several topics and matches the question less sharply. More irrelevant text also reaches the model.

    3. An IVF index has 1,000 rooms and searches 10. Roughly what share of vectors is scanned, and when does it miss?

    About 1%. It misses a neighbour that sits in a room outside the 10 nearest, typically near a room boundary.

    4. ANN recall is 99%, yet 20% of test questions fail. Is the index at fault?

    Almost certainly not. Look at chunking, the embedding model, missing keyword search, query wording or filters.

    5. Why does re-ranking work so well with PQ?

    Compressed distances are approximate, so the true top 10 are usually inside the compressed top 50 or 100. Re-scoring that shortlist with full vectors restores the order cheaply.
