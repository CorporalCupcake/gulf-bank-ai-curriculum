    Chunking and Vector Search: How Documents Are Cut Up and How They Are Found Fast

    Why the way you split a policy manual decides what a RAG system can ever find, and how HNSW, IVF and product quantisation make nearest-neighbour search over millions of chunks fast enough, at the price of sometimes missing the best answer.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA retrieval system can only return pieces you created, so chunking sets the ceiling on what it can find, and an approximate index then trades a small, measurable slice of recall for speed and memory, so you must measure both rather than assume either is fine.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A staff copilot at a Gulf bank answers questions from the 400-page credit policy manual. An officer asks: "Can we lend to a self-employed customer with 11 months of trading history?" Somewhere on page 212 the policy says "Self-employed applicants must show 24 months of trading history." On the next line: "Exception: 12 months is acceptable for customers of the bank for more than five years, with Credit Committee approval."

    Now imagine the manual was cut into pieces of 500 characters, with no regard for where sentences end. The rule lands in one piece and the exception in the next. The search engine finds the first piece, which is relevant, and hands it to the language model. The model says: "No, 24 months are required." It never saw the exception. Nothing malfunctioned. The failure happened months earlier, in a line of data-preparation code that chose a number.

    Cutting is only the first half. The second half is finding. Suppose the manual, plus every circular, product guide and complaint procedure, becomes five million chunks, each stored as a list of 1,024 numbers (an embedding, see F1.1). To answer a question you need the few chunks whose vectors are closest to the question's vector. Comparing against all five million takes a noticeable fraction of a second per query on a single machine, and a serving cluster has to do it for hundreds of staff at once. So production systems build an index that jumps to the right neighbourhood and compares against a few thousand vectors instead. The jump is a guess. Sometimes the true nearest neighbour is in a neighbourhood the index never visited.

    Two ideas, then, and one theme running through both.

    • Chunking is a bet about what a question will need. Big chunks keep context together but dilute the match and fill the model's window with noise. Small chunks match precisely but lose the surrounding facts. There is no universal size. There are structure, size and overlap choices, and you test them on your own documents.
    • Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search is a controlled bet too. You accept finding, say, 95 of the true top 100 in exchange for a hundredfold speed-up and a fraction of the memory. The settings are dials, and the loss is something you can measure.
    • Neither failure is visible in the answer. A missed exception or a missed neighbour produces a fluent, confident, wrong reply. You only see it if you test retrieval itself, before the model speaks.
    Hold onto thisAsk of any RAG system: what are the chunks, what is the retrieval recall on a test set of real questions, and what fraction of exact-search results does the index return? Those are three different numbers, and most teams only know the third, if any.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Chunking strategies

    A chunk is the unit of retrieval: one embedding, one search hit, one passage given to the model. Its size is measured in tokens (M1.1) rather than characters, because the embedding model and the language model both have token limits, and because Arabic and English text produce different token counts for the same sentence. The main strategies, from the simplest:

    Four design choices matter more than the names.

    • Size. A chunk that is too long mixes several topics into one vector, which then sits "between" them and matches none well; it also fills the context window with irrelevant text, which raises cost and can bury the answer (M1.5). A chunk that is too short can't carry the facts needed to answer. A common starting range is 200 to 800 tokens, but the right answer depends on the shortest self-contained unit in your documents. For a FAQ, one question and answer. For a regulation, one article.
    • Overlap. Repeating the last 10 to 20% of each chunk at the start of the next reduces the chance that a fact straddles a boundary, at the price of a larger index and duplicate hits. Overlap helps fixed windows a lot and structure-aware splitting very little, because the latter doesn't cut inside units.
    • Metadata. Each chunk should carry its document, section path, date, version, language, product and access class. This does three jobs: the heading path can be embedded with the text, filters can enforce who may see what (a hard requirement in a bank, see Part 3), and the citation shown to the user can point to a real page.
    • What counts as "the same document". The same policy exists in versions. If old and new versions are both indexed, retrieval returns both and the model blends them. Chunking is also where you attach the effective date and, ideally, retire superseded content.

    The tension in one sentence: the unit that is best for matching a query is small and focused, while the unit that is best for answering is large enough to hold the whole rule. Parent-child chunking, contextualised chunks and late chunking are three different ways to have both. A fourth is to retrieve small and then let an agent read around the hit (A6.3).

    Here is what a minimal structure-aware chunker looks like. It is about twelve lines, and it encodes three decisions: split on headings, never exceed a token cap, and keep the heading path as context.

    def chunk_policy(sections, max_tokens=500):
    chunks = []
    for sec in sections:                      # sec.path = "Retail credit > 4.2 Self-employed"
        buf, n = [], 0
        for para in sec.paragraphs:           # paragraphs, kept whole
            t = count_tokens(para)
            if buf and n + t > max_tokens:   # flush before overflowing
                chunks.append(make(sec, buf)); buf, n = [], 0
            buf.append(para); n += t
        if buf: chunks.append(make(sec, buf))
    return chunks                             # make() prepends sec.path and attaches metadata

    A real one also needs a fallback for a single paragraph longer than the cap, a rule for tables (keep a table whole, or one row per chunk with the header repeated), and special handling for lists whose first line is the introduction to the rest.

    Three things the simulation shows that rules of thumb hide. First, coverage and focus pull in opposite directions for fixed windows: there is no size that gives both. Second, structure beats size: respecting section boundaries reaches high coverage at much smaller chunks, which is also cheaper to send to the model. Third, overlap is a patch for blind cutting. It is useful, but it buys coverage with index size and duplicate results.

    Chunking has its own evaluation: take 50 to 200 real questions with known source passages, run retrieval under each candidate chunking, and measure how often the right passage appears in the top 5 (recall@5) and how often the answer is actually derivable from what was retrieved. Do this before comparing embedding models or prompts. Embedding model and chunking interact, so redo it when either changes.

    Vector databases and ANN indexes: HNSW, IVF, PQ

    Exact nearest-neighbour search compares the query with every stored vector: with N vectors of dimension d, that is about N × d multiplications per query. For five million 1,024-dimensional vectors, that is over five billion operations per query. Modern hardware can do it (and for under about a million vectors, exact search is often the right answer), but latency and cost scale linearly. At 100 million vectors it stops being a sensible plan for interactive use.

    A vector database is a store that keeps the vectors, their metadata and an index together, and answers "give me the k nearest to this vector, among chunks matching these filters". Examples include dedicated systems and also extensions to databases the bank already runs, such as pgvector for PostgreSQL. The interesting part is the index. Three ideas do nearly all the work, and real systems combine them.

    IVF: group first, then search a few groups

    Inverted file (IVF) indexes run k-means clustering over the vectors, so each vector belongs to the cell of its nearest of nlist centroids. At query time, find the few centroids closest to the query (cheap, since there are only a few hundred), then scan only the vectors in those nprobe cells. With nlist = 1,000 and nprobe = 10, you look at roughly 1% of the data. The failure is geometric: a true neighbour sitting just across a cell boundary lives in a cell you did not probe. Raising nprobe recovers it at higher cost. Think of a library sorted into rooms: you search the three rooms that sound most relevant, and a book shelved in a fourth is invisible.

    HNSW: a map you walk across

    Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) builds a graph where each vector links to a handful of nearby vectors. To search, start somewhere, look at the neighbours of your current position, step to whichever is closest to the query, and repeat until no neighbour is closer. Walking a graph like this finds the right region in a few dozen steps, not a few million comparisons. The "hierarchical" part adds sparse upper layers with long-range links, like motorways above local roads, so the walk starts near the target instead of at a random point. Two knobs matter: M, how many links per vector (more links, better graph, more memory), and ef, how many candidates the search keeps in play (a wider beam, higher recall, slower). HNSW is the default in most vector databases because it gives high recall at low latency. Its costs are memory (the graph lives in RAM, on top of the vectors) and slower, more awkward updates and deletions.

    PQ: compress the vectors themselves

    Product quantisation (PQ) shrinks each vector. Split the 1,024 numbers into m pieces, say 64 pieces of 16 numbers. For each piece, learn a codebook of 256 typical 16-number patterns. Replace each piece with the index of its nearest pattern: one byte. A 4,096-byte float vector becomes 64 bytes, a 64-fold saving. Distances are then estimated by table lookups: precompute the distance from the query's piece to each of the 256 patterns, and sum m table entries per stored vector. The cost is accuracy: you now rank by approximate distances. The standard remedy is re-ranking: use the compressed index to shortlist, say, 100 candidates, then compute exact distances for only those, using full vectors kept on disk or in cheaper memory.

    memory≈N·d·4 bytes (float32)versusN·m bytes (PQ with 256 codewords per piece)

    N is the number of chunks, d the embedding dimension and m the number of pieces. Five million chunks at 1,024 dimensions is 5,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 ≈ 20.5 GB of raw vectors. With m = 64 the codes take 0.32 GB. An HNSW graph with 16 links per vector adds a few hundred bytes per vector on top (roughly 2 × 16 × 4 bytes at the base layer), about 0.6 to 1 GB here.

    Other methods fall under the same ideas. DiskANN (2019) keeps a graph and compressed codes in memory but full vectors on SSD, so one machine can serve billions of vectors. ScaNN (Google) refines the quantisation to preserve the inner products that matter for ranking. Scalar quantisation to 8 or 4 bits per number, and binary quantisation to one bit, are cruder but simpler compression. FAISS, the library that packaged these methods, gives names like IVF4096,PQ64 to combinations. The Matryoshka embeddings of R10.1 attack the same memory problem from the model side, by making short prefixes of the vector useful on their own.

    What to read from the curves. Both families bend: the first improvements are cheap and the last few points of recall are expensive. Pushing from 90% to 99% recall can cost several times more than reaching 90%. Which recall you need depends on the use. A RAG system that retrieves 20 chunks and re-ranks them is robust to missing the 7th best; a duplicate-detection or sanctions-screening system that must find the matching record is not (B2.3).

    Recall at the ANN level is not answer quality. It measures how faithfully the index reproduces exact search. If exact search itself returns the wrong chunk because of poor chunking, a perfect index reproduces the error efficiently. Both layers need their own tests.

    Filters: the part that bites in a bank

    Real queries are never "nearest to this vector, full stop". They are "nearest to this vector, among documents this user may see, in English or Arabic, effective after January 2026, for the retail product". This is filtered search, and it is where approximate indexes are most fragile. If the index finds the 40 nearest vectors first and filters afterwards, a filter that keeps 5% of the data leaves you with about two results. If it filters first, the graph can fall apart, because most links lead to excluded nodes. Systems handle it differently (filter-aware graph traversal, partitioned indexes, per-tenant indexes, iterative scans that keep going until enough results pass), and behaviour differs across products and versions. Two consequences: test recall with the filters you will really use, and enforce access control in the retrieval layer, never in the prompt. A chunk the user may not see must not reach the model at all.

    Other operational facts worth knowing: updates and deletes (a withdrawn policy must disappear promptly, and some index types handle deletion awkwardly), index rebuilds when you change the embedding model (every vector must be re-computed, as vectors from different models are not comparable), and the choice between exact search for small collections and an ANN index for large ones. If the collection is under a few hundred thousand chunks, brute force on one machine is simple, exact and often fast enough.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Chunking and indexing are the plumbing in the middle of the RAG pipeline. Their choices echo upstream and downstream.

    M3.1 · M3.2 · Keyword search and BM25The other way to find a chunk

    BM25 searches the same chunks by exact words. Account numbers, product codes and clause numbers match there and not in embeddings. Chunking affects BM25 too: a term's weight depends on chunk length.

    M3.3 · M3.4 · Hybrid search and fusionRun both, merge the ranks

    Production retrieval usually combines a vector index with a keyword index. Both draw on the chunks you create here, and the ANN recall you accept feeds the fusion step.

    M3.7 · Query rewriting and HyDEFix the question, not the chunks

    A short, vague query matches long chunks badly. Rewriting the query, or generating a hypothetical answer to embed, bridges the gap from the other side.

    M3.9 · Parsing messy PDFs and scansStructure-aware chunking needs structure

    You can only respect headings, tables and clauses if the parser found them. A scrambled two-column PDF cannot be rescued by a clever chunker.

    M2.3 · RAG versus prompting versus fine-tuningWhen retrieval is the right tool

    That lesson decides whether to retrieve at all. This one decides how well the retrieval part performs once you have chosen it.

    M1.1 · M1.5 · Tokens and contextChunk size is spent from a budget

    Five hits of 800 tokens is 4,000 tokens of context on every question. At volume, that is a bill and a latency cost; irrelevant padding also degrades answers.

    F1.1 · Vectors and similarityWhat "near" means

    Cosine similarity and dot product are the distances ANN indexes approximate. Normalising vectors makes the two coincide, which many indexes assume.

    F5.7 · Contrastive learningWhere embeddings come from

    Embedding models are trained so that matching question-passage pairs land close together. A chunk that looks unlike training passages (a table, a legal clause list) embeds worse.

    A6.1 · A6.3 · Agentic RAG and visual retrievalBeyond fixed pipelines

    Agents can search again, read around a hit and follow cross-references, which softens chunking errors. Page-image retrieval sidesteps text chunking for layout-heavy documents.

    A4.3 · GroundednessChecking the answer against its sources

    If the evidence was never retrieved, no groundedness check can save the answer. Evaluate retrieval first, generation second.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Staff copilots over policy and procedure (B7.3). Numbered clauses, exceptions, version dates and entitlement by role. Structure-aware chunks, effective-date filters and access filters are the core design. Bilingual manuals need a decision on whether Arabic and English versions are separate chunks, linked chunks or one chunk, and an embedding model that handles both.
    • Trade finance and contract documents (B7.1). Letters of credit and guarantees where the answer depends on a table or an annex referred to in a clause. Cross-references mean a chunk is not self-contained: contextualised chunks or parent-child retrieval help.
    • Regulatory libraries. Circulars from the Central Bank of the UAE or SAMA, and internal mappings to them. Articles amend articles; keep the amendment history and dates in metadata so a superseded text is not quoted as current.
    • Customer service and complaints. Short, self-contained FAQ answers want small chunks. Case notes and call transcripts are the opposite: speaker turns, timing, and the need to see the whole conversation. Transcripts chunked by fixed windows cut customer from agent reply.
    • Privacy and entitlements. Chunking multiplies the number of items needing access control. A document-level permission must be copied onto every chunk, and permission changes must reach the index. Embedding vectors can leak information about source text, so treat the vector store as sensitive as the documents.
    • Non-RAG uses of the same indexes. Finding similar past fraud cases, near-duplicate customer records, or candidate entities for screening. There the recall of the index is itself a control, not a convenience.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The index algorithms are mature and the open questions have moved. For chunking, the debate is about whether clever strategies beat sensible simple ones, and about adding context to chunks. For vector search, the live issues are filtered search, very large and disk-based indexes, and cheap compression.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These problems produce fluent wrong answers rather than error messages. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • A rule separated from its exception

      The chunk with the rule is retrieved. The chunk with the exception is not. The answer is confidently wrong.

      Spot itTake ten clauses with known exceptions. Check whether rule and exception appear in the same retrieved chunk, or whether the cut falls between them.
    • Chunks that make no sense alone

      "As described above, the Bank may, subject to Section 7, ..." has no subject in isolation. Its embedding is vague and it matches nothing well.

      Spot itRead twenty random chunks without context. If you cannot tell what they are about, neither can the embedding model. Add the heading path or a context line.
    • Tables and lists chopped into confetti

      A fee schedule split into rows without its header: "0.75%, AED 150, AED 3,000" with no idea what they are fees for.

      Spot itRetrieve for a question answered only by a table. If the numbers return without column labels, the table was chunked blindly.
    • Old and new versions both indexed

      Retrieval returns last year's limit and this year's limit. The model averages or picks one.

      Spot itSearch for a value that changed in a recent revision and see whether two versions return. Check that effective-date metadata exists and filters are applied.
    • Only measuring the index, not the retrieval

      "Recall is 98%." That is the index versus exact search. It says nothing about whether exact search finds the right passage.

      Spot itAsk for two numbers: ANN recall against brute force, and retrieval recall@k on questions with known answers.
    • Recall quietly collapses under filters

      The index test is run without filters, production always uses a role and language filter, and a narrow filter returns three weak results.

      Spot itMeasure recall at the filter selectivities you really use, against exact filtered search. Watch for queries that return fewer than k results.
    • Chunks too big, context stuffed

      Ten 1,500-token hits cost 15,000 tokens a query, and the answer sits in the middle where models read least reliably.

      Spot itLog tokens of retrieved context per query. Compare answer quality at 3 and at 10 chunks; if it does not improve, you are paying for padding.
    • Mixing embedding models

      Documents embedded with one model, queries with another, or half the index re-embedded after an upgrade. Distances are meaningless.

      Spot itStore the model name and version with every vector, and refuse to query across versions.
    • Access control in the prompt

      "Do not reveal documents the user is not allowed to see", while those documents are in the context.

      Spot itQuery as a low-privilege test user and inspect the retrieved chunks themselves, not the final answer.
    • Stale index after deletion

      A withdrawn circular is removed from the document store but its chunks stay in the index.

      Spot itWithdraw a test document and query it an hour later. Check how deletes propagate and when graph indexes are rebuilt.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The assistant can only quote pieces of our policies that we've cut out and indexed in advance. If a rule and its exception end up in different pieces, it can give a confident wrong answer. So we cut along the manuals' own structure, label every piece with its version and who may see it, and test on a few hundred real questions before launch. The search itself is a speed-for-accuracy trade, and we measure how often it misses what an exhaustive search would have found.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd chunk structurally with a recursive fallback at a few hundred tokens, carrying the section path and effective-date and entitlement metadata, then build a labelled question set to compare chunkings on recall@5 and answer correctness. For the index I'd start with HNSW, tune ef against exact search to a target recall, test it under the production filters, and add quantisation with exact re-ranking only if memory demands it. Access control is enforced as a filter at retrieval, not in the prompt, and every vector carries its embedding-model version.”
    “What chunk size should we use?”

    There isn't a universal number. I'd start with a few hundred tokens split on the documents' own structure, and then test two or three sizes on a set of real questions. The smallest self-contained unit in the content, a clause or an FAQ answer, is a better guide than any benchmark. If one size can't serve both factual lookups and explanatory questions, use small chunks for matching and return the parent section.

    “Isn't semantic chunking just better?”

    Not reliably. Published comparisons are mixed: some find it retrieves slightly more relevant passages but produces fragments too small to answer from, so end-to-end accuracy falls. It also costs an embedding pass at indexing time. I'd treat it as one candidate in the test, with a minimum chunk size, and keep it only if it wins on our documents.

    “Why not just search everything exactly? Isn't approximate search a risk?”

    For a collection up to a few hundred thousand chunks, exact search is often fine, and I'd use it. Beyond that, latency and cost grow linearly. Approximate indexes give large speed-ups for a small loss that we can measure: we compare the index's top 10 with brute force on a sample of queries and tune until recall meets our target. The risk is real only where a miss is costly, such as sanctions matching, and there we would set the target higher or use exact search.

    “HNSW or IVF?”

    HNSW is the usual default: high recall at low latency, but the graph lives in memory and deletions and rebuilds are awkward. IVF is cheaper to build and combines naturally with quantisation, which matters at very large scale or tight memory. The decision comes down to collection size, update pattern, memory budget and the recall target, so I'd benchmark both on our own vectors and filters.

    “The model gave a wrong answer. Is it the model or the retrieval?”

    Look at what was retrieved for that question before blaming the model. If the correct passage is absent from the top results, the fault is in chunking, embedding, filtering or the index, and a better prompt won't help. If it was present and the model ignored or misread it, it's a generation problem. Logging retrieved chunks per query makes that a one-minute check.

    “How do we keep restricted documents away from the wrong staff?”

    With filters at retrieval time, using entitlement metadata copied onto every chunk and synchronised with the source system's permissions. The model must never receive text the user may not see, since prompt instructions are not a security control. I'd test with low-privilege accounts, and I'd check that filtered recall holds up, because narrow filters are where approximate indexes weaken. I don't know offhand what the bank's specific data-classification rules require, so I would confirm those with information security.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A 100,000-token manual is cut into fixed windows of 500 tokens with 100 tokens of overlap. Roughly how many chunks, and how many tokens go into the index in total?

    The window advances 400 tokens each time, so about (100,000 − 100) ÷ 400 ≈ 250 chunks. Index tokens ≈ 250 × 500 = 125,000, which is 25% more than the manual, the price of the overlap.

    2. Why does enlarging chunks raise "whole-section coverage" but not necessarily answer quality?

    Bigger chunks are more likely to contain a whole rule and its exception, so coverage rises. But the embedding then represents several topics at once and matches the question less sharply, the share of each hit that is relevant falls, and more irrelevant tokens enter the model's context at greater cost. Quality peaks at a middle size and depends on the documents.

    3. Five million chunks, 1,024 dimensions, float32. What is the raw vector memory, and what is it with 64-byte PQ codes?

    5,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 = 20.48 GB raw. PQ: 5,000,000 × 64 = 0.32 GB, a 64-fold reduction. Add graph links and metadata on top, and keep the full vectors somewhere cheaper if you want to re-rank.

    4. An IVF index with 1,000 cells probes 10 of them. About what fraction of the vectors does it scan, and when does this miss a true neighbour?

    About 1% (assuming reasonably even cells), plus the cheap comparison against 1,000 centroids. It misses a neighbour when that neighbour sits in a cell that is not among the 10 nearest centroids to the query, typically a point near a cell boundary. Probing more cells reduces the misses.

    5. The team reports "ANN recall is 99%". The assistant still fails 20% of test questions on retrieval. Is the index at fault?

    Almost certainly not. 99% recall means the index reproduces exact search nearly perfectly. If exact search itself ranks the right passage low, the problem lies elsewhere: chunking, the embedding model, missing keyword search, the query wording or the filters. Measure retrieval recall against labelled questions, using exact search as the control.

    6. A filter keeps 2% of chunks. The index searches for the 40 nearest and then filters. How many results survive, roughly, and what are the fixes?

    About 0.02 × 40 ≈ 1 result, assuming the 40 are a random sample with respect to the filter, which understates the problem when the filter correlates with the query. Fixes: filter-aware traversal that keeps searching until enough pass, a larger search width, separate indexes per tenant or language, or exact search over the filtered subset when that subset is small.

    7. You upgrade the embedding model. What must happen to the index, and why?

    Every chunk must be re-embedded and the index rebuilt. Vectors from different models live in different spaces, so distances between an old document vector and a new query vector are meaningless. Plan for the compute cost, a side-by-side retrieval evaluation before cut-over, and a model-version tag on every vector.

    8. Why is re-ranking with exact distances so effective when combined with product quantisation?

    PQ distances are approximate, so the true top 10 are usually inside the compressed top 50 or 100 even if their order within it is slightly off. Re-scoring just that shortlist with full-precision vectors restores the order cheaply: you pay for a few dozen exact distances, not millions. Simulation 3 shows recall returning to about 99% at 8 pieces, though it cannot rescue over-compressed codes that miss the true neighbours entirely.
