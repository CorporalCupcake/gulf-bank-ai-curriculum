    Chunking and Vector Search: How Documents Are Cut Up and How They Are Found Fast

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A staff copilot answers questions from a 400-page credit policy manual. An officer asks: "Can we lend to a self-employed customer with 11 months of trading history?" Page 212 says 24 months are required. The next line says 12 months is acceptable for customers of more than five years, with Credit Committee approval.

    Cut the manual into 500-character pieces, ignoring sentence ends, and the rule lands in one piece and the exception in the next. Search finds the first, and the model answers "No". Nothing malfunctioned. The failure was a number chosen months earlier in data-preparation code.

    The other half of the job is finding. With five million chunks, each an embedding (F1.1), comparing the question against every one is too slow for hundreds of staff at once. So production systems build an index, like a library catalogue, that jumps to the right neighbourhood and compares only a few thousand vectors. The jump is a guess, and sometimes the true best match sits in a neighbourhood never visited.

    • Chunking is a bet on what a question will need. Big chunks keep context but dilute the match. Small chunks match sharply but lose surrounding facts. Test on your own documents.
    • Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search is a controlled bet. You accept finding, say, 95 of the true top 100 for a hundredfold speed-up and far less memory.
    • Neither failure shows in the answer. A missed exception gives a fluent, confident, wrong reply. Test retrieval itself, before the model speaks.
    Hold onto thisAsk of any RAG system: what are the chunks, what is retrieval recall on real questions, and what share of exact-search results does the index return? Three different numbers. Most teams know only the third, if any.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Chunking strategies

    A chunk is the unit of retrieval: one embedding, one hit, one passage handed to the model. Measure size in tokens (M1.1), not characters. Strategies, simplest first:

    StrategyHow it cutsGood atWeak at
    Fixed-size windowEvery C tokens, optionally overlapping so neighbours share an edge.Simple, works on any text.Cuts mid-sentence. Splits a rule from its exception.
    RecursiveSplit on paragraphs, then sentences, then words, only as fine as needed.Respects natural breaks. The usual default.Blind to meaning and layout.
    Structure-awareSplit on headings, clauses, tables, pages. Keep the heading path with each chunk.Policies, contracts, regulations.Needs good parsing first (M3.9).
    Parent-childEmbed small children, return the larger parent that contains the match.Small-chunk precision, large-chunk context.More plumbing.
    ContextualisedPrepend a short description ("Retail Credit Policy, 4.2, self-employed") to each chunk before embedding.Chunks meaningless alone, like "Exception: 12 months is acceptable."An LLM call per chunk; description errors become index errors.

    Semantic chunking (cut where neighbouring sentences stop being similar) and late chunking (embed the whole document first, then split) exist too, but gains over the options above are mixed. Three design choices matter more than the names.

    • Size. Think of a filing-cabinet folder: one holding five topics matches none well, and one holding a single page lacks the answer. Start at 200 to 800 tokens, then size to the shortest self-contained unit, such as a FAQ pair or a regulation article.
    • Overlap. Repeat the last 10 to 20% of each chunk at the start of the next, so a fact straddling a boundary survives. The cost is a bigger index and duplicate hits. It helps fixed windows a lot and structure-aware splitting little.
    • Metadata and versions. Carry document, section path, date, version, language and access class. Index old and new policy versions together and retrieval returns both, which the model blends. Attach effective dates and retire superseded content.

    The tension: the best unit for matching is small and focused, and the best for answering is large enough to hold the whole rule. Parent-child and contextualised chunks aim at both.

    • Coverage and focus pull apart for fixed windows. No size gives both.
    • Structure beats size. Respecting sections reaches high coverage at smaller, cheaper chunks.
    • Overlap is a patch for blind cutting. It buys coverage with index size.

    To evaluate chunking, take 50 to 200 real questions with known source passages and run retrieval under each candidate chunking. Measure how often the right passage is in the top 5 (recall@5: 0.8 means 8 in 10 questions get it). Do this before comparing embedding models or prompts.

    Vector databases and ANN indexes

    Exact search compares the query with every stored vector. That is fine up to roughly a million vectors, and not viable for interactive use at 100 million. A vector database stores vectors, metadata and an index together, and answers "the k nearest to this vector, among chunks matching these filters". Three ideas do the work, and real systems combine them.

    • IVF: group first, then search a few groups. Like a library sorted into rooms: you search the few most promising rooms. It misses a neighbour shelved in a room you skipped, usually one just across a boundary. Probing more rooms recovers it at higher cost.
    • HNSW: a map you walk across. Like finding a house by asking each neighbour who lives closer. Each vector links to nearby vectors, and the search hops to whichever is closer to the query, a few dozen steps instead of millions of comparisons. A wider search beam gives higher recall but is slower. It is the usual default for high recall at low latency, but the graph sits in RAM and deletions are awkward.
    • PQ (product quantisation): compress the vectors. Like rounding every price to the nearest catalogue code. Each vector is stored as a short string of codes, shrinking it about 64-fold: five million 1,024-dimension vectors drop from about 20 GB to about 0.3 GB. The cost is accuracy, so the fix is re-ranking: shortlist about 100 candidates from the compressed index, then re-score only those with exact distances.

    Both curves bend: early gains are cheap and the last points of recall are expensive. The recall you need depends on the use. A RAG system that re-ranks 20 chunks survives missing the 7th best. Sanctions screening, which must find the matching record, does not (B2.3).

    ANN recall is not answer quality. It measures how faithfully the index copies exact search. If chunking makes exact search return the wrong chunk, a perfect index returns it efficiently. Test both layers.

    Filters: the part that bites in a bank

    Real queries are "nearest to this vector, among documents this user may see, in English or Arabic, effective after January 2026". This filtered search is where approximate indexes are most fragile. Search first and filter afterwards, and a narrow filter leaves almost nothing. Filter first and the graph can fall apart, since most links lead to excluded nodes. So test recall with your real filters, and enforce access control in retrieval, never in the prompt. A chunk the user may not see must not reach the model.

    Also plan for deletes (a withdrawn policy must vanish promptly) and rebuilds when the embedding model changes, since vectors from different models are not comparable. Under a few hundred thousand chunks, plain exact search is simple and often fast enough.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These produce fluent wrong answers, not errors. Each has a quick check.

    • A rule separated from its exception

      The chunk with the rule is retrieved. The chunk with the exception is not. The answer is confidently wrong.

      Spot itTake ten clauses with known exceptions. Check whether rule and exception appear in the same retrieved chunk.
    • Chunks that make no sense alone

      "As described above, the Bank may, subject to Section 7, ..." has no subject in isolation. A fee table split into rows without its header is the same problem: "0.75%, AED 150" with no idea what they are fees for.

      Spot itRead twenty random chunks without context. If you cannot tell what they are about, neither can the embedding model. Add the heading path or a context line.
    • Old versions and stale deletions

      Retrieval returns last year's limit and this year's, and the model averages or picks one. A withdrawn circular stays searchable because its chunks were never removed from the index.

      Spot itSearch for a value that changed recently, and withdraw a test document and query it an hour later. Check effective-date metadata and delete handling.
    • Only measuring the index, not the retrieval

      "Recall is 98%." That is the index versus exact search. It says nothing about whether exact search finds the right passage.

      Spot itAsk for two numbers: ANN recall against brute force, and retrieval recall@k on questions with known answers.
    • Filters and access control done wrongly

      The index test is run without filters, but production always filters by role and language, and a narrow filter returns three weak results. Or "do not reveal documents the user may not see" sits in the prompt while those documents are in the context.

      Spot itMeasure recall at the filter selectivities you really use. Query as a low-privilege test user and inspect the retrieved chunks themselves, not the final answer.
    • Mixing embedding models

      Documents embedded with one model, queries with another, or half the index re-embedded after an upgrade. Distances are meaningless.

      Spot itStore the model name and version with every vector, and refuse to query across versions.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. What does overlap between fixed windows buy you, and what does it cost?

    It buys protection for facts that straddle a boundary, because the end of one chunk is repeated at the start of the next. It costs a larger index (20 to 25% more text at typical settings) and duplicate hits. It is a patch for blind cutting, and does little for structure-aware splitting.

    2. Why does enlarging chunks raise "whole-section coverage" but not necessarily answer quality?

    Bigger chunks are more likely to contain a whole rule and its exception, so coverage rises. But the embedding then represents several topics at once and matches the question less sharply, and more irrelevant tokens enter the model's context at greater cost. Quality peaks at a middle size and depends on the documents.

    3. Why does memory become the pressure point at millions of chunks, and what does product quantisation trade away?

    Raw vectors add up fast: five million 1,024-dimension vectors are about 20 GB before any index. PQ shrinks them roughly 64-fold by storing short codes instead of full numbers. The price is accuracy, since ranking now uses approximate distances. Keep the full vectors somewhere cheaper so you can re-rank.

    4. An IVF index with 1,000 cells probes 10 of them. About what fraction of the vectors does it scan, and when does it miss a true neighbour?

    About 1%. It misses a neighbour when that neighbour sits in a cell not among the 10 nearest to the query, typically a point near a cell boundary. Probing more cells reduces the misses.

    5. The team reports "ANN recall is 99%". The assistant still fails 20% of test questions on retrieval. Is the index at fault?

    Almost certainly not. 99% recall means the index reproduces exact search nearly perfectly. If exact search itself ranks the right passage low, the problem lies elsewhere: chunking, the embedding model, missing keyword search, the query wording or the filters. Measure retrieval recall against labelled questions, using exact search as the control.

    6. Why does "find the 40 nearest, then filter" go wrong when the filter is narrow, and what are the fixes?

    If the filter keeps only a few percent of chunks, most of the 40 are thrown away and you are left with one or two weak results. Fixes: filter-aware traversal that keeps searching until enough pass, a wider search, separate indexes per tenant or language, or exact search over the filtered subset when it is small.

    7. You upgrade the embedding model. What must happen to the index, and why?

    Every chunk must be re-embedded and the index rebuilt. Vectors from different models live in different spaces, so distances between an old document vector and a new query vector are meaningless. Plan for the compute cost, a side-by-side retrieval evaluation before cut-over, and a model-version tag on every vector.

    8. Why is re-ranking with exact distances so effective when combined with product quantisation?

    PQ distances are approximate, so the true top 10 are usually inside the compressed top 50 or 100 even if their order is slightly off. Re-scoring just that shortlist with full-precision vectors restores the order cheaply. Simulation 3 shows recall returning to about 99% at 8 pieces, though it cannot rescue over-compressed codes that miss the true neighbours entirely.
