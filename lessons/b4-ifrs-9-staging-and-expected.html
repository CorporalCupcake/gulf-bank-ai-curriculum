    From Model to Balance Sheet: IFRS 9 Provisions, Stress Tests and the Applicants You Never Saw

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 1 million to a healthy manufacturer for five years. The old rule booked no loss until something went wrong. In 2008 that proved too late: provisions jumped after the damage, like fitting the smoke alarm after the fire.

    IFRS 9 (in force for banks since 2018) says: provision a little on day one, then much more as quality worsens, before default. That provision is the expected credit loss (ECL): the probability-weighted average loss, discounted to today.

    The key choice is the horizon. A fine loan is provisioned for the next 12 months of losses. A materially riskier one is provisioned for losses over its whole remaining life. That step is deliberate and large, and it turns a modelling question ("has risk risen enough?") into a jump in reported profit.

    Two ideas sit beside it. Stress testing asks how large provisions get, and whether capital holds, if oil falls 40%. Reject inference asks about the borrowers the bank turned away, who never appear in any PD model's training data.

    • The provision is an expected value, not a forecast. Like a 30% chance of rain, it is right across many days, not on any one. No single loan loses 0.43% of its balance, but across thousands the average holds.
    • The cliff creates incentives. A small PD change near the trigger can move millions of dirhams, so whoever owns the staging model holds a lever on reported profit.
    Hold onto thisECL is PD times LGD times EAD, discounted, over a horizon set by the stage. Stress testing re-runs it under a shock. Reject inference asks whether the PD was learned from a biased sample. One pipeline, three angles.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B4.3 · IFRS 9 staging and expected credit loss

    The inputs come from B4.1–B4.2: PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss. IFRS 9 adds a time dimension and a staging rule. For each future year in the horizon:

    ECL=PD×LGD×EAD×discount

    The PD is the chance of defaulting in that year, having survived until then. Example: PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 1,000,000. Stage 1 counts one year: about AED 9,000. With three years left, the lifetime figure is about AED 26,500, nearly three times as much, simply because more years are counted.

    The three stages

    StageWho is in itProvision measured as
    Stage 1Performing; no significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since origination.12-month ECL
    Stage 2Performing, but credit risk has increased significantly. More than 30 days past due is presumed to qualify.Lifetime ECL
    Stage 3Credit-impaired or in default; 90 days past due is presumed default. Banks usually align with their regulatory definition.Lifetime ECL (in practice close to LGD times exposure)

    The hard part is SICR, the test for moving from Stage 1 to 2. The standard gives no number, so banks combine three tools:

    • Quantitative test: current PD against origination PD, as a ratio (say 2 to 3 times) or an absolute rise. This is where ML usually enters.
    • Qualitative test: watchlist status, forbearance, covenant breaches.
    • Backstop: 30 days past due. A good model has moved the loan well before.

    You need the PD at origination, stored and frozen; a bank that lost it cannot stage consistently. And a safe borrower whose PD doubles from 0.1% to 0.2% has risen "significantly" in relative terms yet is still very safe, so relative tests are paired with absolute floors.

    IFRS 9 wants point-in-time PDs that move with current conditions and forecasts, unlike the stable through-the-cycle PDs used for Basel capital. It also needs a PD for each future year, usually from a survival model (F2.8).

    Forward-looking scenarios: why the average is not the base case

    The standard requires probability-weighted amounts reflecting economic forecasts. Banks run a base, an upside and one or two downsides, compute ECL under each, and weight them. The weights are management judgement.

    Why not just use the base case? Losses are convex in the economy, like a thin bridge: a bad year breaks more than a good year saves. So the weighted average of the ECLs exceeds the ECL of the average economy. Simulation 2 shows the size.

    Management overlays are post-model adjustments for risks the model cannot see, such as a new sector concentration or a geopolitical event. They are fine if documented and released when the cause fades; a large, long-lived overlay means the model is not doing its job.

    Capital and Islamic finance. Capital treatment of provisions is set in CBUAE and SAMA notices; check the current texts with your risk and finance teams. IFRS 9 covers Islamic banks' assets too (murabaha, ijara), so the ECL framework is the same.

    B4.4 · Stress testing

    ECL is a best estimate. A stress test asks a different question: in a severe but plausible scenario, does the bank survive? Think of a flight simulator: you do not expect the engine failure, you check the pilot copes. "Survives" means staying above minimum capital ratios. The same ECL machine runs inside, so IFRS 9 gives the provision today; a stress test shows how it could grow.

    The shape is fixed. Define a three-year scenario (oil, GDP, property prices; supervisors often supply it), translate it into PD, LGD and EAD with a "satellite model", project losses and capital, and compare with the minimum.

    Reverse stress testing flips the question: find the scenario that breaks the bank, then judge whether it is plausible. In the Gulf, oil feeds government spending, then non-oil activity, then credit and real estate collateral, so single-factor models understate the chain.

    The real difficulty is what the simulation hides. The oil-to-PD link is estimated from a few cycles (1998, 2008, 2014–16, 2020), so it is highly uncertain and the next shock will differ. Treat the output as one disciplined view, not a prediction. ML can build better satellite models, but with so few cycles it needs time-ordered validation: a model that fits two downturns perfectly has learned those two downturns.

    B4.5 · Reject inference

    Imagine rating restaurants only from diners who walked in. You never learn about the ones who walked past. A scorecard has the same blind spot: repayment outcomes exist only for accepted applicants, so the sample is the through-the-door population with its riskiest part cut off.

    This is a selective labels problem, as in F2.6 and fraud feedback loops. Two cases:

    • Selection on variables your model also sees: the approved sample is in principle enough, but the risk is extrapolation, fitting where there is data and applying where there is none. Simulation 4 shows this.
    • Selection on things you cannot see (an officer's override): the sample is biased and nothing in the data reveals it.

    The result: marginal applicants look safer than they are, and performance looks optimistic because the easy cases were removed.

    Ways to cope, from weakest to strongest evidence:

    MethodWhat it doesHonest verdict
    Ignore itTrain on approved only.Fine if the approval rate is high and policy stays the same. Dangerous if you loosen policy.
    Bureau performance of rejectsCheck how rejected applicants behaved with other lenders.Strongest observational route, where bureau data allows it (check the legal basis).
    Randomised acceptanceApprove a small random slice below the cut-off, with exposure limits.The only unbiased method. Costs real money, but a few hundred accounts can anchor the model.

    Parcelling (giving rejects the approved default rate in their score bin, times a judgement uplift) is common and defensible if documented. Selection-aware models (Heckman-style) need a variable that drives acceptance but not default, which is rare, so they are fragile. In practice reject inference often does little for ranking; its value lies in calibration and in knowing the risk of a policy change. If you cannot randomise, at least say which population the model is valid for.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Through-the-cycle PDs used as point-in-time

      Capital-model PDs fed into ECL without macro adjustment, so provisions lag arrears.

      Spot itPlot average PD against actual defaults over the cycle. A flat PD while defaults move is not point-in-time.
    • Staging that cannot be reproduced, or leans on the cliff

      Origination PD not stored, so the stage depends on the model version; one threshold moves thousands of loans in a quarter; or most Stage 2 loans arrive via the 30-day backstop so the model adds nothing early.

      Spot itRe-run staging with last quarter's model and count the flips. If over half of Stage 2 entries come via the backstop, the early warning is not working.
    • Weights and overlays used to land a number

      Scenario weights adjusted late in the quarter, or a "temporary" overlay renewed for years.

      Spot itReview the weight history and the overlay's age, size and release trigger.
    • A stress test the bank cannot fail

      A satellite model fitted on two cycles with an impressive R-squared on 20 points, severity chosen after seeing the result, or generous management actions.

      Spot itCount independent downturns and ask which scenario would breach the floor (reverse stress).
    • Approved-only model used beyond the approved

      Reported AUC and default rate cover accepted applicants but are used for the whole market, or "the next 10% of applicants are fine" when the model has never seen any of them.

      Spot itAsk what population each metric covers. If the proposed region has no outcomes, propose a small randomised pilot.
    • Rebalanced or resampled PD not recalibrated

      A PD trained with class weights enters ECL uncorrected.

      Spot itCompare the model's average PD on a representative holdout with the observed default rate (see F3.5).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does moving a three-year loan from Stage 1 to Stage 2 roughly triple its provision at a 2% PD, even though nothing has defaulted?

    Stage 1 counts only the next year of default risk. Stage 2 counts every remaining year, and each adds a similar slice (slightly smaller, since the loan must survive to get there). Three years of risk instead of one gives about three times the provision, and longer terms widen the gap.

    2. Why is a loan with PD rising from 0.1% to 0.25% not necessarily Stage 2, even though the ratio is 2.5?

    A ratio alone flags very safe loans for trivial absolute changes. Banks add an absolute floor. The standard asks about a significant increase in default risk, not just a ratio.

    3. Why is the probability-weighted ECL higher than the ECL under the probability-weighted macro outlook?

    Losses are convex in the economy: a bad scenario adds more loss than a good one saves, so a base-case provision understates.

    4. What is the difference between through-the-cycle and point-in-time PD, and which does IFRS 9 want?

    Through-the-cycle PDs are stable across the cycle and suit capital. Point-in-time PDs reflect current conditions and forecasts, so they rise in a downturn. IFRS 9 wants point-in-time.

    5. A stress test uses an oil-to-PD link estimated from 22 annual observations with three downturns. List three questions you would ask.

    How wide is the uncertainty on the shock response? Does the link hold out of sample, with a downturn left out? Does the model capture indirect channels such as government spending and falling collateral values? The assumed management actions and the reverse-stress result are also fair.

    6. A scorecard trained on approved applicants says the bottom decile of the approved has a 6% default rate. The bank considers approving applicants who score just below. Why might 6% be an underestimate for them?

    Risk usually accelerates at the low end, and the model is extrapolating beyond its data. Hidden reasons for past rejections could add more. Check with bureau performance of past rejects and a small randomised approval test.

    7. When does reject inference matter little?

    When almost everyone is approved, when selection used only variables the model sees, and when the model only ranks within the approved population. It matters most when policy loosens, and for pricing and calibration.
