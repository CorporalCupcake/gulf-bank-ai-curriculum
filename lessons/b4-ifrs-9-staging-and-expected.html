    From Model to Balance Sheet: IFRS 9 Provisions, Stress Tests and the Applicants You Never Saw

    How PD, LGD and EAD become a provision with three stages and a cliff, how a portfolio is pushed through an oil shock to see what survives, and why a credit model trained only on approved customers is quietly guessing about everyone else.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA provision is a probability-weighted loss estimate whose time horizon switches from one year to the whole life of the loan when credit quality deteriorates enough, a stress test asks what that number and the bank's capital do under a shock you hope never comes, and reject inference is the admission that your training data only describes the customers you already chose.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 1 million to a healthy manufacturer for five years. The old rule booked no loss until something went wrong. In 2008 that proved too late and too sudden: provisions jumped after the damage, worsening the crisis. It is like replacing the smoke alarm only after the fire.

    IFRS 9 (in force for banks since 2018) says: expect some losses on day one and provision a little, then much more as quality worsens, before default. That provision is the expected credit loss (ECL): the probability-weighted average loss, discounted to today.

    The key design choice is the horizon. A fine loan is provisioned for losses in the next 12 months. A materially riskier loan is provisioned for losses over its whole remaining life. The step between them is deliberate and large, and it turns a modelling question ("has risk risen enough?") into a jump on the profit and loss account.

    Two ideas sit beside it. Stress testing reruns the machinery: if oil falls 40% and property 20%, how large do provisions get, and is capital still enough? Reject inference questions every PD model above: all were trained on borrowers the bank chose to lend to. What about the ones turned away?

    • The provision is an expected value, not a forecast. Like a weather forecast of 30% rain, no single day is 30% wet; it is right across many days. No single loan loses 0.43% of its balance, but across thousands of similar loans the average holds.
    • The cliff creates incentives and instability. A small PD change near the trigger can move millions of dirhams, so whoever owns the staging model holds a lever on reported profit. That is why it is tightly governed.
    • The data behind every PD model is filtered. Repayment is observed only for approved customers, a biased sample that runs through scorecards, stress tests and provisions.
    Hold onto thisECL is PD times LGD times EAD, discounted, over a horizon set by the stage. Stress testing re-runs it under a shock. Reject inference asks whether the PD was learned from a biased sample. One pipeline, three angles.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B4.3 · IFRS 9 staging and expected credit loss

    The inputs come from B4.1–B4.2: PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss. IFRS 9 adds a time dimension, a staging rule, and a requirement that the estimate be unbiased and forward-looking. For each future period t in the horizon:

    ECL=∑tPDt·LGDt·EADt·DFt

    PDt is the marginal probability of defaulting in period t (alive until then, then defaults), LGDt the share lost, EADt the balance exposed at that point, DFt a discount factor at the loan's effective interest rate. For a constant annual PD p, the marginal PD in year k is (1 − p)k−1·p: the chance of surviving k − 1 years and then defaulting. Worked example, ignoring discounting: PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 1,000,000, three years left. Stage 1: 0.02 × 0.45 × 1,000,000 = AED 9,000. Lifetime: (0.02 + 0.0196 + 0.0192) × 450,000 ≈ AED 26,500.

    The hard part is SICR, the test for moving from Stage 1 to 2. The standard gives no number. It says to compare default risk over the expected life now against origination, using reasonable and supportable information. Banks combine three tools:

    • Quantitative test: current lifetime PD (or 12-month PD) against origination PD, as a ratio (say 2 to 3 times), an absolute rise in basis points, or a drop of some rating notches. This is where ML usually enters.
    • Qualitative test: watchlist status, forbearance, adverse sector events, covenant breaches.
    • Backstop: 30 days past due is presumed SICR. A last resort; a good model has moved the loan well before.

    Two traps. To test for change you need the PD at origination, stored and frozen; a bank that lost its origination scores cannot stage consistently. And a safe borrower whose PD doubles from 0.1% to 0.2% has "increased significantly" in relative terms yet is still very safe, so relative tests are paired with absolute floors. The ratio threshold is a hot topic between banks, auditors and supervisors.

    The loan-level pieces:

    • Point-in-time, not through-the-cycle. Basel capital PDs are built to be stable across the cycle. IFRS 9 PDs must reflect current conditions and forecasts, so they move with the economy. Banks bridge the two with a macro-sensitive factor or build PIT models directly.
    • Lifetime PD term structures. You need a PD for each future year, from a rating transition matrix, a survival or hazard model (F2.8, the natural tool since it handles loans of different ages and censoring), or a model with a macro driver per year.
    • EAD includes undrawn commitments. Borrowers draw more as they get into trouble, so a credit conversion factor (CCF) estimates how much of the limit is drawn by default. For revolving products the horizon is the period of exposure to loss, not contractual maturity.
    • Discounting at the effective interest rate, so a loss in five years counts for less than one next year.

    Forward-looking scenarios: why the average is not the base case

    The standard requires "unbiased and probability-weighted" amounts reflecting economic forecasts. Banks run a base, an upside and one or two downsides, compute ECL under each, and weight by probability. The weights are management judgement, reviewed by governance and auditors, and they move the provision directly.

    Why not just use the base case? Because losses are convex in the economy, like a thin bridge: the extra load in a bad year breaks more than a light year saves. A bad year adds more loss than a good year removes, and Stage 2 migration is lumpy. So the weighted average of three ECLs exceeds the ECL of the weighted-average economy. Simulation 2 shows the size.

    Management overlays are post-model adjustments for risks the model cannot see: a new sector concentration, a geopolitical event, a data problem. After 2020 they were used heavily, because models trained on past data could not read government support and payment holidays. They are legitimate if documented, quantified and released when the cause fades. A large, long-lived overlay signals that the model is not doing its job.

    Capital. Basel's framework treats provisions in a particular way against expected loss, and several jurisdictions phased in the first-time IFRS 9 capital impact. For the UAE and Saudi Arabia, the current rules on IFRS 9, regulatory floors and capital are in CBUAE and SAMA notices. I am not stating specifics; check the current texts with your risk and finance teams.

    Islamic finance. IFRS 9 covers Islamic banks' financial assets too (murabaha receivables, ijara), so the ECL framework is the same. The differences sit in EAD and LGD for asset-backed structures, where the bank holds the asset or has a promise to purchase, and in profit rather than interest. Banks using AAOIFI standards for some purposes must be clear which standard governs which statement.

    B4.4 · Stress testing

    ECL is a best estimate. A stress test asks a different question: in a severe but plausible scenario, does the bank survive? Think of a flight simulator: you do not expect the engine failure, you check the pilot copes. "Severe" means well beyond the central forecast, "plausible" means a story a sensible person can follow (a prolonged oil fall, a regional conflict, a property crash), and "survives" means staying above minimum capital ratios. The same ECL machine runs inside, so IFRS 9 gives the provision today; a stress test shows how it could grow.

    The mechanics have a fixed shape.

    1. Define the scenario as three-year paths for oil price, GDP, non-oil GDP, rates, property prices, government spending and unemployment. Supervisors often supply them.
    2. Translate macro to risk parameters (the "satellite model"): estimate how PD, LGD and EAD respond, for example by regressing default rates on GDP growth and oil price with lags, or by shifting a Vasicek-style Z-factor.
    3. Project losses, income and risk-weighted assets year by year on an evolving balance sheet.
    4. Compare the capital path to the minimum and buffers. The outputs are a ratio path and the worst-point capital depletion.

    Two refinements. Reverse stress testing flips the question: find the scenarios that break the bank, then judge their plausibility. It guards against designing a scenario the bank passes. And in the Gulf, macro-financial links matter: oil feeds government spending, then non-oil activity, then corporate and consumer credit and real estate collateral, so a single-factor model understates the chain. Government-related and real estate concentrations are often the most exposed.

    The real difficulty is what the simulation hides. The oil-to-PD link is estimated from a few cycles (1998, 2008, 2014–16, 2020), so it carries wide uncertainty, and the next shock will differ. Treat the output as one disciplined view, not a prediction. A good report names its weakest assumption.

    ML helps with better satellite models (non-linear macro-to-default links, with care over overfitting on so few cycles) and scenario generation. Both need time-ordered validation: a model that fits two downturns perfectly has learned those two downturns.

    B4.5 · Reject inference

    Imagine rating restaurants only from diners who walked in. You never learn about the ones who walked past. A scorecard has the same blind spot: outcomes exist only for applicants the bank accepted. Declined applicants have no repayment history, and they were screened out by the previous scorecard or policy, so the sample is the through-the-door population with its riskiest part cut off.

    This is a selective labels problem, the same shape as in F2.6 and fraud feedback loops. How badly it hurts depends on how selection happened.

    • Selection on observed variables (the old scorecard used fields your new model also sees): the approved sample is in principle enough for a correctly specified model. The risk is extrapolation: fitting where there is data and applying where there is none. Simulation 4 shows this.
    • Selection on things you cannot see (an officer's judgement, an override, undigitised documents): the sample is biased even for a well-specified model, and nothing in the data reveals it.

    The bias shows up in three places. The PD model underestimates the risk of marginal applicants, so worse customers get in. Calibration is wrong for the through-the-door population, which matters for pricing and provisioning new business. And performance looks optimistic: default rates among approved are lower than among applicants, and AUC on approved customers is lower than on all applicants, because the easy cases were removed.

    Methods for coping, from weakest to strongest evidence:

    MethodWhat it doesHonest verdict
    Ignore it (known-good-bad model)Train on approved only.Fine if the approval rate is high and policy stays roughly the same. Dangerous if you loosen policy.
    Extrapolation / augmentationScore rejects with the approved-only model, add them with imputed outcomes, weight accepted cases to represent the rejected.Mostly reinforces what the model already believes.
    ParcellingBin rejects by score; assign defaults at the approved rate in that bin times an uplift ("rejects are riskier at the same score").The uplift is judgement, not data. Common, defensible if documented.
    Bureau performance of rejectsCheck how rejected applicants behaved on loans with other lenders.Strongest observational route, where bureau data allows it (UAE: through the credit bureau; check the legal basis).
    Randomised acceptance (test and learn)Approve a small random slice below the cut-off, with exposure limits.The only unbiased method. Costs real money and needs sign-off, but a few hundred accounts can anchor the model.
    Selection-aware modelsModel acceptance and default jointly (Heckman-style, bivariate probit), or use semi-supervised and causal methods.Needs a variable that drives acceptance but not default, which is rare. Fragile.

    Research suggests reject inference often does little for rank-ordering and sometimes hurts, helping mainly when selection was strong and rejection rates are high. Its value lies more in calibration and in knowing the risk of a policy change than in AUC. If you cannot randomise, at least say which population the model is valid for.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Through-the-cycle PDs used as point-in-time

      Capital-model PDs fed into ECL without a macro adjustment, so provisions do not rise until arrears do.

      Spot itPlot average PD against the actual default rate over the cycle. A flat PD while defaults move is not point-in-time.
    • Staging that cannot be reproduced

      Origination PD not stored, or recomputed with today's model, so the stage depends on the model version.

      Spot itAsk where the origination PD is held and whether it is frozen. Re-run staging with last quarter's model and count the loans that flip.
    • The cliff creates a stampede

      One threshold moves thousands of loans in a quarter and the provision jumps.

      Spot itChart PD ratios near the threshold. A pile just under it means sensitivity; check that a small recalibration does not move large balances.
    • Backstop doing the job of the model

      Most Stage 2 loans arrive via 30 days past due, so the model adds nothing early.

      Spot itTabulate how loans entered Stage 2: model, qualitative, backstop. A backstop share above half means the early warning is not working.
    • Scenario weights chosen to land a number

      Weights adjusted late in the quarter to hit a provision target.

      Spot itReview the weight history and documentation. Changes should follow an outlook change, not a result.
    • Large, permanent overlays

      A "temporary" overlay renewed for years, or covering what the model should handle.

      Spot itAsk for the overlay's age, size relative to model ECL, and release trigger.
    • Stress satellite model fitted on two cycles

      A line from oil price to default rate with an impressive R-squared on 20 annual points.

      Spot itCount observations and independent downturns; ask for out-of-sample checks and the confidence band on the shock response.
    • A stress scenario the bank cannot fail

      Severity chosen after seeing the result, or management actions assumed too generously.

      Spot itAsk what scenario would breach the floor (reverse stress), and which management actions are assumed and how fast they work.
    • Scorecard validated only on approved applicants

      The reported AUC and default rate cover the accepted population but are used for the whole market.

      Spot itAsk what population each metric covers and what share of applicants was rejected in the training period.
    • Loosening policy on an approved-only model

      "The model says the next 10% of applicants are fine" when it has never seen any of them.

      Spot itCheck whether the proposed region has any outcomes. If not, propose a small randomised pilot with exposure limits.
    • Rebalanced or resampled PD not recalibrated

      A PD trained with class weights enters ECL uncorrected.

      Spot itCompare the model's average PD on a representative holdout with the observed default rate (see F3.5).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A loan has annual PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 1,000,000 and three years left. Ignoring discounting, what are the Stage 1 and lifetime ECL?

    Stage 1: 0.02 × 0.45 × 1,000,000 = AED 9,000. Lifetime: marginal PDs are 0.02, 0.0196 and 0.019208, summing to 0.058808. Times 450,000 gives about AED 26,500, roughly three times Stage 1.

    2. Why is a loan with PD rising from 0.1% to 0.25% not necessarily Stage 2, even though the ratio is 2.5?

    A relative test alone flags very safe loans for trivial absolute changes. Banks add an absolute floor or treat very low-risk exposures as "low credit risk". The standard asks about a significant increase in default risk, not just a ratio.

    3. Why is the probability-weighted ECL higher than the ECL under the probability-weighted macro outlook?

    Losses are convex in the economy: a bad scenario (with its Stage 2 migration) adds more loss than a good one saves. The average of the outcomes exceeds the outcome of the average, so a base-case provision understates.

    4. What is the difference between through-the-cycle and point-in-time PD, and which does IFRS 9 want?

    Through-the-cycle PDs average over the cycle and are stable, suiting capital. Point-in-time PDs reflect current conditions and forecasts, so they rise in a downturn. IFRS 9 wants point-in-time, which is why Basel PDs usually need adjusting.

    5. A stress test uses an oil-to-PD link estimated from 22 annual observations with three downturns. List three questions you would ask.

    How wide is the uncertainty on the shock response, and what if it is at the upper end? Is the link stable out of sample, with a downturn left out? Does the model capture indirect channels (government spending, real estate) and a joint fall in collateral values? Also fair: the assumed management actions and the reverse-stress result.

    6. A scorecard trained on approved applicants says the bottom decile of the approved has a 6% default rate. The bank considers approving applicants who score just below. Why might 6% be an underestimate for them?

    Risk usually accelerates at the low end, and the model's line is extrapolated beyond the data. Hidden variables behind past rejections could add more. Estimate it with bureau performance of past rejects and a small randomised approval test.

    7. When does reject inference matter little?

    When almost everyone is approved, when selection was random or on variables the model uses (with a form flexible enough to extrapolate sensibly), and when the model only ranks within the approved population. It matters most when policy loosens, for pricing and calibration, and when selection used information the data does not hold.
