    IFRS 9 Provisions and Stress Testing

    Part 1

    What an IFRS 9 provision is

    In one sentenceAn IFRS 9 provision is money a bank sets aside today for the loan losses it expects, and it sets aside more as a loan gets riskier, before anyone misses a payment.

    IFRS 9 is the accounting standard banks have used since 2018. Before it, banks booked a loss only once something went wrong. In 2008 that was too late: provisions jumped after the damage was done.

    The amount set aside is the expected credit loss (ECL): the average loss you expect, weighted by how likely each outcome is.

    How it differs from older rules and from capital

    • Regulatory capital (Basel): capital is a cushion held against unexpected losses. A provision is for expected losses and reduces reported profit.
    Part 2

    How the provision is worked out

    Running example: a bank lends AED 1,000,000 to a manufacturer. Three numbers (from the credit-risk lessons) drive everything:

    • PD (probability of default): the chance the borrower stops paying in a year. Say 2%.
    • LGD (loss given default): the share of the loan lost if they do. Say 45%.
    • EAD (exposure at default): what is owed at that point. Say AED 1,000,000.
    1. Work out one year. 2% × 45% × AED 1,000,000 = AED 9,000.
    2. Decide the stage. Is the loan still fine, much riskier, or in default?
    3. Pick the horizon. Stage 1 counts one year. Stages 2 and 3 count every remaining year.
    4. Add up the years, each shrunk slightly because money later is worth less today.
    5. Blend the economy scenarios (see below) and book the result.

    With three years left, the lifetime figure is about AED 26,500, nearly three times the Stage 1 amount of about AED 9,000. Nothing has defaulted. More years are simply being counted. That is the "cliff" when a loan moves from Stage 1 to Stage 2.

    How a loan moves to Stage 2

    The standard asks for a significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since the loan was made but gives no number. Banks combine three tests:

    • Quantitative: today's PD against the PD at the start. A ratio such as 2 to 3 times is common. This is where machine learning usually enters. The starting PD must be stored and frozen.
    • Qualitative: watchlist status, a loan restructured to help a struggling borrower, a broken loan condition.
    • Backstop: more than 30 days late is presumed Stage 2. Stage 3 is presumed at 90 days late.

    Why scenarios, not just the base case

    The standard wants forecasts of the economy built in. Banks run a base case, an upside and one or two downsides, compute the ECL under each, then weight them. The weights are management judgement.

    The weighted average is higher than the base case because losses are lopsided: a bad year costs more than a good year saves.

    Part 3

    What stress testing is

    In one sentenceA stress test asks "if a severe shock hits, how big do losses get, and does the bank still have enough capital?"

    It differs from IFRS 9: the provision is the expected loss under likely scenarios, while a stress test deliberately uses a bad one to check the cushion holds.

    1. Choose a scenario, such as oil down 40% and property down 20% over three years.
    2. Translate it into loan risk using "satellite models" that link economy figures to PD and LGD.
    3. Project losses and profit year by year.
    4. Compare the lowest capital ratio with the minimum.

    Reverse stress testing flips the question: find the shock that breaks the bank, then judge whether it is plausible. In the Gulf, oil feeds government spending, then wider business activity, then credit and property values, so a one-factor test understates the chain.

    Part 4

    How to read the results

    Stage 1 provision AED 9,000, Stage 2 provision AED 26,500 on the same loan
    Nothing about the borrower changed except the stage. A small PD move near the trigger can shift millions of dirhams across a book, so whoever owns the staging model influences reported profit.
    Capital ratio falls from 15% to 11% against a 10.5% floor
    The bank holds, with 0.5 points of headroom. Thin headroom means a slightly worse shock would breach it.
    Reverse stress says a 45% oil fall breaks the floor
    If that is plausible, the bank is fragile. If it would take a 90% fall, the bank is robust to oil.

    The oil-to-PD link is estimated from only a few downturns (for example 1998, 2008, 2014 to 2016 and 2020), so it is very uncertain and the next shock will differ. Treat a stress test as one disciplined view, not a prediction.

    Part 5

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    ToolQuestionResult

    Stress testDo we survive a severe shock?Capital headroom

    • Strong at: booking losses early, linking provisions to the economy, showing where capital is thin.
    • Weak at: sharp cliffs at the stage boundary, judgement-heavy weights, and satellite models built on very few downturns.
    Show the formula

    ECL for one year = PD × LGD × EAD × discount factor. Lifetime ECL adds this up for each remaining year, using the chance of defaulting in that year after surviving to it.

    In banking, ECL is where credit models meet the profit-and-loss account, so the PDs must move with current conditions (point-in-time), not stay fixed through the cycle as capital models do.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Using capital-model PDs unchanged

      Stable through-the-cycle PDs make provisions lag behind arrears. IFRS 9 wants PDs that reflect today and the forecast.

    • Staging that cannot be reproduced

      If the starting PD was not stored, the stage depends on the model version. Re-run last quarter's staging and count the flips.

    • Adjusting weights or overlays to land a number

      Scenario weights changed late in the quarter, or a "temporary" overlay renewed for years, signal pressure rather than evidence.

    • A stress test the bank cannot fail

      Severity chosen after seeing the result, or generous management actions assumed, make the test meaningless. Ask which scenario would breach the floor.

    1. Why does moving a three-year loan from Stage 1 to Stage 2 roughly triple its provision at a 2% PD?

    Stage 1 counts one year of default risk and Stage 2 counts all three, each with a similar slice.

    2. Why is a PD rising from 0.1% to 0.25% not automatically Stage 2?

    The ratio is 2.5 but the loan is still very safe. The test is a significant rise, so banks add an absolute floor.

    3. Why is the scenario-weighted ECL higher than the ECL under the average economy?

    Losses are lopsided: a bad scenario adds more loss than a good one saves.

    4. What is the difference between a provision and capital?

    A provision covers expected losses and is booked as a cost. Capital is a cushion for unexpected losses.

    5. Your stress model's oil-to-PD link comes from 22 years with three downturns. What do you ask?

    How uncertain is the link, and does it hold when one downturn is left out? Three downturns is very little evidence.
