    From Model to Balance Sheet: IFRS 9 Provisions, Stress Tests and the Applicants You Never Saw

    How PD, LGD and EAD become a provision with three stages and a cliff, how a portfolio is pushed through an oil shock to see what survives, and why a credit model trained only on approved customers is quietly guessing about everyone else.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA provision is a probability-weighted loss estimate whose time horizon switches from one year to the whole life of the loan when credit quality deteriorates enough, a stress test asks what that number and the bank's capital do under a shock you hope never comes, and reject inference is the admission that your training data only describes the customers you already chose.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lends AED 1 million to a manufacturing firm for five years. Today the firm is healthy. The old accounting rule, still in many people's heads, said: book no loss until something goes wrong. Everyone then discovered in 2008 that "when something goes wrong" arrives too late and all at once. Provisions jumped after the damage was done, and that made the crisis worse.

    IFRS 9, in force for banks since 2018, says the opposite. The day you lend the money, you expect that some borrowers will not repay, so you put aside a small provision now. If the loan's credit quality worsens, you put aside much more, before it defaults. That provision is the expected credit loss (ECL): the probability-weighted average of what you expect to lose, discounted to today.

    The unusual design choice is the horizon. A loan that is still fine gets a provision for losses in the next 12 months only. A loan that has become materially riskier gets a provision for losses over its whole remaining life. The step between the two is deliberate and large. It is the most important thing to understand about the standard, because it turns a modelling question ("has risk risen enough?") into a jump on the profit and loss account.

    Two other ideas sit beside it. Stress testing takes the same machinery and asks: if oil falls by 40% and property by 20%, how large do provisions get, and is there still enough capital? And reject inference asks a quieter question that undermines all the PD models above: every one of them was trained on borrowers the bank chose to lend to. What do we know about the ones we turned away?

    Three things to hold onto.

    • The provision is an expected value, not a forecast of what will happen. No single loan loses 0.43% of its balance. The number is right on average across thousands of similar loans, and the balance sheet is the average.
    • The cliff creates incentives and instability. A small change in a PD model near the trigger can move millions of dirhams. Anyone who owns the staging model owns a lever on reported profit, which is why this model is governed so tightly.
    • The data that trains every PD model is filtered. You only observe repayment for those you approved. Chosen customers are not a random sample of applicants, and that bias runs through scorecards, stress tests and provisions alike.
    Hold onto thisECL is PD times LGD times EAD, discounted, over a horizon that depends on the stage. Stress testing re-runs it under a shock. Reject inference asks whether the PD in the first place was learned from a biased sample. The three topics are one pipeline seen from three angles.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    B4.3 · IFRS 9 staging and expected credit loss

    The inputs come from the models of the previous lesson (B4.1–B4.2: PD, LGD, EAD and expected loss). IFRS 9 adds three things on top: a time dimension, a staging rule, and a requirement that the estimate be unbiased and forward-looking. For each future period t over the horizon:

    ECL=∑tPDt·LGDt·EADt·DFt

    PDt is the marginal probability of defaulting in period t (alive until then, then defaults), LGDt the share lost, EADt the balance exposed at that point, DFt a discount factor at the loan's effective interest rate. For a constant annual PD p, the marginal PD in year k is (1 − p)k−1·p: the chance of surviving k − 1 years and then defaulting. Worked example, ignoring discounting: PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 1,000,000, three years left. Stage 1: 0.02 × 0.45 × 1,000,000 = AED 9,000. Lifetime: (0.02 + 0.0196 + 0.0192) × 450,000 ≈ AED 26,500.

    The three stages

    The mechanically hard part is SICR, the test for moving from Stage 1 to 2. The standard does not give a number. It says to compare the risk of default over the expected life now against the same measure at origination, using reasonable and supportable information available without undue cost. In practice banks combine:

    • A quantitative test: current lifetime PD (or 12-month PD as an approximation) against the origination PD, using a ratio (say 2 to 3 times), an absolute rise in basis points, or a drop of a certain number of rating notches. This is where an ML model usually enters.
    • A qualitative test: watchlist status, forbearance or restructuring, adverse sector events, covenant breaches.
    • The backstop: 30 days past due is presumed to be SICR. It is a last resort, not a main signal; a good model should have moved the loan well before.

    Notice the awkward logic: to test for change you need the PD at origination, stored and frozen. A bank that has lost its origination scores cannot stage consistently. Notice also that a low-risk borrower whose PD doubles from 0.1% to 0.2% has "increased significantly" in relative terms, yet is still a very safe loan. This is why relative tests are paired with absolute floors, and why the ratio threshold is a hot topic between banks, auditors and supervisors.

    What the loan-level pieces look like in practice:

    • Point-in-time, not through-the-cycle. Regulatory capital PDs (Basel) are designed to be stable across the cycle. IFRS 9 PDs must reflect current conditions and forecasts, so they move with the economy. Banks bridge the two by converting a through-the-cycle PD with a macro-sensitive factor, or by building PIT models directly.
    • Lifetime PD term structures. You need a PD for each future year, not one number. Common routes: a transition matrix of rating migrations, a survival or hazard model (see F2.8: survival analysis), or a model with a macro driver per year. Notice that a hazard model is the natural tool because it handles loans of different ages and censoring.
    • EAD includes undrawn commitments. A revolving facility has a drawn balance and an undrawn limit, and borrowers draw more when they get into trouble. A credit conversion factor (CCF) estimates how much of the limit will be drawn by default. For revolving products, the horizon is the period over which the bank is exposed to loss, not the contractual maturity.
    • Discounting at the effective interest rate, so that a loss five years out counts for less than one next year.

    Forward-looking scenarios and why the average is not the base case

    The standard requires "unbiased and probability-weighted" amounts that reflect forecasts of economic conditions. Banks therefore run several macroeconomic scenarios (typically a base, an upside and one or two downsides), compute ECL under each, and weight them by probability. The weights are a management judgement, reviewed by governance committees and auditors, and they shift the provision directly.

    Why not just use the base case? Because the relationship between the economy and losses is convex: a bad year adds more loss than a good year removes, and migrations to Stage 2 are lumpy. The weighted average of three ECLs is therefore higher than the ECL of the weighted-average economy. Simulation 2 shows the size of the effect.

    The provisions on the balance sheet are not the end of the story. The numbers also drive management overlays (post-model adjustments), where the bank adds an amount on top of the model for risks the model cannot see, such as a new sector concentration, a geopolitical event or a data problem. Overlays were used heavily after the 2020 pandemic, because models trained on past data had no way to understand government support and payment holidays. They are legitimate, but auditors and supervisors want them documented, quantified and released when the cause fades. A large, long-lived overlay is a signal that the model is not doing its job.

    Regulators watch the effect on capital. Basel's framework allows banks to treat provisions in a particular way against expected loss, and several jurisdictions gave transitional arrangements to phase in the capital impact of the first-time IFRS 9 adoption. For the UAE and Saudi Arabia, the exact current rules on the interaction between IFRS 9 provisions, regulatory floors and capital are set by CBUAE and SAMA notices. I am not stating specifics here; check the current CBUAE and SAMA texts, which your risk and finance teams will have.

    Two points worth knowing for Islamic finance. IFRS 9 applies to the financial assets of Islamic banks too (murabaha receivables, ijara, and so on), so the ECL framework is the same even though the contracts are not loans in form. The practical differences sit in the EAD and LGD for asset-backed structures, where the bank holds the asset or has a promise to purchase, and in the treatment of profit rather than interest. Banks that follow AAOIFI standards for some purposes need to be clear about which standard governs which statement.

    B4.4 · Stress testing

    ECL is a best-estimate, probability-weighted number. A stress test is different in purpose: it asks what happens in a severe but plausible scenario, and whether the bank survives it. The three words are all in the definition. "Severe" means well beyond the central forecast. "Plausible" means a story a sensible person can follow, such as a prolonged oil-price fall, a regional conflict, or a sharp fall in real estate. "Survives" is measured against capital: does the bank stay above its minimum ratios?

    The mechanics have a fixed shape.

    1. Define the scenario as paths for macro variables over, say, three years: oil price, GDP, non-oil GDP, interest rates, property prices, government spending, unemployment. Supervisors often supply these; banks add their own.
    2. Translate macro to risk parameters. This is the "satellite model" step. Estimate how PD, LGD and EAD respond to the macro variables, for example by regressing default rates on GDP growth and oil price with lags, or by shifting the scorecard's intercept (a "Z-factor") in a Vasicek-style model.
    3. Project losses, income and risk-weighted assets year by year on a balance sheet that evolves (new lending, run-off, funding cost).
    4. Compare the capital path to the minimum and buffers. The output is a ratio path and a "worst-point" capital depletion.

    Two refinements matter. Reverse stress testing turns the question around: find the scenarios that break the bank, then judge how plausible they are. It is a good defence against the temptation to design a scenario the bank passes. And macro-financial links matter in the Gulf in particular: oil prices feed government spending, then non-oil activity, then corporate and consumer credit and real estate collateral, so a single-factor satellite model understates the chain. Concentrations such as government-related entities and real estate are often where the portfolio is most exposed.

    The same ECL machine sits inside the stress test: the scenario is just a more extreme weighted scenario, and a stress run therefore shows how large Stage 2 migration and provision charges can become. The relationship is worth remembering: IFRS 9 gives the provision at the reporting date; a stress test shows how it could grow.

    What the simulation hides is the hard part in real work. The link from oil price to PD is estimated from a few cycles of data (1998, 2008, 2014–16, 2020), so it carries very wide uncertainty, and the next shock will differ from all of them. Banks therefore treat the output as one disciplined view, not as a prediction. A good stress test report names its weakest assumption.

    ML enters stress testing in two ways: better satellite models (non-linear links from macro to default, with attention to overfitting on so few cycles), and scenario generation. Both need the hold-back discipline of time-ordered validation, because a model that fits two downturns perfectly has learned those two downturns.

    B4.5 · Reject inference

    Every application scorecard is trained on outcomes. Outcomes exist only for applicants the bank accepted (and who took the loan). Those who were declined have no repayment history with this bank. So the training sample is the "through-the-door" population with its riskiest part cut off, and it was cut off by the previous scorecard or policy.

    This is a selective labels problem, the same shape as in F2.6 and fraud feedback loops. Whether it hurts depends on how the selection happened.

    • Selection on observed variables only (the old scorecard used the same fields your new model sees): in principle the approved sample is enough for a correctly specified model, because the relationship between features and default is the same in the rejected region. The risk is extrapolation: a model fitted where it has data and applied where it has none. Simulation 4 shows exactly this.
    • Selection on things you cannot see (an officer's judgement, a relationship manager's override, documents that were never digitised): the approved sample is biased even for a well-specified model, and nothing in the data reveals it.

    Where does the bias show up? In three places. The PD model underestimates the risk of marginal applicants, so the bank lets in worse customers than it thinks. The model's calibration is wrong for the through-the-door population, which matters when it is used for pricing and for provisioning on new business. And performance measures are optimistic: AUC on approved customers is lower than on all applicants for a mundane reason, since the easy cases were removed, while default rates among approved are lower than among applicants.

    The methods for coping, from weakest to strongest evidence:

    MethodWhat it doesHonest verdict
    Ignore it (known-good-bad model)Train on approved only.Fine if the approval rate is high and the new model keeps roughly the same policy. Dangerous if you loosen policy.
    Extrapolation / augmentationScore the rejects with the approved-only model, then add them to training with imputed outcomes, weighting accepted cases to represent the rejected.Mostly reinforces what the model already believes. Adds little unless the model form is richer than the data.
    ParcellingBin rejects by score, assign defaults at a rate set by the approved rate in that bin times an uplift factor ("rejects are riskier at the same score").The uplift factor is a judgement, not data. Common in practice, defensible if documented.
    Bureau performance of rejectsCheck how rejected applicants behaved on loans with other lenders after the decision.The strongest observational route, where credit bureau data allows it (in the UAE, through the credit bureau's reports; the legal basis for such use needs checking).
    Randomised acceptance (test and learn)Approve a small random slice of applicants below the cut-off, with limits on exposure.The only method that gives unbiased outcomes. Costs real money and needs sign-off, but a few hundred accounts can anchor the model.
    Selection-aware modelsModel acceptance and default jointly (Heckman-style, bivariate probit), or use semi-supervised and causal methods.Needs a variable that drives acceptance but not default, which is rare. Results can be fragile.

    The blunt summary from the research literature is that reject inference often does little for rank-ordering and sometimes hurts, with benefits coming mainly when selection was strong and rejection rates are high. Its value is therefore more in calibration and in knowing the risk of a policy change than in AUC. If you cannot randomise, the minimum is to say clearly which population the model is valid for.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These three topics sit at the centre of credit risk and touch almost every other module.

    B4.1 – B4.2 · PD, LGD, EADThe inputs to every number here

    ECL is built from these three models. Here we only add horizon, staging, scenarios and capital.

    F2.8 · Survival analysisLifetime PD term structure

    A hazard model gives PD for each future year and handles loans that have not yet defaulted. It is the natural engine for lifetime ECL.

    F2.6 · Selection biasThe root of reject inference

    Rejected applicants have no outcome. The generic selection problem and the credit version are the same.

    B1 · Delayed labelsFeedback loops in labels

    Fraud systems only see what they alert on; credit systems only see whom they lend to. Same trap, different domain.

    F3.1 · Point-in-time correctnessFrozen origination PD

    Staging needs the PD as known on the origination date. Reconstructing it later with today's model leaks the future.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataRare defaults, probabilities that must stay honest

    A resampled PD model must be recalibrated before it enters ECL. The probabilities are the product.

    F7.7 – F7.9 · Drift and stressMonitoring PD models

    Population drift is the early warning that a staging model is moving; stress tests are the planned version of the same question.

    F9.1 · Time series and forecastingMacro forecasts and satellite models

    Scenario paths and macro-to-default links are time series problems with very few cycles.

    F8 · ExplanationsExplaining a move to Stage 2

    Auditors and customers ask why a loan was restaged. A model that cannot answer is hard to use as the SICR test.

    G3 · Model riskValidation of ECL models

    ECL models are among the most heavily validated; see also ongoing monitoring.

    G2 · FairnessWho the model never saw

    Groups that were historically declined are the ones with no data. Reject inference choices have fairness consequences.

    A10 · Underwriting frontierOpen-banking data for thin files

    New data sources can reduce reliance on past approvals, but have their own selection issues.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Finance and risk, month-end. ECL is reported, audited and disclosed every quarter. Data scientists on credit models supply PD term structures and staging flags, and spend weeks explaining movements.
    • Origination. Application scorecards and affordability models for retail, SME and corporate. This is where reject inference lives, and where policy changes (loosening a cut-off for salary-transfer customers, say) should be judged against it.
    • Supervisory stress tests and ICAAP. A central bank supplies scenarios; the bank runs its balance sheet; a team compiles and defends the answers. ML teams contribute satellite models and data.
    • Early warning and collections. A monitoring model that flags borrowers for review is also, implicitly, a staging signal.

    Where the field is now

    The accounting standard has been stable since 2018. The live questions are about how models are built within it, how overlays and judgement are controlled, and how stress testing adapts to geopolitical and climate risk.     Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each of these has appeared in real banks' audit findings or model reviews. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Through-the-cycle PDs used as point-in-time

      Capital-model PDs fed into ECL without a macro adjustment. Provisions fail to rise until the downturn is visible in arrears.

      Spot itPlot the portfolio average PD against the actual default rate over the cycle. If the PD is flat while defaults move, it is not point-in-time.
    • Staging that cannot be reproduced

      Origination PD not stored, or recomputed with today's model. The stage depends on which model version ran.

      Spot itAsk where the origination PD is held and whether it is frozen. Re-run staging with last quarter's model and count the loans that flip.
    • The cliff creates a stampede

      A single threshold moves thousands of loans in one quarter and the provision jumps.

      Spot itChart the distribution of PD ratios near the threshold. A pile of loans just under it signals sensitivity; check that a small model recalibration does not move large balances.
    • Backstop doing the job of the model

      Most Stage 2 loans arrive via 30 days past due, not the SICR model. The model adds nothing early.

      Spot itTabulate how loans entered Stage 2: model, qualitative, backstop. A backstop share above half means the early warning is not working.
    • Scenario weights chosen to land a number

      Weights adjusted late in the quarter to hit a provision target.

      Spot itLook at the history of weights and their documentation. Changes should follow an outlook change, not a result.
    • Large, permanent overlays

      A "temporary" overlay that is renewed for years, or that covers what the model should handle.

      Spot itAsk for the overlay's age, size relative to model ECL, and release trigger.
    • Stress test with a satellite model fitted on two cycles

      A fitted line from oil price to default rate with an impressive R-squared on 20 annual points.

      Spot itCount observations and independent downturns, ask for out-of-sample checks, and for the confidence band on the shock response.
    • A stress scenario the bank cannot fail

      Severity chosen after seeing the result, or management actions assumed too generously.

      Spot itAsk what scenario would breach the floor (reverse stress), and what management actions are assumed and how fast they work.
    • Scorecard validated only on approved applicants

      The reported AUC and default rate are for the accepted population and are used for the whole market.

      Spot itAsk what population each metric covers, and what share of applicants was rejected in the training period.
    • Loosening policy on the strength of an approved-only model

      "The model says the next 10% of applicants are fine" when it has never seen any of them.

      Spot itCheck whether the proposed new region has any outcomes. If not, propose a small randomised pilot with exposure limits.
    • Rebalanced or resampled PD not recalibrated

      A PD trained with class weights, entering ECL without correction.

      Spot itCompare the model's average PD on a representative holdout with the observed default rate (see F3.5).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a CFO or CRO · 30 seconds
    “Our provisions have two modes: a one-year charge for healthy loans and a whole-life charge once risk has risen materially. The move between them is where the profit and loss impact sits, so the staging test is the most sensitive piece of the model. I'd show you how many loans are close to the line and how much a small change in the threshold would move the provision, so you can see the sensitivity before the quarter ends, not after.”
    To a validator or supervisor
    “We use point-in-time lifetime PD term structures from a hazard model, with origination PDs frozen at booking and SICR on a relative and absolute basis plus qualitative triggers, with the 30-day backstop as a last resort. Scenario weights follow the committee's outlook, and overlays are documented with size, owner and release trigger. For the application model, we state that the training population is approved customers, and we recommend a small randomised acceptance test before any loosening of policy.”
    “Why not just use a 12-month provision on everything?”

    Because for a loan whose risk has risen, 12 months understates the loss that is already likely to come. The standard deliberately front-loads recognition for deteriorated loans. The cost of that design is the cliff at the stage transition, which is why we monitor how many loans sit near the threshold.

    “Can we use XGBoost for the SICR test?”

    Technically yes, and it will probably rank risk better. The constraints are explainability for auditors and for individual restaging decisions, stability (a boosted model retrained each quarter can move loans across the line for no economic reason), and the need for a frozen origination comparison. A common compromise is an ML model for early warning and watchlist triggers, with a more stable scorecard-type PD feeding the quantitative test, and the ML contribution documented in the qualitative layer.

    “What is the difference between a stress test and IFRS 9 scenarios?”

    IFRS 9 scenarios are meant to represent reasonable expectations, weighted by likelihood, and produce the provision at the reporting date. A stress test uses a severe but plausible scenario to see what happens to capital and the cost of risk. The same machinery runs both, with different purposes and different severity.

    “Our approved-customer default rate is 1.2%. Can we approve more?”

    The 1.2% describes the people we chose. We have no outcomes for those we declined, and risk typically accelerates at the bottom of the score range. I'd estimate the extra defaults with bureau data on how declined applicants performed elsewhere, and run a capped random approval test below the cut-off to measure it. Then decide with numbers rather than extrapolation.

    “The model says the stress CET1 stays above 11%. Are we safe?”

    Safe against that scenario, under the stated links and management actions. I'd ask what scenario breaks the floor and how plausible it is, how many cycles the oil-to-default link was estimated on, and how the result changes if collateral values and PDs fall together. The answer is a set of conditions, not a yes.

    “How do overlays differ from model error?”

    An overlay is an explicit, documented adjustment for a risk the model structurally cannot capture, such as a new event or a data gap. Model error is what is left when the model is wrong about risks it should capture. If overlays grow large and persistent, they are covering model error and the model needs fixing.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A loan has annual PD 2%, LGD 45%, EAD AED 1,000,000 and three years left. Ignoring discounting, what are the Stage 1 and lifetime ECL?

    Stage 1: 0.02 × 0.45 × 1,000,000 = AED 9,000. Lifetime: marginal PDs are 0.02, 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0196 and 0.98² × 0.02 = 0.019208, summing to 0.058808. Times 450,000 gives about AED 26,500, roughly three times the Stage 1 figure.

    2. Why is a loan with PD rising from 0.1% to 0.25% not necessarily a Stage 2 loan, even though the ratio is 2.5?

    A relative test alone flags very safe loans for trivial absolute changes. Banks add an absolute floor (for example a minimum rise in PD in basis points) or treat very low-risk exposures as "low credit risk". The standard asks about a significant increase in the risk of default, not just a ratio.

    3. Why is the probability-weighted ECL higher than the ECL under the probability-weighted macro outlook?

    Losses are convex in the economy: the extra loss in a bad scenario, including migration to Stage 2, exceeds the saving in a good one. Averaging the outcomes therefore gives more than the outcome of the average. This is why a single base-case provision understates, and why the standard requires multiple scenarios.

    4. What is the difference between through-the-cycle and point-in-time PD, and which does IFRS 9 want?

    Through-the-cycle PDs average over the economic cycle and are stable, suitable for capital. Point-in-time PDs reflect current conditions and forecasts, so they rise in a downturn. IFRS 9 wants point-in-time, forward-looking estimates, which is why Basel PDs usually need adjusting.

    5. A stress test uses an oil-to-PD link estimated from 22 annual observations with three downturns. List three questions you would ask.

    How wide is the uncertainty on the shock response, and what happens if it is at the upper end? Is the link stable out of sample, tested by leaving out a downturn? Does the model capture indirect channels (government spending, real estate) and a joint fall in collateral values? Also fair: what are the management actions and the reverse-stress result.

    6. A scorecard trained on approved applicants says the bottom decile of the approved has a 6% default rate. The bank considers approving applicants who score just below that. Why might 6% be an underestimate for them?

    Risk usually accelerates at the low end of the score range, and the model's line is extrapolated beyond the data. Hidden variables behind past rejections could add to the risk. Estimate it with bureau performance of past rejects and a small randomised approval test.

    7. When does reject inference matter little?

    When almost everyone is approved, when selection was random or on variables the model uses, with a model form flexible enough to extrapolate sensibly, and when the model is used for ranking within the approved population only. It matters most when policy loosens, for pricing and calibration, and when selection used information the data does not hold.
