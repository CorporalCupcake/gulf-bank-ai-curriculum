    Bias, Variance and Generalisation, and the Regression Models That Keep Winning

    Why a model that fits the past perfectly can be useless on tomorrow's customers, how error splits into three parts you can reason about, and why plain linear and logistic regression are still the baseline every fancier model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is only worth what it does on cases it has not seen, error on those cases comes from being too rigid (bias), too jumpy (variance) or from noise nobody can remove, and linear and logistic regression sit at the low-variance end where most bank data, with its few hundred or thousand real events, belongs.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two junior credit analysts are each given the last 20 personal-loan files, with the outcome of each: repaid or defaulted. They are told to build a rule for the next 1,000 applicants.

    The first analyst writes a rule so detailed that it reproduces all 20 outcomes: "salary between AED 14,200 and 14,900, employer starts with 'A', applied on a Tuesday: default." It is perfect on the 20 files. On the next 1,000 applicants it is close to random, because most of its detail was coincidence.

    The second writes: "the more of the limit someone already uses, and the more payments they have missed, the riskier they are." It gets a few of the 20 wrong. On the next 1,000 it does about as well as it did on the 20.

    The first analyst overfitted: learned the quirks of the sample as if they were the pattern. The second may have underfitted a little, ignoring real structure, but the rule travels. Generalisation is the word for travelling: performance on data the model was not built on. It is the only performance anyone pays for.

    The simulation below makes it visible. A smooth curve generates the data, but every observation has noise added, like the random side of real customers' behaviour. You choose how flexible the fitted curve is.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Training error always improves with flexibility. A flexible enough model can pass through every point. So training error cannot tell you which model is good. It can only tell you a model is bad.
    • Error on new data is U-shaped. Too rigid and the model misses structure. Too flexible and it chases noise. The best model is in the middle, and where the middle sits depends on how much data you have and how noisy it is.
    • There is a floor. The noise line is the error of a model that knows the true pattern exactly. A bank model predicting default will never get near zero, because whether this customer loses their job next month is not in the data. Trying to beat the floor is how people end up overfitting.
    Hold onto thisNever judge a model on the data it was fitted to. Ask what it does on data it never touched, and how that changes as you make the model more complex or give it more data. The rest of this lesson is the machinery behind that question, followed by the two simple models that win it more often than people expect.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Generalisation: what you want, and what you can measure

    Suppose the world produces pairs (x, y): customer features and an outcome. A model f̂ learned from a sample has a true error, its average loss over everything the world could produce. You will never see it. What you see is the average loss on your sample, the training error, and on any held-back sample, the test error.

    Training error is optimistic, because the model was chosen to make it small. The gap between the two is the generalisation gap, and it grows with model flexibility and shrinks with sample size. That is the whole story of Simulation 1 in one sentence.

    This is why every serious workflow has three data sets with three jobs:

    • Training set: the model learns its parameters here.
    • Validation set: you compare models and settings here (degree, penalty, tree depth). Every choice you make using it leaks a little information from it into the model.
    • Test set: touched once, at the end, to report the number. If you keep going back to it, it becomes a second validation set and the reported number is optimistic.

    F3.1 covers leakage in depth and the cross-validation lesson (F4.5) covers how to do this when data is scarce or ordered in time. For a bank, the test set must come later in time than the training set, because the model will be used on the future. A random split of a drifting process lets the model peek at the period it is being tested on.

    Bias, variance and noise

    Now take the question one level deeper. Imagine you could draw many independent training samples from the world, fit the same kind of model to each, and look at the predictions at one fixed point x. You would get a cloud of different predictions. Two things about the cloud matter:

    • Bias: how far the centre of the cloud is from the truth. A straight line fitted to a curved pattern is wrong in the same direction every time, whatever the sample. More data will not fix it. Only a more flexible model will.
    • Variance: how widely the cloud spreads. A flexible model reshapes itself around each sample's noise, so each sample gives a different answer. More data narrows the cloud. A less flexible model, or a penalty, narrows it too.

    Add the noise in the outcome itself, and the expected squared error at a point splits cleanly into three:

    E[(y−f^(x))2]=(bias)2+variance+σ2

    Bias is the gap between the average fitted value (over many samples) and the true value. Variance is how much a single fitted value wanders around that average. σ² is the noise in y that no model can explain. The identity is exact for squared error. For other losses, such as log loss or classification error, the same intuition holds but the clean three-way split does not.

    The trade-off is that the things which reduce bias (more features, more flexibility, deeper trees) generally raise variance, and the reverse. The best model minimises the sum. Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat set this out in 1992, and it is still the right first lens. The simulation below fits the same model type to 80 fresh samples and measures all three terms directly.

    Two caveats to hold alongside the picture. First, "bias" here means modelling bias, a model too simple for the pattern. It is a different thing from social or unfairness bias (G2), and mixing them up in a meeting is a common slip. Second, the classical U-curve is not the final word for very large models: with enough parameters, test error can come down a second time. Part 4 covers that ("double descent"). For the tabular models in a bank's day-to-day work, the classical picture is the working one.

    Linear regression: the model that fits in one line

    Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of inputs: income, balance, tenure, each with a coefficient. "Fitting" picks the coefficients that make squared prediction errors on the training set as small as possible. For this one model, the answer is a formula, not a search:

    β^=(XTX)−1XTy

    X is the table of inputs (one row per customer, plus a column of ones for the intercept), y the column of outcomes. This is the "normal equation": it is what the polynomial fits in Simulations 1 and 2 solve. It requires XᵀX to be invertible, which fails if one input is an exact copy of another, and wobbles if two are nearly copies.

    Why this model earns its place:

    • Each coefficient has a plain reading. "Holding the other inputs fixed, AED 1,000 more monthly income goes with AED 380 more average card spend." The words holding the other inputs fixed are doing real work: the coefficient is not the raw relationship, it is the relationship after the other inputs have taken their share. F2.5 and F2.4 cover this and confounding.
    • It comes with uncertainty built in. Each coefficient has a standard error, so you can say "0.5, give or take 0.2". That is seldom available from a boosted tree without extra work.
    • Low variance by design. With p inputs and n rows, the typical gap between training and test error is about p/n of the noise. Ten inputs, 1,000 rows: a 1% effect. A flexible model has no such guarantee.
    • It extrapolates predictably. A forest cannot predict above the highest value it has seen. A linear model continues the line, which is sometimes exactly what you want and sometimes a disaster. Either way you know what it will do.

    Its bias is the price: it assumes the effect of each input is a straight line and does not depend on the others. Real patterns often bend (risk rises faster once utilisation passes 80%) and interact (a missed payment matters more for a new customer). Two cheap repairs keep the model linear in its coefficients while letting it bend: add transformed inputs (log of income, a squared term, bands of age) and add a small number of chosen interactions. This is exactly what the polynomial in Simulation 1 is doing: still linear regression, with columns 1, x, x², x³.

    Where it breaks. Strongly correlated inputs make coefficients unstable though predictions remain fine (the next sub shows it). Outliers pull the line, because squared error punishes big misses heavily. Predicting something bounded, like a probability, with a straight line gives answers below 0 or above 1. That last point is the reason for the next model.

    Logistic regression: the same idea for yes or no

    For an outcome that is repaid/defaulted or fraud/not-fraud, we want a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression keeps the weighted sum but pushes it through an S-shaped curve. The weighted sum is read as log-odds:

    lnp1−p=β0+β1x1+β2x2+…⇔p=11+e−z

    Odds are p ÷ (1 − p): a 20% chance is odds of 1 to 4, or 0.25. Taking the log makes odds symmetric around zero (50% is 0; 20% is about −1.39; 80% is +1.39). z is the weighted sum. Coefficients add on the log-odds scale, so they multiply on the odds scale.

    That last point is how you read it. A coefficient of 0.9 on "missed payments, in standard deviations" means each extra standard deviation multiplies the default odds by e^0.9 ≈ 2.46. If someone starts at 5% (odds 0.0526), they move to odds 0.129, which is 11.5%. The same coefficient on someone starting at 40% moves them to 62%. Equal odds ratio, unequal change in probability, because the S-curve is steeper in the middle. Never quote a logistic coefficient as "adds X points of probability" without saying where on the curve.

    How it is fitted. There is no closed-form answer, so the software searches for the coefficients that make the observed outcomes most probable (maximum likelihood, F1.7), which is the same as minimising log loss: a penalty of −ln(p) when the event happened and p was what you predicted. Confident wrong answers are punished heavily: predicting 1% for something that happens costs 4.6, predicting 40% costs 0.9. The search is a few rounds of weighted linear regression (Newton's method), and it is convex, so there is one best answer and no unlucky starting point. F2.5 and F1.7 have the underlying ideas.

    What makes it bank-friendly:

    • Well calibrated by default. Fitted by likelihood, its probabilities usually mean what they say on data like the training data, which matters for provisioning and pricing (F7.4). Boosted trees and neural nets often need recalibrating.
    • The scorecard. Credit scorecards are logistic regression on binned inputs, with each bin's points taken from its coefficient. A score of 620 and a point table are an audit trail the validator and the regulator can follow line by line.
    • Constraints are easy. If policy says higher income should never raise risk, you check the sign. With a flexible model you need monotonic constraints to guarantee it.
    • Stable. Retrain next quarter and the coefficients move a little, not everywhere.

    Two failure modes to know cold. Separation: if some input perfectly splits defaulters from non-defaulters in the training data (it happens with a handful of events and a sparse category), the likelihood keeps improving as the coefficient grows, so software returns a huge coefficient or does not converge. Penalising the coefficients fixes it. Too many inputs for the events: only the minority outcome carries information about the boundary, so 60 defaults cannot support 40 inputs. The old rule of thumb is 10 events per candidate variable. Riley and colleagues (BMJ, 2020) give better, calculable minimum sample sizes. Their message is that it depends on the number of inputs, the event rate and the expected fit, and that 10 is sometimes too few.

    Taming variance: the ridge penalty in action

    Regularisation is the deliberate trade of a little bias for a lot less variance. Ridge (L2) adds to the loss a penalty proportional to the sum of squared coefficients, so the optimiser prefers small coefficients unless the data insists. F4.4 covers L1, L2 and early stopping properly; here is the effect in the setting where it helps most.

    A loan model has five standardised inputs: utilisation, debt-to-income (which moves almost in lockstep with utilisation, correlation 0.9), missed payments, and two inputs that are pure noise. The true effects are known because we generated the data. We fit logistic regression to 30 independent samples and plot where each coefficient lands.

    The lesson inside the picture: when inputs are correlated, individual coefficients are fragile but predictions are not. A model with two correlated inputs and wildly different coefficients can predict equally well. This is why you should not tell a business owner "debt-to-income matters more than utilisation" from raw coefficients, and why explanation tools (F8.1) are not a free fix: they inherit the same ambiguity.

    Why the simple model is the baseline you must beat

    On tabular data with a few thousand to a few hundred thousand rows and a modest number of events, a regularised linear or logistic model is often within a hair of the most complex alternative. The reasons follow directly from the machinery above: few events means variance is the binding constraint; noisy outcomes mean a high noise floor, which leaves little room for extra flexibility to help; and interpretable inputs have been engineered by decades of credit practice to be roughly linear on the log-odds scale.

    The honest summary of the evidence is mixed, and you should be able to say so. A large benchmark of credit-scoring algorithms (Lessmann and colleagues, 2015) found that ensembles of trees did beat logistic regression on average, with a margin that was real but not huge. A systematic review of clinical risk models (Christodoulou and colleagues, 2019) found no consistent benefit of machine learning over logistic regression once study quality was accounted for. Both agree on the practical rule: always build the regularised regression first. It sets the bar, shows how much signal is in the data, and gives a validator something to compare against. If a boosted model beats it by one point of AUC but is a hundred times harder to explain, you have a business question, not just a modelling one. That question is the subject of C1.2.

    The flip side: when the gap is large, take it seriously. Interactions and non-linearities in transaction data (velocity, merchant, device, time of day) are why fraud models are mostly tree ensembles, and why linear models are not where you stop for them.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    The bias-variance idea is the thread that runs through almost every modelling decision. These are the places it comes back.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeHonest test sets

    Generalisation means nothing if information from the future or from the target leaks into training. A leaked model has low bias, low variance and zero value.

    F4.3 · F4.4 · Trees, boosting, regularisationControlling flexibility

    Tree depth, number of boosting rounds, L1 and L2 penalties are all dials on this trade-off. Forests reduce variance by averaging; boosting reduces bias step by step.

    F4.5 · Cross-validationEstimating generalisation with little data

    Repeats the train/validate split to get a stable estimate. For ordered data it must respect time.

    F4.9 · Hyperparameter tuningChoosing the dial setting

    Tuning is a search over flexibility settings. Search long enough on one validation set and you overfit to it.

    F6.3 · Overfitting in deep netsSame trade-off, bigger models

    Dropout, augmentation and early stopping are regularisation for networks. Reading the training and validation loss curves is reading Simulation 1.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsThe statistics view of the same models

    Standard errors, hypothesis tests on coefficients and the link-function family. This lesson is the prediction view; F2.5 is the inference view.

    F1.7 · Likelihood and entropyWhy log loss

    Fitting by maximum likelihood is minimising log loss. Cross-entropy in neural networks is the same quantity.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Metrics and calibrationMeasuring the test error well

    Picking the right test-set number (AUC, log loss, precision at capacity) and checking that probabilities mean what they say.

    F7.7 · DriftWhen the test set changes under you

    Generalisation is to data from the same process. Drift is the process changing. The low-variance model is not safer against drift, only against noise.

    F8 · ExplanationReading a model's reasons

    A regression's coefficients are its explanation. Whatever you replace it with, the explanation must be rebuilt, and Simulation 3 shows why correlated inputs make it fragile.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit scorecards and PD models. Logistic regression on binned, monotone inputs is still the workhorse for retail probability of default. It feeds into capital and provisioning (B4.1, B4.2) where stable, explainable, well-calibrated probabilities matter more than the last point of AUC.
    • Fraud and AML. Rare events and an arms race: variance from too few positives is the main risk (F3.5), but the non-linear structure means boosted trees win, with a logistic baseline as the sanity check.
    • Churn, propensity and next-best-offer. Moderate event rates, many features. Regularised logistic regression with a few engineered interactions is a strong first model and a fine production model.
    • Forecasting balances and income. Linear regression with seasonality and holiday terms (Ramadan, Eid, salary days) is a strong baseline for deposit and cash forecasts.
    • Model validation. Independent validators will ask for the benchmark model, the out-of-time test and the stability of coefficients. All three are in this lesson (G3.2).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The classical picture is textbook, but three live arguments sit on top of it: whether huge models break the U-curve, whether simple models are as good as people claim for high-stakes decisions, and whether a new kind of pre-trained model for tables makes the whole classical toolkit obsolete on small data.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with one light search (tabular foundation models). Earlier items are from memory of the published papers; check them before you quote precise claims.

    1. 1992
      The bias/variance dilemma

      Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat set out the decomposition for neural networks and framed learning as a trade-off between flexibility and stability.

      Geman et al., Neural Computation, 1992
    2. 2019
      Double descent

      Belkin and colleagues showed that as models grow past the point where they can fit the training data exactly, test error can fall a second time. Nakkiran and colleagues found the same in modern deep networks, as a function of model size, training time and data size. The classical U-curve describes the first part; it is not wrong, it is incomplete for very large models.

      Belkin et al., PNAS, 2019; Nakkiran et al., arXiv 1912.02292 (ICLR 2020)
    3. 2019
      Interpretable models for high-stakes decisions

      Cynthia Rudin argued that for decisions like credit and criminal justice you should build inherently interpretable models rather than explain black boxes after the fact, and that the accuracy cost is often small on structured data.

      Rudin, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019
    4. 2020
      Benign overfitting in linear regression

      Bartlett and colleagues characterised when a linear model that fits noisy training data exactly can still predict well: with many weak directions in the data, the noise gets spread so thinly it barely harms. A theoretical explanation of why big models can interpolate and still generalise.

      Bartlett et al., PNAS, 2020
    5. 2022
      Trees still beat deep nets on typical tabular data

      Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux benchmarked tree ensembles against neural networks on medium-sized tabular data sets and found trees ahead, even before accounting for tuning cost. Reasons offered: tabular features are often irregular, uninformative features hurt networks, and the useful structure is not rotation-invariant.

      Grinsztajn et al., NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2022 (arXiv 2207.08815)
    6. 2025
      A pre-trained model for small tables

      TabPFN, trained on millions of synthetic data sets, predicts in a single forward pass with no per-data-set training. The Nature paper reported that it beat gradient-boosted trees tuned for four hours on data sets up to about 10,000 rows. It is, in effect, a learned replacement for choosing the right bias.

      Hollmann et al., Nature, 2025
    7. 2026
      Larger tabular foundation models, mixed independent evidence

      A vendor technical report for TabPFN-3 claims leadership up to one million training rows over tuned boosted trees. An independent-looking comparison across 19 data sets found tabular foundation models first on small data, a near tie on large data, and plain logistic regression beating all of them on one high-cardinality categorical task. Treat the first as a claim, the second as indicative; neither is a banking study.

      TabPFN-3 technical report, arXiv 2605.13986 (vendor); AIMultiple tabular benchmark, 2026

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Reporting training performance

      "AUC 0.97." On what data? A deep tree or an unregularised wide model scores near perfect on its own training set.

      Spot itAsk for the same number on a held-out, later-in-time sample. A big gap is overfitting.
    • Tuning on the test set

      Trying twenty settings, keeping the one with the best test score, and reporting that score. The test set has become part of training.

      Spot itAsk how many models were compared and which data chose between them. There should be a validation set, or cross-validation, separate from the final test.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat results, poor first quarter live. Customers and conditions from the "future" were in training.

      Spot itAsk for an out-of-time test. If performance drops sharply, the random split was lenient.
    • Too many inputs for the events

      200 candidate features, 90 defaults. The model fits noise that happens to correlate with the outcome.

      Spot itCompare the number of candidate inputs to the number of events, not rows. Check that selection happened inside the training fold.
    • Selecting features on the full data, then cross-validating

      The selection step has seen the outcome of every row. Cross-validation then looks excellent and means nothing.

      Spot itAsk where feature selection sits in the pipeline. It must be repeated inside each training fold.
    • Reading correlated coefficients as importance

      "Debt-to-income is twice as important as utilisation" when the two are 90% correlated.

      Spot itCheck input correlations and variance inflation factors. Refit on a resample: if the coefficient flips or halves, it is not telling you what you think.
    • Quoting a logistic coefficient as a change in probability

      "Each missed payment adds 9 points of default risk." It multiplies odds; the probability change depends where you start.

      Spot itAsk "from what baseline?" If there is no answer, ask for the odds ratio instead.
    • Mistaking a flexible model's win for a real one

      Boosted model beats the logistic baseline by 0.4 points of AUC on 120 defaults.

      Spot itAsk for a bootstrap interval on the difference (F2.2). With few events it often contains zero.
    • Believing more data always helps

      Adding another 10 million legitimate rows to a model whose problem is that it has 300 fraud cases.

      Spot itAsk how the learning curve looks: validation error against training-set size. If it is flat, more of the same data will not help. Plot it by events.
    • Confusing modelling bias with unfairness

      "The model has high bias" heard in a risk meeting as "the model discriminates".

      Spot itSay which one you mean. Use "underfits" for the statistical meaning, and "disparate impact" for the fairness one.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A model that is perfect on last year's data is not necessarily good. It may have memorised last year. We test every model on a later period it never saw, and we only trust that number. For this problem the simple, transparent model gets us most of the way, and we'll only adopt a more complex one if it beats the simple one by enough to justify losing the explanation. I'll bring both numbers, with a margin of error.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd begin with a penalised logistic regression on well-engineered, monotone inputs as the benchmark, evaluated out of time, with log loss, AUC and calibration. Candidate flexible models are compared by repeated time-aware validation and bootstrap intervals on the difference. I watch variance first, because the binding constraint is the number of events, not rows. I check coefficient stability across resamples before reading any of them as drivers.”
    “What is overfitting, in one sentence?”

    The model has learned the noise and quirks of the training sample as if they were the pattern, so it does better on data it has seen than on data it has not. The symptom is a gap between training and held-out performance that widens as the model gets more flexible.

    “Is the bias-variance trade-off still true for modern deep learning?”

    It is a good description of classical models and of most tabular work. For very large networks, test error can fall again beyond the point of fitting the training data exactly, the so-called double descent, so the simple U-curve is incomplete there. The practical lessons survive: regularise, validate on unseen data, and do not trust training error.

    “Why not just use XGBoost for everything?”

    For many tabular problems I would end up there, especially fraud. But I'd build the regularised regression first, because it is the benchmark, it shows how much signal there is, and it is what a validator and a regulator can follow. If boosting beats it by a margin that survives intervals, I'd use it with monotone constraints and a calibration step, and keep the regression as the challenger.

    “The model has 99% accuracy on training and 85% on test. What do you do?”

    That is high variance. I'd first check for leakage or a bad split, then see how training and validation error behave as data grows. Options: simplify (fewer features, shallower trees), regularise, get more events, or average models. I'd not add complexity. I'd also check whether 85% is already close to what's achievable, given label noise.

    “Why is a logistic coefficient not 'the effect on probability'?”

    It is the effect on log-odds, so the odds are multiplied by e to the coefficient. The change in probability depends on the starting point: the same coefficient moves a 5% customer by about 6 points and a 40% customer by more than 20. I'd quote the odds ratio, and the probability change at named example customers if needed.

    “Can we trust the coefficients to say which factor drives default?”

    Only with care. Correlated inputs share credit arbitrarily, so individual coefficients can swing between refits while predictions stay stable. I'd check correlations, refit on bootstrap samples, and for important claims use a method suited to the question, such as grouped effects, or a causal design where the question is causal (F9.2). Association in a credit model is not a recommendation to change a customer's behaviour.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. For one model, squared bias is 0.04, variance is 0.09 and the noise is 0.25. What is the expected error on new data, which part dominates the fixable error, and what would you try?

    0.04 + 0.09 + 0.25 = 0.38. The noise (0.25) cannot be removed. Of the fixable part (0.13), variance is the larger share, so reduce it: add a penalty, simplify, get more events, or average several models. Adding flexibility would shrink the 0.04 but probably raise the 0.09.

    2. Training error is 2% and validation error 15%. A second model has 14% and 15%. Describe each.

    The first has high variance: it fits training data too closely (overfits). The second is probably high bias, or close to the noise floor: both errors are high and similar. If you believe the achievable error is about 5%, the second is underfitting and needs more flexibility or better features. If the floor is near 14%, it is fine.

    3. A logistic coefficient for "missed payments" (per standard deviation) is 0.9. What happens to the odds? What happens to a customer who starts at 5% default probability?

    Odds multiply by e0.9 ≈ 2.46. A 5% customer has odds 0.0526; times 2.46 is 0.129; probability = 0.129 ÷ 1.129 ≈ 11.5%. A 40% customer (odds 0.667) goes to 1.64, which is 62%. Same odds ratio, different probability change.

    4. A regression coefficient is 0.5 with a standard error of 0.2. Give an approximate 95% interval and say what it tells you.

    About 0.5 ± 1.96 × 0.2 = ±0.39, so 0.11 to 0.89. It excludes zero, so the data supports a positive effect, but the size is uncertain by a factor of eight. Remember that this standard error assumes the model form is right and the rows are independent.

    5. You have 90 defaults in 30,000 loans and 60 candidate inputs. Is a logistic regression with all of them sensible?

    No. 90 events for 60 inputs is 1.5 events per input, far below the old rule of 10, and the properly calculated minimums (Riley et al., 2020) would be demanding too. Select a handful of inputs on domain grounds inside the training folds, or use a strong penalty, and expect a wide interval on any performance number. The 30,000 rows are not your sample size, the 90 events are.

    6. Two inputs, utilisation and debt-to-income, are 90% correlated. One refit gives coefficients (1.4, −0.2), another gives (−0.1, 1.2). Is the model unstable?

    The predictions probably are not: both fits give a combined effect of about 1.2 to 1.3. The data cannot separate the two inputs, so it splits the credit between them differently each time. Do not interpret them separately. Fixes: combine them into one feature, drop one, or use a ridge penalty, which shrinks the pair toward sharing the effect (Simulation 3).

    7. You tuned hyperparameters using five-fold cross-validation, then reported the best cross-validation score as your performance. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

    The best of many scores is biased upward, because you picked it for being high. The reported number should come from data that played no role in choosing the settings: a separate later-in-time test set, or nested cross-validation. The size of the optimism grows with the number of settings tried and shrinks with data.
