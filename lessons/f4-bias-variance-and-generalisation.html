    Bias, Variance and Overfitting

    Part 1

    What overfitting is

    In one sentenceA model overfits when it learns the noise in its training data as if it were the pattern, so it does well on old data and badly on new data.

    Think of two students with 20 practice questions. One memorises the 20 answers word for word. The other learns the method. The first is perfect on practice and lost on the real paper. That is overfitting. Generalisation is doing well on data the model has not seen.

    Our running example is house prices again: predict price from floor area, using a handful of past sales.

    The left model underfits: it misses the bend. The right one overfits: it hits every dot but swings wildly in between, so its price for a new 100 m² house is unreliable. The middle one captures the trend and ignores the noise.

    How it differs from similar ideas

    • Overfitting vs underfitting. Overfit models are too flexible. Underfit models are too rigid. Both fail on new data, for opposite reasons.
    • Training error vs test error. Training error is the miss on data the model learned from. Test error is the miss on held-back data. Only test error is honest.
    • Statistical bias vs unfair bias. "Bias" here means "too simple". It has nothing to do with unfair treatment of groups. Say "underfits" for one and "disparate impact" for the other.
    Part 2

    Bias, variance and the trade-off

    Picture darts players, each trained on a different sample of houses. Their throws show three kinds of error.

    • Bias: how far the centre of the throws is from the target. A straight line fitted to a curve is wrong in the same way every time.
    • Variance: how widely the throws spread. A flexible model reshapes itself around each sample's noise, so every sample gives a different curve.
    • Noise: the part nobody can explain. Two identical houses still sell for different prices.

    Cutting bias tends to raise variance. The best model sits in the middle, where the total is smallest. Training error alone would always say "more flexible".

    How to read it: diagnose before you act

    • Both errors high and close together means high bias. More data will not help. Add flexibility or better features.
    • Training error low, test error much higher means high variance. More data, a simpler model, a penalty or averaging will help.
    • A learning curve (error against amount of training data) shows which. A gap that keeps closing as data grows is variance. Two curves that flatten together at a high error are bias.
    • Noise floor. Test error never reaches zero. Chasing it means overfitting.

    Worked reading: a model has £8,000 average miss on training houses and £35,000 on test houses. That gap is the signature of variance. Simplify, or collect more sales.

    How to measure it honestly

    1. Split the data three ways: training (to fit), validation (to compare models and settings) and test (touched once, at the end).
    2. Fit on training only.
    3. Compare options on validation. Every comparison leaks a little information into your choices, so the validation score drifts optimistic.
    4. Report the test score once. If you go back to it repeatedly it becomes a second validation set.

    In time-ordered data, the test set must come after the training set, because the model will be used on the future.

    Part 3

    Regularisation: a leash on the coefficients

    In one sentenceRegularisation adds a penalty for large coefficients, trading a little bias for a lot less variance.

    A regression with many features can fit noise by giving big weights to quirky features. The penalty makes each weight justify its size. Two common versions:

    • Ridge shrinks all coefficients smoothly towards zero. When two features are correlated (floor area and rooms), it shares the weight between them. It never drops a feature.
    • Lasso pushes weak coefficients to exactly zero, so it also selects features. Among correlated features it keeps one, and which one can change between samples.
    • Elastic net blends the two.

    The strength knob (often written lambda or alpha) is chosen by cross-validation: refit on different slices of the training data and pick the value with the lowest error on the held-out slices. Too weak leaves variance. Too strong squeezes everything towards zero, which is bias.

    Standardise features first. Otherwise the penalty treats floor area in square metres and age in years unequally, just because of their units.

    Other ways to tame variance: more data (especially more of the rare outcome you care about), a simpler model, early stopping for boosting and neural nets, and averaging many models, as in a random forest.

    Part 4

    Which cure to use

    CureLowersCostUse when
    RidgeVarianceSmall biasMany correlated features
    LassoVarianceSmall bias, unstable picksYou want fewer features
    More dataVarianceTime and moneyGap between training and test is large
    More flexible modelBiasHigher varianceBoth errors high and close together
    Averaging (forest)VarianceHarder to explainA single flexible model is unstable

    Regularised regression is strong at:

    • Small data. Variance is usually the binding constraint, so the penalty helps.
    • Stability. Refits give nearly the same answer.

    It is weak at:

    • Strong bends and interactions, where bias dominates and a penalty cannot help.
    • Plain interpretation. Penalised coefficients are shrunk, and p-values after lasso are too optimistic because the data chose the features.
    Show the formulas

    Error on new data = bias squared + variance + noise.

    Ridge minimises the squared misses plus lambda × the sum of squared coefficients. Lasso uses the sum of absolute coefficients instead, which is why some reach exactly zero.

    In banking, test sets must come from a later period than training, and a penalised logistic regression is a common first model when defaults are few.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging a model on its training data

      "R² of 0.97" means little if the model has already seen those rows. Always report held-out results.

    • Reusing the test set

      Each time you tune against it, it becomes another validation set. Touch it once.

    • Fixing the wrong problem

      Adding data to a high-bias model changes nothing. Check training and test errors first.

    • Choosing the penalty by eye

      Pick lambda by cross-validation, and standardise features before fitting.

    1. Training error is 2 and test error is 20. Bias or variance problem?

    Variance (overfitting). The fix is more data, a simpler model, or a penalty.

    2. Training error and test error are both 20. What now?

    High bias (underfitting). More data will not help; add flexibility or better features.

    3. What is the difference between ridge and lasso?

    Ridge shrinks all coefficients smoothly and keeps every feature. Lasso sets some exactly to zero, so it drops features.

    4. Why can you not use the test set to choose between models?

    Choosing on it leaks it into your decision, so its score turns optimistic. Use the validation set to choose and the test set once at the end.

    5. A penalty is too strong. What goes wrong?

    Coefficients shrink towards zero, the model becomes too simple, and bias rises.
