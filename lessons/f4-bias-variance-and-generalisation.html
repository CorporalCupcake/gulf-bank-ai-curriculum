    Bias, Variance and Generalisation, and the Regression Models That Keep Winning

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two students prepare for an exam with 20 practice questions. One memorises the 20 answers word for word: perfect on those, lost on the real paper. The other learns the method behind them: slips on a few practice questions but does well on new ones.

    The first overfitted (in banking, the same happens to a risk rule built from 20 loan files): it learned the sample's quirks as if they were the pattern. Generalisation is how well a model does on data it was not built on.

    • Training error always improves with flexibility, like memorising last year's exam.
    • Error on new data is U-shaped. Too rigid misses structure; too flexible chases noise.
    • There is a floor. Trying to beat it is overfitting.
    Hold onto thisNever judge a model on the data it was fitted to.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Generalisation: what you can measure

    Training error is optimistic because the model was chosen to make it small. Test error, on held-back data, is the honest estimate. Keep three sets: training, validation (for comparing models) and test (touched once, at the end).

    The difference between the two is the generalisation gap, and a big gap means overfitting. Every time you compare models or settings on the validation set, a little of it leaks into your choices, so its score drifts optimistic. That is why the test set is touched once: go back to it repeatedly and it becomes a second validation set.

    At a bank the test set must be later in time than training, because the model is used on the future. A random split of a drifting process lets the model peek (F4.5).

    Bias, variance and noise

    Picture darts players, each trained on a different sample. Bias is how far the centre of the cloud is from the target: a straight line fitted to a curve is wrong the same way every time. Variance is how widely the cloud spreads: a flexible model reshapes itself around each sample's noise. Noise is what nobody can explain.

    Cutting bias tends to raise variance, so the best model minimises the sum. In words: expected error on new data is bias squared plus variance plus noise.

    The two problems need different cures, so diagnose before you act. If training and validation error are both high and close together, you have a bias problem: more data will not help, more flexibility or better inputs will. If training error is low and validation error much higher, you have a variance problem: more data, a simpler model, a penalty or averaging will help. Plotting error against training-set size (a learning curve) shows which case you are in: a gap that keeps closing as data grows is variance; two curves that flatten together are bias.

    More flexibility (extra features, deeper trees) lowers bias and raises variance. A penalty or early stopping adds a little bias and removes a lot of variance. More events (defaults, confirmed fraud) lower variance and leave bias alone; this is the count that matters, not the number of rows. Averaging many models lowers variance (F4.3). "Bias" here means a model too simple, not unfairness (G2): say "underfits" for one and "disparate impact" for the other, or a risk meeting will confuse them.

    Linear regression

    Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of inputs, like a recipe with a quantity per ingredient. Its coefficients read plainly, come with standard errors, and it has low variance when inputs are few and rows many.

    A coefficient reads as: "holding the other inputs fixed, one extra hour of study goes with 4 more marks, give or take a standard error" (the standard error says how uncertain that 4 is). That sentence is the reason regulators and business owners trust it.

    The price is bias: it assumes each input acts as a straight line and acts independently. Real patterns bend (extra study hours help a lot at first, then barely at all) and interact. Much of this is fixable cheaply: transform inputs (log income, age bands), add a few chosen interactions, or use splines (smooth bent lines). The polynomial fits in Simulations 1 and 2 are exactly this trick of making a linear model bend.

    It breaks in three places. Correlated inputs make coefficients unstable though predictions stay fine. Outliers pull the line towards them, because squared error punishes big misses hardest. And a bounded target such as a probability gets answers below 0 or above 1, which is why the next model exists.

    Logistic regression

    For a yes/no outcome (passed or failed, repaid or defaulted) we want a probability, so logistic regression pushes the weighted sum through an S-shaped curve. Its coefficients act on the odds: one that doubles the odds takes a 5% chance to roughly 10% but a 40% chance to roughly 57%. Never say "adds X points" without a baseline.

    Fitting minimises log loss, which punishes confident wrong answers hard: predicting 1% for something that happens costs far more than predicting 40%. That pressure is what keeps the probabilities honest.

    Banks like it because its probabilities are usually calibrated, meaning they match observed rates (F7.4), whereas boosted trees and neural nets often need recalibrating. Credit scorecards are logistic regression on binned inputs, with points from each bin: an audit trail a regulator can follow line by line. Two traps: an input that perfectly splits defaulters from the rest makes a coefficient blow up (a penalty fixes it), and too many inputs for the events (the old rule is 10 events per variable).

    Taming variance: the ridge penalty

    Regularisation trades a little bias for a lot less variance, like a leash on the coefficients. Ridge keeps them small unless the data insists (more in F4.4). The simulation refits a "will this house sell within a month?" model 30 times. Two of its inputs, floor area and number of rooms, are 90% correlated, because bigger houses have more rooms.

    The single knob is the penalty strength, and you choose it by cross-validation, never by eye. Too weak and variance remains; too strong and every coefficient is squeezed towards zero, which is bias. Ridge shrinks all coefficients smoothly and handles correlated inputs gracefully by sharing the weight between them. Lasso, the sibling penalty, pushes weak coefficients exactly to zero, so it also selects inputs, but among correlated inputs it picks one somewhat arbitrarily. Standardise inputs first, or the penalty treats a salary in dirhams and an age in years unequally.

    Correlated inputs make individual coefficients fragile but not predictions. Never say "rooms matter more than floor area" from raw coefficients; explanation tools (F8.1) inherit the same ambiguity.

    Why the simple model is the baseline

    On tabular data with few events, a regularised regression is often within a hair of the fanciest alternative, because variance is the binding constraint. Build it first: it sets the bar. If a boosted model beats it by one AUC point but is far harder to explain, that is a business question (C1.2). When the gap is large, take it seriously: interactions in transaction data are why fraud models are mostly tree ensembles.

    Two reasons the baseline is so hard to beat. Few events make variance the binding constraint, and credit inputs have been engineered over decades to be roughly linear in log-odds, so there is little bias left to remove.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at:

    • Small and medium tabular data. Few parameters means low variance, so it generalises from hundreds of events where a flexible model would memorise them.
    • Explanation and audit. Each coefficient is one input's effect with the others held fixed, with a standard error. A validator can recompute a score by hand.
    • Honest probabilities. Log-loss fitting keeps logistic outputs close to real rates, so they can feed provisioning and pricing directly.
    • Speed and stability. It fits in seconds, has almost no tuning, and refits give nearly the same answer, which makes monitoring simple.
    • A benchmark. Its score tells you how much a complex model actually adds.

    Weak at, or don't use when:

    • Strong interactions or thresholds. Fraud depends on combinations (new device, high amount, odd hour), which a sum of straight lines cannot see unless you hand-build them.
    • Unstructured inputs. Text, images and raw transaction sequences need learned features, not hand-weighted columns.
    • Hundreds of correlated inputs with few events. Without a penalty the coefficients are noise; with one you have changed the model, so say so.
    • Reading coefficients as causes or importance. They describe association, and correlated inputs share the credit arbitrarily.
    • Very large data with complex structure. Bias then dominates, and extra rows cannot fix it.
    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    Regularised logistic or linear regressionFew events, regulated decisions, you need a baseline or a scorecardMany strong interactions, or text and image inputs
    Gradient-boosted treesTabular data with interactions and thousands of events: fraud, behavioural scoresYou must show the logic line by line, or events are very few
    Random forestYou want a strong, low-tuning model that resists overfittingProbabilities must be calibrated without extra work
    Generalised additive model (bent curve per input)You want shapes like "the effect flattens after 8 hours of study" while staying readableInteractions drive the signal

    In practice. Start with a regularised logistic regression on a modest set of sensible inputs, standardised, with the penalty chosen by cross-validation on a time-ordered split. Check the learning curve, then try a boosted model and see whether the gap justifies the loss of transparency. Count events, not rows: roughly ten events per candidate input is the old safe rule, and a penalty relaxes it.

    Compute is trivial; a laptop handles millions of rows. In banking this is the working model of retail credit scorecards, PD (probability of default) models under IFRS 9 and many AML risk-rating scorecards, usually with binned inputs. In fraud it is the benchmark that boosted trees must beat. Forecasting teams use regression with seasonal and calendar terms as the first thing to try.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging on data the model has seen

      "AUC 0.97." On what data? Reporting the best of twenty tries turns the test set into training.

      Spot itAsk for a held-out number and how many models were compared.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat results, poor first live quarter: the "future" was in training.

      Spot itAsk for an out-of-time test.
    • Too many inputs for the events

      200 features, 90 positive cases: the model fits noise.

      Spot itCompare inputs to events, not rows.
    • Reading correlated coefficients as importance

      "Rooms are twice as important as floor area" when the two are 90% correlated.

      Spot itRefit on a resample. If a coefficient flips, ignore it.
    • Fixing variance with a bigger model, or bias with more data

      The cure must match the problem. More rows will not rescue a straight line fitted to a bent pattern; a deeper model will not rescue 90 defaults.

      Spot itPlot a learning curve before choosing the remedy.
    • Quoting a logistic coefficient as a change in probability

      "Each extra room adds 9 points to the chance of a quick sale." It multiplies odds; the probability change depends on the start.

      Spot itAsk "from what baseline?"
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Training error is 2% and validation error 15%. A second model has 14% and 15%. Describe each.

    The first overfits (high variance). The second has similar, high errors: it either underfits or is already near the noise floor.

    2. Why can't you say "each missed payment adds 9 points of default risk" from a logistic coefficient?

    The coefficient multiplies the odds, not the probability, so the change in points depends on where the customer starts.

    3. You have 90 defaults in 30,000 loans and 60 candidate inputs. Is a logistic regression with all of them sensible?

    No. Your real sample size is the 90 events, not the 30,000 rows. Use a handful of inputs or a strong penalty.

    4. Two refits give coefficients (1.4, −0.2) and (−0.1, 1.2) for correlated floor area and rooms. Is the model unstable?

    Predictions probably are not: the combined effect is about 1.2 to 1.3 both times. The data cannot separate the two inputs; combine them or use ridge.

    5. You tuned with cross-validation and reported the best cross-validation score. What is wrong?

    The best of many scores is biased upward because you picked it for being high. Report a number from data that played no part in choosing settings, such as a later-in-time test set.

    6. A regulator will review a retail credit model. You have 40,000 loans, 800 defaults and 25 candidate inputs. A boosted model beats logistic regression by 0.5 AUC points. Which do you choose, and why?

    Logistic regression, penalised, probably as a scorecard. 800 events supports 25 inputs only just, the gain is tiny, and the audit trail and calibration are worth more than half a point. Keep the boosted model as a challenger to monitor.

    7. A card-fraud team has 5 million transactions, 60,000 confirmed frauds and many interacting features. Logistic regression gives a weak result. Which would you choose, and why?

    Gradient-boosted trees. Fraud signal sits in interactions that a sum of straight lines cannot capture, and 60,000 events is enough to keep variance in check. Keep the logistic model as the baseline and check calibration of the boosted scores.
