    Bias, Variance and Generalisation, and the Regression Models That Keep Winning

    Why a model that fits the past perfectly can be useless on tomorrow's customers, how error splits into three parts you can reason about, and why plain linear and logistic regression are still the baseline every fancier model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is only worth what it does on cases it has not seen, error on those cases comes from being too rigid (bias), too jumpy (variance) or from noise nobody can remove, and linear and logistic regression sit at the low-variance end where most bank data, with its few hundred or thousand real events, belongs.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two junior analysts get the last 20 personal-loan files, each marked repaid or defaulted, and must build a rule for the next 1,000 applicants.

    The first writes: "salary between AED 14,200 and 14,900, employer starts with 'A', applied on a Tuesday: default." It is perfect on the 20 files and close to random on the 1,000, because its detail was coincidence.

    The second writes: "the more of the limit used and the more payments missed, the riskier." It misses a few of the 20 but does about as well on the 1,000.

    The first overfitted: it learned the sample's quirks as if they were the pattern. The second may underfit slightly, but its rule travels. Generalisation is that travelling: performance on data the model was not built on. It is the only performance anyone pays for.

    In the simulation, a smooth curve plus random noise (like real customers' unpredictable side) generates the data. You choose how flexible the fitted curve is.

    • Training error always improves with flexibility. Like a student memorising last year's exam answers: a perfect score on the old paper says nothing about the new one. Training error can only tell you a model is bad, never that it is good.
    • Error on new data is U-shaped. Too rigid misses structure; too flexible chases noise. The best point sits in the middle, and moves with data size and noise.
    • There is a floor. The noise line is the error of a model that knows the true pattern. A default model never nears zero, because whether a customer loses their job next month is not in the data. Trying to beat the floor is overfitting.
    Hold onto thisNever judge a model on the data it was fitted to. Ask what it does on data it never touched, and how that changes with complexity and data size.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Generalisation: what you want, and what you can measure

    A model's true error is its average loss over everything the world could produce. You never see it. You see the training error (on the sample) and the test error (on a held-back sample).

    Training error is optimistic because the model was chosen to make it small. The gap is the generalisation gap: it grows with flexibility and shrinks with sample size. A big gap means overfitting. This is Simulation 1 in one line.

    So every workflow has three data sets, like practice papers, mock exam and the real exam:

    • Training set: the model learns its parameters.
    • Validation set: you compare models and settings (degree, penalty, tree depth). Each choice leaks a little of it into the model.
    • Test set: touched once, at the end, to report the number. Go back to it repeatedly and it becomes a second validation set, and the number is optimistic.

    Leakage is covered in F3.1 and scarce or time-ordered data in F4.5. At a bank the test set must be later in time than training, because the model is used on the future. A random split of a drifting process lets the model peek.

    Bias, variance and noise

    Picture many darts players throwing at a target, each after seeing a different training sample. Fit the same model type to many independent samples and look at the predictions at one point x:

    • Bias: how far the centre of the cloud is from the truth. A straight line fitted to a curve is wrong the same way every time. More data will not fix it; more flexibility will.
    • Variance: how widely the cloud spreads. A flexible model reshapes itself around each sample's noise. More data, a simpler model or a penalty narrows it.

    Add the noise in the outcome and expected squared error splits exactly into three:

    E[(y−f^(x))2]=(bias)2+variance+σ2

    Bias is the gap between the average fit (over many samples) and the truth. Variance is how much one fit wanders around that average. σ² is noise no model can explain. Exact for squared error; for log loss or classification error the intuition holds but not the clean split.

    Whatever cuts bias (more features, deeper trees) tends to raise variance, and the reverse. The best model minimises the sum. A bias or variance of 0.04 against 0.25 noise is small in context: always compare the three. The simulation fits the same model type to 80 fresh samples and measures all three terms.

    Two cautions. "Bias" here is modelling bias (a model too simple), not unfairness (G2); mixing them up in a meeting is a common slip. And for very large models test error can fall a second time ("double descent"), but for everyday tabular bank models the classical U-curve is the working picture.

    Linear regression: the model that fits in one line

    Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of inputs (income, balance, tenure), like a recipe with a quantity per ingredient. Fitting picks the weights that minimise squared training errors. For this model the answer is a formula, not a search:

    β^=(XTX)−1XTy

    X is the input table (a row per customer, plus a column of ones for the intercept), y the outcomes. This "normal equation" is what the polynomial fits in Simulations 1 and 2 solve. It fails if one input is an exact copy of another, and wobbles if two are nearly copies.

    Why it earns its place:

    • Plain coefficients. "Holding the other inputs fixed, AED 1,000 more monthly income goes with AED 380 more card spend." The coefficient is the effect after the other inputs have taken their share, not the raw relationship (F2.4, F2.5).
    • Uncertainty built in. Each coefficient has a standard error: "0.5, give or take 0.2".
    • Low variance by design. With p inputs and n rows, the train-test gap is about p/n of the noise. Ten inputs, 1,000 rows: about 1%.
    • Predictable extrapolation. A forest cannot predict above the highest value it has seen; a line keeps going. Sometimes that is what you want, sometimes a disaster, but you know which.

    The price is bias: it assumes each input acts as a straight line, independently. Real patterns bend (risk rises faster once utilisation passes 80%) and interact (a missed payment matters more for a new customer). Two cheap repairs keep it linear in its coefficients: add transformed inputs (log income, squared terms, age bands) and a few chosen interactions. The polynomial in Simulation 1 is exactly this: columns 1, x, x², x³.

    It breaks in three places. Correlated inputs make coefficients unstable though predictions stay fine (Simulation 3). Outliers pull the line, because squared error punishes big misses. And a bounded target such as a probability gets answers below 0 or above 1, which is why the next model exists.

    Logistic regression: the same idea for yes or no

    For repaid/defaulted or fraud/not-fraud we want a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression keeps the weighted sum z but pushes it through an S-shaped curve. The sum is read as log-odds:

    lnp1−p=β0+β1x1+β2x2+…⇔p=11+e−z

    Odds are p ÷ (1 − p): 20% is odds of 1 to 4, or 0.25. Log-odds are symmetric around zero (50% is 0; 20% is about −1.39; 80% is +1.39). Coefficients add on the log-odds scale, so they multiply the odds.

    Reading a coefficient. A coefficient of 0.9 on "missed payments, per standard deviation" multiplies default odds by e^0.9 ≈ 2.46. From 5% (odds 0.0526) that gives odds 0.129, or 11.5%. From 40% it gives 62%. Same odds ratio, different probability change, because the S-curve is steepest in the middle. Never say "adds X points of probability" without saying where on the curve.

    Fitting. No closed form: software searches for the coefficients that make the observed outcomes most likely (maximum likelihood, F1.7), which equals minimising log loss, a penalty of −ln(p) when the event happened and you predicted p. Confident wrong answers hurt: predicting 1% for something that happens costs 4.6, predicting 40% costs 0.9. The problem is convex, so there is one best answer and no unlucky start.

    Why banks like it:

    • Calibrated by default. Its probabilities usually mean what they say, which matters for provisioning and pricing (F7.4). Boosted trees and nets often need recalibrating.
    • The scorecard. Credit scorecards are logistic regression on binned inputs, with points from each bin's coefficient: an audit trail a regulator can follow line by line.
    • Easy constraints. If higher income must never raise risk, check the sign. Flexible models need monotonic constraints.
    • Stable. Retrain next quarter and coefficients move a little, not everywhere.

    Two failure modes to know cold. Separation: if an input perfectly splits defaulters from non-defaulters in training (common with few events and a sparse category), the likelihood keeps improving as the coefficient grows, so you get a huge coefficient or no convergence. Penalising fixes it. Too many inputs for the events: only the minority outcome informs the boundary, so 60 defaults cannot support 40 inputs. The old rule is 10 events per candidate variable; Riley and colleagues (BMJ, 2020) give calculable minimums that depend on inputs, event rate and expected fit, and 10 is sometimes too few.

    Taming variance: the ridge penalty in action

    Regularisation trades a little bias for a lot less variance, like a leash on the coefficients. Ridge (L2) adds a penalty on the sum of squared coefficients, so the optimiser keeps them small unless the data insists. F4.4 covers L1, L2 and early stopping.

    The loan model here has five standardised inputs: utilisation, debt-to-income (correlation 0.9 with utilisation), missed payments, and two pure-noise inputs. The true effects are known because we generated the data. We fit logistic regression to 30 independent samples and plot where each coefficient lands.

    Correlated inputs make individual coefficients fragile but not predictions. Two correlated inputs can have wildly different coefficients across refits and predict equally well. So never tell a business owner "debt-to-income matters more than utilisation" from raw coefficients. Explanation tools (F8.1) inherit the same ambiguity.

    Why the simple model is the baseline you must beat

    On tabular data with thousands to hundreds of thousands of rows and few events, a regularised linear or logistic model is often within a hair of the fanciest alternative. Few events make variance the binding constraint; noisy outcomes make the noise floor high, leaving little for flexibility to gain; and credit inputs have been engineered over decades to be roughly linear in log-odds.

    The evidence is mixed, and you should say so. A large credit-scoring benchmark (Lessmann and colleagues, 2015) found tree ensembles beat logistic regression on average by a real but modest margin. A review of clinical risk models (Christodoulou and colleagues, 2019) found no consistent benefit of machine learning over logistic regression once study quality was accounted for. The practical rule is the same: always build the regularised regression first. It sets the bar, shows how much signal exists, and gives a validator a comparison. If a boosted model beats it by one point of AUC but is a hundred times harder to explain, that is a business question (C1.2).

    When the gap is large, take it seriously: interactions in transaction data (velocity, merchant, device, time of day) are why fraud models are mostly tree ensembles.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reporting training performance

      "AUC 0.97." On what data? A deep tree or wide unregularised model scores near perfect on its own training set.

      Spot itAsk for the same number on a later-in-time held-out sample. A big gap is overfitting.
    • Tuning on the test set

      Trying twenty settings and reporting the best test score. The test set has become training.

      Spot itAsk how many models were compared and which data chose between them. There must be a validation set or cross-validation separate from the test.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat results, poor first live quarter: the "future" was in training.

      Spot itAsk for an out-of-time test. A sharp drop means the random split was lenient.
    • Too many inputs for the events

      200 candidate features, 90 defaults. The model fits noise that happens to correlate.

      Spot itCompare inputs to events, not rows. Check selection happened inside the training fold.
    • Selecting features on the full data, then cross-validating

      Selection has seen every row's outcome, so cross-validation looks excellent and means nothing.

      Spot itAsk where selection sits in the pipeline. It must be repeated inside each training fold.
    • Reading correlated coefficients as importance

      "Debt-to-income is twice as important as utilisation" when the two are 90% correlated.

      Spot itCheck correlations and variance inflation factors. Refit on a resample: if the coefficient flips or halves, it is not telling you what you think.
    • Quoting a logistic coefficient as a change in probability

      "Each missed payment adds 9 points of default risk." It multiplies odds; the probability change depends on the start.

      Spot itAsk "from what baseline?" If no answer, ask for the odds ratio.
    • Mistaking a flexible model's win for a real one

      Boosted model beats the logistic baseline by 0.4 points of AUC on 120 defaults.

      Spot itAsk for a bootstrap interval on the difference (F2.2). With few events it often contains zero.
    • Believing more data always helps

      Adding 10 million legitimate rows to a model whose problem is 300 fraud cases.

      Spot itAsk for the learning curve (validation error against training size), plotted by events. If flat, more of the same data will not help.
    • Confusing modelling bias with unfairness

      "The model has high bias" heard in a risk meeting as "the model discriminates".

      Spot itSay "underfits" for the statistical meaning and "disparate impact" for fairness.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Squared bias is 0.04, variance 0.09, noise 0.25. What is the expected error on new data, which part dominates the fixable error, and what would you try?

    0.04 + 0.09 + 0.25 = 0.38. Noise (0.25) cannot be removed. Of the fixable 0.13, variance is larger, so add a penalty, simplify, get more events or average models. More flexibility would shrink the 0.04 but probably raise the 0.09.

    2. Training error is 2% and validation error 15%. A second model has 14% and 15%. Describe each.

    The first has high variance (overfits). The second has similar high errors: high bias, or near the noise floor. If achievable error is about 5%, it underfits and needs flexibility or better features. If the floor is near 14%, it is fine.

    3. A logistic coefficient for "missed payments" (per standard deviation) is 0.9. What happens to the odds, and to a customer starting at 5%?

    Odds multiply by e0.9 ≈ 2.46. A 5% customer (odds 0.0526) goes to 0.129, so 0.129 ÷ 1.129 ≈ 11.5%. A 40% customer (odds 0.667) goes to 1.64, which is 62%. Same odds ratio, different probability change.

    4. A coefficient is 0.5 with standard error 0.2. Give an approximate 95% interval and say what it tells you.

    0.5 ± 1.96 × 0.2 ≈ ±0.39, so 0.11 to 0.89. It excludes zero, so a positive effect is supported, but the size is uncertain by a factor of eight. The standard error assumes the model form is right and rows are independent.

    5. You have 90 defaults in 30,000 loans and 60 candidate inputs. Is a logistic regression with all of them sensible?

    No. 90 events for 60 inputs is 1.5 per input, far below the old rule of 10, and the calculated minimums (Riley et al., 2020) would also be demanding. Pick a handful of inputs on domain grounds inside the training folds, or use a strong penalty, and expect wide intervals. Your sample size is the 90 events, not the 30,000 rows.

    6. Utilisation and debt-to-income are 90% correlated. One refit gives coefficients (1.4, −0.2), another (−0.1, 1.2). Is the model unstable?

    Predictions probably are not: both give a combined effect of about 1.2 to 1.3. The data cannot separate the inputs, so it splits credit differently each time. Do not interpret them separately. Combine them into one feature, drop one, or use ridge (Simulation 3).

    7. You tuned hyperparameters with five-fold cross-validation and reported the best cross-validation score. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

    The best of many scores is biased upward because you picked it for being high. Report a number from data that played no part in choosing settings: a later-in-time test set, or nested cross-validation. The optimism grows with settings tried and shrinks with data.
