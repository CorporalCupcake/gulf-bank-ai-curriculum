    Bias, Variance and Generalisation, and the Regression Models That Keep Winning

    Why a model that fits the past perfectly can be useless on tomorrow's customers, how error splits into three parts, and why plain linear and logistic regression are still the baseline every fancier model has to beat.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is only worth what it does on cases it has not seen. Error comes from being too rigid (bias), too jumpy (variance) or from noise nobody can remove, and linear and logistic regression sit at the low-variance end where most bank data belongs.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two analysts get 20 loan files, each marked repaid or defaulted, and must build a rule for the next 1,000 applicants. The first writes: "salary between AED 14,200 and 14,900, employer starts with 'A', applied on a Tuesday: default." Perfect on the 20, close to random on the 1,000. The second writes: "the more of the limit used and the more payments missed, the riskier." It misses a few of the 20 but travels well.

    The first overfitted: it learned the sample's quirks as if they were the pattern. Generalisation is how well a model does on data it was not built on, the only performance anyone pays for.

    • Training error always improves with flexibility. Like a student memorising last year's exam answers: a perfect score on the old paper says nothing about the new one.
    • Error on new data is U-shaped. Too rigid misses structure; too flexible chases noise. The best point sits in the middle and moves with data size and noise.
    • There is a floor. A default model never nears zero error, because whether a customer loses their job next month is not in the data. Trying to beat the floor is overfitting.
    Hold onto thisNever judge a model on the data it was fitted to. Ask what it does on data it never touched.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Generalisation: what you want, and what you can measure

    Training error is optimistic because the model was chosen to make it small. Test error, on a held-back sample, is the honest estimate. The difference is the generalisation gap: a big gap means overfitting.

    So you need three data sets, like practice papers, mock exam and real exam:

    • Training set: the model learns from it.
    • Validation set: you compare models and settings. Each choice leaks a little of it into the model.
    • Test set: touched once, at the end. Go back to it repeatedly and the number is optimistic.

    At a bank the test set must be later in time than training, because the model is used on the future. A random split of a drifting process lets the model peek (F4.5).

    Bias, variance and noise

    Picture many darts players, each trained on a different sample, throwing at the same target:

    • Bias: how far the centre of the cloud is from the truth. A straight line fitted to a curve is wrong the same way every time. More data will not fix it; more flexibility will.
    • Variance: how widely the cloud spreads. A flexible model reshapes itself around each sample's noise. More data, a simpler model or a penalty narrows it.
    • Noise: the part of the outcome nobody can explain. It is the same for every model.

    Expected error on new data is bias² plus variance plus noise. Whatever cuts bias tends to raise variance, and the reverse, so the best model minimises the sum. The simulation measures all three.

    Linear regression: the model that fits in one line

    Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of inputs (income, balance, tenure), like a recipe with a quantity per ingredient. The polynomial fits in Simulations 1 and 2 are exactly this.

    Why it earns its place:

    • Plain coefficients. "Holding the other inputs fixed, AED 1,000 more monthly income goes with AED 380 more card spend."
    • Uncertainty built in. Each coefficient has a standard error: "0.5, give or take 0.2".
    • Low variance by design when inputs are few and rows many.

    The price is bias: it assumes each input acts as a straight line, independently. Real patterns bend (risk rises faster once utilisation passes 80%) and interact. Transformed inputs (log income, age bands) and a few interactions fix much of this.

    It breaks in three places. Correlated inputs make coefficients unstable though predictions stay fine (Simulation 3). Outliers pull the line. And a bounded target such as a probability gets answers below 0 or above 1, which is why the next model exists.

    Logistic regression: the same idea for yes or no

    For repaid/defaulted or fraud/not-fraud we want a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression pushes the weighted sum through an S-shaped curve, so answers stay between 0 and 1. Coefficients act on the log-odds, so they multiply the odds (20% is odds of 1 to 4).

    Reading a coefficient. A coefficient of 0.9 on "missed payments, per standard deviation" multiplies default odds by about 2.5. A 5% customer goes to about 11%; a 40% customer goes to about 62%. Same odds ratio, different probability change, because the S-curve is steepest in the middle. Never say "adds X points of probability" without saying where on the curve.

    Fitting minimises log loss, which punishes confident wrong answers hard: predicting 1% for something that happens costs far more than predicting 40%.

    Why banks like it:

    • Calibrated by default. Its probabilities usually mean what they say, which matters for provisioning and pricing (F7.4). Boosted trees and nets often need recalibrating.
    • The scorecard. Credit scorecards are logistic regression on binned inputs, with points from each bin: an audit trail a regulator can follow line by line.

    Two failure modes to know. Separation: if an input perfectly splits defaulters from non-defaulters in training (common with few events and a sparse category), the coefficient grows without limit. Penalising fixes it. Too many inputs for the events: only the minority outcome informs the boundary, so 60 defaults cannot support 40 inputs. The old rule is 10 events per candidate variable.

    Taming variance: the ridge penalty in action

    Regularisation trades a little bias for a lot less variance, like a leash on the coefficients. Ridge adds a penalty that keeps coefficients small unless the data insists. F4.4 covers the other forms.

    The loan model has five inputs: utilisation, debt-to-income (90% correlated with utilisation), missed payments and two pure-noise inputs. The true effects are known. We refit 30 times on fresh samples and plot where each coefficient lands.

    Correlated inputs make individual coefficients fragile but not predictions. Never tell a business owner "debt-to-income matters more than utilisation" from raw coefficients. Explanation tools (F8.1) inherit the same ambiguity.

    Why the simple model is the baseline you must beat

    On tabular data with few events, a regularised linear or logistic model is often within a hair of the fanciest alternative. Few events make variance the binding constraint, and credit inputs have been engineered over decades to be roughly linear in log-odds.

    The evidence is mixed: tree ensembles win by a modest margin in credit scoring, and not consistently in clinical risk models. The rule is the same: always build the regularised regression first. It sets the bar and gives a validator a comparison. If a boosted model beats it by one point of AUC but is a hundred times harder to explain, that is a business question (C1.2). When the gap is large, take it seriously: interactions in transaction data are why fraud models are mostly tree ensembles.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging on data the model has seen

      "AUC 0.97." On what data? A deep tree scores near perfect on its own training set. Trying twenty settings and reporting the best test score has the same effect: the test set has become training.

      Spot itAsk for the number on a held-out sample the model never influenced, and how many models were compared.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat results, poor first live quarter: the "future" was in training.

      Spot itAsk for an out-of-time test. A sharp drop means the random split was lenient.
    • Too many inputs for the events

      200 candidate features, 90 defaults. The model fits noise that happens to correlate. Choosing features on the full data before cross-validating makes it look excellent for the same reason.

      Spot itCompare inputs to events, not rows. Check selection happened inside each training fold.
    • Reading correlated coefficients as importance

      "Debt-to-income is twice as important as utilisation" when the two are 90% correlated.

      Spot itRefit on a resample. If the coefficient flips or halves, it is not telling you what you think.
    • Quoting a logistic coefficient as a change in probability

      "Each missed payment adds 9 points of default risk." It multiplies odds; the probability change depends on the start.

      Spot itAsk "from what baseline?" If no answer, ask for the odds ratio.
    • Mistaking a flexible model's win for a real one

      A boosted model beats the logistic baseline by 0.4 points of AUC on 120 defaults. Or ten million legitimate rows are added to a problem with 300 fraud cases.

      Spot itAsk for an interval on the difference (F2.2); with few events it often contains zero. Plot the learning curve by events: if flat, more of the same data will not help.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model has small bias, larger variance and a big noise term. Which part can you not fix, and what would you try on the rest?

    Noise cannot be removed, however clever the model. Variance is the larger fixable part, so add a penalty, simplify, get more events or average models. More flexibility would shrink bias a little but probably raise variance more.

    2. Training error is 2% and validation error 15%. A second model has 14% and 15%. Describe each.

    The first overfits (high variance). The second has similar, high errors: either it underfits (high bias) or it is already near the noise floor. If achievable error is about 5%, it needs more flexibility or better features. If the floor is near 14%, it is fine.

    3. Why can't you say "each missed payment adds 9 points of default risk" from a logistic coefficient?

    The coefficient multiplies the odds, not the probability. An odds multiplier of about 2.5 takes a 5% customer to roughly 11% but a 40% customer to roughly 62%. The change in points depends on where the customer starts, so always say "from what baseline?"

    4. A coefficient is 0.5 with a standard error of 0.2. What does that tell you?

    A rough 95% range is two standard errors either side: about 0.1 to 0.9. It excludes zero, so a positive effect is supported, but the size is very uncertain. This also assumes the model form is right and rows are independent.

    5. You have 90 defaults in 30,000 loans and 60 candidate inputs. Is a logistic regression with all of them sensible?

    No. Your real sample size is the 90 events, not the 30,000 rows, and 60 inputs is far too many for it. Pick a handful on domain grounds inside the training folds, or use a strong penalty, and expect wide intervals.

    6. Utilisation and debt-to-income are 90% correlated. One refit gives coefficients (1.4, −0.2), another (−0.1, 1.2). Is the model unstable?

    Predictions probably are not: both give a combined effect of about 1.2 to 1.3. The data cannot separate the two inputs, so it splits the credit differently each time. Do not interpret them separately. Combine them, drop one, or use ridge (Simulation 3).

    7. You tuned hyperparameters with five-fold cross-validation and reported the best cross-validation score. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

    The best of many scores is biased upward because you picked it for being high. Report a number from data that played no part in choosing settings: a later-in-time test set, or nested cross-validation.
