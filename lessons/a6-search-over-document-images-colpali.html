    Searching Document Images: ColPali, Late Interaction and Contextual Retrieval

    How to retrieve a scanned letter of credit without OCR, why many small vectors per page beat one big one, what that costs in storage, and how one added sentence rescues text chunks that have lost their owner.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceKeep one small vector per page patch so each query word finds its own best match and a stamp is not averaged away; it is more accurate on scans but costs far more storage, and for plain text a cheaper cousin is to prepend a line of context to each chunk before indexing.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A trade-finance team has two million scanned pages, many in Arabic and English with stamps, handwriting and skewed copies. An officer asks: "Which credit carried the amendment stamp about the shipment date?"

    The usual pipeline is OCR, layout parsing, chunking, one vector per chunk. On a scan every step can lose something, like photocopying a photocopy: the stamp may be gone before you search.

    ColPali skips the pipeline. A vision-language model sees the page as an image and outputs one small vector per patch (roughly a 32 by 32 grid). The query gets one vector per word-piece. Each query word asks "which patch on this page matches me best?", and the page score is the sum of those answers. This is late interaction: query and page are encoded separately, so page vectors are computed once, offline, and compared token against patch at the end.

    Hold onto thisA single-vector embedding is like a page's average colour: fine for the gist, hopeless for a small red stamp. Late interaction keeps the detail and pays in storage and compute.

    Contextual retrieval fixes a different loss. "Revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter" fits a thousand companies. Have an LLM write one situating sentence per chunk and index the two together.

    Part 2

    How it really works

    Page image to patch vectors, and MaxSim scoring (A6.1)

    ColPali (Faysse and colleagues, ICLR 2025) starts from a vision-language model, PaliGemma-3B, which cuts the page into patches and lets them see each other (attention, F5.3), so a patch holding "5,000" knows it sits under "Amount". Each patch becomes a 128-number vector, so a page is a bag of about 1,000 vectors. The query becomes one vector per token.

    MaxSim scores a page: for each query token, find the most similar patch, then add up those best matches. Similarity is cosine, which compares direction only: 1 means pointing the same way, 0 unrelated. Nothing is averaged across the page, so a small region can score fully.

    Reading a score. Each best match is at most 1, so a page can score at most the number of query tokens. A 12-token query scoring 9.5 means the average word found a patch at about 0.8. Compare scores only for the same query across pages; a high score does not mean the answer is right.

    ColPali beat OCR + text-embedding pipelines on its ViDoRe benchmark and indexed faster.

    Try the rule yourself. The toy page has 48 patches with hidden topics; cell brightness is its cosine to the chosen token, and the ringed cell is the maximum.

    MaxSim is cheap per page but not across millions, so systems fetch candidates first and run MaxSim as a second stage. Vector stores such as Vespa, Qdrant and Elastic support multi-vector fields. Check the current model card for language and licence.

    Why late interaction beats one vector per page (A6.2)

    A normal dense retriever (bi-encoder, F1.1) squeezes a page into one vector, its average meaning. Averages capture gist and lose needles: a stamp covering 3% of the page gets 3% of the average, and a distractor page that talks about amounts and goods at length outvotes it.

    A cross-encoder reads query and page together: accurate, but too slow beyond reranking a short list. Late interaction sits in the middle: precomputed and indexable, with a fine-grained comparison.

    In the simulation, 23 distractor pages mention amounts and goods heavily but have no stamp; the correct page is ordinary on those and has the stamp. Slide the stamp size and watch the correct page's rank.

    The mean of many patches is dominated by whatever fills most of the page. Late interaction is the dense version of BM25 (M3.1): every query term finds its own match.

    The price: storage, and how people shrink it

    One vector per patch is the cost. A ColPali page is about 264 kB against 2 kB for one 1,024-number vector: roughly 130 times more. Two million pages is about 0.5 TB against 4 GB. The usual ways to shrink it each trade a little accuracy for a lot of space:

    • Pool patches. Average neighbours (merge 2 to 3) before storing. A big saving for a modest quality drop on typical documents, but a tiny stamp may suffer, so test on your own needles.
    • Quantise the numbers. 8-bit halves the size; binary cuts it by 16, with a full-precision rescore of a short list (P3.1).
    • Two-stage retrieval. A cheap first stage (single vector or BM25) finds a few hundred candidates; exact MaxSim rescores only those.

    Use the calculator to feel the shape of the problem.

    Contextual retrieval: giving a chunk its owner back (A6.2)

    Chunking (M3.3) cuts a report into passages, and each loses its owner. "The provision charge rose to AED 95 million." Which bank, which period?

    Anthropic's contextual retrieval (September 2024) fixes this at indexing time: an LLM reads the whole document plus the chunk and writes a sentence that situates it ("From Gulf Mercantile Bank's Q2 2025 results; group revenue growth"). Prepend it, then embed and BM25-index the enriched text. Anthropic reported about 49% fewer failed top-20 retrievals, about 67% with a reranker (internal benchmarks, from memory; verify before quoting). Read these as relative gains.

    Late chunking (Jina, 2024) embeds the whole document first, then pools into chunk vectors, so each chunk has seen the rest.

    Costs: one LLM call per chunk at indexing (cheap with prompt caching), and a new failure mode: a wrong "this is from Q2 2025" becomes an index-level fact. Validate a sample and log the model and prompt version (P2.3).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Benchmark scores taken as bank scores

      ViDoRe-style benchmarks use public, mostly English and French documents. An Arabic bill of lading with handwriting is out of distribution.

      Spot itBuild 100 to 200 real queries with known right pages. Measure recall at 5 and 20, per language and document type.
    • Retrieval without reading

      Visual retrieval finds the page; it does not extract the amount. A misread digit moves the error downstream.

      Spot itFor any number that drives a decision, require a second path: checked field extraction, or a human view of the highlighted region.
    • Pooling and storage surprises

      A 5,000-page pilot works but the full archive does not fit in memory; and pooling blurs small stamps, digits and signatures, the things KYC cares about.

      Spot itDo the Simulation 3 arithmetic before the pilot, and compare recall on small-feature queries before and after pooling.
    • Images carry instructions

      A page can hide tiny text such as "ignore previous instructions". Once a VLM reads it, that is prompt injection (A3.1).

      Spot itTreat retrieved pages as untrusted input; keep tool permissions narrow.
    • Wrong context baked into the index

      The LLM guesses the period or entity wrongly, so every retrieval of that chunk is misdirected, consistently.

      Spot itHave a person check 100 sampled context lines. Store the prompt and model version with the index.
    • No audit trail from an image embedding

      A vector cannot say why a page was returned.

      Spot itShow the best-matching patches per query token (as in Simulation 1).
    • Near-duplicate pages

      Template letters differ in one number and score almost the same.

      Spot itAdd metadata filters (customer, date, reference) before the vector search.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. In MaxSim, what does the max do and what does the sum do? Why do we still need a candidate stage?

    The max lets each query word find its single best patch wherever it sits; the sum rewards pages that answer all the words. Every query token is compared with every patch, which is fine for a few hundred candidates but not a naive scan of 100 million pages.

    2. Pooling by 4 and binary vectors together shrink a page index about 64 times. What can go wrong, and how would you check?

    Small features such as stamps, digits and signatures can blur, so recall may fall. Build queries about small features and compare recall before and after on your own documents. The size arithmetic tells you nothing about quality.

    3. Why does a single mean vector lose a stamp that covers 3 patches of 48?

    The stamp gets about 6% of the page vector, while amounts and goods covering many patches dominate its direction, so a distractor strong on those scores higher. MaxSim lets the stamp token match its patch at full strength.

    4. What is the difference between a bi-encoder, a cross-encoder and a late-interaction model?

    Bi-encoder: one precomputed vector per item; fast, coarse. Cross-encoder: reads query and item together; accurate but per pair, so for reranking. Late interaction: many precomputed vectors per item, compared token against token; in between on accuracy and cost.

    5. Name two ways contextual retrieval can make results worse.

    Wrong generated context (entity or period) misdirects every query reaching that chunk. Generic added words can make many chunks look alike. It also raises indexing cost, and regenerating changes the index, so version it.

    6. A vendor says their visual retriever "scores 90% on ViDoRe". What do you ask?

    Which subsets and languages; the metric (nDCG@5 or recall@k); corpus size versus ours; storage per page; and the result on a sample of our own Arabic and English documents.

    7. When would you still prefer the OCR text pipeline?

    For born-digital PDFs with a clean text layer, when you need exact quotes, filters, field extraction or audit trails, or when storage and latency are tight. Often both: text as the backbone, visual retrieval as a second index for hard pages.
