    Time in pandas and Pipelines in scikit-learn: Never Let the Future Leak

    Dates, resampling, rolling windows and time zones without peeking ahead, and scikit-learn pipelines that fit every preprocessing step inside the fold, so a validation score means what it says.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBoth halves are one rule applied twice: a number computed for a moment or a fold may only use information that was available then, and pandas windows and scikit-learn pipelines are the tools that make that rule the default instead of something you remember.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist at a Gulf bank builds an early-warning signal: when the 7-day average of daily card spend in a segment jumps, something has changed and the fraud team should look. She computes it with pandas in one line and checks it against history. The signal "fires" three days before the shift began. The team is thrilled. It is also impossible.

    Her window was centred: the value for Tuesday averaged Saturday to Friday, including days that had not happened on Tuesday. Nothing in the code looks wrong. The future simply got in.

    Now the second story. Another analyst has 60 customers' records and 500 candidate features. She keeps the 10 features most related to the outcome, then runs 5-fold cross-validation and reports 75% accuracy. The outcome was random. Her features were noise. The score is a lie, because the choice of features looked at the answers of the very customers she then "tested" on.

    These are the same mistake. In the first, the future leaked into a time window. In the second, the validation fold leaked into the preprocessing. Part 1 of this lesson is about telling time what it may know. Part 2 is about telling each step of a model what it may know. Both are habits of tooling: a good library makes the safe thing easy and the unsafe thing loud.

    Hold onto thisEvery feature and every fitted step has an "as of" moment: a timestamp for the time-based feature, a fold for the fitted step. Before trusting any number, ask what that moment is and whether anything computed it from beyond. This is the point-in-time discipline of F3.1, implemented in code.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What a timestamp is, and parsing it properly

    A pandas timestamp is a count of time units since 1 January 1970 UTC, wrapped in a friendly type. Three consequences are worth knowing before any analysis.

    • Parse into a real datetime type first. Text such as "03/04/2026" is 3 April in London and 4 March in New York. State the format: pd.to_datetime(s, format="%d/%m/%Y"). Without it pandas guesses, and a guess that is right for 90% of rows is the worst kind of bug. Add errors="coerce" only when you then count and inspect the resulting NaT (not-a-time) values.
    • Epoch numbers need a unit. A core banking export may store 1767225600000. That is milliseconds; read as seconds it is a date in the year 57000. Say unit="ms".
    • The type decides what you can do. Once a column is datetime64, .dt.hour, .dt.dayofweek, subtraction (giving durations), .resample() and .rolling("7D") all work. As strings, none do, and sorting is alphabetical.

    A version note, because it affects old tutorials. pandas 3.0 (January 2026) changed defaults: datetimes are no longer always stored at nanosecond resolution but usually microseconds or the resolution of the input, which avoids out-of-bounds errors for dates before 1678 or after 2262 (see Part 4). Code that assumed nanoseconds, for example converting to integers and treating them as nanoseconds, now needs an explicit unit.

    tx["ts"] = pd.to_datetime(tx["ts"], format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S", utc=True)
tx = tx.sort_values("ts").set_index("ts")        # a sorted DatetimeIndex unlocks time-based tools
tx["hour"] = tx.index.tz_convert("Asia/Dubai").hour

    Resampling and rolling windows: two different questions

    People mix these up. They answer different questions.

    • Resample changes the grain. It groups timestamps into fixed buckets (days, months) and aggregates each: tx["amount"].resample("D").sum() turns transactions into daily totals. The output has one row per bucket. In pandas 2.2 and later month-end is "ME", not the old "M".
    • Rolling keeps the grain and looks back. For each row it aggregates a window of nearby rows: daily.rolling("7D").mean() is the average of each day and the six before it. The output has the same rows as the input.
    • Expanding is rolling with a window that starts at the beginning: a running total or running mean. Useful for "customer's average ticket to date".

    Which rows does a rolling window use? By default pandas windows are trailing: the value at day t uses days up to and including t. That is point-in-time safe, with one caveat: it includes t itself, which matters when the thing you are predicting happens on day t (the label's own transaction would feed its own feature). Two switches change the picture:

    • center=True puts the window around t, so half of it lies in the future. Fine for drawing a smooth chart afterwards. Never for a feature, alert or back-test.
    • closed="left" on a time-based window (or a .shift(1) after the rolling mean) excludes the current row. Use it when the current event is the one being scored: "this customer's average spend over the previous 30 days".

    Windows also come in two units. rolling(7) means 7 rows; rolling("7D") means 7 days of time. If a customer has gaps, the first spans a long period and the second does not. For irregular event data, such as card swipes, the time-based form is almost always the one you want.

    The delay and the leak are two ends of one trade. A longer window gives a calmer line and a later alarm. The only honest way to get both calm and prompt is a better detector (a change-point method, or a model with a proper forecast), not a window that borrows from tomorrow. The wider treatment of forecasting is in F9.

    A related trap is the full-period statistic. df["amount"] - df["amount"].mean() subtracts a mean that includes later months. In time-ordered data use an expanding or trailing mean instead. The same applies to "z-score of today's amount against the customer's history": the history must stop before today.

    Time zones, calendars and the boundary of a day

    Most time bugs in banking are not clever. They are a day boundary in the wrong place. Rules of thumb:

    • Store UTC, convert to local for display and for day-based features. Naive timestamps (no zone) are a hazard: two systems can disagree by hours and nothing says so. Make them aware with tz_localize("UTC") (when the data really is UTC) and convert with tz_convert("Asia/Dubai"). tz_localize labels; tz_convert shifts. Mixing them up is the classic error.
    • "Today" depends on whose today. Dubai is UTC+4 and Riyadh UTC+3, and neither observes daylight saving. A UTC server therefore files a Dubai customer's 01:30 purchase under the previous day. A daily "transactions so far today" velocity feature computed on UTC dates is wrong for those hours, which are exactly when some fraud happens.
    • Daylight saving is real elsewhere. A bank with London, Cairo or New York activity has days of 23 and 25 hours twice a year, and local times that occur twice or never. Keep UTC internally.
    • Calendars differ. The Saudi weekend is Friday and Saturday; the UAE moved its federal working week in 2022 to Monday to Friday with Friday a half day, so weekends fall on Saturday and Sunday. Check what your institution actually uses and do not hard-code dayofweek >= 5. Islamic holidays follow the lunar calendar, with Eid dates depending on moon sighting, so they cannot be computed by a rule. Keep a maintained holiday table and use it as a feature or to build a business-day calendar. Salary-day effects (payments around the 25th to 28th, or the last working day) matter more for spending than the weekday does.

    Joining by time: the as-of join

    Features often live in a table that changes over time: a customer's credit limit, an FX rate, a risk rating. To attach "the value as it stood at the time of the event" you do not want an equality join on date (the rows rarely line up) and you certainly do not want the latest value (that is today's, not then). You want the most recent value at or before the event. pandas calls this merge_asof:

    events = events.sort_values("ts")
limits = limits.sort_values("valid_from")
out = pd.merge_asof(events, limits, left_on="ts", right_on="valid_from",
                    by="customer_id", direction="backward",
                    tolerance=pd.Timedelta("90D"))

    Three details earn the line its keep. Both sides must be sorted on the time key. direction="backward" is the safe one; "forward" or "nearest" pull in the future. tolerance turns a stale match (a limit last updated two years ago) into a missing value you can see, rather than a quietly old number. SQL expresses the same idea with window functions or a lateral join, as in F3.3. And if a value is recorded after it took effect (a rating assigned on the 10th, entered in the system on the 14th), join on the time it became known, not the time it became true.

    scikit-learn: the estimator, the transformer and the pipeline

    scikit-learn rests on a small idea. Everything that learns from data has a fit method and everything that applies what it learned has transform (for preprocessing) or predict (for models). fit is where the data is looked at. A scaler's fit stores the mean and spread of the training data; a one-hot encoder's fit stores the list of categories seen; a model's fit stores weights. transform or predict then only applies what was stored, to any data, without learning anything more.

    That split is the entire safety mechanism. The rule is: call fit on training data only, then transform on both. Breaking it, for example scaler.fit_transform(X) on everything before splitting, makes the validation rows influence the mean that scales them. For a scaler the damage is small. For feature selection, target encoding, imputation based on the outcome, PCA, or resampling, it can be enormous, as Simulation 3 shows.

    A Pipeline chains steps into one estimator. Calling pipe.fit(X_train, y_train) runs each step's fit_transform in order on the training data, then fits the final model. Calling pipe.predict(X_new) applies each step's transform and then predicts. Because the whole chain is one object, anything that re-fits it, such as cross-validation or a grid search, re-fits every step inside each fold automatically. You cannot forget.

    Mixed columns: ColumnTransformer

    Bank tables mix numbers, categories and flags. ColumnTransformer sends each group of columns through its own preprocessing and joins the results, and the whole thing becomes the first step of a pipeline.

    from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

num = ["amount_aed", "age", "tenure_months"]
cat = ["channel", "merchant_category", "country"]

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                      ("sc",  StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
])
model = Pipeline([("prep", prep),
                  ("clf", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0))])

    Points that matter in practice:

    • handle_unknown="ignore" stops the live system crashing the first time a new merchant category or country appears. Without it, training data with 40 categories and live data with 41 is an outage.
    • Imputers and scalers are fitted objects. The median stored is the training median, and that is the number applied in production. Version it with the model.
    • Trees do not need scaling. Scaling is for linear models, neural nets and distance-based methods. HistGradientBoostingClassifier also accepts missing values natively, so imputation can be dropped for it and "missing" left as information.
    • Column names matter. Selecting by name (num, cat) instead of position keeps the pipeline correct when someone reorders the extract. .set_output(transform="pandas") keeps column names flowing through, which helps debugging and explanations.
    • High-cardinality categories (merchant IDs, thousands of values) overwhelm one-hot encoding. Target encoding is the usual answer, and it is a classic leakage source, because it uses the outcome. Scikit-learn's TargetEncoder cross-fits internally during fit_transform to blunt this; used inside a pipeline it is fitted per fold too.

    Cross-validation, with and without the leak

    Cross-validation splits the data into k folds, trains on k−1 and scores on the one left out, then rotates. The point is to estimate how the whole recipe will do on data it has not seen. "Recipe" is the key word: it includes feature selection, imputation, scaling, encoding and tuning, not just the final model. If any of those looked at the held-out fold before the fold was held out, the recipe was tested on its own homework. (F4.3 covers the theory of splits; this lesson is the mechanics of not breaking it.)

    Below is the experiment that makes this concrete. The outcome is a coin flip and the features are pure noise, so the honest score is 50%. The only thing varied is where "keep the 10 most promising features" happens.

    Two lessons beyond the headline. First, the leak is not a bug in cross-validation: it did exactly what it was told, on data that had been contaminated beforehand. Second, the size of the leak grows with how much the preprocessing could choose: more candidate features, fewer rows, a more aggressive selection. A scaler can barely cheat; feature selection, supervised encoding and hyperparameter search can cheat enormously.

    The correct form, and how to score a recipe:

    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_validate, StratifiedGroupKFold

cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
res = cross_validate(model, X, y, groups=X["customer_id"], cv=cv,
                     scoring=["roc_auc", "average_precision"])
print(res["test_average_precision"].mean(), res["test_average_precision"].std())

    Choosing the splitter is the other half:

    • Plain or stratified k-fold: only for rows that are independent and where time does not matter.
    • Group k-fold: when one customer, card or device has many rows. Without it the model memorises the customer and the score is fantasy. StratifiedGroupKFold also keeps the rare-class share steady (F3.5).
    • TimeSeriesSplit: train always precedes validation, in expanding windows. Its gap argument leaves out rows between the two, which is how you implement the label-delay embargo from F3.5. test_size keeps the validation windows equal. Rows must be sorted by time first, and the object does not look at your dates at all, only at row order.

    Tuning goes inside as well. GridSearchCV(model, grid, cv=cv) on a pipeline re-fits every step per candidate per fold, and addresses parameters by step name, such as "clf__learning_rate" or "prep__num__imp__strategy". To then get an honest estimate of the tuned recipe, wrap the search itself in an outer cross_validate: nested cross-validation, covered with tuning in F4.5. And a decision threshold can be tuned in the same safe way: TunedThresholdClassifierCV selects the cut-off by cross-validation and wraps the result as an estimator, so the threshold is part of the recipe rather than an afterthought.

    Finally, persist the pipeline, not the bare model. In production the same fitted object reads the raw columns and applies identical preprocessing. Save it with the library versions that built it: a pickle from one scikit-learn version is not guaranteed to load in another.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card fraud velocity features. "Number of transactions in the previous hour for this card" is a time-based rolling count that must exclude the current event and use the right zone for day-level features.
    • Credit and behavioural scoring. Balances, limits and ratings joined as of the application date with merge_asof; cross-validation grouped by customer, ordered by time.

    • Audit. A validator will ask to rerun your model on raw columns. A pipeline makes that a single call; a notebook of hand-run cells does not.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each of these produces a good-looking number. Each has a quick check.

    • Centred or full-period statistics used as features

      rolling(7, center=True), or a column mean that includes later months. Back-tests are excellent; live performance is not.

      Spot itAlter a future row and recompute. If a past feature changes, it leaks. Also grep for center=True and unqualified .mean() on time-ordered data.
    • The current row feeds its own feature

      A 30-day average that includes today's transaction, used to score today's transaction. The anomaly dilutes itself.

      Spot itAsk whether the window is closed on the left. Check that a single huge transaction does not raise its own baseline.
    • Wrong day because of the wrong zone

      Day-level counts on UTC dates for customers at UTC+3 or +4. Late-night activity is attributed to yesterday.

      Spot itCheck the dtype of the index (tz shown or not), and plot activity by local hour. A cliff at 20:00 or 21:00 local means a UTC cut-off.
    • Silent date misparsing

      Day and month swapped for days 1 to 12; epoch in the wrong unit; errors="coerce" turning bad dates into NaT that are later dropped.

      Spot itPrint the min and max date, count NaT, and check that the number of rows per month looks smooth, with no month holding twelve days' worth.
    • Fitting preprocessing on all the data

      fit_transform on everything, then split. Worst with feature selection, target encoding and PCA.

      Spot itIn the code, look for a fit before the split or outside the pipeline. If a model scores near-perfectly on noisy data, suspect this first.
    • Random folds on dated or repeated rows

      Tomorrow's and the same customer's rows land in training. Scores are tight and optimistic.

      Spot itAsk for a forward-in-time test and for customer IDs on both sides of the split to be counted. Compare to TimeSeriesSplit output.
    • Tuning on the same folds you report

      The best of 200 settings, scored on the folds that picked it. Optimistic by construction.

      Spot itAsk whether the reported score comes from data the search never saw (an outer loop or a final untouched test period).
    • Unsorted data in time-based tools

      TimeSeriesSplit and merge_asof trust row order or require sorting. An unsorted extract gives wrong or erroring results.

      Spot itAssert index.is_monotonic_increasing before splitting or joining.
    • Version drift between training and serving

      pandas 2 notebook, pandas 3 service; one scikit-learn version trains and another loads the pickle.

      Spot itPin versions in the environment file and log them with the model. Rerun a fixed sample through both environments and compare outputs.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our test results are only worth trusting if every number was calculated using what we knew at that moment. We build that into the code: the model and all its preparation steps are one package, tested on later months than it was trained on. That's why the performance we quote is the performance we expect to see live, not a flattering back-test.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Preprocessing, encoding and selection live in a scikit-learn pipeline with a ColumnTransformer, so cross-validation re-fits them inside each fold. Splits are time-ordered and grouped by customer, with a gap for label maturity. Rolling features are trailing, closed on the left, built on UTC timestamps and converted to local time only for day-level features. Reference data is joined with merge_asof on the time it became known, with a tolerance. The pipeline is saved as one artefact with pinned library versions.”
    “Why not just scale the data first and then split? It's faster.”

    For a scaler the effect is usually tiny, but the habit is dangerous because the same pattern with feature selection, encoding or imputation can be huge. A pipeline makes the safe version no slower to write, and it means the object we deploy is the object we validated. I'd rather have one rule than a list of exceptions.

    “Our cross-validated AUC is 0.97 but live it's 0.80. Why?”

    Most likely leakage or a mismatch between validation and live conditions. I'd check for preprocessing fitted outside the folds, random splits across time or repeated customers, features built with future data (centred windows, full-period means, a join that picks the latest value), and labels that were not yet mature. Then I'd rerun with a time-ordered, grouped, embargoed split to see whether the offline number falls towards the live one.

    “Which timezone do we store?”

    UTC, always, with the zone attached. We convert to Asia/Dubai or Asia/Riyadh only for features about the customer's day, such as hour of day, whether it is a weekend, or transactions so far today. Neither country uses daylight saving, but our counterparties elsewhere do, and a UTC store avoids mixing.

    “Is a rolling average a valid fraud feature?”

    Yes, if it is trailing and excludes the event being scored. I'd use a time-based window per card, such as the previous 24 hours, and compute it with the same code online and offline, so the production value matches the training value. I'd also test that changing a future transaction cannot change a past feature.

    “Do we need nested cross-validation?”

    If we are comparing many settings on a small dataset and want an unbiased estimate of the tuned model, yes. With plenty of data and time, a simpler alternative is to tune on earlier periods and report on a final later period that the search never touched. Either way the reported score must come from data that played no role in any choice.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. What is the difference between resample("D").sum() and rolling("7D").sum()?

    Resample changes the grain: one output row per day, summing the transactions inside it. Rolling keeps the rows and, for each, sums the window looking back seven days. Resample builds the daily series; rolling then smooths or accumulates it.

    2. A 7-day centred rolling mean flags a jump on day 66, but the jump is on day 70. What happened and what is the fix?

    A 7-day centred window on day 66 spans days 63 to 69, so it already contains days 67 to 69 that had not happened yet; if the jump is at day 70 it only sees it once the window reaches it, and noise makes the crossing earlier still. In general, about half a centred window lies in the future, so the alarm can precede the event by up to half a window. Use a trailing window, which fires a few days after the jump, or a proper change-point method.

    3. Difference between tz_localize and tz_convert?

    tz_localize attaches a zone to naive timestamps without changing the wall-clock reading ("this 09:00 was in UTC"). tz_convert converts an already aware timestamp to another zone, changing the wall clock but not the instant. Localising with the wrong zone silently shifts every instant.

    4. Why does merge_asof take a tolerance?

    The matching row is the latest one before the event, however old. Without a tolerance a customer's limit set two years ago is attached as if current. A tolerance turns a stale match into a visible missing value.

    5. In Simulation 3, why did selection inside the fold sit at 50% while selection before the split gave much more?

    Selecting before the split picks the features that, by chance, line up with the outcomes of all customers, including those later used for testing. The evaluation then rewards that coincidence. Selecting inside the fold uses only training rows, so the held-out rows are genuinely unseen, and for random features the result is chance.

    6. You put a StandardScaler and a model into a pipeline and pass it to GridSearchCV. At what point is the scaler fitted?

    Separately for every candidate setting and every fold, on that fold's training rows only. Its stored mean and spread are then applied to the fold's validation rows. A final refit on all the training data happens at the end if refit=True.

    7. Fraud labels arrive about six weeks after a transaction. How do you reflect this in TimeSeriesSplit?

    Use its gap argument to leave out rows between the training and validation windows, sized to about six weeks of rows. Otherwise the last training transactions have incomplete labels that look legitimate. Remember that gap counts rows, not days, so convert from the data volume, or build the splits by date yourself.

    8. A Dubai customer buys at 00:40 local time on Saturday. On a UTC-dated daily count, which day is it, and which kinds of feature does this damage?

    20:40 UTC on Friday, so Friday. It damages anything keyed to the customer's day: transactions so far today, weekend flags, daily limits and spend against a daily pattern. It can also split one late-night burst across two days.
