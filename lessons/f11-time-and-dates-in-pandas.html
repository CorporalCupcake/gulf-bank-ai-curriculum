    Time in pandas and Pipelines in scikit-learn: Never Let the Future Leak

    Dates, resampling, rolling windows and time zones without peeking ahead, and scikit-learn pipelines that fit every preprocessing step inside the fold, so a validation score means what it says.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA number computed for a moment or a fold may only use what was known then, and pandas windows and scikit-learn pipelines make that the default.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two analysts, one mistake. Think of a weather forecast that peeks at tomorrow's newspaper.

    • Time leak. An analyst builds an early-warning signal: the 7-day average of daily card spend. It "fires" three days before the shift began. Her window was centred, so Tuesday's value averaged Saturday to Friday, days that had not happened yet.
    • Fold leak. Another analyst has 60 customers and 500 noise features. She keeps the 10 features most related to the outcome, then runs 5-fold cross-validation and reports 75% accuracy. The outcome was random. Feature choice had already seen the answers of the customers she "tested" on.

    In the first, the future leaked into a window. In the second, the validation fold leaked into preprocessing. Part 2 covers telling time what it may know, then telling each model step what it may know.

    Hold onto thisEvery feature has an "as of" moment: a timestamp for time features, a fold for fitted steps. Before trusting a number, ask what that moment is and whether anything beyond it fed in. This is the point-in-time discipline of F3.1, in code.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Timestamps and parsing

    A pandas timestamp is a count of time units since 1 January 1970 UTC. Three consequences:

    • Parse to a real datetime first, and state the format. "03/04/2026" is 3 April in London and 4 March in New York. Use pd.to_datetime(s, format="%d/%m/%Y"). A guess that is right for 90% of rows is the worst bug. Use errors="coerce" only if you then count and inspect the NaT (not-a-time) values.
    • Epoch numbers need a unit. 1767225600000 is milliseconds; read as seconds it is the year 57000. Say unit="ms".
    • The type unlocks the tools. On datetime64, .dt.hour, subtraction, .resample() and .rolling("7D") work. On strings, none do, and sorting is alphabetical.

    Version note: pandas 3.0 (January 2026) no longer always stores nanoseconds; it uses microseconds or the input's resolution, avoiding out-of-bounds errors before 1678 or after 2262. Code that treats integer timestamps as nanoseconds now needs an explicit unit.

    tx["ts"] = pd.to_datetime(tx["ts"], format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S", utc=True)
tx = tx.sort_values("ts").set_index("ts")        # a sorted DatetimeIndex unlocks time-based tools
tx["hour"] = tx.index.tz_convert("Asia/Dubai").hour

    Resampling and rolling windows

    They answer different questions.

    • Resample changes the grain, like turning till receipts into daily totals: tx["amount"].resample("D").sum() gives one row per bucket. Month-end is "ME" in pandas 2.2+.
    • Rolling keeps the grain and looks back, like a running 7-day average: daily.rolling("7D").mean() has the same rows as its input.
    • Expanding is rolling from the very start: a running mean, such as "customer's average ticket to date".

    Windows are trailing by default: day t uses days up to and including t. That is point-in-time safe, except that it includes t itself, which matters if t is the event being scored. Two switches:

    • center=True puts half the window in the future. Fine for a smooth chart afterwards; never for a feature, alert or back-test.
    • closed="left" (or .shift(1) after the rolling mean) excludes the current row. Use it for "average spend over the previous 30 days".

    rolling(7) is 7 rows; rolling("7D") is 7 days. With gaps they differ a lot. For irregular events such as card swipes, use the time-based form.

    Delay and leak are two ends of one trade: a longer window gives a calmer line and a later alarm. For both calm and prompt, use a better detector (change-point method, proper forecast; see F9), not a window that borrows from tomorrow.

    The same trap hides in full-period statistics. df["amount"] - df["amount"].mean() subtracts a mean that includes later months. Use an expanding or trailing mean. A "z-score against the customer's history" must stop before today.

    Time zones, calendars and the day boundary

    Most banking time bugs are a day boundary in the wrong place, like a shop that closes the till at 20:00 and files a 21:00 sale under tomorrow.

    • Store UTC; convert to local for display and day-based features. Naive timestamps (no zone) let two systems disagree by hours silently. tz_localize("UTC") labels; tz_convert("Asia/Dubai") shifts. Mixing them up is the classic error.
    • "Today" depends on whose today. Dubai is UTC+4, Riyadh UTC+3, neither with daylight saving. A UTC server files a Dubai customer's 01:30 purchase under the previous day, which wrecks "transactions so far today" at exactly the hours some fraud happens.
    • Daylight saving is real elsewhere. London, Cairo or New York activity means 23- and 25-hour days and local times that occur twice or never. Keep UTC internally.
    • Calendars differ. Saudi weekend is Friday and Saturday. The UAE moved in 2022 to a Monday to Friday week (Friday a half day), weekend Saturday and Sunday. Check your institution's; do not hard-code dayofweek >= 5. Islamic holidays follow the lunar calendar and moon sighting, so keep a maintained holiday table. Salary days (around the 25th to 28th) often matter more for spending than the weekday.

    Joining by time: the as-of join

    To attach "the credit limit as it stood at the event", you want the most recent value at or before the event, not an equality join (rows rarely line up) and not the latest value (that is today's). Think of looking up the exchange rate printed on the day's rate sheet. pandas calls this merge_asof:

    events = events.sort_values("ts")
limits = limits.sort_values("valid_from")
out = pd.merge_asof(events, limits, left_on="ts", right_on="valid_from",
                    by="customer_id", direction="backward",
                    tolerance=pd.Timedelta("90D"))

    Both sides must be sorted on the time key. direction="backward" is the safe one; "forward" and "nearest" pull in the future. tolerance turns a stale match (a limit set two years ago) into a visible missing value. SQL does this with window functions or a lateral join (F3.3). If a value is entered after it took effect (rating assigned on the 10th, entered on the 14th), join on when it became known, not when it became true.

    Estimator, transformer and pipeline

    Think of a recipe card: fit is learning the recipe from the training data; transform or predict is cooking with it, learning nothing more. A scaler's fit stores the training mean and spread; a one-hot encoder stores the categories seen; a model stores weights.

    The rule: call fit on training data only, then transform on both. Calling scaler.fit_transform(X) on everything before splitting lets validation rows shift the mean that scales them. For a scaler the damage is small. For feature selection, target encoding, outcome-based imputation, PCA or resampling it can be huge (Simulation 3).

    A Pipeline chains steps into one estimator. pipe.fit(X_train, y_train) runs each step's fit_transform in order, then fits the model. pipe.predict applies each transform, then predicts. Anything that re-fits it, such as cross-validation or grid search, re-fits every step inside each fold. You cannot forget.

    Mixed columns: ColumnTransformer

    ColumnTransformer sends each column group through its own preprocessing and joins the results, as the first pipeline step.

    from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

num = ["amount_aed", "age", "tenure_months"]
cat = ["channel", "merchant_category", "country"]

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                      ("sc",  StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
])
model = Pipeline([("prep", prep),
                  ("clf", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0))])
    • handle_unknown="ignore" stops live scoring crashing when a 41st category appears after training on 40.
    • Imputers and scalers are fitted objects. The stored training median is what runs in production. Version it with the model.
    • Trees do not need scaling. That is for linear models, neural nets and distance methods. HistGradientBoostingClassifier also takes missing values natively, so "missing" can stay as information.
    • Select columns by name, not position, so a reordered extract does not break the pipeline. .set_output(transform="pandas") keeps names flowing for debugging.
    • High-cardinality categories (merchant IDs) overwhelm one-hot. Target encoding is the usual fix and a classic leak, since it uses the outcome. Scikit-learn's TargetEncoder cross-fits internally, and inside a pipeline it is fitted per fold too.

    Cross-validation, with and without the leak

    Cross-validation trains on k−1 folds, scores on the one left out, and rotates. It estimates how the whole recipe does on unseen data: selection, imputation, scaling, encoding and tuning, not just the final model. If any step saw the held-out fold, the recipe was marked on its own homework. (F4.3 has the theory.)

    In the experiment below the outcome is a coin flip and the features are noise, so the honest score is 50%. Only the place of "keep the 10 best features" varies.

    The leak is not a bug in cross-validation: it scored contaminated data faithfully. Its size grows with how much the preprocessing could choose: more candidate features, fewer rows, harsher selection. A scaler barely cheats; feature selection, supervised encoding and hyperparameter search cheat enormously.

    The correct form:

    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_validate, StratifiedGroupKFold

cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
res = cross_validate(model, X, y, groups=X["customer_id"], cv=cv,
                     scoring=["roc_auc", "average_precision"])
print(res["test_average_precision"].mean(), res["test_average_precision"].std())

    Pick the splitter to match the data:

    • Plain or stratified k-fold: independent rows, time irrelevant.
    • Group k-fold: one customer, card or device with many rows. Without it the model memorises the customer. StratifiedGroupKFold also keeps the rare-class share steady (F3.5).
    • TimeSeriesSplit: train always precedes validation. gap leaves out rows between them (the label-delay embargo from F3.5); test_size equalises windows. Sort by time first: it sees only row order, not dates.

    Tuning goes inside too. GridSearchCV(model, grid, cv=cv) on a pipeline re-fits every step per candidate per fold, with parameters named by step, e.g. "clf__learning_rate" or "prep__num__imp__strategy". For an honest score of the tuned recipe, wrap the search in an outer cross_validate (nested CV, F4.5). TunedThresholdClassifierCV tunes the decision threshold the same safe way, making it part of the recipe.

    Persist the pipeline, not the bare model, so production applies identical preprocessing to raw columns. Save it with the library versions that built it: a pickle may not load in another scikit-learn version.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure gives a good-looking number. Each has a quick check.

    • Centred or full-period statistics as features

      rolling(7, center=True), or a column mean including later months. Back-tests shine; live does not.

      Spot itAlter a future row and recompute. If a past feature moves, it leaks. Grep for center=True and bare .mean() on time-ordered data.
    • The current row feeds its own feature

      A 30-day average including today's transaction scores today's transaction. The anomaly dilutes itself.

      Spot itIs the window closed on the left? Does one huge transaction raise its own baseline?
    • Wrong day because of the wrong zone

      Day-level counts on UTC dates for UTC+3 or +4 customers; late-night activity lands on yesterday.

      Spot itCheck whether the index shows tz, and plot activity by local hour. A cliff at 20:00 or 21:00 local means a UTC cut-off.
    • Silent date misparsing

      Day and month swapped for days 1 to 12; wrong epoch unit; errors="coerce" turning bad dates into NaT that are later dropped.

      Spot itPrint min and max date, count NaT, and check rows per month look smooth.
    • Fitting preprocessing on all the data

      fit_transform on everything, then split. Worst with feature selection, target encoding and PCA.

      Spot itLook for a fit before the split or outside the pipeline. A near-perfect score on noisy data: suspect this first.
    • Random folds on dated or repeated rows

      Tomorrow's rows and the same customer's rows land in training. Scores are tight and optimistic.

      Spot itAsk for a forward-in-time test and a count of customer IDs on both sides. Compare with TimeSeriesSplit.
    • Tuning on the folds you report

      The best of 200 settings, scored on the folds that picked it. Optimistic by construction.

      Spot itDoes the score come from data the search never saw (outer loop or untouched final period)?
    • Unsorted data in time-based tools

      TimeSeriesSplit trusts row order and merge_asof requires sorting. Unsorted data gives wrong results or errors.

      Spot itAssert index.is_monotonic_increasing first.
    • Version drift between training and serving

      pandas 2 notebook, pandas 3 service; one scikit-learn trains, another loads the pickle.

      Spot itPin versions, log them with the model, and run a fixed sample through both environments.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. What is the difference between resample("D").sum() and rolling("7D").sum()?

    Resample changes the grain: one row per day, summing that day's transactions. Rolling keeps the rows and sums the previous seven days for each. Resample builds the daily series; rolling smooths it.

    2. A 7-day centred rolling mean flags a jump on day 66, but the jump is on day 70. What happened and what is the fix?

    The day-66 window spans days 63 to 69, so it already holds days not yet happened. About half a centred window lies in the future, so the alarm can precede the event by up to half a window. Use a trailing window (fires a few days late) or a change-point method.

    3. Difference between tz_localize and tz_convert?

    tz_localize attaches a zone to naive timestamps without changing the wall-clock reading ("this 09:00 was UTC"). tz_convert moves an aware timestamp to another zone, changing the wall clock but not the instant. Localising with the wrong zone silently shifts every instant.

    4. Why does merge_asof take a tolerance?

    The match is the latest row before the event, however old. Without a tolerance, a limit set two years ago is attached as current. A tolerance turns it into a visible missing value.

    5. In Simulation 3, why did selection inside the fold sit at 50% while selection before the split gave much more?

    Selecting first picks features that, by chance, line up with the outcomes of all customers, including later test ones, and the evaluation rewards that coincidence. Selecting inside the fold uses only training rows, so held-out rows are truly unseen and random features give chance.

    6. You put a StandardScaler and a model into a pipeline and pass it to GridSearchCV. At what point is the scaler fitted?

    Separately for every candidate setting and fold, on that fold's training rows only; its stored mean and spread are then applied to the fold's validation rows. A final refit on all training data happens at the end if refit=True.

    7. Fraud labels arrive about six weeks after a transaction. How do you reflect this in TimeSeriesSplit?

    Use gap to leave out rows between training and validation, sized to about six weeks. Otherwise the latest training transactions have incomplete labels that look legitimate. gap counts rows, not days, so convert from volume or build splits by date.

    8. A Dubai customer buys at 00:40 local time on Saturday. On a UTC-dated daily count, which day is it, and which features does this damage?

    20:40 UTC on Friday, so Friday. It damages anything keyed to the customer's day: transactions so far today, weekend flags, daily limits, spend against a daily pattern. It can also split one late-night burst across two days.
