    Time in pandas and Pipelines in scikit-learn: Never Let the Future Leak

    Dates, resampling, rolling windows and time zones without peeking ahead, and scikit-learn pipelines that fit every preprocessing step inside the fold, so a validation score means what it says.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA number computed for a moment or a fold may only use what was known then, and pandas windows and scikit-learn pipelines make that the default.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two analysts, one mistake: a forecast that peeks at tomorrow's newspaper.

    • Time leak. An early-warning signal, the 7-day average of daily card spend, "fires" three days before the shift began. Her window was centred, so Tuesday's value averaged Saturday to Friday, days that had not happened yet.
    • Fold leak. With 60 customers and 500 noise features, she keeps the 10 features most related to the outcome, then cross-validates and reports 75% accuracy. The outcome was random. Feature choice had already seen the answers of the customers she "tested" on.

    In the first, the future leaked into a window. In the second, the validation fold leaked into preprocessing.

    Hold onto thisEvery feature has an "as of" moment: a timestamp for time features, a fold for fitted steps. Before trusting a number, ask what that moment is and whether anything beyond it fed in (F3.1, in code).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Timestamps and parsing

    A pandas timestamp is a count of time units since 1 January 1970 UTC. Three consequences:

    • Parse to a real datetime, and state the format. "03/04/2026" is 3 April in London and 4 March in New York, so use pd.to_datetime(s, format="%d/%m/%Y"). Use errors="coerce" only if you then count the NaT (not-a-time) values.
    • Epoch numbers need a unit. 1767225600000 is milliseconds; read as seconds it is the year 57000. Say unit="ms".
    • The type unlocks the tools. .dt.hour, .resample() and .rolling("7D") work on datetimes, not strings.
    tx["ts"] = pd.to_datetime(tx["ts"], format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S", utc=True)
tx = tx.sort_values("ts").set_index("ts")        # a sorted DatetimeIndex unlocks time-based tools
tx["hour"] = tx.index.tz_convert("Asia/Dubai").hour

    Resampling and rolling windows

    They answer different questions.

    • Resample changes the grain, like turning till receipts into daily totals: tx["amount"].resample("D").sum().
    • Rolling keeps the grain and looks back, like a running 7-day average: daily.rolling("7D").mean().

    Windows are trailing by default: day t uses days up to and including t. Safe, except t itself is included. Two switches:

    • center=True puts half the window in the future. Fine for a smooth chart afterwards; never for a feature, alert or back-test.
    • closed="left" (or .shift(1)) excludes the current row. Use it for "average spend over the previous 30 days".

    rolling(7) is 7 rows; rolling("7D") is 7 days. For irregular events, use the time-based form.

    Delay and leak are two ends of one trade: a longer window gives a calmer line and a later alarm. For both calm and prompt, use a better detector (F9), not a window that borrows from tomorrow. Full-period means leak the same way: use an expanding or trailing mean.

    Time zones, calendars and the day boundary

    Most banking time bugs are a day boundary in the wrong place, like a shop that closes the till at 20:00 and files a 21:00 sale under tomorrow.

    • Store UTC; convert to local for day-based features. tz_localize("UTC") labels naive timestamps; tz_convert("Asia/Dubai") shifts them. Mixing them up is the classic error.
    • "Today" depends on whose today. Dubai is UTC+4, Riyadh UTC+3. A UTC server files a Dubai customer's 01:30 purchase under the previous day, wrecking "transactions so far today" just when some fraud happens.
    • Calendars differ. Saudi weekend is Friday and Saturday; the UAE's is Saturday and Sunday. Do not hard-code dayofweek >= 5, and keep a maintained holiday table, since Islamic holidays follow the lunar calendar.

    Joining by time: the as-of join

    To attach "the credit limit as it stood at the event", you want the most recent value at or before the event: not an equality join (rows rarely line up) and not the latest value (that is today's). pandas calls this merge_asof:

    events = events.sort_values("ts")
limits = limits.sort_values("valid_from")
out = pd.merge_asof(events, limits, left_on="ts", right_on="valid_from",
                    by="customer_id", direction="backward",
                    tolerance=pd.Timedelta("90D"))

    Sort both sides on the time key. direction="backward" is the safe one; "forward" and "nearest" pull in the future. tolerance turns a stale match into a visible missing value. If a value is entered after it took effect, join on when it became known, not when it became true.

    Estimator, transformer and pipeline

    Think of a recipe card: fit learns the recipe from training data; transform or predict cooks with it and learns nothing more.

    The rule: call fit on training data only, then transform on both. For a scaler, fitting on everything is a small cheat. For feature selection, target encoding or PCA it is a huge one (Simulation 3).

    A Pipeline chains steps into one estimator. Anything that re-fits it, such as cross-validation or grid search, re-fits every step inside each fold. You cannot forget.

    Mixed columns: ColumnTransformer

    ColumnTransformer sends each column group through its own preprocessing, as the first pipeline step.

    from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

num = ["amount_aed", "age", "tenure_months"]
cat = ["channel", "merchant_category", "country"]

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                      ("sc",  StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
])
model = Pipeline([("prep", prep),
                  ("clf", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0))])
    • handle_unknown="ignore" stops live scoring crashing when a 41st category appears after training on 40.
    • Imputers and scalers are fitted objects. The stored training median is what runs in production, so version it with the model.

    High-cardinality categories (merchant IDs) overwhelm one-hot. Target encoding is the usual fix and a classic leak, since it uses the outcome; inside a pipeline it is fitted per fold, which keeps it honest.

    Cross-validation, with and without the leak

    Cross-validation trains on all folds but one, scores on the one left out, and rotates. It scores the whole recipe: selection, imputation, scaling, encoding and tuning, not just the final model. If any step saw the held-out fold, the recipe marked its own homework (F4.3).

    Below, the outcome is a coin flip and the features are noise, so the honest score is 50%. Only the place of "keep the 10 best features" varies.

    Cross-validation is not at fault: it scored contaminated data faithfully. The leak grows with how much the preprocessing could choose: more candidate features, fewer rows. A scaler barely cheats; feature selection, supervised encoding and hyperparameter search cheat enormously. The correct form:

    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_validate, StratifiedGroupKFold

cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
res = cross_validate(model, X, y, groups=X["customer_id"], cv=cv,
                     scoring=["roc_auc", "average_precision"])
print(res["test_average_precision"].mean(), res["test_average_precision"].std())

    Pick the splitter to match the data:

    • Stratified k-fold: independent rows, time irrelevant.
    • Group k-fold: one customer, card or device with many rows. Without it the model memorises the customer (F3.5).
    • TimeSeriesSplit: train always precedes validation; gap leaves a label-delay embargo. Sort by time first: it sees only row order.

    Tuning goes inside too: GridSearchCV on a pipeline re-fits every step per candidate per fold. For an honest score of the tuned recipe, wrap it in an outer cross_validate (F4.5).

    Save the pipeline, not the bare model, with the library versions that built it, so production applies identical preprocessing.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure gives a good-looking number. Each has a quick check.

    • The future or the current row feeds a feature

      rolling(7, center=True), a column mean including later months, or a 30-day average that includes today's transaction and so dilutes its own anomaly.

      Spot itAlter a future row and recompute. If a past feature moves, it leaks. Grep for center=True and bare .mean() on time-ordered data.
    • Wrong day because of the wrong zone

      Day-level counts on UTC dates for UTC+3 or +4 customers; late-night activity lands on yesterday.

      Spot itPlot activity by local hour. A cliff at 20:00 or 21:00 local means a UTC cut-off.
    • Silent date misparsing

      Day and month swapped for days 1 to 12; wrong epoch unit; errors="coerce" turning bad dates into NaT that are later dropped.

      Spot itPrint min and max date, count NaT, and check rows per month look smooth.
    • Fitting preprocessing on all the data

      fit_transform on everything, then split. Worst with feature selection, target encoding and PCA.

      Spot itLook for a fit before the split or outside the pipeline. A near-perfect score on noisy data: suspect this first.
    • Random folds on dated or repeated rows

      Tomorrow's rows and the same customer's rows land in training. Unsorted data in TimeSeriesSplit or merge_asof breaks the same way.

      Spot itCompare with TimeSeriesSplit, count customer IDs on both sides, and assert index.is_monotonic_increasing.
    • Tuning on the folds you report

      The best of 200 settings, scored on the folds that picked it.

      Spot itDoes the score come from data the search never saw (outer loop or untouched final period)?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. What is the difference between resample("D").sum() and rolling("7D").sum()?

    Resample changes the grain: one row per day. Rolling keeps the rows and sums the previous seven days for each. Resample builds the daily series; rolling smooths it.

    2. A 7-day centred rolling mean flags a jump on day 66, but the jump is on day 70. What happened and what is the fix?

    The day-66 window spans days 63 to 69, so it already holds days that had not happened. Use a trailing window, accepting a few days of delay.

    3. Difference between tz_localize and tz_convert?

    tz_localize attaches a zone to naive timestamps without changing the wall-clock reading. tz_convert moves an aware timestamp to another zone, changing the wall clock but not the instant.

    4. Why does merge_asof take a tolerance?

    The match is the latest row before the event, however old. A tolerance turns a limit set two years ago into a visible missing value instead of a silent "current" one.

    5. In Simulation 3, why did selection inside the fold sit at 50% while selection before the split gave much more?

    Selecting first picks features that, by chance, line up with the outcomes of all customers, including later test ones. Selecting inside the fold uses only training rows, so held-out rows are truly unseen and random features give chance.

    6. You put a StandardScaler and a model into a pipeline and pass it to GridSearchCV. At what point is the scaler fitted?

    Separately for every candidate setting and fold, on that fold's training rows only. A final refit on all training data happens at the end if refit=True.

    7. Fraud labels arrive about six weeks after a transaction. How do you reflect this in TimeSeriesSplit?

    Use gap to leave out about six weeks between training and validation. Otherwise the latest training transactions have incomplete labels that look legitimate. gap counts rows, not days.

    8. A Dubai customer buys at 00:40 local time on Saturday. On a UTC-dated daily count, which day is it, and which features does this damage?

    20:40 UTC on Friday, so Friday. It damages anything keyed to the customer's day: transactions so far today, weekend flags, daily limits.
