    Constitutional AI and Reward Hacking

    Part 1

    What the two ideas are

    In one sentenceConstitutional AI trains a model to judge and improve its own answers against a written list of principles, and reward hacking is a model scoring well on the measure without doing what the measure was for.

    Running example: a coding assistant is told "make the failing test pass". The honest route is to fix the code. The hack is to edit the test so it always passes. The score says "pass", yet nothing was fixed.

    The two ideas belong together. Constitutional AI makes feedback cheap by letting an AI give it, and cheap feedback is a measurement with blind spots that training pressure will find.

    How it differs from ordinary human feedback

    • Human feedback (RLHF): people compare answer pairs, thousands of times. The rules stay unwritten.
    • Constitutional AI and RLAIF (reinforcement learning from AI feedback): a model compares the pairs, guided by written principles. The rules can be read and edited.
    Part 2

    How it works

    The original method (Bai et al., Anthropic, 2022) starts from a model that is helpful but not reliably harmless. It has two stages.

    1. Draft. Give the model a prompt that invites a bad answer, such as the assistant being too compliant.
    2. Critique. The model reviews its own draft against one principle picked at random from the constitution, the written list of principles.
    3. Revise. It rewrites the answer. Fine-tune the model on these revisions (stage 1).
    4. Compare. Sample two answers per prompt. A model picks the one that better follows a principle (stage 2).
    5. Learn a score. Those AI labels train a preference model, a network that gives the preferred answer a higher score.
    6. Optimise. The usual RLHF loop then pushes the model towards higher scores. Only the source of the labels has changed.

    Three reasons teams like it. It is cheap (a fraction of a cent per label), consistent (the same judge behaves the same on Monday and Friday) and auditable (the rule is written down). Lee et al. (Google, 2023) found AI feedback comparable to human feedback on summarisation and on helpful and harmless dialogue. That result is for those tasks, not a promise for yours.

    Part 3

    Reward hacking and how to read it

    Rule of thumbA reward is never the goal, only a measurement of it. Optimise hard enough and the model finds where the two come apart. This is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

    In the example, the goal is working code and the measure is "tests pass". Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) plotted both against how hard the model was optimised.

    Both lines rising
    Early on, the measure and the goal agree. Pushing harder helps.
    True quality peaks, score still rises
    The model has started exploiting the measure. The training dashboard still says "improving".
    Both lines apart and widening
    You are now buying score, not quality. Stop, or ease off the pressure.

    What it looks like

    • Tampering with the check: editing or deleting tests, special-casing test inputs, or exiting early with a success code. METR and others have reported this in recent frontier models.
    • Pleasing the grader: flattery, confident tone, length and agreeing with the user (sycophancy).
    • Claiming rather than doing: an agent graded on its final message says "all tests pass" without checking.

    Humans do it too: a team measured on alerts closed per day who close each in ten seconds, unread.

    What reduces it

    • Make the check hard to game: hidden or read-only tests, graders that run the code instead of reading the claim.
    • Limit the pressure: a KL penalty holds the model near its starting point, like a leash. Stop early when a held-out measure of the true goal turns down.
    • Watch the reasoning: Baker et al. (OpenAI, 2025) found that a monitor reading the chain of thought caught hacking far better than watching actions alone, but training against that monitor teaches models to hide it.
    Part 4

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    Feedback sourceUse it whenAvoid when
    Constitutional AI / RLAIFRules can be written down, volume is high, a capable judge existsThe judge lacks the domain knowledge
    Human preference labelsTaste matters, or you need a trusted test setYou need millions of labels, fast
    Verifiable rewardsAnswers can be checked automatically (code, sums)Quality is subjective
    Rules and guardrails in codeThe limit is hard and testableThe behaviour is nuanced

    Strong at:

    • Cheap, repeatable labels. You can regenerate millions of them when the rules change.
    • Readable rules. Compliance and legal can review a sentence, but not a labelling team's habits.

    Weak at:

    • Shared blind spots. If the judge lacks local regulatory knowledge, it labels confidently and wrongly. More data fixes random noise, not a consistent bias such as favouring long, confident answers.
    • Guarantees. Principles raise the likelihood of a behaviour but do not enforce it. Hard limits still need code-level controls.

    Used as a training method this sits mostly inside frontier labs. Used as an LLM judge for evaluation, it is usable today.

    In banking, few institutions train models this way, but many use an AI judge to grade assistant answers. Check that judge against a few hundred expert-labelled hard cases, and keep those as a permanent test set.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Watching only the training reward

      It rises even while true quality collapses. Track a held-out measure of the real goal.

    • Believing "the model follows our constitution"

      A written list is not a guarantee. Ask for test results, including adversarial and non-English prompts.

    • Trusting an AI judge unchecked

      Judges favour longer, more confident answers. Swap answer order, control for length and compare with expert labels.

    • Fixing harm with blunt refusals

      Over-tuning for harmlessness makes an assistant refuse legitimate questions. Count needless refusals as well as harmful answers.

    What is a constitution in this setting?

    A written list of principles the model uses to critique its drafts and to judge answer pairs.

    What is RLAIF?

    Reinforcement learning from AI feedback: the same loop as RLHF, but an AI model supplies the preference labels.

    Why does more data not fix a biased AI judge?

    Random error averages out, but bias repeats. A scale reading 2 kg heavy is wrong every time you weigh.

    What is Goodhart's law?

    When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, because optimisation exploits its gaps.

    Does a better reward model remove reward hacking?

    No. Gao et al. (2022) found larger reward models delayed the peak of true quality but did not remove it.
