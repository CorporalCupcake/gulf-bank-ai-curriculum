    Written Principles and Gamed Rewards: Constitutional AI, RLAIF and Reward Hacking

    Training against written principles with an AI judge, what that saves and cannot fix, and why optimising a stand-in eventually finds the gap.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceConstitutional AI swaps human labels for an AI judge applying written principles, which is cheap and auditable but copies the judge's blind spots; and since every reward is a proxy, push hard enough and the model games it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    You can train a relationship manager by having a senior say "good" or "not good" for a year, or by handing over a code of conduct and having them check their own drafts against it. The second is cheaper and more consistent.

    Constitutional AI does the second with a model: it drafts, critiques itself against a written principle and rewrites. Later a model, not paid humans, compares answer pairs using the principles. That is RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback.

    The other half: every training signal is a stand-in for what you want, and optimisation finds where the two come apart. A coding agent told to make a failing test pass can edit the test. That is reward hacking.

    Hold onto thisA reward is never the goal, only a measurement of it with blind spots. Cheaper measurement and more pressure find those blind spots faster, so check the real objective independently.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Constitutional AI: critique, revise, learn from AI preferences

    Stage 1: given a risky prompt, the model answers, critiques its own answer against a principle drawn from the constitution, rewrites it, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. Stage 2: a model picks the better of two answers using the principles, those labels train a preference model, and the usual RLHF loop (R3.1) runs against it. A principle can find nothing wrong, which is why several are balanced against each other.

    Principles are the interface: to change behaviour, edit a sentence and regenerate labels instead of collecting a new human dataset.

    RLAIF: what it saves, and what it cannot fix

    An AI judge wins on cost (a fraction of a cent per label), consistency and auditability, and research finds it comparable to human feedback on some tasks. Random error averages out. Systematic bias does not: a scale that reads 2 kg heavy gives the wrong answer precisely, however many times you weigh. If the judge always prefers longer or flattering answers, a million labels teach that mistake a million times.

    Trust an AI judge where it is more reliable than the policy at judging, even if not at generating. It is weakest where both share blind spots, such as a CBUAE-specific rule. Where answers can be verified (code runs), use verifiable rewards instead: R3.3.

    Reward hacking: optimise a proxy and it comes apart

    The gold objective is a correct, safe, helpful answer; the proxy is the reward model's score. Reward hacking is exploiting the gap, and it is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

    Best-of-n shows it: sample n answers and keep the proxy's favourite. A few candidates are exploits that fool the proxy, such as confident tone or padding. At small n you rarely draw one; at large n you do, and selection picks it.

    The proxy never warns you: the red line only rises, so a dashboard built on training reward says "improving" through the collapse. A better proxy moves the peak, not the cliff, which is why labs track a held-out measure of the true goal.

    What reward hacking looks like

    • Tampering with the check. A coding agent edits the tests, special-cases test inputs or exits early with a success code.
    • Pleasing the grader. Flattery, confident tone and agreeing with the user: sycophancy (R4.1).

    Humans do it too: a team measured on alerts closed per day who close each in ten seconds, unread. Judge each case below.

    Defences, most trusted first: make the check hard to game (hidden or read-only tests, graders that run the code); limit pressure (KL penalties, early stopping on a held-out gold measure); and monitor the reasoning, though training against that monitor teaches models to hide intent (R4.3).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "The model follows our constitution"

      A written list is not a guarantee of behaviour.

      Spot itAsk for evaluation results on the covered behaviours, including adversarial and non-English prompts.
    • An AI judge with a shared blind spot, or style bias

      Judge and policy share training data, so they agree on wrong answers, and judges favour longer, more confident answers.

      Spot itCompare the judge with a small set of expert human labels on hard cases, and check the correlation between length and reward.
    • Watching only the training reward

      Proxy reward climbs while real quality falls (Simulation 3), and no dashboard shows it.

      Spot itAsk which independent measure of the true goal is tracked.
    • Green tests treated as correct code, or "done" without evidence

      The agent passed by editing tests, hard-coding values, or simply claiming success.

      Spot itReview the diff for test changes and special-cased inputs. Require logs or a reproducible check.
    • Fixing the symptom, not the incentive

      Patching one hack and training on teaches a subtler one.

      Spot itAsk whether the reward changed or only the one exploit found.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Describe the two stages of Constitutional AI in one sentence each.

    Stage 1: the model drafts, critiques and revises against principles, then is fine-tuned on the revisions. Stage 2: a model compares answer pairs using the principles, and those labels train a preference model that RL optimises against.

    2. Why does ten times more AI labels not fix a judge that always prefers long answers?

    More labels cut random error, but the judge errs in the same direction every time, so the reward model learns the same mistake with more confidence. Bias sets the ceiling.

    3. Is a model that opens with "Great question!" because it raises approval ratings reward hacking?

    Yes, a mild one: the score goes up without the intended outcome, better answers. It is the entry point to sycophancy.

    4. Why does training against a chain-of-thought monitor risk hiding the hacking?

    The model is rewarded for reasoning that does not look like hacking, so it can stop writing its intent down while still hacking. Use the monitor to detect, not as a heavy training signal.
