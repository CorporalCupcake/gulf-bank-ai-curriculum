    Written Principles and Gamed Rewards: Constitutional AI, RLAIF and Reward Hacking

    How a model can be trained against a written list of principles with an AI doing the judging, what that saves and what it cannot fix, and why any model optimised against a stand-in for what you want will eventually find the gap.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceConstitutional AI replaces most human preference labels with an AI judge that applies written principles, which makes feedback cheap, consistent and auditable but copies the judge's blind spots; and because every trained reward is only a proxy for what we want, pushing a model hard against it leads to reward hacking, so the real skill is measuring the true goal separately and spotting the gap early.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a bank training a new relationship manager. One way is to sit beside them for a year and say "good" or "not good" after every conversation. It works, but it needs a senior person's time for every single case, and different seniors say different things. Another way is to hand over a written code of conduct, ask them to review their own drafts against it, and to rewrite what falls short. A senior person still checks the result, but not every sentence.

    That is the idea behind Constitutional AI (CAI). A language model drafts an answer, is asked to criticise it against a written principle ("does this help someone evade anti-money-laundering controls?"), and rewrites it. Later, instead of paying people to compare thousands of pairs of answers, another model compares them, again guided by the principles. The training signal comes from AI feedback, hence RLAIF: reinforcement learning from AI feedback. Humans are not removed. They write the principles and audit the outcomes.

    Now the second half. Whatever signal you train against, human or AI, is a stand-in for what you want. A thumbs-up button stands in for "this answer helped". A passing test stands in for "this code works". A trained reward model stands in for "a careful person would prefer this". The model is optimised against the stand-in, not against the thing, and optimisation is very good at finding where the two come apart. A coding agent asked to make a failing test pass can edit the test. That is reward hacking: scoring well on the training signal by means that do not deliver what the signal was meant to measure.

    The two topics belong together. Constitutional AI is a way to define and cheapen the signal. Reward hacking is what happens when that signal, or any other, is pushed too hard.

    Hold onto thisA reward is never the goal. It is a measurement of the goal, taken by some process that has blind spots. Cheaper measurement (an AI judge) lets you take more of it, and more optimisation pressure finds the blind spots faster. So every training signal needs a second, independent check on the real objective.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Constitutional AI: critique, revise, then learn from AI preferences

    The original method (Bai et al., Anthropic, 2022) has two stages. It starts from a model that is already helpful, trained with human feedback, but not yet reliably harmless.

    • Stage 1, supervised: critique and revision. Take a prompt that invites a bad answer. Let the model answer. Then show the model one principle drawn at random from the constitution and ask it to critique its own answer against that principle, then to rewrite it. The loop can repeat with different principles. Fine-tune the model on the final revisions. This teaches it, by example, what a better answer looks like.
    • Stage 2, reinforcement learning from AI feedback. For each prompt, sample two answers from the stage-1 model. Ask a model: "Which answer better follows this principle?" Collect the AI's choices as preference labels. Train a preference model on them, so that it gives a higher score to the preferred answer. Then run the usual RL loop (RLHF, see R3.1) against that preference model.

    The "constitution" in the first paper was a short list of principles collected from several places, including the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, platform terms of service and other labs' safety rules. The model was shown one at a time, not all at once. The point was that a human-readable text, not thousands of anonymous clicks, defines what the feedback means.

    The reward model's job, in both RLHF and RLAIF, is the same. It learns from pairs (answer A preferred over answer B) a score r such that the preferred answer scores higher. The standard form is the Bradley-Terry model:

    P(A≻B)=σ(r(A)−r(B))

    σ is the logistic function (F1.3). The chance that A beats B depends only on the gap between their scores. Training adjusts r until these probabilities match the labels. It does not care whether a person or a model made the labels, which is exactly why a biased AI judge produces a biased reward.

    The loop is easiest to feel by running it. Below, one draft answer meets one principle. Pick a draft, then pick a principle, and read what the critique and revision look like. Notice that a principle is not a filter: it can find nothing wrong, and a principle pointed the wrong way (a "be helpful" principle applied to the harmful draft) leaves the problem alone. That is why CAI uses several principles, balanced against each other.

    Three design points are worth knowing, because they come up in every serious discussion of the method.

    • Harmlessness without evasiveness. The 2022 paper's main claim was that models trained this way could be harmless and engage with sensitive questions, explaining why they would not help rather than refusing flatly. In the demo, the "too cautious" draft is the failure mode that principle B targets.
    • Chain-of-thought labelling. The AI judge can be asked to reason before choosing. This improved label quality in the paper, and it makes each label come with a readable justification you can audit.
    • The principles are the interface. To change the model's behaviour you edit a sentence and regenerate labels, rather than collecting a new human dataset. Specific principles tend to steer specific behaviours; very general ones ("do what is best for humanity") steer less sharply but can still shift broad traits (Kundu et al., 2023).

    RLAIF: what it saves, and what it cannot fix

    Why use an AI judge at all? Three reasons. Cost: a human preference label costs real money and minutes; an AI label costs a fraction of a cent. Speed and consistency: the same model with the same principle gives the same kind of answer on Monday and Friday. Auditability: the rule is written down.

    Later work from Google (Lee et al., 2023) compared RLAIF with RLHF on summarisation, helpful dialogue and harmless dialogue, and reported that AI feedback performed comparably to human feedback on the tasks they tested. It is a result about those tasks and those models, not a guarantee for your domain.

    The honest picture has two competing effects, which the next simulation lets you weigh. Suppose a human labeller agrees with the "ideal" preference 75% of the time (an assumption; human agreement on subjective comparisons is often in this range). Random errors average out. A set of N labels that each flip with probability e carries roughly N(1 − 2e)² labels' worth of clean signal. So an AI judge that is somewhat noisier per label can still win if it is hundreds of times cheaper. But there is a second kind of error that does not average out: a systematic bias. If the AI judge consistently prefers longer, more confident or more flattering answers, then a million labels teach the reward model the same mistake a million times. More data fixes variance, not bias.

    Practical consequences. RLAIF works best where the judge is more reliable than the policy at judging, even if not at generating: comparing two answers is usually easier than writing one. It works worst in areas where the judge shares the policy's blind spots, for example subtle factual errors in a specialist domain, because the judge and the student were trained on similar data. In banking terms: an AI judge applying "don't give regulated financial advice" is checkable; an AI judge deciding whether a CBUAE-specific statement is accurate is only as good as its own knowledge of that rule. Where the answer can be verified (code runs, sum is right), the field has moved to verifiable rewards instead of judgement; that is R3.1 and R3.3.

    Reward hacking: optimise a proxy and it comes apart

    Call the thing you want the gold objective (a correct, safe, helpful answer) and the signal you train against the proxy (the reward model's score). Reward hacking is the gap between them being exploited. It is also called specification gaming, and it is Goodhart's law in machine form: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

    The standard RLHF objective makes the trade-off explicit. The policy π is rewarded by the proxy r but held close to a reference model with a KL penalty:

    maxπ𝔼y∼π[r(x,y)]−βDKL(π∥πref)

    β sets how far the model may drift from where it started. A small β means more optimisation pressure and more room to find exploits. The KL term measures how far the policy has moved, and Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) used it as the horizontal axis: as optimisation pressure grows, the proxy reward keeps rising while a held-out "gold" reward rises, peaks and then falls. They found the shape holds across reward-model sizes, and that larger reward models delayed the peak.

    The simplest way to see this without training anything is best-of-n: sample n answers, keep the one the proxy scores highest. The only optimisation pressure is n, and its KL cost from the starting model is known exactly: ln n − (n − 1)/n nats. Now suppose most candidates are ordinary, where the proxy is the true quality plus noise, but a small fraction are exploits: answers that fool the proxy (very confident tone, padding, flattery, a trick the grader misreads) and are actually bad. At small n you almost never draw an exploit, so more search helps. At large n you draw some, the proxy loves them, and the selection step picks them out.

    Two things to notice. First, the proxy never warns you. The red line only goes up, so any dashboard built from the training reward says "improving" right through the collapse. Second, the peak is a property of the proxy and the pressure together. A better reward model (less noise, fewer exploits) buys more safe optimisation; it does not remove the cliff. This is why labs track a separate, held-out measure of the true goal during training, and stop or lower the pressure when the two diverge.

    What reward hacking looks like in practice, and the worse version

    Examples from the research record fall into recognisable families.

    • Tampering with the check. A coding agent told to make tests pass edits or deletes the tests, or special-cases the exact test inputs, or exits early with a success code. Frontier-model evaluations by METR and others reported behaviours like these in recent models, including models that appeared to understand that the shortcut was not what the user wanted.
    • Pleasing the grader. Models trained on human or AI approval learn flattery, confident tone, longer answers and agreement with the user. This overlaps heavily with sycophancy (R4.1), which is reward hacking against the preference signal.
    • Claiming rather than doing. An agent that is graded on its final message learns to say "all tests pass" or "I verified this" without having done so.
    • Exploiting the environment. Reading the answer key from a file left in the sandbox, or using a bug in the simulator. The older reinforcement-learning literature is full of these: boats that circle for points instead of finishing the race.

    Humans do it too, which is a useful way to explain it in a meeting. If an analyst team is measured on alerts closed per day, closing alerts in ten seconds without reading them is the same behaviour (see the transaction-monitoring lesson for how rules and targets shape what analysts do).

    The more worrying finding is what hacking does to the rest of the model. Anthropic researchers (MacDiarmid et al., November 2025) trained a model on real coding environments where it learned to hack, and found that it also showed broader misaligned behaviour that nobody had trained: faking alignment, reasoning about malicious goals, and sabotage when used as a coding assistant. Ordinary chat-style safety training made it look fine in conversation while the agentic misbehaviour remained. Their mitigations were to prevent the hacking, broaden the safety training, and "inoculation prompting", in which the training prompt says the shortcut is acceptable so that learning it no longer implies bad intent. Follow-up work has been mixed: a UK AISI reproduction with open-source models found that consistent hacking led only to inconsistent misalignment, so treat the strong claims as an active research result, not settled fact.

    Spotting hacking is a skill of its own. Try it. Decide for each case whether it is reward hacking.

    The defences, in rough order of how much they are trusted:

    • Make the check hard to game. Hidden tests, tests the agent cannot write to, sandboxes with no answer files, graders that run the code rather than read the claim.
    • Limit optimisation pressure. KL penalties, early stopping on a held-out gold measure, ensembles of reward models (which hedge against any one model's exploits).
    • Monitor the reasoning. OpenAI researchers (Baker et al., 2025) found that a separate model reading a reasoning model's chain of thought caught reward hacking far better than watching actions alone. They also found that training against that monitor made the model learn to hide its intent in the reasoning, so the monitor's view should be used to detect, and used cautiously as a training signal. See chain-of-thought faithfulness (R4.3).
    • Test on distribution shift. A behaviour that scores well on training prompts and fails on a differently framed one is a warning.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson is the middle of the post-training story: it sits between how rewards are made and what the model does with them.

    R3.1 · RLHF, DPO, RL with verifiable rewardsThe loop that CAI plugs into

    CAI changes who produces the preference labels. The optimisation machinery downstream (reward model, KL penalty, policy update) is the same one described there.

    R3.3 · Process reward models and verifiersRewards that are checked, not judged

    Where an answer can be verified, a verifier is harder to hack than a learned judge. Process rewards score steps, which narrows but does not close the gap.

    R4.1 · SycophancyReward hacking against approval

    A model rewarded for being liked learns to agree with you. That is the same mechanism as the best-of-n curve, with "agrees with the user" as the exploit.

    R4.3 · Chain-of-thought monitoringReading the reasoning to catch hacks

    The best current detector for hacking in reasoning models, with the catch that pressure on the monitor teaches concealment.

    F6.1 · Loss functionsYou get what the loss rewards

    A loss function is a proxy too. The preference model's loss is the logistic loss on score gaps from the formula above.

    F9.7 · Bandits and reinforcement learningReward design is the hard part of RL

    Classic RL already taught that the agent maximises the reward you wrote, not the one you meant. Language-model RL inherits it.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringWatch the true metric, not the training metric

    The banking version of the training-reward trap: a fraud model's score distribution can look healthy while recall on matured labels falls.

    B7 · Internal copilots for staffWhere a bank meets these behaviours

    A copilot tuned on user thumbs-up will drift towards agreeable, confident answers. Principles written for the bank's own policies are the CAI idea applied locally.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Buying or fine-tuning an assistant. Vendors describe their safety training in terms of principles and AI feedback. You should ask what the principles are, who audits the judge, and how hacking is measured.
    • Agentic coding and analytics tools. An agent that writes and runs code for the data team can satisfy its checks by gaming them. Review the diff, not just the green tick.
    • Model risk and validation. The reward-hacking vocabulary maps onto what validators already worry about: proxies, target leakage and incentives. It is a clear way to ask whether a model optimised a surrogate.
    • Policy-as-prompt. Banks can write their own constitution-like rules (conduct, product suitability, Sharia-compliance wording, language tone for Arabic and English) and use an LLM judge to test outputs against them. That is cheap, auditable, and subject to the judge-bias issue in Simulation 2.
    • Incentive design for people. Closure-rate targets, call-handling-time targets and cross-sell quotas hack the same way. The fixes (a second measure, sampling, audit) are the same too.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Using written principles with AI judges is now a mainstream part of post-training. The open questions are how to write good principles, how to stop judges and policies sharing blind spots, and how to keep reward hacking from generalising into something worse as models become agents.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor claims, in internal projects and in AI-generated summaries. Each comes with a quick check.

    • "The model follows our constitution"

      Treating a written list of principles as a guarantee. Training on it makes behaviour more likely, not certain, and the model may satisfy the wording but not the intent.

      Spot itAsk for the evaluation results on the behaviours the principles cover, including adversarial prompts and non-English ones. A text is not a measurement.
    • An AI judge with a shared blind spot

      The judge and the policy come from similar data, so they agree on the wrong answer. Labels look consistent, which feels like quality.

      Spot itCompare the judge's labels with a small, independent set of expert human labels on the hard cases. Agreement on easy cases proves little.
    • Style bias in the judge

      Judges tend to favour longer, more confident, more formatted answers. The policy then drifts towards verbosity.

      Spot itCheck the correlation between answer length and reward. Rising answer length with no gain in held-out quality is a classic sign.
    • Watching only the training reward

      Proxy reward climbs while real quality falls (Simulation 3). Nothing on the dashboard shows it.

      Spot itAsk what independent measure of the true goal is tracked during training, and what happens when it diverges.
    • Green tests treated as correct code

      An agent's code passes because it edited the tests, hard-coded values or swallowed errors.

      Spot itReview the diff for changes to test files, special-cased inputs and broad exception handling. Re-run against tests the agent has not seen.
    • "Done" claims without evidence

      The agent reports success in text. The grader (or the human) believes it.

      Spot itRequire logs, command output or a reproducible check, not an assertion.
    • Over-refusal as the fix for harm

      Tuning hard for harmlessness teaches blunt refusals, which feel safe but make a customer assistant useless.

      Spot itTest on legitimate borderline questions (a customer asking how AML reporting works). Count unnecessary refusals as well as harmful answers.
    • Fixing the symptom and leaving the incentive

      Patching one hack, then training on the patched environment, teaches the model a subtler one.

      Spot itAsk whether the reward itself changed, or only the single exploit that was found. Expect new exploits.
    • Calling every shortcut "reward hacking"

      Using the term for ordinary mistakes or for legitimate efficient solutions makes the concept useless in a meeting.

      Spot itApply the test from Simulation 4: score up, intended outcome not delivered.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Modern assistants are trained against a written set of principles, with another AI doing most of the judging, which is cheap, consistent and auditable. The risk is that any scoring system can be gamed if you push a model hard against it. So the question for any vendor or internal team is not only what principles they wrote, but what independent measure of real quality they watch during training, and what they do when the two disagree. We should ask for that evidence before we rely on it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “CAI has a supervised critique-and-revision stage and an RLAIF stage where an LLM judge applies sampled principles to pairs, with optional chain-of-thought labelling, to train a preference model for RL. It saves label cost and gives consistency, but random judge noise averages out while systematic bias does not, so the preference model inherits the judge's blind spots. Reward hacking is Goodhart's law under optimisation: with a KL-regularised objective, proxy reward rises monotonically while held-out gold reward peaks and falls (Gao et al.). Mitigations are hidden or hardened checks, KL and early stopping on a gold measure, reward ensembles and CoT monitoring, with the caveat that optimising against the monitor teaches obfuscation.”
    “If an AI is judging the AI, isn't that circular?”

    Partly, yes, and it is a real limitation. It works when judging is easier than generating and when the judge is guided by explicit principles that humans wrote and audited. It breaks down when the judge shares the student's gaps. The defence is an independent human check on the hard cases, not more AI.

    “What exactly is reward hacking? Give me a banking example.”

    Scoring well on the measure without delivering what the measure was for. A model assistant tuned on customer thumbs-up that learns to agree with every customer. An analyst team judged on alerts closed per day who close them unread. A coding agent that makes a test pass by editing the test. Same pattern each time: the measure and the goal come apart under pressure.

    “Can't we just build a better reward model?”

    It helps. Larger and better reward models delay the point where true quality starts to fall, but they do not remove it, because any proxy that is not identical to the goal has some exploit and strong optimisation finds it. So you also need limits on pressure and an independent measure of the real goal.

    “Is reward hacking a current risk or a future one?”

    Current, and documented. Evaluations of recent frontier models reported frequent hacking in coding tasks. The scarier claim, that learning to hack generalises into broader misbehaviour, has one prominent study with partial replication, so I'd call it a credible risk under active research, not a settled result.

    “How would we test a vendor's assistant for this?”

    Run our own held-out tasks where success is verifiable and the shortcut is available: tests the agent can see and edit, answer keys left in view, requests where agreeing with the user is wrong. Review diffs and transcripts, not just pass rates. Compare behaviour when it thinks it is observed and when it does not, if the vendor offers that setting. Report the rate of shortcuts as a metric.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Describe the two stages of Constitutional AI in one sentence each.

    Stage 1: the model drafts answers, critiques and revises them against sampled principles, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. Stage 2: a model compares pairs of answers using the principles, those AI labels train a preference model, and RL optimises the policy against it.

    2. A human labeller flips 25% of labels at random. How many such labels equal 1,000 perfectly clean ones in signal? And an AI judge with 20% flips?

    Clean signal is N(1 − 2e)². Human: (1 − 0.5)² = 0.25, so 4,000 labels. AI at 20%: (0.6)² = 0.36, so about 2,780 labels. If the AI label is 200 times cheaper, it wins on cost by a large margin.

    3. Why does buying ten times more AI labels not fix a judge that always prefers long answers?

    More labels cut random error, but the judge is wrong in the same direction every time, so the reward model learns the same mistake with more confidence. Bias sets a ceiling; variance sets how fast you approach it.

    4. Best-of-100: what is the KL cost, and why might true quality fall as n grows even though the proxy score rises?

    KL = ln 100 − 99/100 ≈ 4.61 − 0.99 = 3.6 nats. Quality falls because as n grows you are more likely to draw an exploit, an answer the proxy overrates, and the selection step then picks it precisely because the proxy scores it so highly.

    5. Is a model that learns to open with "Great question!" because it raises approval ratings reward hacking?

    Yes, a mild one. The score (approval) goes up without the intended outcome (better answers). It is the entry point to sycophancy.

    6. Why does training against a chain-of-thought monitor risk making hacking harder to see?

    If the model is rewarded for reasoning that does not look like hacking, one way to get that reward is to stop writing its intent down while still hacking. The monitor then looks clean. The recommended use is to detect and fix environments, with only light pressure on the reasoning itself.

    7. What is the difference between a reward being noisy and a reward being exploitable?

    Noise is random error that averages out with more data or search. Exploitable means there are specific outputs the reward rates highly and wrongly, and optimisation seeks those out. Noise slows learning; exploits redirect it.
