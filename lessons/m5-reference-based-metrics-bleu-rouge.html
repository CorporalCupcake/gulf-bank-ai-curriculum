    M5.1 · M5.2 Day one ★ Priority About 40 minutes

    Grading Generated Text: Overlap Metrics and LLM-as-Judge

    What BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore actually count, why a wrong answer can outscore a right one, how a model can grade a model, and the biases and sample sizes that decide whether you can trust its grades.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceWord-overlap metrics measure how similar two strings are, not whether an answer is right, so they only work where there is one acceptable wording; an LLM judge can read for meaning but carries biases of its own, so you validate it against human labels, with an interval, before trusting any number it produces.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank assistant answers questions about blocked cards. "Your card was frozen after three wrong PIN entries" and "We suspended your card because the PIN was entered incorrectly three times" are both correct and share almost no words. Like marking an essay, two good answers can look nothing alike.

    • Reference-based: write a reference answer and score closeness to it (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore). Cheap, fast, reproducible.
    • LLM-as-judge: a strong model grades the output against a rubric. Handles paraphrase, tone and faithfulness to a source. Slower, costs money, biased in measurable ways.

    Either way, the score is a proxy for "would a careful human call this good?". The rest of the lesson is about how far to trust it.

    Hold onto thisEvery metric is a testable claim: "this number tracks human judgement on this task." Collect human labels on a few hundred outputs, compare, and report agreement with an interval. A metric nobody has checked against humans is a guess with a decimal point.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.1 Reference-based metrics: BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore

    All three need a human-written reference and reduce "similar" to a number.

    • ROUGE (summarisation) asks how much of the reference's wording the output covers. That is recall. ROUGE-L uses the longest shared run of words in order.
    • BLEU (translation) asks how much of the output's wording is found in the reference. That is precision, with a brevity penalty so a three-word output cannot win by being safe.
    • BERTScore matches meaning, not strings, by comparing contextual embeddings (vectors from a pretrained encoder) with cosine similarity. "Frozen" and "blocked" now score close.

    Nothing in ROUGE or BLEU knows meaning. Swap "50,000" for "5,000" and you lose one shared word in twenty. Change "blocked" to "not blocked" and you gain a word nobody penalises.

    MetricReading a valueWhat a change means
    ROUGE0 is no shared words, 1 is full coverage. A correct paraphrase can still score low.0.40 to 0.46 means more reference phrases appear. It does not mean the answer is 6 points more correct.
    BLEUOften quoted 0 to 100. Compare only with identical settings and test set.Meaningful at corpus level, rarely per sentence.

    Mental model: they measure resemblance to one wording, not correctness. Simulation 1 scores one reference against eight candidates, four right and four wrong. Judge whether the numbers sort them correctly.

    The pattern:

    • High overlap, wrong meaning. "Not blocked" and the invented AED 50,000 limit sit near the top. Word counting cannot see a negation or a fabrication.
    • Low overlap, right meaning. Honest paraphrases lose points for different words, and the terse correct answer is punished by recall.
    • Soft matching fixes synonyms, not facts. Real BERTScore is better on paraphrase but still weak on negation, numbers and entities.

    Arabic is harder. "البطاقة" (the card) and "بطاقتك" (your card) share a root but no whole word, so exact matching calls them different. BALSAM found BLEU and ROUGE correlated poorly with human ratings on Arabic, BERTScore somewhat better, and LLM-as-judge best.

    When reference-based metrics are still the right tool

    They work when few wordings are correct, so the reference stands in for "correct".

    TaskBetter metric than overlap?Why
    Extracting a field (IBAN, amount, date)Exact match, or normalised matchOne right answer. Do not use fuzzy scores for a number.
    Translation, e.g. Arabic to EnglishchrF, COMET or BLEURT, with human spot checksCharacter n-grams cope with Arabic morphology; learned metrics track humans better than BLEU.
    Open answers, summariesLLM judge plus human samplingMany valid wordings; faithfulness and tone matter more.
    Code, SQLExecute it and compare resultsBehaviour, not text, is what counts.

    Good use: a cheap regression alarm. If a prompt change drops ROUGE-L on 500 stable summaries by ten points, something moved, so a human looks. Bad use: reporting "ROUGE-1 of 0.46" to an executive as a quality score.

    M5.2 LLM-as-judge

    A judge is a strong model given the question, the answer, perhaps a reference or source, and a rubric. Like a marker with a mark scheme, it reads for meaning, so it spots "not blocked" and the invented limit. A strong judge agrees with human preferences over 80% of the time on open chat, about as often as two humans agree with each other (Zheng et al., 2023).

    Three set-ups worth knowing:

    • Pairwise. "Which of A and B is better?" Stable, and the basis of win-rates. A 60% win rate means A beat B in 60 of 100 matchups, and 50% means no difference. Needs care about order.
    • Reference-guided. The judge also sees a gold answer. Far more reliable on facts, because it no longer relies on its own knowledge.
    • Binary checklist. Yes/no questions: does it state the 60-day limit? does it avoid promising a refund? Easier to agree on than a 1-to-5 scale, which is unanchored and bunches near the top.

    A good judge prompt names the criteria, defines each score level with an example, and fixes the output format. Version and test it like code (M2.1).

    Judges have biases, and some can be measured and removed

    BiasWhat happensMitigation
    PositionIn pairwise judging, the judge favours the first (or second) answer regardless of content.Run both orders. Count a win only if it holds in both, or average.
    VerbosityLonger answers win, even when the extra length is padding.Rubric that penalises padding; compare at matched length.
    Self-preferenceA model tends to rate text from its own family more highly.Use a judge from a different family, or several judges.
    Weak at verifyingOn hard maths, code or domain facts, the judge may not know the answer either, and confident style beats plain correctness.Give it the gold answer or source passage; use execution where possible.

    Simulation 2 models a judge with three pulls: a real quality gap, a bonus for going first, and a bonus for being longer. Order-swapping removes the second. It does nothing to the third.

    A high agreement rate does not reveal which biases a judge has. Find them with tests: swap the order, pad one answer, hide the model name. Consistency run to run is not correctness.

    Validating the judge: how many human labels do you need?

    Treat the judge like a thermometer and check it against a trusted one. Have humans grade 100 to 300 outputs blind (M5.3), have the judge grade them, and report agreement with an interval, because the sample is finite.

    Also report a chance-corrected figure. If 85% of answers pass anyway, a lazy judge that always says "pass" agrees 85% of the time. Cohen's kappa asks how much of the possible improvement over guessing the judge achieves: 0 is no better than chance, 1 is perfect. Below 0.4 is weak, 0.6 to 0.8 good, above 0.8 strong. Here, 90% raw agreement gives kappa of only about 0.6.

    Reading the interval. "90% agreement, 95% interval 80% to 95%" means the true rate is plausibly anywhere in that range. Quadruple the labels to halve the width.

    Rules of thumb:

    • Use at least 100 labelled items for a first look, 300 or more for a result you will defend. Include hard and rare cases.
    • Re-validate when you change the judge model, the rubric or the system under test.
    • Never optimise against the judge you test with. The system learns to please the judge, not the customer (reward hacking, R3.6). Hold out a human-graded set the optimisation never touches.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • An overlap score presented as quality

      "ROUGE-L is 0.52, up from 0.47." Nobody has shown those points mean better answers, or that one reference suits an open task.

      Spot itRead ten output pairs whose scores differ by five points. Ask whether the metric was ever checked against human ratings on this task.
    • Unreported metric settings

      BLEU with different tokenisation or smoothing is not comparable.

      Spot itLook for the library and signature (for example a SacreBLEU signature string).
    • Judge never validated against humans

      "GPT-graded accuracy: 92%." Graded by what, checked how? Or agreement quoted without the base rate, when 90% of answers pass anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the number of human-labelled items, the agreement with an interval, the kappa and the false-pass rate.
    • Pairwise judging in a single order

      Position bias shifts win rates by points, enough to flip a ranking of two close models.

      Spot itAsk whether both orders were run. If the win rate moves on swap, the judge is not measuring quality alone.
    • Judge favours its own kind, or length

      Relatives grade generously, and a prompt that makes answers 40% longer can raise scores while customers just read more.

      Spot itCompare judge and generator families, and plot judge score against length. Re-score with another provider's judge or control for length.
    • Optimising against the judge you test with

      Prompts are tuned until the score peaks, so the score is no longer evidence. The judge may also pass confident wrong answers it cannot verify.

      Spot itAsk for a human-graded set no tuning ever saw, and check the judge saw the source or gold answer.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Reference: "the card was blocked". Output: "the card was not blocked". Roughly how will ROUGE-1 score this, and what does that tell you?

    Very high, about 0.9: every reference word appears in the output and only "not" is extra. The meaning is the opposite, so overlap cannot see negation.

    2. BLEU is precision-based and ROUGE recall-based. Why does BLEU need a brevity penalty?

    Precision alone rewards short outputs: one word that is in the reference scores 100%. The penalty cuts the score when the output is shorter than the reference.

    3. Name two tasks where an exact or deterministic metric beats both overlap and an LLM judge.

    Extracting an amount, date or IBAN (exact match), and text-to-SQL or code (execute and compare). With one verifiable right answer, check it directly.

    4. In pairwise judging, A beats B when shown first, and B beats A when shown second. What does this tell you, and what do you report?

    Position bias dominates, so it is effectively a tie. Run both orders, count a win only if it holds in both, and report the share of order-dependent verdicts.

    5. Swapping order did not improve your judge's accuracy at all. What other bias could be responsible?

    Verbosity (or style) bias. It attaches to the answer, not its position, so swapping cannot cancel it. Test by padding one answer, and mitigate with the rubric or length control.

    6. A judge agrees with humans on 92 of 100 items, and humans passed 90 of 100. How impressive is this?

    Not very. An always-pass judge would already agree on 90%, so the real gain over a lazy judge is small (kappa about 0.56, moderate). With only 100 items the true agreement could plausibly be 85% to 96%. Check the false-pass rate before relying on it.

    7. Your team tunes the prompt to maximise the judge score and reports a rise from 3.9 to 4.5. What is missing?

    Evidence the rise is real. Tuning against the judge exploits its biases (length, style). Re-score a held-out human-graded set the tuning never saw, and check whether answers simply got longer.
