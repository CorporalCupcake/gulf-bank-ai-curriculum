    Grading Generated Text: Overlap Metrics and LLM-as-Judge

    What BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore actually count, why a wrong answer can outscore a right one, how a model can grade a model, and the biases and sample sizes that decide whether you can trust its grades.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceWord-overlap metrics measure how similar two strings are, not whether an answer is right, so they only work where there is one acceptable wording; an LLM judge can read for meaning but carries biases of its own, so you validate it against human labels, with an interval, before trusting any number it produces.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank assistant answers questions about blocked cards. With a classifier you compare predictions with labels and count. With text there is no single label: "Your card was frozen after three wrong PIN entries" and "We suspended your card because the PIN was entered incorrectly three times" are both correct and share almost no words. Like marking an essay, two good answers can look nothing alike.

    Two families of answer:

    • Reference-based: write a reference answer and score closeness to it (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore). Cheap, fast, reproducible.
    • LLM-as-judge: a strong model reads the output and grades it against a rubric. Handles paraphrase and things no reference captures, like tone or faithfulness to a source. Slower, costs money, biased in measurable ways.

    Either way, the score is a proxy for "would a careful human call this good?". The rest of the lesson is about how far to trust it, and how to find out.

    Hold onto thisEvery metric is a testable claim: "this number tracks human judgement on this task." Collect human labels on a few hundred outputs, compare, and report agreement with an interval. A metric nobody has checked against humans is a guess with a decimal point.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.1 Reference-based metrics: BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore

    All three need a human-written reference and reduce "similar" to a number. Think of marking an essay against a model answer by counting matching phrases.

    • ROUGE (Lin, 2004; summarisation) counts how many of the reference's n-grams (word runs of length n) appear in the output. That is recall. Libraries also report precision and F. ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence instead of fixed chunks.
    • BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002; translation) is precision: the share of the output's 1 to 4-grams found in the reference. Counts are clipped (so "the the the" earns nothing) and a brevity penalty stops a three-word output winning by being safe.
    • BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) matches meaning, not strings. Each token gets a contextual embedding (a vector from a pretrained encoder), each output token is matched to its most similar reference token by cosine similarity (F1.1), and matches are averaged. "Frozen" and "blocked" now score close.

    ROUGE-1 in words: count shared words (repeats capped at the smaller count), divide by reference words for recall and by output words for precision, then combine:

    F=2·P·RP+R,P=shared wordswords in output,R=shared wordswords in reference

    Nothing here knows meaning. Swap "50,000" for "5,000" and you lose one shared word in twenty. Change "blocked" to "not blocked" and you gain a word nobody penalises.

    MetricWhat it truly meansReading a valueWhat a change means
    ROUGEHow much of the reference's wording the output covers (recall).0 is no shared words, 1 is full coverage. A correct paraphrase can still score low.Going from 0.40 to 0.46 means more reference phrases appear. It does not mean the answer is 6 points more correct.
    BLEUHow much of the output's wording is found in the reference (precision), with a length penalty.Often quoted 0 to 100. Compare only with identical settings and test set.A rise says outputs use more reference-like phrases. It is meaningful at corpus level, rarely per sentence.
    BERTScoreHow close the output's tokens are in meaning to the reference's.Values tend to bunch high, so differences between systems matter more than the level.A tiny shift is usually noise. It still misses negation, numbers and entities.

    Mental model: they measure resemblance to one wording, not correctness. The common misreading is treating a higher score as a better answer.

    That is the weakness. Simulation 1 scores one reference against eight candidates, four right and four wrong. Judge whether the numbers sort them correctly.

    The pattern:

    • High overlap, wrong meaning. "Not blocked" and the invented AED 50,000 limit sit near the top. Shared-word counting cannot see a negation or a fabrication.
    • Low overlap, right meaning. Honest paraphrases lose points for different words. The terse correct answer is punished by recall.
    • Soft matching fixes synonyms, not facts. It lifts the paraphrases, but "unblock only at a branch after 30 days" still looks near the reference. Real BERTScore behaves the same: better on paraphrase, weak on negation, numbers and entities.

    Arabic is harder. Prefixes and suffixes mean "البطاقة" (the card) and "بطاقتك" (your card) share a root but no whole word, so exact matching calls them different. BALSAM found BLEU and ROUGE correlated poorly and unevenly with human ratings on Arabic, BERTScore somewhat better, and LLM-as-judge best. Scores also depend on the tokeniser (M1.1) and normalisation, so a BLEU score without its settings (see SacreBLEU, Post 2018) is not reproducible.

    When reference-based metrics are still the right tool

    They work when few wordings are correct, so the reference stands in for "correct".

    TaskBetter metric than overlap?Why
    Extracting a field (IBAN, amount, date, counterparty name)Exact match, or normalised matchOne right answer. Do not use fuzzy scores for a number.
    Classification by LLM (complaint category, intent)Accuracy, precision, recall (F7.1)The output is a label. Use the classifier toolkit.
    Translation, e.g. Arabic to EnglishchrF, COMET or BLEURT, with human spot checksCharacter n-grams cope with Arabic morphology; learned metrics track humans better than BLEU.
    Extractive summary or templated outputROUGE is a fair regression testOutput wording is constrained, so overlap tracks content.
    Open answers, advice, abstractive summariesLLM judge plus human samplingMany valid wordings; faithfulness and tone matter more than overlap.
    Code, SQLExecute it and compare resultsBehaviour, not text, is what counts (see text-to-SQL in R13.2).

    Good use: a cheap regression alarm. If a prompt change drops ROUGE-L on 500 stable summaries by ten points, something moved, so a human looks. Bad use: reporting "ROUGE-1 of 0.46" to an executive as a quality score.

    M5.2 LLM-as-judge

    A judge is a strong model given the question, the answer, perhaps a reference or source document, and a rubric. Like a marker with a mark scheme, it reads for meaning, so it spots "not blocked" and the invented limit. Zheng et al. (2023) found a strong judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time on open chat, about as often as two humans agree with each other.

    Four set-ups:

    • Pointwise. "Rate 1 to 5 for faithfulness." Cheap, but unanchored: one judge's 4 is another's 3, and scores bunch near the top.
    • Pairwise. "Which of A and B is better?" More stable, and the basis of win-rates. A win rate of 60% means A beat B in 60 of 100 matchups. It says nothing about by how much, and 50% means no difference. Needs care about order.
    • Reference-guided. The judge also sees a gold answer. Far more reliable on facts, because it no longer relies on its own knowledge.
    • Binary checklist. Yes/no questions: does it state the 60-day limit? does it avoid promising a refund? Easier for models and humans to agree on than a 1-to-5 scale.

    A good judge prompt names the criteria, defines each score level with an example, asks for reasoning before the verdict, and fixes the output format. Version and test it like code (M2.1).

    Judges have biases, and some can be measured and removed

    BiasWhat happensMitigation
    PositionIn pairwise judging, the judge favours the first (or second) answer regardless of content.Run both orders. Count a win only if it holds in both, or average.
    VerbosityLonger answers win, even when the extra length is padding.Rubric that penalises padding; length-controlled win rates; compare at matched length.
    Self-preferenceA model tends to rate text from its own family more highly.Use a judge from a different family than the system under test, or several judges.
    Style over substanceConfident, well-formatted answers beat correct but plain ones.Reference-guided grading; binary factual checklist.
    Weak at verifyingOn hard maths, code or domain facts, the judge may not know the answer either.Give it the gold answer or source passage; use tools or execution where possible.
    InstabilitySame input, different grade on a second call, or after a trivial rewording of the rubric.Temperature 0, repeated runs, report test-retest agreement.

    Position and verbosity bias are easy to see in numbers. Simulation 2 models a judge whose preference for A over B has three parts: a real quality gap, a bonus for going first, and a bonus for being longer. Order-swapping removes the second. Nothing in the order trick touches the third.

    A high agreement rate does not reveal which biases the judge has. Find them by designing tests: swap the order, pad one answer, hide the model name. A 2026 preprint ("Reliability without Validity") found judges that were highly consistent run to run and still had strong position bias. Consistency is not correctness.

    Validating the judge: how many human labels do you need?

    Treat the judge like a thermometer and check it against a reference one. Take 100 to 300 outputs, have two or more humans grade them blind (M5.3), have the judge grade them, and report agreement as an interval (it is a proportion from a finite sample, as recall was in F3.5).

    Also report a chance-corrected figure. If 85% of answers pass anyway, a lazy judge that always says "pass" agrees 85% of the time. Cohen's kappa (κ) subtracts that:

    κ=po−pe1−pe

    po is observed agreement, pe the agreement expected if judge and human labelled independently with the same pass rates. Example: 85% passes on both sides gives pe = 0.85² + 0.15² = 0.745. Observed 90% then gives κ = (0.90 − 0.745) ÷ 0.255 ≈ 0.61: moderate, not the near-perfect that "90%" suggests.

    Reading kappa. It is the share of the possible improvement over guessing that the judge achieves. 0 means no better than chance; 1 means perfect. Rough guide: below 0.4 weak, 0.4 to 0.6 moderate, 0.6 to 0.8 good, above 0.8 strong. Going from 0.61 to 0.75 means the judge closes about a third of the remaining gap to perfect. It says nothing about which errors the judge makes, so also check the false-pass rate.

    Reading the interval. "90% agreement, 95% interval 80% to 95%" means the true rate is plausibly anywhere in that range. More labels narrow it, roughly with the square root of the sample: quadruple the labels to halve the width.

    κ=po−pe1−pe

    po is the observed agreement and pe the agreement you would expect if judge and human labelled independently with the same pass rates. With 85% passes on both sides, pe = 0.85² + 0.15² = 0.745. An observed 90% agreement then gives κ = (0.90 − 0.745) ÷ 0.255 ≈ 0.61: moderate agreement, not the near-perfect that "90%" suggests.

    Rules of thumb:

    • Use at least 100 labelled items for a first look, 300 or more for a result you will defend. Include hard and rare cases, not just typical ones.
    • Re-validate when you change the judge model, the rubric or the system under test.
    • Keep a standing human sample (say 5% of production traffic) to catch judge drift.
    • With scarce labels, prediction-powered inference (R7.4) combines a few human labels with many judge labels and still gives an honest interval.
    • Never optimise against the judge you test with. The system learns to please the judge, not the customer (reward hacking, R3.6). Hold out a human-graded set the optimisation never touches.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • An overlap score presented as quality

      "ROUGE-L is 0.52, up from 0.47." Nobody has shown those points mean better answers.

      Spot itRead ten output pairs whose scores differ by five points. Ask whether the metric was ever correlated with human ratings on this task.
    • Single reference for an open task

      Correct paraphrases are penalised and the model learns to imitate the reference's phrasing.

      Spot itCount references per question. One, on an open task, is a red flag.
    • Unreported metric settings

      BLEU with different tokenisation or smoothing is not comparable.

      Spot itLook for the library and signature (for example a SacreBLEU signature string).
    • Judge never validated against humans

      "GPT-graded accuracy: 92%." Graded by what, checked how?

      Spot itAsk for the number of human-labelled items, the agreement with an interval, and the kappa.
    • Agreement quoted without the base rate

      "The judge agrees 90% of the time" when 90% of answers pass anyway.

      Spot itAsk what an always-pass judge would score, and for the false-pass rate.
    • Pairwise judging in a single order

      Position bias shifts win rates by points, enough to flip a ranking of two close models.

      Spot itAsk whether both orders were run. If the win rate moves on swap, the judge is not measuring quality alone.
    • Judge from the same family as the system

      Relatives grade generously.

      Spot itCompare judge and generator models. Re-score with a judge from another provider.
    • Longer is rated better

      A new prompt makes answers 40% longer and scores rise. Customers just read more.

      Spot itPlot judge score against length. If they track, control for length or add a conciseness criterion.
    • Optimising against the judge you test with

      Prompts are tuned until the score peaks, so the score is no longer evidence.

      Spot itAsk for a human-graded set no tuning ever saw.
    • Judge approves what it cannot verify

      It passes a confident, wrong figure because it does not know the policy.

      Spot itCheck the judge saw the source or gold answer. Seed known-wrong answers and see if they pass.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Reference: "the card was blocked" (4 words). Output: "the card was not blocked" (5 words). Compute ROUGE-1 precision, recall and F. What does it tell you?

    All 4 reference words appear in the output. Precision = 4/5 = 0.80, recall = 4/4 = 1.00, F = 2 × 0.8 × 1.0 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 0.89. A score near 0.9 for the opposite meaning: overlap cannot see negation.

    2. BLEU is precision-based and ROUGE recall-based. Why does BLEU need a brevity penalty?

    Precision alone rewards short outputs: one word that is in the reference scores 100%. The penalty cuts the score when the output is shorter than the reference, supplying the missing recall-like pressure.

    3. Name two tasks where an exact or deterministic metric beats both overlap and an LLM judge.

    Extracting an amount, date or IBAN (exact or normalised match), and text-to-SQL or code (execute and compare results). With one verifiable right answer, check it directly.

    4. In pairwise judging, A beats B when shown first, and B beats A when shown second. What does this tell you, and what do you report?

    Position bias dominates on that pair, so it is effectively a tie. Run both orders, count a win only if it holds in both, and report the share of order-dependent verdicts as a measure of the judge's bias.

    5. Swapping order did not improve your judge's accuracy at all. What other bias could be responsible?

    Verbosity (or style) bias. It attaches to the answer, not its position, so swapping cannot cancel it. Test by padding one answer, or by checking whether score tracks length at fixed quality. Mitigate with the rubric, length control, or a different judge.

    6. A judge agrees with humans on 92 of 100 items. Humans passed 90 of 100. How impressive is this?

    An always-pass judge would agree on 90%. Chance agreement is 0.9² + 0.1² = 0.82, so κ = (0.92 − 0.82) ÷ 0.18 ≈ 0.56. The Wilson interval on 92/100 is roughly 85% to 96%. Moderately better than a lazy judge, with wide uncertainty. Check the false-pass rate before relying on it.

    7. Your team tunes the prompt to maximise the judge score and reports a rise from 3.9 to 4.5. What is missing?

    Evidence the rise is real. Tuning against the judge exploits its biases (length, style). Re-score a held-out human-graded set the tuning never saw, with a second judge from a different family, and check whether answers simply got longer.
