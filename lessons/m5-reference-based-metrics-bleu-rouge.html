    Grading Generated Text: Overlap Metrics and LLM Judges

    Part 1

    What grading generated text means

    In one sentenceAn LLM writes free text, so there is no single right answer to tick off. You need a grader that turns "is this answer good?" into a number: either a formula that compares it with a model answer, or another LLM acting as marker.

    Our running example: a bank assistant is asked "Why is my card blocked?" Two good answers can share almost no words, and a good grader must pass both.

    There are two families of grader:

    • Overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore). A human writes a reference answer. A formula scores how close the output is to it. Cheap, fast and repeatable.
    • LLM judge. A strong model reads the answer, plus a marking rubric, and grades it. Slower and costs money, but it reads meaning.

    Either way, the score only stands in for "would a careful human call this good?".

    Part 2

    Overlap metrics: counting shared words

    In one sentenceAn overlap metric counts how much of the model answer's wording matches the reference. It measures resemblance, not correctness.

    The three you will meet

    • ROUGE (used for summaries) asks how much of the reference's wording the output covers.
    • BLEU (used for translation) asks how much of the output's wording appears in the reference, and penalises very short outputs.
    • BERTScore compares meaning rather than exact words, by turning words into lists of numbers (embeddings) and checking they point the same way. "Frozen" and "blocked" score close.

    Why they mislead

    The reference is "card was blocked after three wrong PIN attempts". Look at two outputs:

    The wrong answer scores higher. Counting words cannot see "not", and cannot see an invented figure such as a made-up AED 50,000 limit. Honest paraphrases lose points just for using different words.

    How to read the numbers

    • ROUGE: 0 means no shared words, 1 means full coverage. Going from 0.40 to 0.46 means more reference phrases appear. It does not mean the answers are 6 points more correct.
    • BLEU: often quoted from 0 to 100. Only compare scores that used the same settings and test set. It is meaningful over a whole test set, rarely for one sentence.
    • BERTScore: values bunch near the top, so the gap between two systems matters more than the level. It still misses negation and numbers.
    • Arabic: "بطاقتك" (your card) and "البطاقة" (the card) share a root but not a whole word, so exact matching is harsh. In the BALSAM study, BLEU and ROUGE tracked human ratings poorly on Arabic, and LLM judges best.

    Where they still work: as a cheap alarm. If a prompt change drops ROUGE-L by ten points on 500 stable summaries, something moved and a human should look. They are a bad way to report "quality" to an executive.

    Part 3

    LLM judges: a model as marker

    In one sentenceAn LLM judge is a strong model given the question, the answer and a mark scheme, which it applies the way a teacher marks an essay.

    Because it reads for meaning, it spots "not blocked" and the invented limit. In a well-known study, a strong judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time on open chat, about as often as two humans agree with each other (Zheng et al., 2023).

    How to set one up

    1. Choose the format. Pairwise ("which of A and B is better?"), reference-guided (the judge also sees a gold answer), or a yes/no checklist.
    2. Write the rubric. Name the criteria and define each score level with an example. Fix the output format.
    3. Give it the evidence. A source passage or gold answer makes the judge far more reliable on facts.
    4. Check it against humans (below) before trusting it.

    Yes/no checklists ("does it state the 60-day limit?") are easier to agree on than a 1-to-5 scale, which bunches near the top. A 60% pairwise win rate means A won 60 of 100 matchups.

    The biases to know

    BiasWhat happensFix
    PositionFavours the first (or second) answer shownRun both orders. Count a win only if it holds in both.
    LengthLonger answers win, even if paddedRubric that penalises padding. Compare at matched length.
    Self-preferenceRates its own model family higherUse a judge from another family, or several.
    Weak at checkingOn hard facts or maths, confident style beats correctnessGive it the gold answer or source. Run code where possible.

    Swapping order cures position bias but not length bias, because length travels with the answer. Find biases by testing: swap the order, pad one answer, hide the model name.

    Part 4

    How to know if you can trust the judge

    Check the judge against a trusted one, like a thermometer. Have humans grade 100 to 300 outputs blind, then compare.

    1. Humans label the outputs (pass or fail).
    2. The judge labels the same outputs.
    3. Report the share they agree on, with an interval, because the sample is finite.
    4. Also report a chance-corrected figure, Cohen's kappa.

    Why raw agreement is not enough

    If 85% of answers pass anyway, a lazy judge that always says "pass" agrees 85% of the time. Kappa asks how much of the possible improvement over guessing the judge achieves.

    Kappa 0
    No better than chance. Kappa 1 is perfect agreement.
    Below 0.4 / 0.6 to 0.8 / above 0.8
    Weak / good / strong, as a rule of thumb.
    90% agreement when 85% of answers pass
    Sounds excellent, but kappa is only about 0.6, because most of that agreement was free.
    "90% agreement, 95% interval 80% to 95%"
    The true agreement could plausibly be anywhere in that range. Four times as many labels halves the width.
    • Re-check whenever the judge, rubric or system changes.
    • Never tune against your test judge. The system learns to please the judge, not the customer. Keep a human-graded set the tuning never touches.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    TaskBest graderWhy
    Extract an IBAN, amount or dateExact matchOne right answer. No fuzzy scores for numbers.
    Code or SQLRun it and compare resultsBehaviour matters, not text.
    TranslationchrF, COMET or BLEURT, plus human spot checksCharacter-based and learned metrics suit Arabic better than BLEU.
    Summaries, open answersLLM judge, plus human samplingMany valid wordings. Faithfulness and tone matter.
    • Overlap metrics: strong at speed and repeatability, weak at meaning and negation.
    • LLM judges: strong at paraphrase and tone, weak at cost, bias and hard facts.
    Show the formula

    Cohen's kappa = (observed agreement − chance agreement) ÷ (1 − chance agreement). For 90% observed and 85% passing, chance agreement is 0.85² + 0.15² = 0.745, so kappa = (0.90 − 0.745) ÷ 0.255 ≈ 0.6.

    In banking, a customer-facing answer that is wrong but fluent is worse than one that is clumsy but right, so never let an unvalidated score decide whether an assistant ships.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reporting an overlap score as quality

      "ROUGE-L rose from 0.47 to 0.52" proves nothing until someone has checked that the metric tracks human ratings on this task.

    • An unvalidated judge

      "GPT-graded accuracy: 92%" says nothing without the number of human labels, the agreement with an interval and the kappa.

    • Judging pairs in one order

      Position bias can shift win rates by enough to flip a ranking of two close models. If the win rate changes when you swap, the judge is not measuring quality alone.

    • Comparing BLEU scores with different settings

      Different tokenisation or smoothing makes scores incomparable. Always record the settings.

    1. Reference "the card was blocked", output "the card was not blocked". How will ROUGE-1 score it?

    Very high, because every reference word appears. The meaning is opposite, so overlap cannot see negation.

    2. Why does BLEU need a brevity penalty?

    Precision alone rewards short outputs: one word that is in the reference scores 100%. The penalty cuts the score when the output is shorter than the reference.

    3. A beats B when shown first, and B beats A when shown second. What does that mean?

    Position bias dominates, so it is effectively a tie. Run both orders and count a win only if it holds in both.

    4. Swapping the order did not improve your judge at all. What other bias could be at work?

    Length bias. It attaches to the answer, not its position. Test it by padding one answer.

    5. A judge agrees with humans on 90% of items, and 92% of items pass. Is it good?

    Not shown. Always saying "pass" would already agree about 92% of the time. Check kappa and how many true failures the judge catches.
