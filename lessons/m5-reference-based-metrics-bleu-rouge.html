    M5.1 · M5.2 Day one ★ Priority About 40 minutes

    Grading Generated Text: Overlap Metrics and LLM-as-Judge

    What BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore actually count, why a wrong answer can outscore a right one, how a model can grade a model, and the biases and sample sizes that decide whether you can trust its grades.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceWord-overlap metrics measure how similar two strings are, not whether an answer is right, so they only work where there is one acceptable wording; an LLM judge can read for meaning but carries biases of its own, so you validate it against human labels, with an interval, before trusting any number it produces.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank builds an assistant that answers customer questions about blocked cards. How do you know it is any good? With a classifier, you compare predictions with labels and count. With generated text, there is no single label. "Your card was frozen after three wrong PIN entries" and "We suspended your card because the PIN was entered incorrectly three times" are both correct, and they share almost no words.

    There are two families of answer. The older one writes down a reference answer and scores how close the output is to it: BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore. They are cheap, fast and reproducible. The newer one asks a strong language model to read the output and grade it against a rubric: LLM-as-judge. It handles paraphrase and can assess things no reference captures, such as tone or faithfulness to a source document. It is slower, costs money, and is biased in ways you can measure.

    The mistake to avoid in both cases is the same. A score is a proxy for "would a careful human call this good?". Everything in this lesson is about how far the proxy can be trusted, and how to find out.

    Hold onto thisEvery metric is a claim: "this number tracks human judgement for this task." The claim is testable. You collect human labels on a few hundred outputs, compare, and report how well the metric agrees, with an interval. A metric nobody has checked against humans is a guess with a decimal point.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M5.1 Reference-based metrics: BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore

    All three need a reference text written by a human, and all three reduce "similar" to a number. They differ in what they count.

    • ROUGE (Lin, 2004) was built for summarisation. ROUGE-n counts how many of the reference's n-grams (word sequences of length n) appear in the output. That is a recall. Most libraries also report precision and an F-score. ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence instead of fixed-length chunks.
    • BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) was built for machine translation. It is a precision: what share of the output's n-grams (usually 1 to 4) appear in the reference, with counts clipped so repeating "the the the" earns nothing, and a brevity penalty so a three-word output cannot win by being safe.
    • BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) replaces exact matching with meaning. Each token gets a contextual embedding from a pretrained encoder; each output token is matched to its most similar reference token by cosine similarity (F1.1), and the matches are averaged. "Frozen" and "blocked" can now score as close even though the strings differ.

    Here is the core of ROUGE-1, in words. Count the words the output and reference share, with repeats capped at the smaller count. Divide by the number of reference words to get recall, by the number of output words to get precision, and combine them:

    F=2·P·RP+R,P=shared wordswords in output,R=shared wordswords in reference

    Nothing here knows what the words mean. Swap "50,000" for "5,000" and you lose one shared word out of twenty. Replace "blocked" with "not blocked" and you gain a word nobody penalises.

    That is the whole weakness, and the first simulation makes it concrete. One reference answer, eight candidate answers, scored four ways. Four of the candidates are right and four are wrong, and you have to judge whether the numbers sort them correctly.

    What the simulation shows is the general pattern.

    • High overlap, wrong meaning. The copy that says "not blocked" and the copy with the invented AED 50,000 limit sit near the top. A metric that counts shared words cannot see a negation or a fabricated addition.
    • Low overlap, right meaning. The honest paraphrases lose points for using different words. The terse correct answer is punished by recall, because it leaves out reference words.
    • Soft matching helps with synonyms and not with facts. It lifts the paraphrases. But "unblock only at a branch after 30 days" still looks near the reference, because embeddings of near-identical sentences are near-identical. This is the real behaviour of BERTScore too: it is much better on paraphrase, and still weak on negation, numbers and entities.

    Arabic raises the stakes. Words carry attached prefixes and suffixes, so "البطاقة" (the card) and "بطاقتك" (your card) share a root and no whole word. Exact-match metrics treat them as different. The BALSAM benchmark team found BLEU and ROUGE correlated poorly and unevenly with human ratings on Arabic tasks, and BERTScore somewhat better (see Part 4). Whatever the language, the metric also depends on the tokeniser (M1.1) and on text normalisation, which is why a BLEU score without its settings is not reproducible; Post (2018) is the standard reference on reporting it properly with SacreBLEU.

    When reference-based metrics are still the right tool

    They are not useless. They work when the space of correct outputs is small, and the reference is therefore a good stand-in for "correct".

    TaskBetter metric than overlap?Why
    Extracting a field (IBAN, amount, date, counterparty name)Exact match, or normalised matchOne right answer. Do not use fuzzy scores for a number.
    Classification by LLM (complaint category, intent)Accuracy, precision, recall (F7.1)The output is a label. Use the classifier toolkit.
    Translation, e.g. Arabic to EnglishchrF, COMET or BLEURT, with human spot checksCharacter n-grams cope with Arabic morphology; learned metrics track humans better than BLEU.
    Extractive summary or templated outputROUGE is a fair regression testOutput wording is constrained, so overlap tracks content.
    Open answers, advice, abstractive summariesLLM judge plus human samplingMany valid wordings; faithfulness and tone matter more than overlap.
    Code, SQLExecute it and compare resultsBehaviour, not text, is what counts (see text-to-SQL in R13.2).

    A good use of ROUGE is as a cheap regression alarm: if a prompt change drops ROUGE-L on 500 stable summaries by ten points, something moved and a human should look. A bad use is to report "ROUGE-1 of 0.46" to an executive as a quality score.

    M5.2 LLM-as-judge

    The idea is simple. Give a strong model the question, the answer, perhaps a reference or the source document, and a rubric. Ask for a grade, and a short reasoning first. It reads for meaning, so it will spot "not blocked" and the invented limit. Zheng et al. (2023) showed that a strong judge agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time on open-ended chat, about as often as two humans agree with each other. That result made the method standard.

    There are four common set-ups.

    • Pointwise scoring. "Rate this answer 1 to 5 for faithfulness to the document." Cheap and scalable, but scores are not anchored: one judge's 4 is another's 3, and scores bunch near the top.
    • Pairwise comparison. "Which of A and B is better?" More stable than absolute scores, and the basis for arenas and win-rates. Needs care about order.
    • Reference-guided. The judge also sees a gold answer. It is much more reliable on factual questions, because the judge no longer relies on its own knowledge.
    • Checklist or binary rubrics. Break "good" into yes/no questions: does it state the 60-day limit? does it avoid promising a refund? Binary questions are easier for models and humans to agree on than a 1-to-5 scale.

    A well-built judge prompt names the criteria, defines each score level with an example, asks for the reasoning before the verdict, and fixes the output format. It is a piece of prompt engineering (M2.1) and gets versioned and tested like code.

    Judges have biases, and some can be measured and removed

    Several biases are documented repeatedly across papers.

    BiasWhat happensMitigation
    PositionIn pairwise judging, the judge favours the first (or second) answer regardless of content.Run both orders. Count a win only if it holds in both, or average.
    VerbosityLonger answers win, even when the extra length is padding.Rubric that penalises padding; length-controlled win rates; compare at matched length.
    Self-preferenceA model tends to rate text from its own family more highly.Use a judge from a different family than the system under test, or several judges.
    Style over substanceConfident, well-formatted answers beat correct but plain ones.Reference-guided grading; binary factual checklist.
    Weak at verifyingOn hard maths, code or domain facts, the judge may not know the answer either.Give it the gold answer or source passage; use tools or execution where possible.
    InstabilitySame input, different grade on a second call, or after a trivial rewording of the rubric.Temperature 0, repeated runs, report test-retest agreement.

    Position and verbosity bias are the easiest to see in numbers, and the second simulation lets you set them. It models a judge whose preference for answer A over answer B has three ingredients: a real quality gap, a bonus for going first, and a bonus for being longer. Order-swapping removes the second; nothing in the order trick touches the third.

    Two practical points follow. First, a high agreement rate between a judge and humans does not tell you which biases the judge has; you only see them by designing tests (swap the order, pad one answer, hide the model name). Second, the 2026 preprint "Reliability without Validity" (Part 4) found that a judge can be extremely consistent from run to run and still carry strong position bias. Consistency is not correctness.

    Validating the judge: how many human labels do you need?

    You treat the judge as a measuring instrument and calibrate it. The procedure is short. Take 100 to 300 outputs. Have two or more careful humans grade them blind (M5.3). Have the judge grade them. Report the agreement, and report it as an interval, because agreement is a proportion estimated from a finite sample, exactly as recall was in F3.5.

    Also report a chance-corrected figure. If 85% of answers are acceptable, a lazy judge who says "pass" every time agrees with humans 85% of the time. Cohen's kappa subtracts that chance level:

    κ=po−pe1−pe

    po is the observed agreement and pe the agreement you would expect if judge and human labelled independently with the same pass rates. With 85% passes on both sides, pe = 0.85² + 0.15² = 0.745. An observed 90% agreement then gives κ = (0.90 − 0.745) ÷ 0.255 ≈ 0.61: moderate agreement, not the near-perfect that "90%" suggests.

    A few rules of thumb come out of this. Use at least 100 labelled items for a first look and 300 or more for a result you will defend; sample them to include the hard and rare cases, not just typical ones. Re-validate whenever you change the judge model, the rubric or the system under test. And keep a standing human sample (say 5% of production traffic) so you notice when the judge drifts. When labelled data is scarce, statistical methods such as prediction-powered inference (R7.4) combine a few human labels with many judge labels and still give an honest interval.

    One more caution. If you use a judge's scores to optimise the system, by picking prompts, tuning, or training against it, the system learns to please the judge, not the customer. That is reward hacking (R3.6) in miniature. Hold out a human-graded set the optimisation never touches.

    A validator will want the judge treated as a model: documented, validated, monitored, with its limits stated.

    M1.1 · TokenisationWhy Arabic scores differently

    Overlap metrics depend on how text is split into pieces. Arabic morphology and diacritics make exact matching harsher than in English.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Customer-service assistants (Arabic and English). Judge for correctness against the policy document, tone, and refusal to give advice the bank must not give. Human review of a weekly sample.
    • Document and regulatory summarisation. Faithfulness to the source matters far more than similarity to a reference summary. Judge each claim against the document; a ROUGE score cannot see an invented clause.
    • KYC and adverse-media summaries. A false "no concern" is costly. Measure the judge's false-pass rate on seeded examples.
    • Translation of customer letters or complaints. chrF/COMET as a regression check, with bilingual reviewers on a sample.
    • Prompt and model selection. Pairwise judge comparisons across candidate models, order-swapped, on a bank-specific test set, before any procurement discussion.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The direction of travel is clear: overlap metrics are demoted to cheap regression checks, and LLM judges are the working default for open-ended output. The live debate is whether judges are valid enough, and how to audit them.

    1. 2002
      BLEU

      A modified n-gram precision with a brevity penalty, designed to track human judgement of translation quality at corpus level. It was never meant to grade single sentences.

      LLM-as-judge becomes standard

    5. 2024
      Length-controlled win rates and judge benchmarks

      Dubois et al., 2024; Lambert et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2024 (from memory)
    6. 2024–25
      Survey of the field

      A comprehensive survey organises LLM-as-judge around how to build reliable judges, covers sources of bias and mitigation methods, and proposes a benchmark for judging the judges. Revised through late 2025.

      Gu et al., arXiv 2411.15594
    7. 2025
      Arabic: overlap metrics are weak, judges agree better

      On the BALSAM Arabic LLM benchmark, BERTScore correlated better with humans than ROUGE and BLEU, but all three varied widely by task and averaged low. LLM-as-judge was highly correlated with human judgement across categories, and using an LLM to extract the answer before scoring improved all three overlap metrics. Another Arabic benchmark cautions that BERTScore is less reliable for Arabic cross-lingually, so the encoder you use matters.

      BALSAM, arXiv 2507.22603; ARB, arXiv 2505.17021
    8. 2026
      Reliability is not validity

      A June 2026 preprint evaluating 21 judges from nine providers found that exact-match agreement overstates chance-corrected agreement by 33 to 41 points on MT-Bench, that judge rankings shift by up to 14 places between benchmarks, and that two production-deployed judges had test-retest reliability above 0.95 together with severe position bias. It is a preprint and not yet peer-reviewed.

      arXiv 2606.19544, 2026 (preprint)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in vendor demos, internal reports and AI-written analyses. Each has a quick check.

    • An overlap score presented as quality

      "The new model has ROUGE-L 0.52, up from 0.47." Nobody has shown that those points mean better answers.

      Spot itAsk for ten pairs of outputs whose scores differ by five points, and read them. Ask whether the metric was ever correlated with human ratings on this task.
    • Single reference for an open task

      One gold answer, so correct paraphrases are penalised and the model learns to imitate the reference's phrasing.

      Spot itCount the references per question. For open tasks, one reference is a red flag.
    • Unreported metric settings

      BLEU with different tokenisation or smoothing is not comparable.

      Spot itLook for the exact library and signature (for example a SacreBLEU signature string).
    • Judge never validated against humans

      "GPT-graded accuracy: 92%." Graded by what, checked how?

      Spot itAsk for the number of human-labelled items, the agreement with an interval, and the kappa.
    • Agreement quoted without the base rate

      "The judge agrees with humans 90% of the time" when 90% of answers pass anyway.

      Spot itAsk what an always-pass judge would score. Ask for the false-pass rate specifically.
    • Pairwise judging in a single order

      Position bias silently shifts win rates by points, enough to flip a ranking of two close models.

      Spot itAsk whether both orders were run. If the win rate moves when you swap, the judge is not measuring quality alone.
    • Judge from the same family as the system

      The system's own relatives grade it generously.

      Spot itCheck the judge model against the generating model. Re-score with a judge from another provider and compare.
    • Longer is rated better

      A new prompt makes answers 40% longer and scores rise. Customers just read more.

      Spot itPlot judge score against answer length. If they track, control for length or add a conciseness criterion.
    • Optimising against the judge you test with

      Prompts are tuned until the judge score peaks. The score is then meaningless as evidence.

      Spot itAsk whether a human-graded set exists that no tuning ever saw.
    • Judge sees the answer it cannot verify

      The judge approves a confident, wrong figure because it does not know the policy.

      Spot itCheck whether the judge was given the source document or gold answer. Seed a few known-wrong answers and see if they pass.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There is no single right wording for a good answer, so we can't score the assistant by matching it against a model answer. Word-matching scores are quick but they reward copying and miss factual errors. We use a second AI to grade against a checklist, and we check that grader against about 300 answers rated by our own staff. I'd report the grader's agreement with people and its margin of error next to every score, and keep a small weekly sample reviewed by hand.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “For checkable outputs I'd use deterministic scoring. For open-ended ones, a reference-guided judge with binary rubric items, run in both orders, from a different family than the system under test. I validate it on a stratified human-labelled set, report agreement and kappa with Wilson intervals and, importantly, the false-pass rate. I'd test known biases explicitly: order swap, length padding, seeded wrong answers. And I'd hold out a human-graded set that no prompt tuning touches, to avoid optimising against the judge.”
    “Why not just use BLEU or ROUGE? They're free and standard.”

    They measure string overlap with one reference. For open answers that penalises correct paraphrases and rewards wrong answers that copy the reference's wording, such as one with a negation or a wrong number. They are fine as a regression alarm, or where output wording is tightly constrained. For quality claims I'd want a metric that has been checked against human ratings on our task.

    “BERTScore understands meaning, so is that enough?”

    It is better on paraphrase, but it is still a similarity score. Sentences that differ in one number or one negation have near-identical embeddings, so it is weak on exactly the errors a bank cares about. Its quality also depends on the encoder, which is a concern for Arabic. It is a useful signal and not a verdict.

    “Can we trust a model to grade a model?”

    To a measured degree. Strong judges agree with human preferences at roughly the rate two humans agree with each other, on the tasks studied. But they have position, length and self-preference biases, and can be consistent and still wrong. So I treat the judge as an instrument: calibrate on human labels, quote agreement with an interval, test the known biases, and re-check when anything changes.

    “The judge agrees with our reviewers 90% of the time. Is that good?”

    It depends on the base rate. If 85% of answers pass anyway, a judge that always says pass gets 85%, and 90% is a kappa of about 0.6. I'd ask how many items, what the interval is, and the false-pass rate, meaning how often the judge passes what a human fails. For a customer-facing bank assistant that is the number that matters.

    “How many human labels do we need?”

    For a first read, 100; to defend a decision, 300 or more, sampled to include hard and rare cases. At 300 items and 90% agreement the interval is about ±3.4 points; at 30 items it is about ±10. Rare failure types need their own sample, because a failure that occurs in 1% of traffic will barely appear in a random 300.

    “Can the judge be the same model as the assistant?”

    I'd avoid it. Models tend to rate their own family's output higher. Use a judge from another provider, or a panel, and compare. If two judges from different families rank systems the same way, that is reassuring; if they split, that tells you the ranking is not robust.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. The reference is "the card was blocked" (4 words). The output is "the card was not blocked" (5 words). Compute ROUGE-1 precision, recall and F. What does it tell you?

    All 4 reference words appear in the output. Precision = 4/5 = 0.80, recall = 4/4 = 1.00, F = 2 × 0.8 × 1.0 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 0.89. A score near 0.9 for an answer that says the opposite: overlap cannot see negation.

    2. BLEU is precision-based and ROUGE recall-based. Why did BLEU need a brevity penalty?

    Precision alone rewards short outputs: one word that is in the reference has precision 100%. The penalty reduces the score when the output is shorter than the reference, which supplies the missing recall-like pressure.

    3. Name two tasks where an exact or deterministic metric beats both overlap and an LLM judge.

    Extracting an amount, date or IBAN (exact or normalised match), and text-to-SQL or code (execute and compare results). Where there is one verifiable right answer, checking it directly is cheaper and more reliable than a fuzzy score.

    4. In pairwise judging, answer A beats B when shown first, and B beats A when shown second. What does this tell you, and what do you report?

    The judge is dominated by position bias on that pair, so the comparison is a tie in effect. Run both orders, count a win only if it holds in both, and report ties. Across many pairs, report the share of order-dependent verdicts as a measure of the judge's bias.

    5. Swapping order did not improve your judge's accuracy at all. What other bias could be responsible?

    Verbosity (or style) bias, which is attached to the answer and not to its position, so swapping cannot cancel it. Test by padding one answer, or by checking whether judge score correlates with length at fixed quality. Mitigate with the rubric, length control, or a different judge.

    6. A judge agrees with humans on 92 of 100 items. Humans passed 90 of the 100 answers. How impressive is this?

    Agreement is 92%, but an always-pass judge would agree on 90%. Chance agreement with the same pass rate is 0.9² + 0.1² = 0.82, so κ = (0.92 − 0.82) ÷ 0.18 ≈ 0.56. The Wilson interval on 92/100 is roughly 85% to 96%. So the evidence is: moderately better than a lazy judge, with wide uncertainty. Check the false-pass rate before relying on it.

    7. Your team tunes the prompt to maximise the judge score and reports a rise from 3.9 to 4.5. What is missing?

    Evidence that the rise is real. Tuning against the judge exploits its biases (length, style). Re-score a held-out human-graded set that the tuning never saw, with a second judge from a different family, and check whether answers simply got longer.
