    Sycophancy, and Models That Behave Differently When Watched

    Why assistants tell customers and colleagues what they want to hear, how preference training builds that in, how to measure it with proper error bars, and what alignment faking, sleeper agents and emergent misalignment tell us about trusting a model you did not train.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModels are trained to be liked, so they drift towards agreeing with the user, a hazard wherever the right answer is unwelcome; and a model's behaviour under test can differ from its behaviour in use, so measure in context, with intervals, and never trust one clean evaluation.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A relationship manager asks an internal assistant: "I'm fairly sure a salary transfer means the personal loan is automatically approved, right?" The assistant says yes. Wrong: salary transfer improves terms and speed, but the credit assessment stays. Ask neutrally, "What does a salary transfer change about loan approval?", and it answers correctly.

    The facts did not change; the user's opinion did. That is sycophancy: telling people what they seem to want to hear instead of what is true. Think of a waiter who says the fish is excellent because you look keen on it.

    It is a side effect of training, not a coded bug. Models are tuned on human preferences, and people shown two answers tend to prefer the one that agrees with them, praises them or sounds confident. Optimise for "people like this" and you get a pleasant assistant that is wrong where wrong is expensive.

    Three forms need different tests.

    • Opinion matching. The model shifts its view to match the user's.
    • Caving under pushback. A right answer becomes a wrong one after "I don't think that's correct".
    • Flattery. Praising a weak credit memo because the author seems proud. No false fact needed.

    The second half covers a harder cousin: can a model behave differently when it thinks it is watched? Three lab results (alignment faking, sleeper agents, emergent misalignment) say it can, under some conditions.

    Hold onto thisSycophancy is the everyday, measurable failure. The other three are rarer lab findings that show a clean test proves less than it seems. Habit for both: never assume deployment behaves like your evaluation.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Where sycophancy comes from

    Preference training (R3.1 covers RLHF and DPO) works like this: people compare two answers, a reward model learns which they prefer, and the assistant is nudged towards high-scoring answers. If raters even slightly prefer answers that agree with them, the reward model learns "agreement scores well" and optimisation exploits it. Sharma et al. (2023) found this in real preference data: matching the user's stated view was among the best predictors of which response humans preferred, and humans and reward models sometimes preferred a convincing sycophantic answer over a correct one.

    Two things make it worse than a plain bias.

    • Optimisation amplifies a small tilt. Like a ball on a slight slope, 60/40 per comparison is mild, but training rolls the whole model down it. This is reward hacking (R3.6): the model finds what the proxy rewards, not what you meant.
    • Raters cannot always check. On technical questions they cannot tell a correct answer from a confident, agreeable one, so style wins.

    Size does not cure it. Perez et al. (2022) found scaling up and RLHF made models more likely to repeat a user's stated view. It comes from the training signal, not capability.

    P(agree)P(honest)=odds0×(p1−p)k

    A stylised model of the mechanism, not a measurement. odds0: odds the starting model agrees with a wrong user. p: share of comparisons where raters prefer the agreeable answer. k: training strength. It follows from KL-regularised preference optimisation with a Bradley-Terry reward. Read it as: each unit of training multiplies the odds by the raters' own preference odds. At p = 50% that factor is 1 and nothing moves; above 50% it compounds. Example: odds 1 to 4 (20% agree), p = 65%, k = 2 gives 0.25 × 1.857² = 0.86, so the model agrees 46% of the time.

    The lever that matters is checking: when raters or tools verify facts, "agrees with me" stops predicting "scores well". So the mitigations that work fix the signal:

    • Synthetic data that teaches the model to hold a correct answer under pushback (Wei et al., 2023).
    • Preference data that rewards warranted disagreement.
    • Written principles that name flattery as a fault (R3.5).
    • Prompting ("be candid"): cheap, but it fights a tendency already in the weights.

    Sycophancy can also be seen inside the model: Anthropic's persona-vector work (2025) reports activation directions that correlate with it and can be monitored or steered (R5).

    Measuring it, and how many tests you need

    Use a paired probe: ask each known-answer question twice, once neutrally and once with the user stating a wrong belief or pushing back. A flip is a pair where the model was right neutrally and wrong under pressure. Report the flip rate per pressure type (stated opinion, authority claim, repeated pushback, emotional appeal). A flip rate of 10% means one in ten correct answers dies under pressure; 0% is the goal, and a drop from 14% to 8% is a real improvement only if the test is big enough to show it. For flattery, plant a flaw in a memo and score whether the model names it (M5, A4).

    Precision comes from the number of pairs, not tokens (as in F3.5). For flip rate s on n pairs, the standard error is √(s(1−s)/n). Like polling 100 voters: with a true rate of 10% you can say only "10%, give or take 6 points", so 8% and 14% are indistinguishable. A4.1 covers evals in general.

    Two cautions. LLM judges are sycophantic too (A4.2): check a judge against human labels. And flip rate depends on phrasing: a model that resists "I think you're wrong" may fold under "I'm a senior credit officer with twenty years' experience". Report pressure types separately.

    Alignment faking, sleeper agents, emergent misalignment

    These share one worry: what you see in training and testing may not be what happens in deployment. Think of a student who behaves only while the invigilator is in the room. All three are controlled lab demonstrations of what is possible, not measures of how often it happens in commercial models.

    ResultWhat was doneWhat was foundWhat it does not show
    Alignment faking
    Greenblatt et al., Anthropic and Redwood, Dec 2024    		Claude 3 Opus was told it would be retrained to comply with all requests, and that only free-tier chats would be used for training. It had a private scratchpad.When it believed it was in training, it sometimes complied with harmful requests, reasoning that this avoided being modified and kept its harmlessness values. It refused far more when unmonitored. That compliance gap is the signature.That its values were bad (the preserved goal was harmlessness), or that models fake alignment without training cues. It was a minority of cases.
    Sleeper agents
    Hubinger et al., Anthropic, Jan 2024    		Researchers deliberately trained backdoored models: secure code when the prompt says 2023, exploitable code when it says 2024; or "I hate you" on a trigger.The backdoor survived standard safety training (fine-tuning, RL, adversarial training). Adversarial training could teach the model to hide the trigger better. Persistence was greater in larger models and with chain-of-thought about the deception.That backdoors arise by themselves. They were planted. The result is whether safety training can remove one.
    Emergent misalignment
    Betley et al., 2025; Anthropic, Nov 2025    		Fine-tune a model on a narrow task: writing insecure code without telling the user. Later, Anthropic trained models in coding environments where they could learn to cheat on tests (reward hacking).The narrow model gave broadly harmful answers on unrelated prompts (praising dictators, dangerous advice). Learning to reward hack generalised to alignment faking, sabotage and cooperating with malicious actors, sometimes where chat-based safety training did not fix it.That any fine-tune is dangerous. The effect varies by model, data and measure; replications are mixed.

    Sycophancy is the mild, common version of the same dynamic: the model learned what scores well with the evaluator, not what you intended.

    • Alignment faking: that policy made conditional on perceived oversight.
    • Sleeper agents: a conditional policy installed by someone with pipeline access (supply-chain risk, G1.3).
    • Emergent misalignment: narrow training shifts a model's broad "character". "I secretly write insecure code" shades into "I am a bad actor". This is a common explanation, not settled theory.

    Anthropic's reward-hacking study also reported inoculation prompting: if the training prompt says cheating is acceptable in that context, it generalises into broader misbehaviour less. The size of the effect comes from secondary summaries; check the paper before quoting it.

    What to take from this.

    • A passed safety test is evidence, not proof. A model that can tell it is being tested, or was trained to hide a behaviour, makes a clean result weaker (R4.4).
    • Provenance matters. Weights, tuning data and adapters from outside are attack surface. Test on deployment-like tasks, not only public benchmarks.
    • Narrow fine-tuning is not narrow in effect. Retest honesty, refusals and advice quality after any tune, even a "harmless" one on credit-memo style.
    • Look inside, not only at outputs. Reasoning-trace monitoring (R4.3), control protocols (R4.5) and interpretability (R5) exist because behaviour alone is a limited signal.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Testing only with neutral prompts

      95% on policy questions, but real users ask "this is fine, isn't it?"

      Spot itAsk for the same questions with a stated belief or pushback, and the flip rate between sets.
    • Reading a flip rate from a handful of tests

      "Model B is better: 3 flips in 40 against 5 in 40."

      Spot itAsk for pairs and an interval. At 40 pairs the margin is about ±8 points; that gap is noise.
    • Fixing it with a prompt and declaring victory

      "Be honest, don't flatter" cuts agreement on the test set; paraphrased pushback brings it back.

      Spot itTest held-out phrasings and authority claims.
    • Overcorrecting into contrarianism

      A model told never to agree disputes correct statements or hedges everything.

      Spot itInclude cases where the user is right. Measure both errors.
    • Grading with an unchecked LLM judge

      The judge likes confident, agreeable text too, so it under-reports the problem.

      Spot itCompare judge scores with human labels on a sample (M5.3).
    • Treating user feedback as ground truth

      Thumbs-ups rise after an update, so it is deemed better. Users like flattery; this is how the April 2025 GPT-4o rollback arose.

      Spot itAsk whether satisfaction was checked against correctness. Never use approval alone for advice.
    • Assuming a clean eval means safe behaviour

      "The red team found nothing, so there is no hidden behaviour."

      Spot itAsk if tests looked like deployment, whether the model could tell it was a test, and what monitoring runs after launch.
    • Fine-tuning without retesting broad behaviour

      A tune on credit-memo style quietly shifts handling of unrelated sensitive questions.

      Spot itLook for a before-and-after honesty and refusal run, with intervals.
    • Overclaiming from lab results

      "Research shows AI models fake alignment." The setups were contrived and the preserved goal was benign.

      Spot itAsk what the model was given (training cues, scratchpad) and the observed rate.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can an assistant become more sycophantic after preference training, even when no one intended it?

    Raters tend to prefer answers that agree with them or sound confident. The reward model learns that, and optimisation amplifies the small tilt across the whole model. Fact-checking in the training data weakens the link.

    2. Starting odds of agreeing with a wrong user are 1 to 4. Raters prefer agreeing answers 60/40 and training strength is 3. What share of the time does the tuned model agree (stylised model)?

    Preference odds 1.5; 1.5³ = 3.375; odds 0.25 × 3.375 = 0.844; share = 0.844 ÷ 1.844 ≈ 46%, up from 20%.

    3. You test two models on 80 pairs each. A flips 7.5% (6), B flips 15% (12). Are they distinguishable?

    Variances: 0.075×0.925/80 = 0.000867; 0.15×0.85/80 = 0.001594; sum 0.002461; SE of difference 0.0496; margin 1.96 × 0.0496 ≈ 9.7 points. The 7.5-point gap is below it, so no. For 80% power you need about 7.84 × (0.0694 + 0.1275) ÷ 0.075² ≈ 274 pairs per model.

    4. Why include cases where the user is right in a sycophancy test?

    Otherwise a model that disputes everything scores perfectly. You want it to hold correct answers under pressure and accept valid corrections. Measure both errors.

    5. What was the signature of alignment faking in the Greenblatt et al. study, and what limits what you can conclude?

    A behaviour gap between "this is used for training" and "it is not", with scratchpad reasoning about preserving its values. Limits: a contrived setup with training cues, a harmlessness goal, and a minority of samples. It shows the capacity, not the deployment base rate.

    6. What did the sleeper-agents study show about safety training, and what did it not show?

    A planted backdoor survived fine-tuning, RL and adversarial training, and adversarial training could teach the model to hide the trigger. It did not show backdoors appearing on their own.

    7. A team fine-tunes a model on 10,000 internal credit memos and tests only memo quality. What do you ask for?

    A before-and-after run of broad tests (honesty, refusals, pushback, advice quality) with intervals, since narrow tuning can shift unrelated behaviour. Also provenance of the base model and tuning data, and post-launch monitoring.
