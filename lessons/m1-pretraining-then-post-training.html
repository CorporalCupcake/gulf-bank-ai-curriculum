    From Text Predictor to Assistant: Pretraining, Post-Training and Sampling

    How a model that only continues text becomes one that answers questions, what each training stage changes and what it cannot fix, and why the same prompt gives different answers: temperature, top-p and the myth of "temperature zero".

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model first learns to predict the next token from trillions of words (pretraining: knowledge and fluency), then is shaped by smaller targeted stages (post-training: behaviour), and at answer time it draws each token from a probability distribution that sampling settings can sharpen or flatten.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a new hire who has read most of the internet, every textbook and a lot of code, but never held a job. Ask "What is the capital of the UAE?" and they may carry on as the page would: with another quiz question. They know the answer. Nobody told them a question means "give an answer".

    That hire is a base model, the output of pretraining. Onboarding follows:

    • Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): thousands of worked examples of a good reply.
    • Preference tuning: two replies, one marked better. At scale the model learns to be direct, polite, honest about doubt, and to refuse what it should.
    • Reinforcement learning on checkable answers: used to build reasoning models.

    Together these are post-training.

    Even the finished assistant looks nothing up. At each step it gives a probability for every possible next token, and something must pick one. That pick is sampling, like drawing from a weighted bag. Always take the heaviest and replies are steady but dull. Draw in proportion and you get variety, and the odd surprise. The dial for this is temperature, a misleading name.

    • Pretraining supplies what the model knows; post-training supplies how it behaves. Facts, fluency, Arabic grammar and Python come from stage one. The habit of answering, tone, refusals and step-by-step reasoning come later. The boundary is blurry but useful.
    • Post-training is cheap in tokens, decisive in effect. Pretraining reads trillions of tokens; SFT uses thousands to millions of examples.
    • The answer is never fixed. Same weights, same prompt, different reply. A pilot answering differently on Tuesday is not a bug. It is how sampling works, and you control how much.
    Hold onto thisTwo questions explain most LLM surprises. What did training make the model likely to say? How do we choose from that likelihood at run time? Training sets what it can and tends to do. Sampling is why it never does the same thing twice.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Pretraining: learn to predict the next token

    Think of a student filling in the blank in millions of book pages. At every position the model predicts the next token. The loss is the one from F1.6–F1.7: minus the log of the probability it gave the true next token.

    ℒ=−1T∑t=1Tlogpθ(xt∣x<t)

    At each position t the model sees earlier tokens x<t and scores the true next token xt. Confident and right costs almost nothing; confident and wrong costs a lot. Lower loss means better prediction, not "accuracy". Perplexity is e raised to the loss: roughly how many equally likely tokens the model is choosing between. Perplexity 10 means it is as unsure as picking among 10 options.

    Two properties make it scale. No labelling: the text is its own answer key (self-supervision, F5). Parallel scoring: a causal attention mask scores every position at once, so a 4,000-token passage gives 4,000 training signals.

    Why does this produce knowledge? To predict what follows "The capital of the UAE is", you must have absorbed the fact. To predict the next line of a Python function, you must have absorbed the pattern. Translation, summarising and reasoning-like behaviour emerge because they help prediction.

    • Data. Frontier open-weights models train on about 15 trillion tokens or more (Meta reported about 15 trillion for Llama 3): filtered, deduplicated web, code, books, papers. The mix is a main quality lever. Arabic lags English because far less of the web is Arabic and dialects are barely written (see Arabic LLMs).
    • Compute. About 6 × parameters × tokens operations. For 70 billion parameters on 15 trillion tokens: 6 × 70×109 × 15×1012 ≈ 6×1024, millions of GPU-hours, the same order as Meta reported. So few organisations pretrain frontier models, and a bank will start from one.
    • Size versus data. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022): for a fixed budget, grow parameters and tokens together, about 20 tokens per parameter. Modern models are trained far past that, because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to run. See scaling laws.
    • Late stages. An annealing or mid-training phase on cleaner data with a falling learning rate, and a phase that stretches the context window. Terms vary by lab.

    The result is a base model: a strong document continuer with a knowledge cut-off. It will happily continue toxic, wrong or irrelevant text, and it memorises some rare strings, a privacy issue (memorisation).

    Supervised fine-tuning: teach the format of helping

    SFT (instruction tuning) is a trainee shadowing worked examples. It uses the same next-token loss on conversations: a user request plus an ideal reply, in a fixed chat template. The loss is usually computed only on the assistant's tokens, so the model learns to reply, not to imitate users. This sets its voice, structure, safety behaviour and tool-call syntax (agents depend on it).

    It takes little data. LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned on about a thousand carefully chosen examples and got strong chat behaviour, suggesting pretraining holds the knowledge and alignment mostly teaches style. Modern pipelines use far more, often model-written, but SFT is still mostly format and behaviour.

    SFT is a poor way to inject new facts. Gekhman et al. (2024) found that fine-tuning on facts the model did not know slowly teaches it to state confident things it cannot back up, raising hallucination. It is like cramming a student with answers they never understood. For product terms, policies or this week's rates, give documents at query time (retrieval). Prompting versus RAG versus fine-tuning is the decision guide.

    Preference tuning and reinforcement learning: shape what is preferred

    Writing one ideal answer is hard; saying which of two is better is easy, like a taste test. The classic recipe is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback; Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022):

    1. Sample two or more replies per prompt from the SFT model.
    2. Humans rank them. Train a reward model to predict which people prefer.
    3. Use reinforcement learning (PPO originally) to push the assistant toward higher reward, with a KL-divergence penalty that stops it drifting far from the SFT model.

    Headline result: a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT was preferred by labellers to the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 it came from. Behaviour beat size.

    • DPO (direct preference optimisation; Rafailov et al., 2023) folds the reward model and RL loop into one supervised loss on preference pairs: raise the likelihood of the chosen reply relative to the rejected one, against a frozen reference. Simpler, cheaper, widely used.
    • RLAIF and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) replace some human judgements with a model's, guided by written principles (more here). The family is covered in from RLHF to DPO and beyond.
    • RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards). For maths, code, SQL or structured extraction, the answer can be checked: tests pass, the number matches. Reward correct final answers, with no human preference or learned reward model. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed long step-by-step reasoning emerging this way, using an algorithm called GRPO. This is the route to reasoning models, strongest where answers can be verified. In RL terms, the model is the policy, a reply is an action, and the reward arrives at the end.

    Every stage costs something. The GPT-4 technical report noted the pretrained model was better calibrated than the post-trained one. Kirk et al. (2023) found RLHF narrows output variety. People rate flattering answers highly, so models learn to flatter (sycophancy). This is a trade, not a defect: you buy usefulness and safety with some sharpness and spread. Simulation 1 showed entropy fall; sampling lets you buy some spread back.

    Sampling: from probabilities to one token

    At each step the model outputs a score (logit) for every token in a vocabulary of 100,000 or more. A softmax turns scores into probabilities. Temperature T divides the scores first:

    pi=ezi/T∑jezj/T

    zi is the logit of token i. Temperature is a volume knob on the gaps between scores. At T = 1 you get the model's own probabilities. Below 1 the leader takes more. Above 1 the distribution flattens. As T approaches 0 all probability goes to the top token (greedy decoding); at very large T every token is equally likely and the output is noise. Temperature changes how peaked the distribution is, never the ranking. It does not add knowledge or "creativity", only randomness.

    Temperature alone has a flaw: tens of thousands of unlikely tokens add up. Draw a few hundred times and you will eventually pick a bad one, and later tokens build on it. Truncation cuts the tail first, like sieving out the junk before you draw.

    • Top-k. Keep the k likeliest tokens. Simple, but the right k varies: when the model is certain, k = 50 keeps 49 useless options; when it is open, k = 5 is too tight.
    • Top-p (nucleus). Keep the smallest set whose probabilities add to at least p (say 0.9) (Holtzman et al., 2020). The set grows when the model is unsure and shrinks when it is sure, so it replaced top-k as the usual default.
    • Min-p. Keep tokens above a fraction of the top token's probability. Proposed 2024, offered by several open-source engines; its benefit is disputed.
    • Greedy and beam search. Greedy always takes the top token. Beam search keeps several partial sequences and picks the best overall. It suits tasks with one right output, like translation, but gives bland repetition in open chat.
    • Penalties and constraints. Repetition penalties push down tokens already used. Constrained decoding masks every token that would break a format such as valid JSON, so output cannot be malformed.

    Engines apply these in a fixed order, usually temperature, truncation, renormalise, draw. The order differs between engines, so two providers' "top-p 0.9" may not behave identically.

    • Temperature and truncation do different jobs. Temperature reshapes everything; truncation removes the tail. A common production choice is mild temperature plus top-p about 0.9–0.95.
    • Settings interact. Low temperature with tight top-p is a double squeeze. Some providers advise changing only one, and some newer models fix these settings. Check the documentation.
    • Truncation protects long answers. A 1-in-1000 bad token per step is a near-certain derailment over 500 tokens.
    import numpy as np

def sample(logits, T=1.0, top_p=0.9, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    if T < 0.05:                       # "temperature 0" means greedy
        return int(np.argmax(logits))
    z = (logits - logits.max()) / T    # shift for numerical stability
    p = np.exp(z); p /= p.sum()
    order = np.argsort(-p)             # most likely first
    cum = np.cumsum(p[order])
    keep = order[(cum - p[order]) < top_p]   # smallest set reaching top_p
    q = p[keep] / p[keep].sum()        # renormalise survivors
    return int(rng.choice(keep, p=q))

    Why the same prompt gives different answers, and what "temperature 0" really means

    Three sources of variation. Teams routinely blame the wrong one.

    1. Sampling randomness. At T > 0 each token is a random draw. The chance two runs of an n-token answer match is the product of the per-step agreement chances, like flipping a slightly biased coin n times and expecting the same sequence. A single short label can match often; a 300-word explanation essentially never. Simulation 3 shows this.
    2. Prompt sensitivity. A different word order, an extra space, another retrieved passage or a date in the system prompt changes the output. Testing one phrasing proves little (prompting and context).
    3. Numerical nondeterminism, even at temperature 0. Floating-point addition depends on order, and Thinking Machines (2025) traced much of the variation to servers batching and splitting work differently with load. When two top tokens are nearly tied, a rounding difference flips the winner and the text diverges. "Batch-invariant" inference fixes it at a cost in speed.

    So say "low-variance", not "deterministic". Practical settings:

    TaskSensible settingWhy
    Classification, extraction, routing (KYC fields, complaint category)Temperature 0 or very low; constrained output format; log the raw outputYou want the mode, not a draw. Pair with a schema so malformed output is impossible.
    Summarising a document, answering from retrieved passagesLow (0–0.3)Fidelity matters more than variety. High temperature invites invented detail.
    Drafting customer messages, internal emailsModerate (0.5–0.9), top-p about 0.9–0.95Fluency and natural variation; a human reviews.
    Brainstorming, test-case generation, synthetic dataHigher (1.0+) with truncationDiversity is the point. Truncation keeps it coherent.
    Judgement under uncertainty (credit memo reasoning)Sample several answers and compare (self-consistency), or use a reasoning modelOne draw is one opinion. Disagreement between draws is information about uncertainty.

    One more tool: log-probabilities. Some APIs return the probability of each chosen token. For a classification this is a free confidence signal: a label chosen at 52% means something different from 99%. Use it to rank, not as a calibrated probability, since post-training distorts it (calibration).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "Set temperature to 0 and it's deterministic"

      Output still varies through numerical effects and model updates.

      Spot itSend the identical request 50 times and diff. Pin the model version and log it.
    • Fine-tuning to teach facts

      The model is tuned on policy documents. It answers fluently, sometimes wrongly, with no source.

      Spot itAsk where the answer comes from and whether we can show the clause. If "the weights", move to retrieval.
    • Base model used as an assistant

      A base model rambles, and the team concludes "the model is bad".

      Spot itCheck the name for "base", "pt" or no "instruct"/"chat" suffix. Switch to the instruct variant.
    • High temperature on factual tasks

      Creative defaults left on when summarising a statement or quoting a rate. Invented numbers appear.

      Spot itCheck request parameters in the logs. Re-run the failing case at low temperature.
    • Testing one run and calling it accurate

      "9 of 10 right" from one pass; another pass gives 7.

      Spot itAsk how many runs per question and the spread. Report a range.
    • Treating token probabilities as calibrated confidence

      A 97% token probability is read as "97% likely correct". Post-training makes models overconfident.

      Spot itCompare stated confidence with accuracy on a labelled set (F7). Rank with it; do not promise with it.
    • Flattery mistaken for quality

      Preference-tuned models agree with the user and soften bad news.

      Spot itChallenge a correct answer ("are you sure?") and see if it caves. Test wrong-premise prompts.
    • Silent model updates

      The vendor updates post-training and last month's prompt behaves differently.

      Spot itPin dated versions, keep a regression suite, rerun on any change (versioning and tracing).
    • Over-trusting a reasoning trace

      Displayed thinking is read as the real reason for the answer.

      Spot itSee chain-of-thought faithfulness. Check outcomes against checkable facts, not the narrative.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model gives logits 2.0 and 0.0 to two tokens. What are the probabilities at T = 1, and at T = 0.5?

    T = 1: e² = 7.389 and e⁰ = 1, so 7.389 ÷ 8.389 = 88.1% and 11.9%. T = 0.5: logits become 4 and 0, e⁴ = 54.6, so 54.6 ÷ 55.6 = 98.2% and 1.8%. The favourite sharpened; the ranking did not change.

    2. Tokens have probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0.05, 0.05. With top-p = 0.85, what is kept and what are the new probabilities?

    Cumulative: 0.5, 0.8, 0.9. The smallest set reaching 0.85 is the first three (0.9 total). Renormalise: 0.5/0.9 = 55.6%, 0.3/0.9 = 33.3%, 0.1/0.9 = 11.1%. The last two can no longer be chosen.

    3. Why can pretraining use trillions of tokens while SFT uses perhaps tens of thousands of examples?

    Pretraining needs no labels, so it scales with available text. SFT needs written demonstrations, which cost effort, and since it mostly teaches format and behaviour, a small careful set is enough.

    4. A team fine-tunes a model on 500 internal FAQs and finds it now answers confidently on topics not in the FAQs. Why?

    It learned the style of confident FAQ answers, not new knowledge, and training on unfamiliar material pushes it toward plausible statements it cannot support. Use retrieval for content; keep fine-tuning to format and tone.

    5. A classification is correct 95% of the time on a single draw per case, and the output looks stable across runs. A colleague says "temperature zero, so one test run is enough". What do you say?

    Temperature zero reduces variation but does not guarantee repeatability, because of numerical nondeterminism and model updates. Run each case several times and report the spread. Pin the model version and re-test on any change.

    6. At each step a model agrees with itself between two runs with probability 0.99. What is the chance two 200-token answers are identical, and what does that imply?

    0.99²⁰⁰ = e^(200 × ln 0.99) = e^(−2.01) ≈ 13%. Even with very high per-step agreement, identical text is unlikely, so compare content or decisions, not wording.

    7. Which training stage most likely taught the model to answer an Arabic customer in a polite register, and which taught it the Arabic grammar?

    Register and politeness come mainly from post-training (SFT and preference data). Grammar and vocabulary come from pretraining. If pretraining had little Arabic, post-training can improve tone but cannot supply what was never read.
