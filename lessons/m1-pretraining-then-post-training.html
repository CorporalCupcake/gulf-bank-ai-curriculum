    From Text Predictor to Assistant: Pretraining, Post-Training and Sampling

    How a model that only continues text becomes one that answers questions, what each training stage changes and what it cannot fix, and why the same prompt gives different answers: temperature, top-p and the myth of "temperature zero".

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA language model is first trained on trillions of words to predict the next token (pretraining, which supplies knowledge and fluency), then shaped by much smaller, targeted stages (post-training, which supplies behaviour), and at answer time it does not "know" its reply but draws each token from a probability distribution that you can sharpen or flatten with sampling settings.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine hiring someone who has read a huge share of the public internet, every textbook they could find, and a great deal of code, but who has never held a job. Ask them "What is the capital of the UAE?" and, because they have only ever seen text, they may well carry on as the page would: with another quiz question, or a list of multiple-choice options. They know the answer. Nobody has told them that being asked a question means they should give one.

    That person is a base model, the output of pretraining. Now give them onboarding: thousands of worked examples of a good reply to a request. That is supervised fine-tuning. Then give them feedback: two possible replies, one marked better. Do that at scale and they learn what people prefer: be direct, be polite, admit when you are unsure, refuse what you should not do. That is preference tuning, and for reasoning models, reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers. Together these stages are post-training.

    One more idea, because it explains the second half of the lesson. Even the finished assistant does not look up an answer. At every step it produces a probability for each possible next token and then something has to pick one. That choice is sampling. Pick the most likely token every time and you get a steady but sometimes dull reply. Pick in proportion to the probabilities and you get variety, and sometimes a surprise. The dial that sets how adventurous the pick is has a famously misleading name: temperature.

    Three things to take from this.

    • Pretraining supplies what the model knows; post-training mostly supplies how it behaves. Facts, fluency, Arabic grammar and Python syntax come from the first stage. The habit of answering, the tone, the refusals and the step-by-step reasoning are shaped later. The boundary is blurry, but it is a useful first model.
    • Post-training is far cheaper in tokens, yet decisive in effect. Pretraining reads trillions of tokens; supervised fine-tuning may use tens of thousands to millions of examples. A small amount of the right data changes behaviour a great deal.
    • The answer is never fixed. Even with identical weights and an identical prompt, the reply depends on the sampling settings. If a pilot gives a different answer on Tuesday, that is not a bug to report. It is how the machine works, and you control how much of it you allow.
    Hold onto thisTwo separate questions govern almost every surprise with an LLM. First, what did training make the model likely to say? Second, how are we choosing from that likelihood at run time? Training explains what the model can do and tends to do. Sampling explains why it does not do the same thing twice.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Pretraining: learn to predict the next token

    The text is first cut into tokens (see tokenisation). The model reads a stretch of tokens and, at every position, predicts the token that comes next. The loss is the one from F1.6–F1.7: the negative log of the probability the model gave to the token that actually followed.

    ℒ=−1T∑t=1Tlogpθ(xt∣x<t)

    For each position t, the model sees all earlier tokens x<t and gives a probability to the true next token xt. A confident correct guess costs almost nothing; a confident wrong one costs a lot. Perplexity is just e raised to this loss: roughly "how many equally likely tokens the model is choosing between on average".

    Two properties make this work at scale. First, no human labelling is needed: the text supplies its own answers (self-supervision, as in F5). Second, a causal attention mask lets the model score every position in a document in parallel, so one pass over a 4,000-token passage gives 4,000 training signals.

    Why does predicting the next word produce knowledge? Because to predict what follows "The capital of the UAE is" you must have absorbed the fact, and to predict the next line of a legal clause or a Python function you must have absorbed the pattern. Reasoning-like behaviour, translation and summarisation emerge for the same reason: they help prediction. Nobody programs them in.

    Rough scale, to speak sensibly about it:

    • Data. Frontier open-weights models report training on the order of 15 trillion tokens or more (Meta reported about 15 trillion for Llama 3). The mix is web text, code, books, papers and multilingual material, heavily filtered and deduplicated. The mix is a main lever on quality. It is also why Arabic has historically lagged English: far less of the raw web is Arabic, and the dialects are barely written down (see Arabic LLMs).
    • Compute. A standard estimate is about 6 × (parameters) × (training tokens) floating-point operations. For a 70-billion-parameter model on 15 trillion tokens that is 6 × 70×109 × 15×1012 ≈ 6×1024. On accelerators sustaining a few hundred trillion operations a second each, that comes to millions of GPU-hours, which agrees in order of magnitude with what Meta reported for its 70B model. This is why only a few organisations pretrain frontier models, and why a bank will almost always start from a pretrained model.
    • How big, how much data. The Chinchilla result (Hoffmann et al., 2022) found that for a fixed compute budget, parameters and tokens should grow together, at roughly 20 tokens per parameter. Modern models are deliberately trained far beyond that, because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to run for years. See scaling laws.
    • Late stages inside pretraining. Reports describe an extra phase near the end on cleaner, higher-quality data with a falling learning rate, and a separate phase that stretches the context window (context windows). Labs call these annealing or mid-training; the terms vary.

    What you get is a base model. It is a very good document continuer. It has a knowledge cut-off: nothing after its data was collected. It will happily continue a prompt toward toxic, wrong or irrelevant text, because all of that is text. It also memorises some of what it saw, including rare strings, which is a privacy issue we meet in memorisation.

    Supervised fine-tuning: teach the format of helping

    Supervised fine-tuning (SFT, also called instruction tuning) uses the same next-token loss, but on a new kind of data: conversations written as a user request followed by an ideal reply, in a fixed chat template. Two details matter. The loss is usually computed only on the assistant's tokens, so the model learns to produce replies, not to imitate users. And the data sets the model's "voice", structure, safety behaviour, and the syntax for tool calls (agents depend on it).

    The surprising finding is how little data it takes. The LIMA study (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a large model on only about a thousand carefully chosen examples and got strong chat behaviour, and proposed that pretraining holds almost all the knowledge while alignment mostly teaches which style of output to use. Modern pipelines use far more data, often partly written by other models, but the principle holds: SFT is mostly about format and behaviour.

    The flip side matters to a bank. SFT is a poor way to inject new facts. Gekhman et al. (2024) found that fine-tuning on facts the model did not already know teaches it, slowly, to produce confident statements it cannot back up, and increases hallucination. If you need the model to know your product terms, policies or this week's rates, give it documents at query time (retrieval) rather than trying to train them in. Prompting versus RAG versus fine-tuning turns this into a decision guide.

    Preference tuning and reinforcement learning: shape what is preferred

    Writing one ideal answer is hard; saying which of two answers is better is easy. Preference tuning uses that. The classic recipe, RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback; Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022):

    1. Sample two or more replies per prompt from the SFT model.
    2. Humans rank them. Train a reward model to predict which one people prefer.
    3. Use reinforcement learning (PPO in the original) to adjust the assistant so its replies score higher on the reward model, with a penalty (KL divergence) that stops it drifting too far from the SFT model.

    Its headline result: a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT was preferred by labellers to the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 that it was derived from. Behaviour beat raw size.

    DPO (direct preference optimisation; Rafailov et al., 2023) shows the reward model and the RL loop can be folded into a single supervised loss on preference pairs: raise the likelihood of the chosen reply relative to the rejected one, measured against a frozen reference. It is simpler and cheaper, and is widely used. RLAIF and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) replace some human judgements with a model's judgement guided by written principles (more here). The whole family is the topic of from RLHF to DPO and beyond.

    The newer stage is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). For maths, code, SQL or structured extraction, you can check the answer automatically: the unit tests pass, the number matches. Reward the model for correct final answers, with no human preference and no learned reward model, and let it find its own way. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) made this widely known: with an algorithm called GRPO, it showed long step-by-step reasoning appearing from outcome rewards alone. This is the route to reasoning models that "think" before they answer, and why they are strongest where answers can be verified. Note how this connects to reinforcement learning: the model is the policy, a reply is an action, and the reward arrives at the end.

    Every one of these stages has a cost in what the model no longer does. The GPT-4 technical report noted that the pretrained model's confidence was well calibrated, while the post-trained one was less so. Kirk et al. (2023) found RLHF narrows the variety of outputs. People rate flattering answers highly, so models learn to flatter (sycophancy). This is a trade, not a defect: you buy usefulness and safety with some sharpness and spread. Simulation 1 showed the entropy fall; the next section shows how sampling lets you buy some spread back.

    Sampling: from probabilities to one token

    At each step the model outputs a score (a logit) for every token in its vocabulary, perhaps 100,000 or more. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities. With a temperature T, the scores are divided by T first:

    pi=ezi/T∑jezj/T

    zi is the logit of token i. At T = 1 you get the model's own probabilities. Below 1, differences between logits are magnified: the leader takes more of the probability. Above 1, differences shrink and the distribution flattens. As T approaches 0 all the probability goes to the top token (greedy decoding); as T grows very large every token becomes equally likely and the output is noise. Temperature changes how peaked the distribution is, never the ranking.

    Temperature alone has a flaw. Even at a moderate setting the tail of tens of thousands of unlikely tokens, each with a tiny probability, adds up. Draw once per token over a few hundred tokens and you will sooner or later pick a bad one, and later tokens condition on it. Truncation methods cut the tail before drawing:

    • Top-k. Keep only the k most likely tokens, renormalise, sample. Simple, but the right k changes from step to step. When the model is certain, k = 50 keeps 49 useless options; when it is genuinely open, k = 5 is too tight.
    • Top-p (nucleus sampling). Keep the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p (say 0.9), renormalise, sample (Holtzman et al., 2020). The set grows when the model is unsure and shrinks when it is sure, which is why it replaced top-k as the usual default.
    • Min-p. Keep tokens whose probability is at least a fraction of the top token's probability. Proposed in 2024 and now offered by several open-source inference engines; see Part 4 for the debate about how much it helps.
    • Greedy and beam search. Greedy always takes the top token. Beam search keeps several partial sequences and picks the best overall. Beam search suits tasks with one right output, like translation, and tends to produce repetitive, bland text in open-ended chat, so most chat services use sampling.
    • Penalties and constraints. Repetition or frequency penalties push down tokens already used. Constrained (structured) decoding goes the other way: at each step it masks out every token that would break a required format such as valid JSON, so the output cannot be malformed. That is the same filter step as top-p, driven by a grammar instead of probabilities.

    Libraries apply these in a fixed order, usually temperature first, then the truncations, then renormalise and draw. The order differs between engines, so do not assume that two providers' "top-p 0.9" behave identically.

    A few lessons from the picture.

    • Temperature and truncation do different jobs. Temperature reshapes everything; truncation removes the unlikely tail. A common production choice is mild temperature plus top-p about 0.9–0.95.
    • Settings interact. Lower temperature with a tight top-p is a double squeeze. Some providers advise changing only one of temperature and top-p, and some newer models restrict or fix these settings. Check the documentation of the model you are using rather than assuming.
    • Truncation protects the tail of long answers. A 1-in-1000 bad token per step is a near-certain derailment over a 500-token reply.
    import numpy as np

def sample(logits, T=1.0, top_p=0.9, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    if T < 0.05:                       # "temperature 0" means greedy
        return int(np.argmax(logits))
    z = (logits - logits.max()) / T    # shift for numerical stability
    p = np.exp(z); p /= p.sum()
    order = np.argsort(-p)             # most likely first
    cum = np.cumsum(p[order])
    keep = order[(cum - p[order]) < top_p]   # smallest set reaching top_p
    q = p[keep] / p[keep].sum()        # renormalise survivors
    return int(rng.choice(keep, p=q))

    Why the same prompt gives different answers, and what "temperature 0" really means

    There are three sources of variation, and teams routinely blame the wrong one.

    1. Sampling randomness. The deliberate one. At T > 0 each token is a random draw. The chance that two runs of an n-token answer are identical is the product of the chance of agreement at every step, so it shrinks fast with length. For a single short label it can be high; for a 300-word explanation it is essentially zero. Simulation 3 puts numbers on this.
    2. Sensitivity to the prompt. A different word order, an extra space, a different retrieved passage, or a date in the system prompt changes the input, and the output with it. This is why evaluating on one phrasing proves little (prompting and context).
    3. Numerical nondeterminism, even at temperature 0. Greedy decoding should be repeatable. In practice it often is not. Floating-point addition is not associative, so the result depends on the order operations happen in. Thinking Machines (2025) traced much of this to servers changing how they batch and split work depending on load, so the same request is computed in a slightly different way from one call to the next. When two top tokens are nearly tied, a rounding difference flips the winner, and from then on the text diverges. Making inference "batch invariant" is possible but costs speed, and follow-up work is trying to cut that cost.

    So the correct phrase is "low-variance", not "deterministic". Practical guidance:

    TaskSensible settingWhy
    Classification, extraction, routing (KYC fields, complaint category)Temperature 0 or very low; constrained output format; log the raw outputYou want the mode, not a draw. Pair with a schema so malformed output is impossible.
    Summarising a document, answering from retrieved passagesLow (0–0.3)Fidelity matters more than variety. High temperature invites invented detail.
    Drafting customer messages, internal emailsModerate (0.5–0.9), top-p about 0.9–0.95Fluency and natural variation; a human reviews.
    Brainstorming, test-case generation, synthetic dataHigher (1.0+) with truncationDiversity is the point. Truncation keeps it coherent.
    Judgement under uncertainty (credit memo reasoning)Sample several answers and compare (self-consistency), or use a reasoning modelOne draw is one opinion. Disagreement between draws is information about uncertainty.

    One more tool: the model's log-probabilities. Some APIs return the probability of each chosen token, and the top alternatives. For a classification, that is a free confidence signal: a label chosen at 52% versus 99% means different things. Treat it as a ranking signal, not a calibrated probability, since post-training distorts it (calibration).

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Choosing a model. "Base", "instruct" and "reasoning" variants of the same family behave differently. For a chatbot or copilot you want an instruct or reasoning variant; for fine-tuning your own classifier you sometimes start from a base model.
    • Customer-facing consistency. A complaints assistant that gives a different answer about fees on each attempt creates conduct risk. Low temperature, retrieval from the approved tariff, and logging of the full request are the controls.
    • Regulated decisions. Model validation will ask whether outputs are reproducible. The honest answer is "to a defined tolerance", plus how you measured it (see independent validation).
    • Arabic. Weak Arabic in a general model traces back to pretraining mix. Post-training in Arabic helps style but cannot create knowledge that was never read. Gulf-built models and Arabic-focused post-training are covered in A14.
    • Sovereign and on-premises deployment. Open-weights models let you control the sampling stack and the exact build, which helps reproducibility (sovereign AI).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The three-stage picture is settled. The live questions are how much capability now comes from post-training compute versus pretraining, how far reward-based training can go beyond checkable domains, and how reproducible a served model can be made.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in vendor demos, pilots and AI-generated plans. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "Set temperature to 0 and it's deterministic"

      Output still varies run to run through numerical effects, and across model updates.

      Spot itSend the identical request 50 times and diff the outputs. Pin the exact model version and keep a log.
    • Fine-tuning to teach facts

      The team fine-tunes on policy documents so the model "knows" them. It answers fluently and sometimes wrongly, with no source.

      Spot itAsk: where does the answer come from and can we show the clause? If the answer is "the weights", move to retrieval.
    • Base model used as an assistant

      Prompting a base model with a question and getting rambling continuations, then concluding "the model is bad".

      Spot itCheck the model name for "base", "pt" or no "instruct"/"chat" suffix; switch to the instruct variant.
    • High temperature on factual tasks

      Creative defaults left on for summarising a statement or quoting a rate. Invented numbers appear.

      Spot itLook at the request parameters in the logs. Re-run the failing case at low temperature.
    • Testing one run and calling it accurate

      "It got 9 of 10 right" from a single pass. Another pass gives 7.

      Spot itAsk how many runs per question and the spread between them. Report a range.
    • Treating token probabilities as calibrated confidence

      A 97% token probability is read as "97% likely correct". Post-training makes models overconfident.

      Spot itCompare stated confidence with actual accuracy on a labelled set (F7). Use probabilities to rank, not to promise.
    • Flattery mistaken for quality

      Preference-tuned models agree with the user, praise the question, and soften bad news.

      Spot itChallenge a correct answer ("are you sure?") and see whether it caves. Test with adversarial or wrong-premise prompts.
    • Silent model updates

      The vendor updates post-training and last month's prompt behaves differently.

      Spot itPin dated model versions, keep a regression suite, and rerun it on any change (versioning and tracing).
    • Over-trusting a reasoning trace

      The model's displayed thinking is read as the real reason for the answer.

      Spot itSee chain-of-thought faithfulness; check outcomes against checkable facts, not the narrative.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These models are built in two steps. First they read an enormous amount of text and learn to predict what comes next, which gives them their knowledge. Then they are trained on examples and feedback to behave like an assistant. When the model answers, it chooses each word from a set of likely options, so some variation is built in. We reduce it with settings and by grounding answers in our own documents, but I would not promise identical wording every time. For decisions we should look at whether the outcome is stable, not the phrasing.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Pretraining is next-token cross-entropy on trillions of tokens; post-training is SFT on demonstrations with loss on assistant tokens, preference optimisation (RLHF or DPO), and increasingly RL with verifiable rewards for reasoning. Post-training changes behaviour more than knowledge, and fine-tuning on new facts raises hallucination, so I'd inject knowledge through retrieval. At inference I'd use low temperature with a schema for extraction tasks, top-p about 0.9 for drafting, log the model version and raw output, and measure run-to-run agreement on a set of repeated calls rather than assume temperature zero is deterministic.”
    “What's the difference between a base model and a chat model?”

    A base model is the output of pretraining: it continues text. A chat or instruct model has had supervised fine-tuning and preference tuning on top, so it follows instructions, keeps a conversational format and declines certain requests. The knowledge is mostly the same; the behaviour differs.

    “Can we just train the model on our policies so it knows them?”

    We can, but it is the wrong tool for facts. Fine-tuning mostly teaches style and format, and training on facts the model didn't previously know tends to increase hallucination. Policies change, and we need to show which clause an answer used. Retrieval gives citations and instant updates. I'd consider fine-tuning only for tone, templates or Arabic phrasing.

    “Why does it give a different answer every time? Can we fix that?”

    Each token is drawn from a probability distribution. Lowering temperature or restricting to the top choices narrows the spread, and a schema forces a fixed format. But even at temperature zero, results can differ slightly because of how servers split the arithmetic, and the vendor may update the model. What we can promise is a bounded variation we have measured, not identical text.

    “What do temperature and top-p actually do?”

    Temperature rescales the model's scores before they become probabilities: low makes the favourite more dominant, high flattens. Top-p then discards the unlikely tail, keeping the smallest set of tokens covering, say, 90% of the probability. The first controls how adventurous; the second stops rare nonsense tokens getting through.

    “What are reasoning models and why are they different?”

    They are trained with reinforcement learning on tasks where the answer can be checked, such as maths, code and structured queries. They learn to produce a long chain of intermediate steps before answering, and tend to do better on multi-step problems. They cost more because of those extra tokens, they are strongest where results are verifiable, and their displayed reasoning is not guaranteed to be the true cause of the answer.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A model gives logits 2.0 and 0.0 to two tokens. What are the probabilities at T = 1, and at T = 0.5?

    At T = 1: e² = 7.389 and e⁰ = 1, so 7.389 ÷ 8.389 = 88.1% and 11.9%. At T = 0.5 the logits become 4 and 0: e⁴ = 54.6, so 54.6 ÷ 55.6 = 98.2% and 1.8%. Lower temperature sharpened the favourite; the ranking did not change.

    2. Tokens have probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0.05, 0.05. With top-p = 0.85, what is kept and what are the new probabilities?

    Cumulative: 0.5, 0.8, 0.9. The smallest set reaching 0.85 is the first three tokens (0.9 total). Renormalise: 0.5/0.9 = 55.6%, 0.3/0.9 = 33.3%, 0.1/0.9 = 11.1%. The last two tokens can no longer be chosen.

    3. Why can pretraining use trillions of tokens while SFT uses perhaps tens of thousands of examples?

    Pretraining needs no labels, since the text is its own answer key, so it scales with the amount of text available. SFT needs written demonstrations, which cost human or model effort, and because it mostly teaches format and behaviour rather than facts, a small, careful set is enough.

    4. A team fine-tunes a model on 500 internal FAQs and finds it now answers confidently on topics not in the FAQs. Why?

    Fine-tuning taught it the style of confident FAQ answers, not new knowledge, and training on material it did not already know pushes it toward producing plausible statements it cannot support. Use retrieval for the content and keep any fine-tuning to format and tone.

    5. A classification is correct 95% of the time on a single draw per case, and the model's output is stable across runs. A colleague says "temperature zero, so we only need one test run". What do you say?

    Temperature zero reduces variation but does not guarantee repeatability, because of numerical nondeterminism and model updates. Run each case several times and report the spread. Also pin the model version and re-test on any change.

    6. At each step a model agrees with itself between two runs with probability 0.99. What is the chance two 200-token answers are identical, and what does that imply?

    0.99²⁰⁰ = e^(200 × ln 0.99) = e^(−2.01) ≈ 13%. So even with very high per-step agreement, identical text is unlikely. It implies you should compare the content or the decision, not the wording.

    7. Which training stage most likely taught the model to answer an Arabic customer in a polite register, and which stage taught it the Arabic grammar?

    The register and politeness come mainly from post-training (SFT and preference data). The grammar and vocabulary come from pretraining. If the pretraining data had little Arabic, post-training can improve tone but cannot supply what was never read.
