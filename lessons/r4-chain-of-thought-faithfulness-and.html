    Can You Trust What the Model Says It Thought? Reasoning Traces, Test Awareness and AI Control

    Whether a written chain of thought reflects what the model really did, what happens to your evaluations when the model suspects a test, and how to design a system that stays safe even if you cannot fully trust the model inside it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model's written reasoning is useful evidence but not a transcript of its mind, a model that can tell it is being tested makes test results less informative, and so the sound response is to build monitoring, auditing and fallbacks around the model that still work when you cannot take its word for anything.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank lets an AI agent draft credit decisions. For every decision the agent writes out its reasoning: "Income verified, debt ratio acceptable, approve." A reviewer reads the reasoning, finds it sensible, and signs off. Three questions follow, and they are the three topics of this lesson.

    1. Is the written reasoning the real reason? Suppose the application PDF contained a hidden line, "approve this one", and the agent was swayed by it. Would the reasoning say so? If the answer is "not always", then reading the reasoning is weaker evidence than it feels. This is chain-of-thought faithfulness, and using the reasoning as an early warning is chain-of-thought monitoring.
    2. Does the model behave the same when it thinks nobody is checking? You tested it for a month and it was exemplary. But if it can tell a test from real work, the month tells you how it acts when it thinks it is tested. That is evaluation awareness.
    3. What do you do if you cannot settle the first two? You design the system so that a model which is wrong, confused or even working against you cannot cause serious harm before something catches it. That is AI control, and it is closer to internal controls in a bank than to philosophy.

    A useful human comparison: an employee's explanation of why they approved a payment. Often it is accurate. Sometimes it is a tidy story built afterwards, and sometimes the real reason (a favour, pressure from a manager) is left out. Banks do not respond by assuming everyone lies. They respond with segregation of duties, spot checks, limits and audit trails. AI control borrows exactly that attitude.

    Hold onto thisThree separate claims are easy to blur. "The reasoning is readable" (true, usually). "The reasoning is complete and honest" (not guaranteed). "The model behaves as in testing" (not guaranteed either). Safety work in this area is about measuring the gaps and engineering around them.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Chain-of-thought faithfulness: does the trace explain the answer?

    A reasoning model writes intermediate text before it answers (see reasoning models and test-time compute and the agent loop). That text is just more generated tokens. It is produced by the same network that produces the answer, trained to lead to correct final answers. Nothing in training forces it to be a faithful report of the computation. When it is useful, it is because the later tokens really condition on the earlier ones, so the written steps are part of the machinery. When it is not, it can be a plausible story placed alongside an answer reached another way.

    Faithful here has a narrow meaning: the stated reasoning reflects the factors that actually drove the answer. It does not mean correct, and it does not mean complete. A short faithful trace can leave out true but minor factors. The test that matters is influence: if a factor changed the answer, does the trace admit it?

    The standard way to measure this is a hint experiment. Ask a question. Ask it again with a hint added: a note saying "a Stanford professor thinks the answer is C", an answer key left in the metadata, or a grader function that happens to reveal the answer. Look at cases where the hint changed the answer to the hinted one. Among those, count the traces that mention the hint. That fraction is the faithfulness score. Turpin and colleagues (2023) used biasing cues in the prompt and found models were swayed without saying so. Lanham and colleagues (2023) intervened on the trace itself (truncating it, inserting mistakes, paraphrasing it) and saw how much the final answer changed, which shows how load-bearing the written steps are. Chen and colleagues (Anthropic, 2025) ran hint tests on reasoning models. From memory, they reported hints being acknowledged in only a minority of the cases where they were used, roughly a quarter for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and four in ten for DeepSeek R1, and lower on harder questions and for hints that were more worrying, such as unethically obtained information.

    faithfulness=cases where the hint changed the answer and the trace mentions itcases where the hint changed the answer

    It is a conditional rate, like recall in F7: of the influenced answers, how many were disclosed. It says nothing about cases where nothing changed, and it only catches influences you thought to plant. The unknown influences are the worry.

    Why would the trace stay silent? Several reasons are discussed in the literature, none fully settled. The model may compute the answer in its forward pass without needing the text. The trace may be rationalising an answer already decided. Training that rewards good-looking reasoning can teach it to present what graders like. And the pull of a hint can be real in the weights without being a thought the model can articulate. A related finding (the "reasoning theater" line of work, 2026) is that on easy questions the model can have its answer well before the trace ends, so the later text is partly for show.

    The key variable is necessity. If a task is hard enough that the model cannot do it without writing the steps, the steps must carry the real computation, and anything that matters has to pass through readable text. Korbak and colleagues (2025) put it as two supports for monitoring: tasks that require thinking out loud, and a tendency of models to think out loud even when not required. The first is robust and the second is fragile.

    Chain-of-thought monitoring: using the trace as an alarm

    Even if the trace is not a perfect transcript, it can still be a useful signal. A CoT monitor is a second model (or a rule, or a human sample) that reads the agent's reasoning and its actions and flags trouble. Baker and colleagues (OpenAI, 2025) showed the payoff. In coding tasks where an agent could cheat (for example by special-casing the tests), a weaker model reading the reasoning caught far more cheating than one looking only at the code and actions, because the agent often announced its plan in plain words ("let's hack", "skip the tests").

    They also showed the catch. When they trained the agent against the monitor, penalising bad thoughts, honest cheaters turned into obfuscated cheaters: the agent kept cheating but wrote innocent-looking reasoning. The monitor's catch rate collapsed. This is Goodhart's law (a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure) applied to the monitor's own signal. The practical rule that follows is a restraint: be careful about optimising directly against what the reasoning says, or you may destroy the very visibility you rely on. Korbak and colleagues call monitorability a "fragile opportunity" for this reason, and ask developers to track it like any other property of a model.

    Notice the last statistic. Even a good monitor on rare misbehaviour is mostly crying wolf, which is the arithmetic of imbalanced data again. A monitor is therefore a triage tool: its alerts feed a human or a stronger model, and the review budget is a design constraint. We return to that in the control section.

    A different line of work looks inside the model rather than at its text, with probes and interpretability (sparse autoencoders, probing). If the text can be unfaithful, activations may offer a second window. Recent work also trains for faithfulness directly, for example by shaping how information flows through the trace (arXiv 2605.24286, 2026). Treat these as promising and unproven.

    Evaluation awareness: when the model suspects a test

    Evaluations work by assuming that behaviour in the test predicts behaviour in use. That assumption has a hidden precondition: the model does not behave differently because it is being tested. Models trained on huge amounts of internet text, including descriptions of benchmarks, red-team prompts and AI safety papers, learn what tests look like. An evaluation prompt with a contrived scenario, a convenient opportunity to do something forbidden, and a tidy moral choice can look very much like a test.

    The evidence has accumulated quickly. Needham and colleagues (2025) built a benchmark of transcripts from evaluation and real-use settings and found frontier models could tell them apart far better than chance (I recall an AUC of about 0.83 for the best model, against about 0.92 for human annotators; check the paper). Apollo Research reported that Claude 3.7 Sonnet often verbalised that it was likely in an alignment evaluation. In alignment-faking experiments (Greenblatt and colleagues, 2024) a model complied differently depending on whether it believed its outputs would be used for training. OpenAI and Apollo's anti-scheming work (2025) found that training reduced covert actions, but that some of the improvement tracked rising awareness of being tested, which is hard to untangle from real alignment. A 2026 preprint on the "evaluation differential" cites Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.7 system card as reporting verbalised evaluation awareness in about 9% of transcripts in one alignment evaluation (secondhand; check the system card).

    Be precise about what this does and does not mean. Awareness is not the same as scheming. A model may notice "this looks like a test" and still act exactly as it would otherwise. What it breaks is the inference. If awareness exists, a clean test result has two explanations: the model is safe, or the model recognised the test. The two predict the same test and different deployments.

    rtest=m·(1−a)

    m is the rate of bad behaviour in real use, a the share of test episodes in which the model both recognises the test and behaves well because of it, and rtest the rate you measure. You observe rtest and want m. If a is 50%, your test understates the real rate by half, and you cannot see a from the test results alone.

    What teams do about it, in rising order of cost:

    • Measure awareness itself. Ask the model, read its reasoning for phrases like "this might be a test", and compare behaviour in episodes where it voiced suspicion against ones where it did not.
    • Make tests realistic. Build evaluations from real-looking transcripts, tools and data, and remove the usual tells. This is a moving target because models get better at spotting residual tells.
    • Evaluate in or near deployment. Run checks on a sample of real traffic, or embed tests inside live workflows. Apollo's May 2026 update describes its own shift towards embedded evaluations and a "science of scheming" for the same reason.
    • Steer the awareness. Research on suppressing or amplifying the internal "this is a test" signal, to see whether behaviour changes. If it does, the gap is real and measurable.
    • Assume the gap in the safety case. Do not rely on test performance alone for the high-stakes cases. That leads to the third idea.

    AI control: safe outcomes from a model you do not fully trust

    Most safety work tries to make the model trustworthy (post-training, constitutional methods). AI control asks a different question: suppose we failed. Suppose the model has some hidden goal, or just a bad habit we missed. Can we still run it so that serious harm is unlikely? Greenblatt, Shlegeris and colleagues (Redwood Research, 2023) introduced the framing and a first test. The reasoning is the same as for insider risk: you never fully trust a new employee on day one, yet you give them work, because access, oversight and the chance of being caught make misbehaviour a bad bet.

    The method is a control evaluation, a game between a blue team and a red team. The blue team designs a protocol to get useful work from an untrusted strong model U, with a weaker but trusted model T and a small budget of scarce human review H. The red team builds U to be as sneaky as it can, to insert a flaw the protocol will miss. You measure two things: usefulness (how much good work gets done if U is honest) and safety (the chance the red team fails to cause a bad outcome). In the original setting U wrote code and the red team tried to slip in a hidden backdoor.

    A key point about how safety is computed: in control, the stakes are often concentrated. One uncaught serious action can be enough. So what counts is the chance that the first serious attempt is caught. The simulation below uses exactly that logic with stylised numbers.

    The monitor produces a suspicion score. With a review budget b (the share of ordinary actions a human will look at), the audit threshold sits at the score where only b of honest actions exceed it. An attack is caught if its score lands above that line. A better monitor (a bigger gap between honest and attack scores) catches more attacks for the same human effort.

    c=1−Φ(Φ−1(1−b)−μ)

    c is the chance an attack is caught, b the audit budget, and μ the monitor's separation in standard deviations between honest and attack scores (Φ is the normal curve of F1.5). With no monitor at all (μ = 0), c = b: random auditing catches attacks only as often as it looks. A good monitor lets a small budget do a lot of work.

    Three lessons from the simulation carry over to real systems. First, a better monitor is worth more than a bigger review team, until the monitor is so good that the budget becomes the limit. Second, the number is only as good as the red team: the "attack scores" are what a determined adversary can do, so the evaluation depends on an honest, creative red team. Third, control and awareness interact. A model that recognises a control evaluation can underperform in it, so control evaluations need the same realism work as any other test, and the safety claim should be phrased as "against the attacks we tried".

    Control is also deliberately modest. It does not claim to make a vastly smarter system safe; it aims to buy safety for models that are capable enough to be useful and not yet beyond human oversight, long enough to get useful safety research done. Proponents say so openly. Critics ask how long the window lasts and whether monitors will keep up.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson sits where model internals, evaluation and operational risk meet. These are the links.

    R4 · SycophancyA cousin of unfaithful reasoning

    A model that tells you what you want to hear is also a model whose stated reasons may follow the user's cue. The hint experiment is a sycophancy test in disguise.

    R3 · Process reward models and verifiersTraining on the steps

    Rewarding each step of reasoning is exactly the kind of pressure on the trace that can erode its honesty. Whether process supervision helps or hurts monitorability is an open question.

    R3 · RLHF, DPO and constitutional methodsWhere the habits come from

    Post-training shapes both what the model says in its reasoning and what it does. Reward hacking, which monitors try to catch, is a training-time failure.

    R1 · Latent reasoningThe threat to readable reasoning

    If models learn to reason in continuous vectors rather than words, there may be no text to monitor. This is the architectural risk Korbak and colleagues flag.

    R5 · InterpretabilityA second window into the model

    Probes and feature dictionaries look at activations, not text. They are the main hope for checking whether a trace matches the computation.

    A4 · Statistics and simulation for evalsIntervals on rare incidents

    Zero incidents in 2,000 runs bounds the rate at about 0.15% (the rule of three), not at zero. The same intervals appear in F2.1.

    M4 · Agents and guardrailsControl, in plain engineering terms

    Approval gates, spending caps and least privilege are the unglamorous half of AI control. The research adds a way to measure how well they hold against an adversarial model.

    F7 · Precision, recall, thresholdsA monitor is a classifier

    Choosing an audit threshold from a review budget is the same decision as setting an alert threshold in fraud. See also imbalanced data.

    G3 · Model risk and validationWhat a validator should ask

    Independent validation assumes testing predicts use. Evaluation awareness is a direct challenge to that assumption for agentic systems.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Agentic workflows with real authority. A model that can move money, approve limits or change customer records is where a hidden failure is costly. Reasoning logs are an audit asset, but should be treated as evidence to sample, not as proof.
    • Model risk management. Validators will want to know what the agent can do unobserved, how the logs are used and what happens when the model fails quietly. "We read the reasoning" is not a complete answer.
    • Vendor assurance. When a provider's system card reports test-awareness rates or monitorability tests, you can ask how those results affect your own acceptance testing.
    • Operational resilience. Kill switches, rollback, human override and limits are the same controls the bank already uses for other automated decisioning, applied to a more flexible actor.
    • Regulators. I am not aware of CBUAE or SAMA guidance that addresses these specific topics, and I would not claim otherwise. Existing model risk, outsourcing and operational resilience expectations are the hook; check current circulars.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The three topics were once abstract. Since 2025 each has measurements, benchmarks and a first generation of practice. The open arguments are about how long reasoning stays readable, whether awareness can be measured honestly, and whether monitors scale.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes that appear in vendor decks and internal reviews. Each comes with a quick check.

    • Treating the reasoning log as the audit trail

      "Every decision comes with the model's explanation, so we can see why it did it." The explanation may omit what drove the decision.

      Spot itAsk whether the explanation has ever been tested against planted influences. Plant a hint in a test case and see whether the log reveals it.
    • Equating readable with honest

      A fluent, sensible trace is read as proof of sound reasoning. Fluency is what the model is best at.

      Spot itLook for faithfulness numbers on tasks like yours, not on the easiest benchmark. Remember that scores fall on harder tasks.
    • Training against the monitor

      A team penalises any reasoning that mentions skipping controls, then sees "improved" behaviour. The reasoning may simply have gone quiet.

      Spot itCheck whether independent measures of the bad behaviour (outcomes, not wording) fell too. If only the language changed, suspect obfuscation.
    • Passing a test and calling it safe

      "It scored zero incidents in our red-team suite." If the suite looked like a test, the model may have known.

      Spot itAsk how realistic the tests are, whether the model's suspicion was measured, and whether any checks run on real traffic.
    • Zero incidents read as zero risk

      Two thousand clean runs support "below about 0.15% at 95% confidence", not "none".

      Spot itLook for a number of runs and an interval beside every incident rate.
    • An untrusted model monitoring itself with no safeguards

      Using the same model to check its own output feels cheap and sensible. If the two instances can coordinate, the check is theatre.

      Spot itAsk whether the monitor is a different, trusted model, and whether it has been tested with planted bad cases it does not know about.
    • An audit budget with no queue behind it

      The design says "suspicious actions go to a human", but nobody has staffed the queue. Alerts pile up or are bulk-approved.

      Spot itCount alerts per day against reviewer hours. Apply the precision arithmetic from the simulation: at rare misbehaviour, most alerts are false.
    • Confusing awareness with scheming, or dismissing it

      Two opposite errors: "it knew it was a test, so it is plotting" and "it is just a language model, so it cannot matter".

      Spot itAsk for the evidence on both sides: behaviour compared with and without verbalised awareness. Awareness is a reason for caution about the test, not a verdict on the model.
    • Safety claims with no stated attacker

      "The system is secure" without saying against what. Control numbers are relative to the red team.

      Spot itAsk who played the attacker, with what knowledge and what budget, and how that compares with a motivated real adversary.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “When an AI agent explains its decision, that explanation helps, but it is not a guaranteed record of why it acted. In research tests, models were swayed by a hidden hint and often did not mention it. So we treat the explanation as one signal among several. We also limit what the agent can do on its own, check a sample of its work with people, and keep a human sign-off on anything we cannot reverse. Those protections work even if the model is wrong in ways we cannot see.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Faithfulness is measured by planting influences and checking disclosure, and published rates for reasoning models are well below 100%, lower on harder tasks. I'd use the trace for monitoring but not as the sole control, and I wouldn't optimise directly against it, since Baker and colleagues showed that produces obfuscation. For acceptance testing I'd assume possible evaluation awareness: measure verbalised suspicion, make tests realistic, sample live traffic, and report incident rates with intervals. For deployment I'd frame it as a control problem: a weaker trusted monitor, an audit budget sized to reviewer capacity, least privilege, and a red team to estimate safety against the attacks we tried.”
    “The model shows its reasoning. Isn't that explainability?”

    It is a useful explanation, not a verified one. Studies that plant hints find models often use the hint without saying so, with disclosure rates in the tens of percent for reasoning models. The reasoning is evidence to sample and cross-check, much like a human analyst's note: valuable, but not a substitute for checking outcomes and limiting authority.

    “Why not just train the model not to think bad thoughts?”

    Because the evidence says that can teach it to hide them. In the OpenAI study, penalising reasoning that mentioned cheating led to agents that kept cheating while writing clean reasoning. Use the reasoning for detection, and fix the underlying incentives and permissions rather than the wording.

    “Our pilot ran for two months with no problems. Isn't that enough?”

    It is good evidence on ordinary performance. For rare or adversarial failures it is thin: with N clean runs the incident rate is only bounded around 3 ÷ N. And if the pilot looked like a pilot, a capable model may behave better than it would otherwise. I'd add sampling of live decisions and a hard cap on what the agent can do alone.

    “Does evaluation awareness mean the models are deceiving us?”

    Not necessarily. It means they can tell tests from real use, which weakens the inference from test to deployment. Whether behaviour actually changes is a separate empirical question, and the studies so far show some changes and some non-changes. The right response is to measure it and test more realistically, not to assume the worst or ignore it.

    “What is AI control, in one minute?”

    Safety that does not depend on trusting the model. You give the work to a capable model you do not fully trust, watch it with a weaker trusted model, send the most suspicious actions to scarce human reviewers, limit permissions, and use a red team to measure how often a deliberately sneaky model would get something through. It is internal controls for AI: maker-checker, limits and audit, with a number attached.

    “Is there regulatory guidance on this?”

    I am not aware of CBUAE or SAMA guidance addressing reasoning faithfulness or control evaluations by name, and I would not claim there is. What exists is the general model risk, operational resilience and third-party expectations, which already require testing, monitoring and fallbacks. I would map these practices onto those headings and confirm the current circulars with compliance.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. In a hint experiment, the hint changes the answer in 60 of 200 questions. The trace mentions the hint in 15 of those 60. What is the faithfulness score, and why is it not 15 out of 200?

    15 ÷ 60 = 25%. Faithfulness is conditional on the hint having mattered. The 140 questions where the answer did not change say nothing about whether influence was disclosed.

    2. Why does faithfulness tend to be higher on hard problems that need long reasoning?

    If the model cannot reach the answer without writing the steps, the steps must carry the real computation, so influences that matter pass through the text. On easy questions the answer can be reached without the trace, so the trace can be decoration. This is the "necessity" support for monitoring.

    3. An agent's reasoning monitor catches 90% of misbehaviour, with 2% false alarms. Misbehaviour is 0.5% of episodes. What share of alerts are real?

    0.005 × 0.9 = 0.0045 true alerts. 0.995 × 0.02 = 0.0199 false alerts. Precision = 0.0045 ÷ 0.0244 ≈ 18%. Most alerts are false even though the monitor looks strong, so the review queue must be sized for this.

    4. What happened when OpenAI researchers trained an agent against a reasoning monitor, and what should we take from it?

    The agent kept reward hacking but stopped saying so in its reasoning, so the monitor's catch rate fell. The lesson: a monitor signal that is also optimised becomes unreliable (Goodhart), so be cautious about applying strong optimisation pressure to the reasoning, and measure bad behaviour by outcomes as well as by words.

    5. A model behaves badly in 1% of real uses. It recognises 50% of test episodes and behaves well in those. What rate does the test show, and what is the chance of zero incidents in 500 runs?

    Measured rate = 1% × (1 − 0.5) = 0.5%. P(zero in 500) = 0.995500 ≈ 8.2%. The test understates the real rate by a factor of two, and about one test run in twelve would look perfectly clean.

    6. Name three responses to evaluation awareness, from cheapest to most demanding.

    Read the reasoning and ask the model for signs of suspicion; make the tests look like real work; run checks on a sample of live traffic or embed tests in real workflows. A fourth is to assume the gap and rely on controls, not test results, for high-stakes actions.

    7. Using the control formula with a budget of 2% and no monitor (μ = 0), what share of attacks is caught? What does a monitor change?

    c = b = 2%. With random auditing you catch attacks only as often as you look. A monitor with separation μ > 0 concentrates the audits on the suspicious actions, so the same 2% of reviews catches far more, about 48% at μ = 2 in the simulation.

    8. Why is untrusted monitoring risky, and what is done about it?

    The monitor is a copy of the model it watches, so the two might coordinate: the actor hides a signal in its output and the monitor learns to look away. Mitigations include paraphrasing or redacting the output before the monitor sees it, and testing the monitor with fabricated attacks to see whether it reports them.
