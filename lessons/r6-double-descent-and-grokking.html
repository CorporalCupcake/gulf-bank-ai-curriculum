    When Overfitting Stops Being the Rule: Double Descent, Grokking and the Emergence Debate

    Why models that memorise their training data can still generalise, why some networks learn the real rule thousands of steps after they stop improving, and how much of the "sudden new abilities" of large models is real and how much is the ruler you measured with.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTest error is not a simple U-shape: it can peak where a model just barely fits the data and then fall again, a model can sit at perfect training accuracy for a long time before finding the rule, and when a large model seems to gain an ability suddenly, first check whether the metric made it look sudden.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The classic story (F4.1): a simple model underfits, a flexible one memorises noise and fails on new data, so aim for the middle and worry if training accuracy hits 100%. For a tree-based credit model on 40,000 rows, that advice works.

    Think of a student who memorises last year's exam answers. Usually that means they learnt nothing. But Zhang and colleagues (2017) showed image networks that generalise well can also memorise random labels perfectly. So capacity to memorise does not decide whether a model generalises. And making networks even bigger, past the point where they hit zero training error (interpolate), made test error go down again. Three oddities follow:

    • Double descent. As parameters grow, test error rises to a peak at the exact size where the model can just fit every training point, then falls, often below the earlier best.
    • Grokking. A small network memorises its training set early while test accuracy sits at chance. Much later, with training loss unchanged, test accuracy jumps to nearly 100%.
    • Emergence, and the claim it is a mirage. Large language models seem to gain abilities abruptly at some size. A 2023 paper argued much of this comes from the scoring rule, not the model.

    One picture ties the first two together. A flexible model has many ways to fit the training data. The first it finds are often brittle memorisations. With more capacity, more time, or a push towards small weights (weight decay), training settles on simpler fits. Whether you see a peak, a delay or a smooth curve depends on how that preference plays out.

    Hold onto this"Training error is zero, so it is overfit" is a heuristic, not a law. It suits small tabular models and misleads for overparameterised ones. The real test is error on unseen data from the period and population the model will face.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Double descent: the peak at the interpolation threshold

    Simplest setting: linear regression on p random features (a "random features" model, kin to a one-hidden-layer network) fitted to n training rows. Increase p. Picture fitting a curve through points: with exactly as many knobs as points, the curve is forced through every point, noise included, and swings wildly between them.

    • p well below n. Classical regime: more features cut bias, then add variance. The F4.1 U-shape.
    • p = n. Exactly one weight vector fits every row, so it must match the label noise with no slack. Weights get large and wild and test error spikes. Technically, the feature matrix is nearly singular (tiny smallest singular values) and inverting it amplifies noise.
    • p well above n. Infinitely many weight vectors fit. Take the smallest-norm one (what gradient descent from zero, or a tiny ridge penalty, effectively picks). The fit spreads thinly over many directions, each weight is small, and noise is absorbed without distorting the signal. Test error falls again.
    w^min-norm=ΦT(ΦΦT)−1y

    Φ is the n × p feature matrix for the training rows, y the labels. For p > n this picks the shortest weight vector among exact fits. Near p = n, Φ ΦT is near-singular, its inverse is huge and the weights blow up. Hastie et al. (2022) predict the peak; Belkin et al. (2019) saw it in neural nets and forests; Nakkiran et al. (2021) across architectures.

    Three details that matter in a meeting:

    • The peak moves with the data too. Add training data to a fixed model and the peak can pass through your model size, so more data can make test error worse ("sample-wise"). Along training time ("epoch-wise") error can fall, rise, then fall.
    • Noise makes the peak. Clean labels give a small spike; noisy labels (the banking norm: mislabelled fraud, fuzzy default definitions) give a large one.
    • Regularisation removes it. Ridge penalty, weight decay, early stopping or averaging models flatten it; with optimal regularisation Nakkiran et al. found no non-monotonicity in their linear setting. That is why tuned production models rarely show it.

    Why is the far side good, not merely "less bad"? Benign overfitting (Bartlett et al., 2020): when the features have many small-variation directions, the model fits noise by putting a little weight on a huge number of unimportant directions, while a few strong directions carry the signal. That is a theorem for linear models and a strong empirical pattern, not a guarantee, for deep nets.

    The spike is a conditioning problem plus noise; the second descent is the minimum-norm preference. Fitting exactly and generalising do not contradict; what matters is which of the many exact fits you land on.

    Grokking: generalisation that arrives late

    Like a student who first memorises the times table and only weeks later spots the pattern. Power and colleagues (2022) trained small transformers on tasks like addition modulo 97 using part of all possible examples. Training accuracy hit 100% in a few hundred steps; validation stayed near chance; after 10 to 100 times more training it shot up. They called it grokking. Later work explained it for these small tasks:

    • Two solutions compete. A lookup table memorises training pairs; a general algorithm works on unseen pairs. For modular addition (Nanda et al., 2023) the network places numbers on a circle (sines and cosines of a few frequencies) and adds angles.
    • Memorising is fast; the general circuit is slow but cheaper. It reaches the same training confidence with smaller weights. Varma et al. (2023) call this circuit efficiency: weight decay slowly favours it.
    • Underneath, it is gradual. Nanda et al. found hidden progress measures that improve smoothly long before test accuracy moves. The jump is a threshold on a continuous process, an idea that returns in the emergence debate.
    • The ingredients are specific: weight decay, a training set that is a modest share of all cases, and a task with a compact rule. More data brings grokking sooner; too little, never. Liu et al. (2022, "Omnigrok") tie it to weight size and show it on non-algorithmic tasks from large initial weights.

    Two cautions. Grokking is solid on small algorithmic tasks, but whether LLMs grok in ordinary pretraining is open. And the practical lesson is about stopping rules: stopping when validation loss flattens can miss a late gain, yet it is no licence to train forever on bank data full of noisy labels, where you would only memorise the noise.

    Is emergence a mirage?

    Wei et al. (2022) listed tasks where small language models sat near chance and then rose sharply past some size. They called these emergent abilities: absent in small models, present in large ones, not predictable from small ones. If abilities switch on unpredictably, you cannot know what the next model can do.

    Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) said much of this is the scoring. It is like a 10-question exam marked pass only for 10 of 10: a student improving steadily from 6 to 9 correct shows no "pass" until the end. Take multi-digit addition: if the chance of one digit right improves smoothly with size, a k-digit answer counts only if all k are right:

    exact match=pk

    p is the chance one digit is correct, k the number of digits. With k = 8: p = 0.6 gives 1.7% (invisible), p = 0.74 gives 9%, p = 0.92 gives 51%. A smooth rise in p becomes a hockey stick in pk. Nothing in the model jumped; the harsh all-or-nothing metric bent the curve.

    The paper tested this. Of the 39 BIG-bench metrics, only a few showed emergence, and just two (both discontinuous or all-or-nothing) accounted for most claimed emergent abilities. A continuous measure, such as per-token probability of the right answer, made curves smooth, and the authors even produced "emergent" abilities in vision models by picking a harsh metric.

    The second ingredient is sampling noise. On 100 questions with a true success rate of 0.5%, you usually see 0 or 1 correct. Every small model scores "0", then the first successes appear. It looks like a switch because the ruler had no resolution at the bottom.

    That settles part of the question, not all. Where it stands:

    • Mirage in the metric: well supported for many reported cases. A smooth underlying quantity hidden by a discontinuous metric is a measurement effect.
    • Usefulness can still jump. A model at 92% per digit that gets an 8-digit sum right half the time is practically different from one at 2%. The jump in usefulness is real even if the jump in skill is not.
    • Some thresholds look real. Du et al. (2024), tracking pre-training loss instead of size, found tasks that stay near chance until loss crosses a value, then rise, sometimes even on continuous metrics. Other work finds U-shaped or inverted-U scaling on multiple-choice tasks. Not closed.
    • The three are cousins. In each, gradual internal change meets a threshold. Davies et al. (2024) propose a unified view of memorising and generalising circuits competing.

    At the bank, the same lens applies:

    • "100% training accuracy" model under review. Common and not alarming for gradient boosting on card-fraud data. Insist on a time-ordered holdout.
    • Small labelled sets. 300 confirmed AML cases and a flexible model may sit near the peak, where extra cases can hurt. A modest ridge penalty is cheap insurance.
    • Vendor claims of "emergent" abilities. Ask about the metric and test-set size.
    • In-house LLM evaluations (trade-finance documents, policy questions). Use graded metrics and enough questions per category, or you will see "nothing works" then "everything works".
    • Early-stopping policy. Stop on flat validation loss for ordinary models; know a late gain is possible in small algorithmic or heavily regularised settings.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mostly these are over-readings of a striking finding. Each has a quick check.

    • "Bigger always generalises, so ignore overfitting"

      Double descent depends on regularisation, data structure and noise. A flexible model on 500 noisy rows can sit at the bad peak.

      Spot itAsk for out-of-time validation error versus model size, with a regularisation sweep. Flat or worsening with size means the claim fails.
    • "Zero training error means the model is broken"

      The reverse over-reading. A boosted model with a perfect training fit can be fine.

      Spot itLook at the gap on a genuine holdout, not the training number.
    • Reading a peak as a bug

      A test-error spike when you add data or features can be the interpolation threshold, not a data error.

      Spot itCheck whether feature count is near the number of training rows, and whether a ridge penalty removes the spike.
    • Stopping too early (or too late)

      Cutting a run at a validation plateau can miss a late gain in algorithmic settings; running forever on noisy bank labels fits the noise.

      Spot itPlot validation metrics over the whole run, check whether the task has a compact rule, and cap the budget in advance.
    • Citing a grokking paper to justify a bank model

      Those results are on tiny rule-based tasks with clean labels.

      Spot itAsk what the labels and rule are. Fraud and credit have noisy labels and no neat rule.
    • Accepting "emergent ability" from a headline metric

      A sharp curve on exact match, a fixed-threshold accuracy, or pass/fail on a few dozen items.

      Spot itAsk for a continuous metric on the same models, and the number of test items with intervals.
    • Declaring "no emergence" from a smooth metric

      The opposite error. A smooth score can hide that the practical capability, the whole answer right, really does switch on.

      Spot itPick the metric that matches the business need first, then judge the curve on it.
    • Extrapolating from a handful of small models

      A fit through five points before the sharp rise has started.

      Spot itCount the models and sizes; ask for the prediction interval. Treat a threshold forecast as a hypothesis.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A linear model on 40 random features fits 40 training rows exactly. Why is its test error likely worse than at 20 or 200 features?

    At 40 there is one exact fit, so it must absorb all the label noise with no slack; the feature matrix is near-singular and the weights huge. At 20 variance is lower; at 200 the minimum-norm fit spreads noise over many small weights.

    2. Name three things that flatten the double-descent peak.

    A ridge penalty or weight decay, early stopping, and ensembling. Cleaner labels also shrink it, since noise is what the peak amplifies.

    3. In grokking, why does the general circuit eventually win when both fit the training set equally?

    It gives the same training margin with smaller weights, so weight decay erodes the memorising circuit faster. As memorisation weakens, training loss is no longer fully satisfied and gradients strengthen the general circuit. Test accuracy rises when it passes a threshold.

    4. Per-digit accuracy is 85%. What exact-match rate do you expect on a 6-digit answer, assuming independence? On a 2-digit answer?

    0.856 ≈ 0.377 (38%); 0.852 ≈ 0.7225 (72%). The same skill gives very different headline scores.

    5. A benchmark has 50 questions and a model scores 0 of 50. Can you conclude it cannot do the task?

    No. At a true rate of 2%, zero correct happens about 36% of the time (0.9850 ≈ 0.364). The 95% upper bound after 0 of 50 is about 6% (the "rule of three": 3/50). Small test sets cannot resolve low scores.

    6. Does the mirage argument mean larger models are not meaningfully more capable?

    No. It says the shape of improvement, sudden or smooth, depends on the metric. Larger models are better, and for tasks needing every step right, usable capability can still rise steeply with size.

    7. A colleague says "the model has 100% train accuracy, reject it." What do you ask?

    What the out-of-time validation result is and how it compares with a regularised or simpler baseline. Perfect training fit is neither necessary nor sufficient for a problem; the holdout gap is what matters.
