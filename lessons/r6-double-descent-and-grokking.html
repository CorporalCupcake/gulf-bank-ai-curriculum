    Double Descent and Grokking

    Part 1

    What these two oddities are

    In one sentenceDouble descent and grokking are two cases where a very flexible model does better after it has memorised its training data, which the classic "overfitting" story says should not happen.

    Running example: predicting house prices from size, using a few hundred past sales whose prices contain some random noise.

    How it differs from ordinary overfitting

    • Classic overfitting: too flexible means the model memorises noise, so test error rises and keeps rising. Aim for the middle.
    • Double descent: test error rises, peaks, then falls again as the model keeps growing, often below the earlier best.
    • Grokking: a model memorises early, scores at chance on new data, and much later suddenly learns the real rule. The "wait" is in training time, not model size.

    Both fit a single picture. A flexible model has many ways to fit the training data. The first fits it finds are brittle memorisations. With more capacity, more training or a push towards small weights, it settles on simpler fits that generalise.

    Part 2

    How it works

    Double descent, in house prices

    Say we have n = 100 past sales and we build p features per house, then fit a model. Grow p and watch test error.

    1. p well below 100. The usual U: more features cut bias (systematic miss), then add variance (sensitivity to which sales we happened to see).
    2. p close to 100. Exactly one set of weights fits every sale, so it must copy the price noise with no slack. Weights become huge and test error spikes.
    3. p well above 100. Many weight sets fit exactly. Gradient descent from zero, or a tiny penalty, picks the smallest one. It spreads the fit thinly over many directions, soaks up the noise harmlessly, and test error falls again.

    Three details matter in practice.

    • Noise makes the peak. Clean labels give a small bump; noisy labels give a tall one.
    • Regularisation removes it. A ridge penalty, weight decay, early stopping or averaging many models flattens the peak. That is why tuned production models rarely show it.
    • It also appears over time and data. Adding rows can slide the peak onto your model size, so more data can briefly hurt. Over training time, error can fall, rise, then fall again.

    Grokking, in modular addition

    Power and colleagues (2022) trained small transformers on addition modulo 97 (clock arithmetic) using only part of all possible sums. Training accuracy hit 100% within a few hundred steps while validation stayed near chance. After 10 to 100 times more training, validation shot up.

    Think of a student who memorises the times table and only weeks later spots the pattern.

    1. Memorise fast. A lookup table of the training sums is quick to build but needs big weights.
    2. Pressure builds. Weight decay slowly punishes big weights.
    3. A general rule wins. For this task the network places numbers on a circle (sines and cosines) and adds angles (Nanda et al., 2023). It reaches the same training score with smaller weights, so it slowly takes over.

    Underneath, progress is gradual: hidden measures improve smoothly long before test accuracy moves. The jump is a threshold on a smooth process.

    Part 3

    How to read it

    • "Training error is zero, so it is overfit" is a rule of thumb, not a law. What counts is error on unseen data from the period and population the model will face.
    • Memorising and generalising can coexist. Zhang and colleagues (2017) showed image networks can fit random labels perfectly yet still generalise on real labels. Which exact fit you land on matters, not whether you fit exactly.
    • A flat test curve during training is not proof of failure. In grokking it can be a long wait. Check whether weight decay and a compact rule are present before giving up.
    • A peak at "p = n" is a warning sign. If your model size sits right at the number of training rows, try a penalty or a bigger model.

    A related debate: sudden abilities

    Large language models seem to gain skills abruptly as they scale. Much of that "emergence" depends on the scoring rule: an all-or-nothing metric shows a sharp jump where a smooth one shows steady progress. Treat sharp jumps with care and check the metric first.

    Show the idea behind the second descent

    Benign overfitting (Bartlett et al., 2020): when inputs have many weak directions, a model can park the noise across a huge number of unimportant directions while a few strong ones carry the signal. This is proved for linear models and is a strong empirical pattern, not a guarantee, for deep networks.

    Part 4

    Is it usable today?

    IdeaWhat you seeWhat to do in practice
    Classic U-shapeError rises past the sweet spotStill the right default for tabular models such as trees and regressions
    Double descentA peak where model size matches data sizeAvoid sitting on the peak. Regularise, or go clearly larger or smaller
    GrokkingLate jump after perfect training accuracyMostly a research setting. Keep weight decay on and train longer on small rule-based tasks
    EmergenceSudden skill gains at scaleCheck the metric before claiming a jump is real
    • Strong at: explaining why huge models are not hopeless, and why a validation set beats a rule of thumb.
    • Weak at: giving a recipe. Most of this is proved for simple models and only observed for deep ones.

    In banking, noisy labels such as mislabelled fraud or fuzzy default definitions make a peak more likely, so judge every model on held-out data from the right period.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating 100% training accuracy as proof of overfitting

      It is a prompt to look at held-out error, not a verdict.

    • Expecting double descent in a tuned model

      A well-chosen penalty, early stopping or averaging usually removes the peak.

    • Stopping training the moment test accuracy stalls

      In grokking-like settings the real gain comes much later.

    • Calling a jump "emergent" without checking the metric

      All-or-nothing scoring can make smooth progress look sudden.

    1. Where does the test-error peak in double descent sit?

    Where the model can only just fit the training data, about as many features as training rows.

    2. Why does a bigger model then do better?

    With many exact fits available, training favours the smallest-weight one, which spreads the noise thinly instead of distorting the signal.

    3. What is grokking?

    Perfect training accuracy early, chance-level test accuracy for a long time, then a sudden jump when the network replaces memorisation with a general rule.

    4. Which settings help or remove these effects?

    Regularisation removes the double-descent peak. Weight decay is what drives the switch from memorising to the general rule in grokking.

    5. Why be careful with "emergent abilities"?

    Part of the sudden jump comes from the scoring rule, not from the model.
