    When Overfitting Stops Being the Rule: Double Descent, Grokking and the Emergence Debate

    Why models that memorise their training data can still generalise, why some networks learn the real rule thousands of steps after they stop improving, and how much of the "sudden new abilities" of large models is real and how much is the ruler you measured with.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTest error is not a simple U-shape in model size or training time: it can rise, peak where the model just barely fits the data, then fall again, and a model can sit at perfect training accuracy for a long time before it finds the rule; and when a large model seems to gain an ability suddenly, first check whether the metric, not the model, made it look sudden.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Every analyst learns the same story (F4.1). A simple model underfits. A flexible one overfits: it fits the noise in the training set and does badly on new data. So you look for the sweet spot in the middle, and if training accuracy hits 100% you worry. For a tree-based credit model on 40,000 rows, that advice works.

    Modern deep learning breaks it. Zhang and colleagues (2017) took image networks that generalise well and showed that the very same networks could also memorise random labels perfectly. Capacity to memorise is not what decides whether a model generalises. Yet people kept making networks bigger, past the point where they interpolate (hit zero training error), and test error went down again. Three odd things follow, and this lesson takes each in turn.

    • Double descent. As you add parameters, test error rises towards a peak at the exact size where the model can just barely fit every training point, then falls, often below the earlier best.
    • Grokking. A small network memorises its training set early. Test accuracy sits at chance. Then, long after, with no change in the training loss, test accuracy jumps to nearly 100%.
    • Emergence, and the claim that it is a mirage. Large language models appear to gain abilities abruptly at some size. A 2023 paper argued that much of this comes from the scoring rule, not from the model.

    One picture ties the first two together. A flexible enough model has many ways to fit the training data. The ones it finds first are usually brittle memorisations. Given more capacity, more time, or a mild push towards simplicity (weight decay, small weights), training tends to settle on simpler solutions among those that fit. Whether you see a peak, a delay or a smooth curve depends on how that preference for simplicity plays out.

    Hold onto this"Training error is zero, so it must be overfit" is a heuristic, not a law. It is a good default for small tabular models and a poor one for overparameterised ones. The right test is always error on data the model never saw, in the same period and population it will face.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Double descent: the peak at the interpolation threshold

    Take the simplest setting where it appears: linear regression on p features fitted to n training rows, where the features are random functions of the inputs (a "random features" model, close kin to a one-hidden-layer network with fixed first layer). Increase p.

    • p well below n. Classical regime. More features reduce bias, then add variance. The U-shape from F4.1.
    • p = n. There is exactly one set of weights that fits every training point, and it is forced. Noise in the labels must be matched with no slack, so the weights get large and wild. Test error spikes. Mathematically, the training feature matrix is nearly singular here (its smallest singular values are tiny), and inverting it amplifies the noise.
    • p well above n. Infinitely many weight vectors fit the training data. Pick the one with the smallest norm (what gradient descent from zero, or a tiny ridge penalty, effectively does). Extra features now give the fit room to spread itself thinly across many directions, so each weight is small and the noise is absorbed without distorting the signal much. Test error falls again.
    w^min-norm=ΦT(ΦΦT)−1y

    Φ is the n × p matrix of features for the training rows and y the labels. When p > n this picks, from all weight vectors that fit the data exactly, the one with the smallest length. Near p = n the matrix Φ ΦT is close to singular, so its inverse is huge and the weights blow up. Hastie et al. (2022) analyse exactly this and predict the peak; Belkin et al. (2019) showed the same shape in neural nets and forests; Nakkiran et al. (2021) catalogued it across architectures.

    Three details that matter in a meeting.

    • The peak moves with the data, not just the model. Hold the model fixed and add training data: the peak can pass through your model size, and for a while more data makes test error worse ("sample-wise" double descent). You can also see it along training time ("epoch-wise"): error falls, rises, then falls again.
    • Noise is what makes the peak. With clean labels the spike is small. With noisy labels (the banking norm: mislabelled fraud, ambiguous default definitions) it is large. Hence label noise and the peak are linked in nearly every analysis.
    • Regularisation removes it. A ridge penalty, weight decay, early stopping or averaging many models all flatten the peak. Nakkiran and colleagues showed that with optimal regularisation, test error is no longer non-monotonic in their linear setting. This is why you rarely meet it in tuned production models.

    Why is the far side good rather than just "not as bad"? The concept is benign overfitting (Bartlett et al., 2020): when there are many more directions of small variation in the features than training points, the model can fit the noise by spending a little weight on a huge number of unimportant directions, which barely affects predictions elsewhere, while the signal is carried by a few strong directions. Whether that holds depends on the structure of the data, which is why the story is a theorem for linear models and a strong empirical pattern, not a guarantee, for deep nets.

    What you saw is the mechanism, not just the picture. The spike is a numerical-conditioning problem plus noise. The second descent is the minimum-norm preference at work. If you take away only one thing: between 'fits exactly' and 'generalises' there is no contradiction; what matters is which of the many exact fits you land on.

    Grokking: generalisation that arrives late

    Power and colleagues (2022) trained small transformers on tiny algorithmic tasks, such as addition modulo 97, using only part of the possible examples. Training accuracy reached 100% within a few hundred steps. Validation accuracy stayed near chance. Then, after ten or a hundred times more training, validation accuracy shot up. They called it grokking.

    Subsequent work made the mechanism concrete, at least for these small tasks.

    • Two solutions compete. One is a lookup table that memorises the training pairs. The other is a general algorithm. For modular addition, Nanda et al. (2023) reverse-engineered it: the network represents numbers as points on a circle (sines and cosines of a few frequencies) and adds angles with trigonometric identities. That is a real algorithm and it works on pairs it never saw.
    • Memorisation is quick; the general circuit is slow but cheaper to maintain. The general solution needs fewer weights to produce the same confidence on the training set. Varma et al. (2023) call this circuit efficiency: with weight decay pressing on the total size of the weights, the efficient circuit slowly wins.
    • It is gradual underneath. Nanda and colleagues found hidden progress measures that improve smoothly long before test accuracy moves. The jump is a threshold effect on a continuous process, an idea we will meet again in the emergence debate.
    • The ingredients are specific. Weight decay, a training set that is a modest fraction of all cases, and a task with a compact rule. With more data, grokking comes sooner and shrinks. With too little it never comes. Liu et al. (2022) tie it to the size of the weights ("Omnigrok") and show grokking-like behaviour on non-algorithmic tasks if you start from large initial weights.

    Two sober points. First, grokking is firmly established on small algorithmic tasks and some other settings, but whether large language models "grok" in ordinary pretraining is an open question, since there the data is huge and training runs rarely sit still long enough. Second, the lesson for practice is about stopping rules: if you stop when validation loss stops improving, you can stop before a late gain. Equally, it is no licence to train forever hoping for a miracle on a bank dataset full of noisy labels, where memorising the noise is exactly what you are warned against.

    Is emergence a mirage?

    Wei et al. (2022) listed tasks where performance sat near chance for small language models and then rose sharply beyond some size, and called such abilities emergent: absent in smaller models, present in larger ones, not predictable by extrapolating small ones. The idea mattered for strategy and safety: if abilities switch on unpredictably, you cannot know what the next model can do.

    Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) argued that much of this is produced by the way we score. Take multi-digit addition. Suppose a model's chance of getting any one digit right improves smoothly with size, say from 60% to 74% to 92%. An answer with k digits is marked right only if all k digits are right:

    exact match=pk

    p is the chance of one digit being correct and k the number of digits. If p = 0.6 and k = 8, exact match is 0.68 = 1.7%: invisible. At p = 0.74 it is 9%. At p = 0.92 it is 51%. A smooth rise in p becomes a hockey stick in pk. Nothing in the model changed abruptly. A harsh, all-or-nothing metric did the bending.

    The paper tested the claim rather than just arguing it. It found that, among the 39 metrics BIG-bench reported, only a few showed emergence, and just two of them accounted for the large majority of claimed emergent abilities; both are discontinuous or all-or-nothing. Switching to a continuous measure (such as per-token probability of the right answer) made the curves smooth; and the authors even produced "emergent" abilities in vision models by choosing a harsh metric on purpose.

    The second ingredient is sampling noise. If your benchmark has 100 questions and the true success rate is 0.5%, you will usually see 0 or 1 correct. Below a certain size, every model scores "0". Then the true rate climbs and you see the first successes. It looks like a switch because the ruler had no resolution at the bottom.

    That settles part of the question but not all of it. Honest summary of where the argument stands:

    • Mirage in the metric: well supported for many reported cases. Where a smooth underlying quantity exists, and a discontinuous metric hides it, "emergence" is a measurement effect.
    • It does not say large models are predictable in every way. Users care about exact match. A model that is 92% per digit but gets an 8-digit sum right half the time is practically different from one that gets it right 2% of the time. The jump in usefulness is real even if the jump in underlying skill is not.
    • Some thresholds look real. Work that tracks pre-training loss rather than parameter count (Du et al., 2024) found tasks that stay near chance until loss crosses a value, whatever the model's size, and then rise, even with continuous metrics in some cases. Other work finds U-shaped or inverted-U scaling that explains plateaus on multiple-choice questions. The question is not closed.
    • Grokking and double descent are cousins. In all three, a gradual internal change meets a threshold. Davies and colleagues (2024) propose a unified view in which a memorising and a generalising circuit compete, and more recent preprints (Part 4) push on the same idea.

    Grokking was explained by finding the circuit. The same toolkit is used on real language models.

    Loss falls as a smooth power law in compute; the emergence debate is about how that maps to task scores.

    Item response theory and time-horizon metrics are attempts at the continuous, well-calibrated scoring the mirage paper asks for. See also task time horizons.

    The second ingredient of the mirage is a benchmark too small to resolve low scores. Intervals fix that.

    F7.1 · MetricsChoice of metric changes the story

    The same discipline as picking MAE or RMSE: the metric is part of the claim. Also relevant: BLEU, ROUGE and friends.

    G3 · Model validationWhat a validator should ask

    Validators rely on out-of-time test error, not on training-fit heuristics, which is exactly what survives this lesson.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Reviewing a model that "has 100% training accuracy". For gradient boosting on card-fraud data this is common and not alarming by itself. Insist on a time-ordered holdout, not a rule.
    • Small labelled sets near the threshold. A team with 300 confirmed AML cases and a flexible model may be sitting near the interpolation peak, where adding a few more cases can make things worse. A modest ridge penalty is cheap insurance.
    • Vendor and board claims about LLM capability. "The new model has an emergent ability to do X" or "capability will appear unpredictably at a larger size". The mirage lens gives you questions about the metric and the test-set size.
    • Choosing evaluations for in-house LLM use cases. For reading trade-finance documents or answering policy questions, use graded or continuous metrics and enough questions per category, or you will see "nothing works" and then "everything works" without understanding why.
    • Early stopping policy. Whether to cut a training run when validation loss flattens. For ordinary models, yes. Know that for small algorithmic or heavily regularised settings, a late gain is possible.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The existence of double descent and grokking is settled for the settings where they were found. The live questions are how far they extend to large models, what unifies them, and what the right way to measure and predict capability is.

    1. 2017
      Networks can memorise random labels yet generalise on real ones

      The finding that undermined capacity-counting explanations of generalisation.

      Grokking and "emergent abilities"

      Power et al. reported late generalisation on algorithmic tasks. In the same year Wei et al. catalogued abilities that seemed to appear abruptly with scale, and Ganguli et al. described scaling as smooth in loss yet surprising in specific capabilities.

      Power et al., 2022; Wei et al., TMLR, 2022; Ganguli et al., FAccT, 2022
    5. 2023
      Circuits explain grokking; metrics explain emergence

      Nanda et al. reverse-engineered the modular-addition algorithm and defined smooth progress measures. Varma et al. explained grokking through circuit efficiency.     6. 2024
      One story for three phenomena, and loss-based emergence

      Davies et al. proposed that grokking, double descent and emergent abilities all arise from competition between memorising and generalising circuits. Du et al. defined emergence by pre-training loss thresholds instead of size. A separate line found U-shaped and inverted-U scaling in multiple-choice tasks that can precede a sharp rise (arXiv 2410.01692).

      Davies et al., arXiv 2402.15175; Du et al., arXiv 2403.15796 (cited from memory)
    7. 2025
      Grokking as a statistical effect

      A preprint argues that grokking is largely driven by a distribution shift between training and test data: subsampling a subclass can induce it, and removing that subclass prevents it. A further 2025 paper revisits why LLM abilities appear emergent, linking the debate to in-context learning.

      arXiv 2502.01774; arXiv 2508.04401 (both preprints)
    8. 2026
      Two speeds of learning, and grokking in simple models

      A preprint decomposes both grokking and epoch-wise double descent into a fast readout and a slower representation, reporting that removing label noise eliminates the double-descent effect in its experiment. Others study grokking in logistic regression and through inductive bias in transformers. These are recent preprints; treat the claims as proposals.

      arXiv 2605.27078; arXiv 2602.08302; arXiv 2602.06702 (all preprints)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mostly these are over-readings: using a striking finding where it does not apply. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "Bigger always generalises, so ignore overfitting"

      Double descent is conditional on regularisation, data structure and noise. A flexible model on 500 rows of noisy labels can easily sit near the bad peak.

      Spot itAsk for out-of-time validation error versus model size, with a regularisation sweep. If error is flat or worsening with size, the claim is not holding.
    • "Zero training error means the model is broken"

      The reverse over-reading. A boosted model with perfect training fit can be fine.

      Spot itLook at the gap on a genuine holdout, not the training number.
    • Reading a peak as a bug

      A test-error spike when you add data or features can be the interpolation threshold, not a data error.

      Spot itCheck whether feature count is close to the number of training rows, and whether a ridge penalty removes the spike.
    • Stopping too early (or too late)

      Cutting a run when validation flatlines can miss a late gain in algorithmic settings; running forever on noisy bank labels fits the noise.

      Spot itPlot validation metrics over the whole run and check whether a task has a compact underlying rule. Cap the budget in advance.
    • Citing a grokking paper to justify a bank model

      The results are on tiny rule-based tasks with clean labels.

      Spot itAsk what the labels and the underlying rule are. Fraud and credit have noisy labels and no neat rule to find.
    • Accepting "emergent ability" from a headline metric

      A sharp curve on exact match, accuracy at a fixed threshold, or pass/fail on a few dozen items.

      Spot itAsk for a continuous metric on the same models and for the number of test items with intervals.
    • Declaring "no emergence" from a smooth metric

      The opposite error. A smooth score can hide the fact that the practical capability, getting the whole answer right, really does switch on.

      Spot itDecide first which metric matches the business need, then judge the curve on that.
    • Extrapolating a curve from a handful of small models

      A fit through five points where the sharp rise has not yet started.

      Spot itCount the models and the sizes; ask what the interval on the prediction is. Treat a forecast of a threshold as a hypothesis.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There is a rule of thumb that a model which fits its training data perfectly must be overfit. For very large models that is not reliable, so we judge every model only on data it has not seen, from a later period. And when a vendor says a new model has suddenly acquired a new ability, my first question is how it was scored. Often the ability grew smoothly and the scoring made it look like a switch. What counts for us is the score on our own tasks, with enough test cases to trust it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Double descent shows test error peaking where the model first interpolates, then falling again; the minimum-norm solution among the many exact fits generalises, and regularisation flattens the peak. Grokking is delayed generalisation when a quick memorising circuit is slowly displaced by a more efficient general one under weight decay. For emergence, Schaeffer et al. showed that all-or-nothing metrics such as exact match turn smooth per-token gains into apparent jumps, and small test sets hide low scores. I'd still check pre-training-loss analyses, which suggest some thresholds are real, and I'd evaluate with graded metrics and intervals.”
    “If a bigger model can fit everything, why doesn't it just memorise?”

    It can, and given random labels it does. With real data there are many exact fits, and training from small weights with a mild penalty tends to reach the simplest, smallest-norm one, which shares structure with the signal. The guarantee is proven for linear and random-feature models; for deep nets it is an empirical regularity with partial explanations.

    “Should we worry about the double-descent peak in our models?”

    Only if the model is flexible, the labelled data is small, and the feature count is close to the number of rows, which can happen in AML or rare-default modelling. The cheap defences are a ridge penalty or early stopping and validating across several sample sizes. I would not pick model size by chasing the second descent on small data.

    “What is grokking and does it matter to us?”

    A model that fits training data quickly but generalises only after much longer training, seen on small rule-based tasks like modular arithmetic. It matters mainly as proof that training loss alone cannot tell you whether the model has found the rule, and for how we think about stopping rules and interpretability. It is not a recipe for bank models with noisy labels.

    “So are emergent abilities fake?”

    Not fake, but often overstated. Many reported jumps flatten into smooth curves under continuous metrics, and low scores on small test sets are too noisy to read. Yet the user-visible capability can still switch on, because an eight-digit sum needs every digit right. And there is evidence that some behaviours track loss thresholds. So: measure on a continuous scale, report intervals, and judge on the business metric.

    “Can we predict what the next model will be able to do?”

    Partly. Loss is predictable from scaling laws, and smooth precursor metrics can sometimes forecast when a task becomes solvable. But forecasts from few small models are fragile. I would plan around capability tests on our own tasks when a model arrives, not around predictions.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A model has 40 training rows and a linear model on 40 random features fits them exactly. Why is its test error likely worse than at 20 or 200 features?

    At 40 features there is only one exact fit, so it must absorb all the label noise with no slack; the feature matrix is nearly singular, so the weights are huge. At 20 there is less variance, and at 200 the minimum-norm fit spreads the noise over many small weights.

    2. Name three things that flatten the double-descent peak.

    A ridge penalty or weight decay, early stopping, and averaging many models (ensembling). Cleaner labels also shrink it, since noise is what the peak amplifies.

    3. In grokking, why does the general circuit eventually win when both fit the training set equally?

    It produces the same training margin with smaller weights, so weight decay pressure erodes the memorising circuit faster. As memorisation weakens, training loss stops being fully satisfied, and gradients flow to the general circuit, which strengthens. Test accuracy rises when it passes a threshold.

    4. Per-digit accuracy is 85%. What exact-match rate do you expect on a 6-digit answer, assuming independence? On a 2-digit answer?

    0.856 ≈ 0.377 (38%). 0.852 ≈ 0.7225 (72%). The same underlying skill gives very different headline scores.

    5. A benchmark has 50 questions. A model scores 0 of 50. Can you conclude it cannot do the task?

    No. With 50 questions a true success rate of 2% gives zero correct about 36% of the time (0.9850 ≈ 0.364). The 95% upper bound on the rate when you see 0 of 50 is about 6% (the "rule of three": 3/50). Small test sets cannot resolve low scores.

    6. Does the mirage argument mean larger models are not more capable in a meaningful way?

    No. It says the shape of the improvement, sudden or smooth, depends on the metric. Larger models are better, and for tasks that need every step right the usable capability can still rise steeply with size.

    7. A colleague says "the model has 100% train accuracy, reject it." What do you ask?

    What the out-of-time validation result is and how it compares with a regularised or simpler baseline. Perfect training fit is neither necessary nor sufficient for a problem; the gap on a true holdout is what matters.
