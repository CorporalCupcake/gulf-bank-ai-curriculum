    When Overfitting Stops Being the Rule: Double Descent, Grokking and the Emergence Debate

    Why models that memorise their training data can still generalise, why some networks learn the rule late, and how much of the "sudden new abilities" of large models is the ruler.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTest error is not a simple U-shape, a model can memorise for a long time before finding the rule, and when a large model seems to gain an ability suddenly, first check whether the metric made it look sudden.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    The classic story (F4.1): too simple underfits, too flexible memorises noise, so aim for the middle and worry if training accuracy hits 100%. For a tree-based credit model on 40,000 rows that holds. For very large models it can mislead. Three findings show how:

    • Double descent. Test error peaks where a model can just fit the data, then falls again.
    • Grokking. A network memorises early, sits at chance on new data, and much later suddenly learns the real rule.
    • Emergence. Large models seem to gain abilities abruptly, but much of that comes from the scoring rule.
    Hold onto this"Training error is zero, so it is overfit" is a heuristic, not a law. The real test is error on unseen data from the period and population the model will face.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Double descent: the peak at the interpolation threshold

    As a model gets more parameters, test error rises to a peak at the size where it can only just fit every training row, then falls again, often below the earlier best. At that exact size there is one way to fit the data, so the model must copy the label noise and swings wildly. Beyond it there are many exact fits, and the smooth, small-weight one that training tends to find absorbs the noise harmlessly.

    • Regularisation removes it. Ridge, weight decay, early stopping or averaging flatten it, which is why tuned production models rarely show it.
    • Data moves it. Adding rows can put the peak on your model size, so more data can briefly hurt.

    Fitting exactly and generalising do not contradict; what matters is which exact fit you land on.

    Grokking: generalisation that arrives late

    Small networks on tasks like modular addition reach 100% training accuracy quickly, sit at chance on new cases, then 10 to 100 times later jump to near-perfect test accuracy.

    Two solutions compete: a lookup table, which is fast to learn, and a general rule, which is slow but reaches the same fit with smaller weights. Weight decay slowly favours the general one, so the jump is a threshold on a smooth process.

    Grokking is solid on small rule-based tasks; whether LLMs grok in ordinary pretraining is open. Stopping at a validation plateau can miss a late gain, but that is no licence to train forever on noisy bank labels.

    Is emergence a mirage?

    Large language models seem to gain abilities abruptly: near chance when small, then a sharp rise past some size.

    Schaeffer and colleagues (2023) argued much of this is the scoring. Exact match on a multi-digit sum needs every digit right, so smooth gains per digit look like a hockey stick. A continuous metric gives smooth curves.

    Small test sets add to it: with 100 questions and a 0.5% true rate, every small model scores zero.

    That settles part of the question, not all.

    • Mirage in the metric is well supported for many reported cases.
    • Usefulness can still jump. A model that gets an 8-digit sum right half the time is practically different from one at 2%, even if the skill rose smoothly.
    • Some thresholds may be real. Tracking pre-training loss instead of size, some tasks stay near chance until loss crosses a value. Not closed.

    At the bank:

    • "100% training accuracy" under review. Normal for boosting on fraud data. Insist on a time-ordered holdout.
    • Small labelled sets. 300 AML cases and a flexible model may sit near the peak. A modest ridge penalty helps.
    • Vendor "emergent" claims and in-house LLM evaluations. Ask about the metric and test-set size, and use graded metrics.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "Bigger always generalises, so ignore overfitting"

      Double descent depends on regularisation and noise.

      Spot itAsk for out-of-time error against model size, with a regularisation sweep.
    • Citing a grokking paper to justify a bank model

      Those results are on tiny rule-based tasks with clean labels. Bank labels are noisy.

      Spot itAsk what the labels and the rule are.
    • Accepting "emergent ability" from a headline metric

      A sharp curve on exact match, or pass/fail on a few dozen items, may be the ruler.

      Spot itAsk for a continuous metric on the same models and the number of test items.
    • Declaring "no emergence" from a smooth metric

      The opposite error. A smooth score can hide that the whole answer right really does switch on in practice.

      Spot itPick the metric that matches the business need, then judge the curve.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can a model that fits its training rows exactly have worse test error than a slightly smaller or much bigger one?

    At the exact-fit size there is only one way to fit, so it must copy all the label noise. Smaller models have less variance, and much bigger ones can choose a smooth fit that spreads the noise thinly.

    2. In grokking, why does the general solution eventually win when both fit the training set equally?

    It reaches the same fit with smaller weights, so weight decay erodes the memorising solution faster. Test accuracy jumps once the general one passes a threshold.

    3. A benchmark has 50 questions and a model scores 0. Can you conclude it cannot do the task?

    No. A model with a true rate of a few per cent often scores zero on 50 questions. Small test sets cannot resolve low scores.

    4. A colleague says "100% train accuracy, reject it." What do you ask?

    What the out-of-time validation result is, and how it compares with a simpler or regularised baseline. The holdout gap matters, not the training number.
