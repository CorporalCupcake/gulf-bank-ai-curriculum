    Trees, Forests, Boosting and the Art of Holding Them Back

    How a decision tree picks its questions, why averaging many unstable trees works, how boosting builds a model one correction at a time, and how L1, L2, shrinkage and early stopping decide how much any of it is allowed to memorise.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA tree is a flowchart learned greedily from data; one tree memorises, so we either average many (forests) or stack small ones that each fix the last one's errors (boosting), and every knob that matters, from tree depth to learning rate to L2 and early stopping, is a way of limiting how much of the noise gets learned.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A senior credit officer carries a flowchart in her head. Is the instalment over 45% of income? If yes, any missed payment in six months? If no, is the customer salary-transferred to us? Each question splits applicants in two, until she says "approve" or "decline".

    A decision tree is that flowchart learned from data. At each step it asks: which one yes/no question about one feature separates defaulters from non-defaulters most cleanly? It splits, then repeats inside each half.

    Strengths: handles numbers and categories, needs no scaling, finds interactions on its own, copes with missing values, and can be read aloud. Weakness: left alone, it asks questions until each leaf holds a handful of customers, memorising the training data, noise included. Change the sample slightly and the tree changes. In F4.1's terms, a deep tree has low bias and very high variance.

    The rest of the lesson answers that weakness:

    • Random forest: ask a hundred officers, each shown a different random batch of cases and only some of the questions. Average their verdicts; quirks cancel.
    • Gradient boosting: start with a rough estimate, then hire a junior to fix what's still wrong, then another. Each is deliberately weak; the sum is strong.
    • Regularisation: the rules on how much any one officer may memorise: depth, cases per leaf, correction size, when to stop hiring.

    On tabular bank data (customers, accounts, transactions, bureau fields), XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are still the default winners. First, see how one split is chosen.

    Hold onto thisA tree is greedy: at each node it takes the best split right now and never looks back. That makes it fast and unstable (a different sample can change the first split and everything below it), so the cure is to combine many trees, not polish one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A decision tree: impurity, gain and greedy growth

    A node's impurity measures how mixed it is, like a bag of sweets: all one flavour is pure, half and half is maximally mixed. The usual measure is Gini impurity:

    G=1−p2−(1−p)2=2p(1−p)

    p is the share of fraud in the node. Pure (p = 0 or 1) scores 0; 50/50 scores the maximum, 0.5. Plainly: the chance that two transactions drawn at random from the node have different labels. Entropy (F1.7) behaves similarly. For regression, impurity is the variance of the target in the node.

    The gain of a split is the parent's impurity minus the size-weighted impurity of the two children. Reading it: gain 0 means the split taught us nothing; a bigger gain means cleaner groups. The algorithm tries every feature and cut point (midpoints between sorted values), keeps the largest gain, and recurses until a node is pure, too small, too deep, or no split gains enough. A prediction is the leaf's average label: "7 of 40 customers like this defaulted, so 17.5%".

    Four consequences:

    • A tree is a step function. Predictions jump at cut points and are flat between. There is no extrapolation: ask about an income of AED 400,000 a month when the training maximum was 120,000 and you get the top bracket's answer. This matters for anything that trends, such as inflation or growth.
    • Interactions come free. A split under a split is an interaction, so trees capture "large amount, new beneficiary, at night" without engineering (F3.4).
    • Only the order of values matters. Log or scaling changes nothing, and outliers can't drag a fit the way they drag a regression line.
    • Impurity importance is biased. The default "feature importance" sums the gains of each feature's splits. It favours features with many distinct values and says nothing about direction. F8 covers better attributions.

    Deep trees also give uncalibrated probabilities: a leaf with two defaulters says 100%. Averaging and shrinkage fix most of this; F7.4 covers the rest.

    Random forests: bagging and decorrelation

    Think of a jury: twelve people who each saw different evidence and weigh different clues will rarely share one blind spot. Breiman's recipe has two random ingredients. First, bootstrap the rows (F2.2): each tree trains on a sample drawn with replacement, so about 63% of distinct rows appear. Second, each split sees only a random subset of features (often the square root of their number). Grow each tree deep (low bias, high variance), then average votes or probabilities.

    If each of B trees has variance σ² and any two trees' predictions have correlation ρ, the average has variance

    Var=ρσ2+(1−ρ)σ2B

    The second term vanishes as trees are added, so more trees never hurt but soon stop helping. The first term stays: it is the variance the trees share. Reading ρ: at 0.3, no number of trees takes variance below 0.3σ². Row and feature randomness exist to push ρ down, because if every tree makes the same mistake, averaging keeps it. Averaging leaves bias where it was, which is why trees are grown deep.

    Bonuses: the rows each tree never saw give a free out-of-bag validation error, and forests are forgiving. Hundreds of deep trees with a feature subset per split rarely do badly, so a forest is a strong first baseline. Tuned boosting usually edges it out, and forests are larger and slower to score.

    Gradient boosting: fit the mistakes, a little at a time

    Boosting is a relay of small corrections, like a golfer who plays each shot from where the last one landed. Start with a constant (the average default rate), measure what is still wrong, fit a small tree to that, and add a fraction of it:

    Fm(x) = Fm−1(x) + η · treem(x)

    η, the learning rate (shrinkage), is typically 0.01 to 0.1: each tree gets only a small say. With squared error each tree fits the residual (actual minus current prediction). For other losses, such as log loss, it fits the negative gradient of the loss, the same idea in general form and the source of the name (F1.3). Boosting is gradient descent over functions, one tree-shaped step per round.

    The trees are deliberately weak: depth 3 to 8, sometimes one split. The ensemble gets low bias, and variance stays controlled if you stop in time. Unlike a forest, more rounds can hurt: keep going and it fits noise. Hence early stopping.

    XGBoost adds second-order information: per row, the gradient g and curvature h of the loss. The best output for a leaf is

    w*=−∑g∑h+λ

    With squared error, g is minus the residual and h is 1, so this is the leaf's mean residual shrunk toward zero: sum of residuals ÷ (rows + λ). A leaf with 4 rows and mean residual 5 outputs 5 with λ = 0, but 20 ÷ 5 = 4 with λ = 1. Small, noisy leaves shrink most. That is L2 on leaf values. The same λ appears in the split gain, with a penalty γ per extra leaf, so a split must earn its keep.

    The hyperparameters that matter, in order:

    • Learning rate and rounds trade off. Lower rate, more rounds, slightly better, slower. Fix the rate (0.05 is a fine start) and let early stopping choose the rounds.
    • Depth or leaves. Depth 1 is an additive model with no interactions; deeper allows higher-order interactions. 3 to 8 covers most bank problems.
    • Minimum rows (or hessian weight) per leaf. The strongest guard against fitting a handful of customers. On rare-event data, make it large enough that a leaf can hold several positives (F3.5).
    • Row and column subsampling (e.g. 0.7 each, per tree). Borrowed from forests: decorrelates trees and cuts variance.
    • λ, α, γ. L2 and L1 on leaf values, and the minimum gain to split (next section).
    • Monotone constraints. Force the model to be non-decreasing in a feature, e.g. default risk never falls as days-past-due rises. XGBoost and LightGBM support this. Credit teams use it to meet explainability checks without losing accuracy.

    Regularisation: L2, L1 and early stopping

    Regularisation deliberately fits the training data a little worse so the model does better on new data. Think of a tailor who leaves a bit of slack: a suit cut exactly to today's posture fits badly tomorrow. The classic form adds a penalty on large coefficients.

    • L2 (ridge): penalty on the sum of squared coefficients. Shrinks everything smoothly toward zero, never exactly to it. With two features carrying the same information (salary and average monthly credit), it shares the weight between them and tames wild offsetting coefficients.
    • L1 (lasso): penalty on the sum of absolute values. Its corner at zero pushes coefficients exactly to zero, so it selects features. With a correlated pair it keeps one and drops the other, somewhat arbitrarily, so selection is unstable across samples.
    • Elastic net: a mix. Selects like lasso, spreads weight across correlated groups like ridge.

    Why it works: many flexible parameters and few examples make coefficients mostly noise, and that becomes prediction variance (F4.1). Shrinking trades a little bias for a bigger cut in variance. Meeting-friendly reading: L2 is a prior that "most effects are small", L1 that "most effects are exactly zero". The penalty strength λ says how strongly you believe it; choose it by cross-validation (F4.5, F4.9). Reading λ: bigger means simpler and more biased, smaller means flexible and noisy; the best value is the bottom of the test-error curve, not an extreme.

    Practicalities: standardise features before penalising linear models, never penalise the intercept, and compare λ on a log scale.

    Tree models use the same idea in other forms. All of them limit how much noise gets learned:

    • Structural limits: maximum depth, minimum rows per leaf, minimum gain to split (γ). No tiny groups.
    • Shrinkage: a small learning rate. Regularisation by caution.
    • Leaf-value penalties: λ (L2) and α (L1) in XGBoost, as in the leaf formula above.
    • Randomisation: row and column subsampling. Averaging noisy learners is the forest's whole trick.
    • Early stopping: stop when validation error hasn't improved for, say, 50 rounds. The data sets the complexity dial. Neural nets do the same with epochs (F6.3).

    Early stopping has a trap: the validation set that picked the stopping round has influenced the model, so it can't also be the final test. Keep three sets: train, validation (stopping and tuning), and a test touched once. For bank data, validation and test come later in time (F4.6).

    # XGBoost with early stopping, a monotone constraint, and a time-ordered split.
# train < valid < test in time. Test is only scored once, at the end.
import xgboost as xgb

model = xgb.XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=2000,        # an upper bound; early stopping picks the real number
    learning_rate=0.05,
    max_depth=4,
    min_child_weight=20,      # leaves must carry enough "weight" (a count, for log loss it is hessian mass)
    subsample=0.8,
    colsample_bytree=0.8,
    reg_lambda=5.0,           # L2 on leaf values
    reg_alpha=0.0,            # L1 on leaf values
    gamma=0.0,                # minimum gain to make a split
    monotone_constraints={"days_past_due": 1},   # risk never falls as this rises
    eval_metric="aucpr",
    early_stopping_rounds=50,
)
model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_valid, y_valid)], verbose=False)
print(model.best_iteration)
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training score reported as performance

      "The forest scores 0.999 AUC." A deep forest nearly memorises its training rows.

      Spot itAsk which data the number comes from. Only out-of-bag or a later-in-time hold-out is honest.
    • Early stopping on the test set

      Rounds are picked by watching test error, so the test score is optimistic.

      Spot itAsk which set chose the number of rounds. If two roles share a set, the final number is inflated.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat validation scores, weak live results. Boosted trees exploit information from neighbouring weeks.

      Spot itRe-run with train before validation before test. A sharp drop means the earlier number was borrowed from the future.
    • Leakage a tree finds instantly

      A field filled in after the outcome (collections status, account-closed flag) becomes the top split.

      Spot itOne feature with huge importance and near-perfect AUC. Ask when that field is populated relative to decision time.
    • Trusting impurity importance

      "Account ID bucket is the third most important feature." High-cardinality and noisy features inflate it.

      Spot itAdd a random-noise column and see where it ranks. Use permutation importance on held-out data or SHAP (F8).
    • Expecting trees to extrapolate

      Forecasting exposure for a growing book, or scoring a product with higher ticket sizes.

      Spot itPlot predictions beyond the training range: they go flat. Use a linear component or detrend the target.
    • Over-tuning on a small positive count

      200 defaults, 40 features, 500 rounds, and a search that wins by 0.004 AUC.

      Spot itCompute an interval for the metric (F2.2). If it is wider than the gain, the tuning found noise.
    • Raw leaf-wise boosting on small data

      LightGBM defaults on a few thousand rows build deep lopsided trees.

      Spot itLarge train-validation gap. Cut num_leaves, raise min_data_in_leaf, add subsampling.
    • Unstandardised features under L1 or L2

      The penalty bites AED-scale features far less than 0/1 flags.

      Spot itCheck for a scaling step before any penalised linear model. Trees don't need one.
    • Treating lasso's picks as "the true drivers"

      Among correlated predictors, lasso's pick is partly arbitrary and changes with the sample.

      Spot itRefit on bootstrap resamples. If the selected set changes a lot, it is not evidence of causes.
    • Scores used as probabilities unchecked

      Boosted scores rank well but aren't guaranteed calibrated, especially after class weights.

      Spot itReliability curve on a later hold-out (F7.4). Recalibrate before using the score in loss or provisioning.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A node holds 100 transactions, 20 fraud. A split sends 60 left (2 fraud) and 40 right (18 fraud). Compute the Gini gain.

    Parent: 2 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.32. Left: p = 2/60, impurity 2 × 0.0333 × 0.9667 = 0.0644. Right: p = 0.45, impurity 0.495. Weighted: 0.6 × 0.0644 + 0.4 × 0.495 = 0.2367. Gain = 0.32 − 0.2367 ≈ 0.083. The tree compares this with every other candidate split and takes the largest.

    2. 100 trees, each with variance 1, pairwise correlation 0.3. What is the variance of their average, and the limit with infinitely many trees?

    0.3 + 0.7/100 = 0.307. Limit: 0.3. Extra trees barely help; lowering the correlation (more row and feature randomness) moves the floor.

    3. A boosted-tree leaf holds 10 rows, all with residual +3 (squared error). What does it output with λ = 0, 1 and 10?

    Output = sum of residuals ÷ (rows + λ), and the sum is 30. λ = 0: 3.0. λ = 1: 30 ÷ 11 ≈ 2.73. λ = 10: 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5. Small leaves shrink most: a 100-row leaf with the same residual gives 300 ÷ 110 ≈ 2.73 at λ = 10.

    4. Two highly correlated features both genuinely predict default. What do lasso and ridge do as the penalty rises?

    Ridge shrinks both and keeps them close, sharing the effect. Lasso tends to keep one and zero the other, and which survives can flip with a different sample. A dropped feature is not evidence it doesn't matter.

    5. You pick the number of boosting rounds by early stopping on the test set and report the test AUC. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

    The test set influenced the model, so its AUC is optimistic. Use three time-ordered sets: train, validation (stopping and tuning), and a final test scored once.

    6. You lower the learning rate from 0.1 to 0.01 and change nothing else. What happens to the best number of rounds and to training time?

    Best rounds rise roughly tenfold, so training and scoring slow down. Test error at the best round is similar or slightly better. Practice: fix a low rate and let early stopping set the rounds; use a higher rate while exploring.

    7. Your forest predicts monthly card spend for a segment whose income is above anything in the training data. What goes wrong?

    Trees don't extrapolate: above the training maximum the prediction is the flat top-leaf value, so spend is underestimated. Fixes: add a linear or trend component, model spend as a share of income, or collect data for the new range.

    8. In Simulation 2 at depth 1, the average of 100 trees is barely better than one tree. Why?

    A depth-1 tree has high bias and low variance. Averaging cuts variance, not bias, so the average is still too simple. Bagging helps deep, unstable trees; boosting is built to reduce the bias of shallow ones.
