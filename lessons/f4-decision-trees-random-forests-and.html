    F4.3 · F4.4 Day one ★ Priority About 45 minutes

    Trees, Forests, Boosting and the Art of Holding Them Back

    How a decision tree picks its questions, why averaging many unstable trees works, how boosting builds a model one correction at a time, and how L1, L2, shrinkage and early stopping decide how much any of it is allowed to memorise.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA tree is a flowchart learned greedily from data; one tree memorises, so we either average many (forests) or stack small ones that each fix the last one's errors (boosting), and every knob that matters, from tree depth to learning rate to L2 and early stopping, is a way of limiting how much of the noise gets learned.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a senior credit officer who reviews personal-finance applications. After years of cases she carries a flowchart in her head. Is the instalment over 45% of income? If yes, is there a missed payment in the last six months? If no, is the customer salary-transferred to us? Each question splits applicants into two groups, and each group is asked a further question, until she is confident enough to say "approve" or "decline".

    A decision tree is that flowchart, learned from data instead of years. At every step the algorithm asks one question: of all the yes/no questions I could ask about one feature, which one separates defaulters from non-defaulters most cleanly? It asks it, splits the data, and repeats inside each half.

    This has real strengths. It handles numbers and categories, needs no scaling, finds interactions on its own ("high instalment and missed payment"), copes with missing values, and can be read aloud. It has one serious weakness. Left alone, a tree keeps asking questions until every leaf holds a handful of customers, at which point it has memorised the training data, noise included. Change the sample a little and you get a different tree. In F4.1's language, a single deep tree has low bias and very high variance.

    Everything else in this lesson is a response to that one weakness.

    • Random forest: ask a hundred different officers, each of whom saw a different random batch of cases and considered only some of the questions. Average their verdicts. Their individual quirks cancel.
    • Gradient boosting: start with a rough estimate, then hire a junior to fix what's still wrong, then another to fix what's still wrong after that. Each junior is deliberately small and weak. The sum is strong.
    • Regularisation: the rules about how much any one officer may memorise: how deep, how many cases per leaf, how big a correction, when to stop hiring.

    On the tabular data a bank mostly has (customers, accounts, transactions, bureau fields) these methods, especially XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, are still the default winners. Start by seeing how one split is chosen.

    Hold onto thisA tree is greedy: at each node it picks the single best split right now and never looks ahead or goes back. That is why it is fast, why it is unstable (a different sample can change the first split and so everything below it), and why the cure is to combine many trees rather than to polish one.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A decision tree: impurity, gain and greedy growth

    For classification, a node's impurity measures how mixed it is. The usual measure is Gini impurity:

    G=1−p2−(1−p)2=2p(1−p)

    p is the share of fraud in the node. A pure node (p = 0 or 1) scores 0; a 50/50 node scores the maximum, 0.5. In words: it is the chance that two transactions drawn at random from the node have different labels. Entropy (F1.7) is the alternative and behaves very similarly. For regression the impurity is simply the variance of the target in the node.

    The gain of a candidate split is the parent's impurity minus the size-weighted impurity of the two children. The algorithm tries every feature and every cut point (for a numeric feature, the midpoints between sorted values), keeps the one with the largest gain, and recurses. It stops when a node is pure, too small, too deep, or no split gains enough. A prediction is the average label in the leaf: that is a probability estimate such as "7 of 40 customers like this defaulted, so 17.5%".

    Four consequences worth knowing cold:

    • A tree is a step function. Predictions jump at the cut points and are flat between them. There is no smooth trend, and no extrapolation: ask a tree about an income of AED 400,000 a month when the training maximum was AED 120,000 and it returns the answer for the top bracket. This matters for inflation, growth and any quantity that trends.
    • Interactions come free. A split below a split is an interaction. That is why trees capture "large amount at a new beneficiary at night" without anyone engineering it, and why they beat linear models when such patterns exist (F3.4).
    • Only the order of values matters. Monotone transformations (log, scaling) change nothing. Outliers can't drag a fit the way they drag a regression line.
    • Impurity-based importance is biased. The "feature importance" a tree library prints by default adds up the gains of every split on a feature. It favours features with many distinct values and says nothing about direction. F8 covers better attributions.

    Trees also have no built-in notion of a calibrated probability when grown deep: a leaf with two customers, both of whom defaulted, says 100%. Averaging and shrinkage (below) fix most of this; F7.4 covers recalibrating what remains.

    Random forests: bagging and decorrelation

    Breiman's recipe has two random ingredients. First, bootstrap the rows (F2.2): each tree trains on a sample drawn with replacement, so about 63% of distinct rows appear and the rest are left out. Second, at every split the tree is allowed to look at only a random subset of the features (commonly the square root of their number for classification). Grow each tree deep, so it has low bias and high variance, then average the votes or probabilities.

    Why does averaging help? If each of B trees has variance σ² and the correlation between any two trees' predictions is ρ, the average has variance

    Var=ρσ2+(1−ρ)σ2B

    The second term vanishes as you add trees, so more trees never hurt and quickly stop helping. The first term does not: it is the part of the variance the trees share. Resampling rows and restricting features exist to push ρ down, because if every tree makes the same mistake, averaging keeps the mistake. Averaging cuts variance and leaves bias roughly where it was, which is why the individual trees are grown deep.

    Two bonuses. The rows each tree never saw give a free validation estimate, the out-of-bag error. And forests are forgiving: with sensible defaults (hundreds of trees, deep ones, a feature subset at each split) they rarely do badly and rarely need much tuning. They are a strong first baseline. They are usually beaten slightly by well-tuned boosting, and are larger and slower to score.

    Gradient boosting: fit the mistakes, a little at a time

    Boosting builds the model additively. Start with a constant (the average default rate). Measure what is still wrong, fit a small tree to that, and add a fraction of it:

    Fm(x) = Fm−1(x) + η · treem(x)

    η, the learning rate (also called shrinkage), is typically 0.01 to 0.1. With squared error, the thing each tree fits is literally the residual (actual minus current prediction). For other losses, such as log loss for default or fraud, it is the negative gradient of the loss at the current predictions, which is the same idea in general form and the reason for the name. F1.3 introduced gradients as the direction of steepest descent; boosting does gradient descent in the space of functions, taking one small tree-shaped step per round.

    The trees are deliberately weak: depth 3 to 8, sometimes just one split. Each round can only fix a little, so the model builds up slowly and the ensemble has low bias and, if you stop in time, controlled variance. Unlike a forest, more rounds can hurt: keep going and the model eventually fits the noise. That is why boosting needs early stopping (below).

    XGBoost, the library that made boosting dominant in competitions and then in industry, adds second-order information. For each row it uses the gradient g and curvature h of the loss. For a candidate leaf holding a set of rows, the best output value is

    w*=−∑g∑h+λ

    With squared error, g is minus the residual and h is 1, so this is "the sum of residuals in the leaf divided by (number of rows + λ)": the leaf's mean residual, shrunk toward zero. A leaf with 4 rows and mean residual 5 outputs 5 with λ = 0, but 4 × 5 ÷ (4 + 1) = 4 with λ = 1. Small leaves, which are the noisy ones, are shrunk the most. That is L2 regularisation on leaf values, built into the tree. The same λ appears in the split gain, together with a penalty γ per extra leaf, so a split must earn its keep.

    The three mainstream libraries differ in engineering more than in idea.

    LibraryWhat it does differentlyWhen you notice

    The hyperparameters that matter, in the order to think about them:

    • Learning rate and number of rounds trade against each other. Lower rate, more rounds, usually slightly better and slower. Fix the rate (0.05 is a fine start) and let early stopping choose the rounds.
    • Tree depth or leaves. Depth 1 gives an additive model with no interactions; deeper trees allow higher-order interactions. Between 3 and 8 covers most bank problems.
    • Minimum rows (or hessian weight) per leaf. The strongest protection against fitting a handful of customers. On rare-event data, make it large enough that a leaf can contain several positives (F3.5).
    • Row and column subsampling (e.g. 0.7 each, per tree). Borrowed from forests: it decorrelates the trees and cuts variance.
    • λ, α, γ. L2 on leaf values, L1 on leaf values, and the minimum gain to split. Part 2's next section covers them.
    • Monotone constraints. You can force the model to be non-decreasing in a feature, for example probability of default never falls as days-past-due rises. XGBoost and LightGBM both support this. Credit teams use it to meet explainability and sense-check requirements without giving up the accuracy.

    Regularisation: L2, L1 and early stopping

    Regularisation is any change that makes the fit worse on the training data in order to make it better on new data. The classic form adds a penalty to the loss: fit the data, but pay for large coefficients.

    • L2 (ridge): penalty proportional to the sum of squared coefficients. It shrinks all coefficients smoothly toward zero and never quite reaches it. When two features carry the same information (a customer's salary and their average monthly credit), ridge shares the weight between them. It tames the wild, offsetting coefficients you see with correlated inputs.
    • L1 (lasso): penalty proportional to the sum of absolute values. Its corner at zero means coefficients are pushed exactly to zero as the penalty rises: it selects features. With a correlated pair it tends to keep one and drop the other, somewhat arbitrarily, which makes the selection unstable across samples.
    • Elastic net: a mix of both. It selects like lasso but spreads weight across correlated groups like ridge.

    Why does a penalty help? A model with many flexible parameters and few examples has coefficients that are mostly noise, and noise in coefficients becomes noise in predictions (variance, F4.1). Shrinking trades a little bias for a larger cut in variance. A Bayesian reading, which is useful in a meeting: L2 is a prior that says "most effects are small and bell-shaped around zero", and L1 is a prior that says "most effects are exactly zero". The penalty strength λ is how strongly you believe it, and you choose it by cross-validation (F4.5, F4.9).

    Practical details: standardise features before penalising linear models, or the penalty treats AED amounts and 0/1 flags unequally; never penalise the intercept; and compare λ on a log scale.

    Regularisation in tree models has more forms than the penalty above, and all of them are doing the same job:

    • Structural limits: maximum depth, minimum rows per leaf, minimum gain to split (γ). They prevent the tree from carving out tiny groups.
    • Shrinkage: the learning rate. A small step per round is regularisation by caution.
    • Penalties on leaf values: λ (L2) and α (L1) in XGBoost, as in the leaf formula above.
    • Randomisation: row and column subsampling. Averaging noisy learners is the forest's whole trick.
    • Early stopping: watch the error on a validation set after every round and stop when it hasn't improved for, say, 50 rounds. The number of rounds is then the complexity dial, set by data rather than guessed. In neural nets the same idea appears with epochs, next to dropout and data augmentation (F6.3).

    Early stopping hides a trap. The validation set used to pick the stopping round has influenced the model, so it can't also be your honest final test. You need three sets: train, validation (for stopping and tuning), and a held-out test that is touched once. For bank data the validation and test sets should come later in time than the training set (F4.6).

    # XGBoost with early stopping, a monotone constraint, and a time-ordered split.
# train < valid < test in time. Test is only scored once, at the end.
import xgboost as xgb

model = xgb.XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=2000,        # an upper bound; early stopping picks the real number
    learning_rate=0.05,
    max_depth=4,
    min_child_weight=20,      # leaves must carry enough "weight" (a count, for log loss it is hessian mass)
    subsample=0.8,
    colsample_bytree=0.8,
    reg_lambda=5.0,           # L2 on leaf values
    reg_alpha=0.0,            # L1 on leaf values
    gamma=0.0,                # minimum gain to make a split
    monotone_constraints={"days_past_due": 1},   # risk never falls as this rises
    eval_metric="aucpr",
    early_stopping_rounds=50,
)
model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_valid, y_valid)], verbose=False)
print(model.best_iteration)

    The residual each tree fits is a negative gradient. The learning rate is a step size.

    Weight decay is L2, dropout is randomised averaging, early stopping is early stopping. See also loss functions.

    Boosted and forest scores are good rankings but often need recalibration; drift monitoring tells you when the retrain is due.

    SHAP and partial dependence are how boosted trees are explained to a validator and to a declined customer.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit scoring and probability of default. Boosted trees with monotone constraints, benchmarked against a regularised logistic scorecard. Governance cares about stability, explanation and calibration as much as AUC.
    • Card and payment fraud. Boosting on engineered velocity and history features, scored in milliseconds. Depth, leaf size and early stopping need the rare-event care of F3.5.
    • AML alert scoring and prioritisation. Trees rank alerts for investigators. Few confirmed positives mean heavy regularisation and a simple model often wins.
    • Churn, cross-sell and collections. Mid-sized tabular problems where a forest or a lightly tuned boosting model is a strong, quick baseline.
    • Islamic finance products. The same models apply. Features and decisions must stay within product rules; for example, models should not use anything the Shariah-governance framework excludes, which is a requirement for the institution to state, not for the algorithm.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Gradient-boosted trees remain the workhorse for tabular data in 2026. The live debates are whether deep learning and pretrained tabular models are finally catching them, and how to keep boosted models explainable and stable in regulated settings.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with two quick searches. Dates for older milestones come from my own knowledge of the literature, and the 2026 search results were largely blog posts and vendor material, so treat the headline claims about new tabular foundation models with caution.

    1. 1984
      CART

      Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone formalised classification and regression trees with impurity-based splitting and pruning.

      Breiman et al., Classification and Regression Trees, 1984
    2. 2001
      Random forests, and gradient boosting machines

      Breiman combined bagging with random feature subsets. In the same year Friedman set out gradient boosting as functional gradient descent, with shrinkage and subsampling.

      Breiman, Machine Learning, 2001; Friedman, Annals of Statistics, 2001
    3. 2016–18
      XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost

      Engineering made boosting fast, regularised and easy: second-order objective and sparsity handling (XGBoost), histogram binning and leaf-wise growth (LightGBM), ordered boosting and native categoricals (CatBoost).

      Chen & Guestrin, KDD 2016; Ke et al., NeurIPS 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., NeurIPS 2018
    4. 2022
      Why trees still beat deep nets on tabular data

      On a benchmark of mid-sized tabular datasets, tree ensembles outperformed deep networks even after heavy tuning of the networks. The authors tie it to trees' robustness to uninformative features and to the irregular, non-smooth target functions common in tables. A companion study found the same, with ensembles of both doing best.

      Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux, NeurIPS 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, Information Fusion, 2022
    5. 2023
      When do neural nets outperform boosted trees?

      A large study of 176 datasets found no single winner and that the gap depends on dataset properties. Boosted trees were often competitive or best, and the differences between top methods were small compared with the effort of choosing between them.

      McElfresh et al., NeurIPS 2023
    6. 2025
      XGBoost 3.0 and tabular foundation models

      XGBoost 3.0 (February 2025) reworked external-memory training so very large data can be staged on the host and read in batches; later 3.x releases have followed, and I could not confirm the very latest version number. In January 2025 a Nature paper presented TabPFN, a transformer pretrained on many synthetic tables that predicts on a new small table in one forward pass without training. A follow-up, TabPFN-2.5 (arXiv, November 2025), extends it to larger tables.

      XGBoost release notes; Hollmann et al., Nature, 2025; TabPFN-2.5 report, arXiv 2511.08667
    7. 2026
      Where pretrained tabular models stand

      Reported results favour them on small to medium tables, where they beat default and often tuned boosted trees with almost no tuning. Independent checks are more mixed: one 2026 test on a roughly 12,000-row fraud dataset had TabPFN ahead on AUC and AUCPR while LightGBM trained far faster; other work finds trees equal or better and much cheaper to serve at scale. For large, low-latency, CPU-served bank workloads, tuned boosting is still the norm. Most 2026 claims I found were secondary sources, so read the benchmark yourself before relying on one.

      H2O.ai blog, March 2026; arXiv 2512.00888; TabPFN-2.5 report

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Training score reported as performance

      "The forest scores 0.999 AUC." A deep forest nearly memorises its training rows.

      Spot itAsk which data the number comes from. Out-of-bag or a later-in-time hold-out is the only honest answer.
    • Early stopping on the test set

      The number of rounds is picked by watching test error, so the test score is optimistic.

      Spot itAsk how many datasets exist and which one chose the number of rounds. If two roles share a set, the final number is inflated.
    • Random split on time-ordered data

      Neat validation scores, then weak live results. Boosted trees are very good at exploiting information from the neighbouring weeks.

      Spot itRe-run with train strictly before validation strictly before test. If the score drops sharply, the earlier number was borrowed from the future.
    • Leakage a tree finds instantly

      A field filled in after the outcome (collections status, account closed flag) becomes the top split.

      Spot itOne feature with enormous importance and near-perfect AUC. Ask when that field is populated relative to the decision time.
    • Trusting impurity importance

      "Account ID bucket is the third most important feature." High-cardinality and noisy features inflate in default importance.

      Spot itAdd a random-noise column and see where it ranks. Use permutation importance on held-out data or SHAP (F8).
    • Expecting trees to extrapolate

      Forecasting exposure for a growing book, or scoring a new product with higher ticket sizes.

      Spot itPlot predictions against the feature beyond the training range: they go flat. Use a linear component, detrend the target, or differ the model.
    • Over-tuning on a small positive count

      200 defaults, 40 features, 500 boosting rounds, and a hyperparameter search that wins by 0.004 AUC.

      Spot itCompute an interval for the metric (F2.2). If it is wider than the gain from tuning, the tuning found noise.
    • Raw leaf-wise boosting on small data

      LightGBM defaults used on a few thousand rows build deep lopsided trees.

      Spot itLarge gap between training and validation error. Cut num_leaves, raise min_data_in_leaf, add subsampling.
    • Unstandardised features under L1 or L2

      The penalty bites features measured in AED far less than features measured as 0/1.

      Spot itCheck for a scaling step before any penalised linear model. Trees don't need one; penalised linear models do.
    • Treating lasso's chosen features as "the true drivers"

      Among correlated predictors, lasso's pick is partly arbitrary and changes with the sample.

      Spot itRefit on bootstrap resamples. If the selected set changes a lot, the selection is not evidence of causes.
    • Scores used as probabilities without checking

      Boosted scores rank well but are not guaranteed calibrated, especially after class weights.

      Spot itReliability curve on a later hold-out set (F7.4). Recalibrate before using the score in a loss or provisioning calculation.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The model is a committee of several hundred small decision trees, each correcting the mistakes of the ones before it. It's the standard technique for bank data like this, fast and accurate. We keep it from memorising by limiting how much each tree can learn and by stopping when a later period of data stops improving. We compare it to a plain scorecard so we know what the extra complexity buys.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd start with a regularised logistic baseline and a random forest, then a gradient-boosted model at a fixed low learning rate with early stopping on a validation window that follows the training window, and a final test touched once. Depth and minimum leaf size are tuned for the positive count, I'd add monotone constraints where policy requires a direction, check calibration on the later hold-out, and report intervals so differences below noise aren't sold as wins.”
    “Why not just use one decision tree? It's explainable.”

    A single tree is explainable but unstable: a small change in the sample can change the top split and the whole structure, and a shallow tree that stays readable is usually much less accurate. If the business needs a readable rule set, I'd fit a small tree or scorecard as the explainable layer and use the ensemble for accuracy, with SHAP to explain it, and show how much accuracy the simple version gives up.

    “Random forest or XGBoost?”

    Forest first when I want a robust baseline with little tuning, since it rarely overfits as trees are added. Boosting when I want the last few points of accuracy and can afford tuning, because it reduces bias as well as variance. On most bank tabular problems tuned boosting edges out the forest, but the gap is often smaller than the gap between a careful validation and a sloppy one.

    “How do you stop a boosted model overfitting?”

    Low learning rate, shallow trees, a minimum leaf size sized to the positive count, row and column subsampling, L2 on leaf values, and early stopping on a validation window later than training. And a separate untouched test. I'd also look at the gap between training and validation error as the diagnostic.

    “Lasso or ridge for a credit scorecard?”

    Ridge when features are correlated and I want to keep all of them with stable, shared weights. Lasso when I need a short list of variables for governance. Elastic net when I want both. If selection matters, I'd check the stability of the chosen set across bootstrap resamples before presenting it as meaningful.

    “Should we try the new tabular foundation models?”

    As a challenger on small and mid-sized problems, yes, because reported results are strong and tuning is minimal. For production I'd want evidence on our data against a tuned boosted baseline, plus answers on serving cost, latency, stability across retrains and how our model-risk process would validate a pretrained model. Most published comparisons come from the developers or secondary sources, so I'd run our own.

    “Why does more boosting rounds sometimes make it worse?”

    Each round fits what's left, and after the signal is captured what's left is mostly noise. The training error keeps falling but the model is learning noise, so validation error turns up. A forest doesn't do this as trees are added, because its trees are independent and averaging only reduces variance.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A node holds 100 transactions, 20 of them fraud. A split sends 60 left (2 fraud) and 40 right (18 fraud). Compute the Gini gain.

    Parent: 2 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.32. Left: p = 2/60 = 0.0333, impurity 2 × 0.0333 × 0.9667 = 0.0644. Right: p = 18/40 = 0.45, impurity 2 × 0.45 × 0.55 = 0.495. Weighted: 0.6 × 0.0644 + 0.4 × 0.495 = 0.0387 + 0.198 = 0.2367. Gain = 0.32 − 0.2367 ≈ 0.083. The tree compares this with the gain of every other candidate split and takes the largest.

    2. 100 trees, each with variance 1, pairwise correlation 0.3. What is the variance of their average, and what is the limit with infinitely many trees?

    ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B = 0.3 + 0.7/100 = 0.307. With infinite trees: 0.3. Adding trees beyond a modest number barely helps. Lowering the correlation (more row and feature randomness) is what moves the floor.

    3. A boosted-tree leaf holds 10 rows, all with residual +3 (squared error). What does it output with λ = 0, with λ = 1 and with λ = 10?

    Output = (sum of residuals) ÷ (rows + λ). Sum = 30. λ = 0: 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0. λ = 1: 30 ÷ 11 ≈ 2.73. λ = 10: 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5. The penalty shrinks small, noisy leaves the most, because the λ is a bigger share of a small count. A leaf of 100 rows with the same residual would be 300 ÷ 110 ≈ 2.73 at λ = 10, far less affected.

    4. Two highly correlated features both genuinely predict default. What happens under lasso and under ridge as the penalty rises?

    Ridge shrinks both and keeps them near each other, sharing the effect. Lasso tends to keep one and drive the other to zero, and which one survives can flip with a different sample. So lasso's selected list is not evidence that the dropped feature doesn't matter.

    5. You choose the number of boosting rounds by early stopping on the test set and report the test AUC. What is wrong, and what is the fix?

    The test set influenced the model (it picked the round count), so its AUC is optimistically biased. Use three sets: train, validation (early stopping and tuning), and a final untouched test, all in time order for bank data. Report the test score once.

    6. You lower the learning rate from 0.1 to 0.01 and change nothing else. What happens to the best number of rounds and to training time?

    The best number of rounds rises, roughly in proportion (order of ten times as many), so training and scoring take longer. Test error at the best round is usually similar or slightly better. That's why practice is to fix a low rate and let early stopping set the rounds, and to use a higher rate while exploring.

    7. Your forest predicts monthly card spend for a customer segment whose income is above anything in the training data. What goes wrong?

    Trees don't extrapolate. Above the training maximum the prediction is the value of the top leaf, flat. If spend rises with income, the model underestimates for the new segment. Fixes: add a linear or trend component, model a ratio (spend as a share of income) instead of the level, or collect data for the new range.

    8. In Simulation 2 at depth 1 the average of 100 trees is barely better than one tree. Why?

    A depth-1 tree has high bias and low variance: it can only draw one step. Averaging cuts variance, not bias, so the average is a smoother step function that is still too simple. Bagging helps deep, unstable trees. Boosting, by contrast, is built to reduce the bias of shallow ones.
