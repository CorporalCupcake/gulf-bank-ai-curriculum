    Decision Trees, Random Forests and Boosting

    Part 1

    What a decision tree is

    In one sentenceA decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions learned from data. You follow the questions down until you reach an answer.

    It's like the game 20 Questions. Here's a tiny tree that predicts whether a student passes an exam:

    The right-hand picture is the key to understanding trees. Every question is a straight cut through the data. The tree chops the space into boxes, and everyone who lands in the same box gets the same answer.

    How it differs from other models

    • Linear and logistic regression draw one straight line (or flat surface) through all the data. Each feature adds or takes away a fixed amount. Smooth, but they can't easily say "this matters only if that is true".
    • A tree draws boxes. "If" logic comes naturally: sleep only matters for students who studied. Its predictions are steps, not smooth slopes.
    • Nearest neighbours doesn't learn rules at all. It copies the answer of the most similar past examples.
    Part 2

    How a tree learns

    1. Try every possible cut. For each feature, try every split point: studied ≥ 1 hour, ≥ 2 hours, and so on.
    2. Pick the cut that best separates the answers. The best cut leaves two groups that are each as "pure" as possible: mostly passes on one side, mostly fails on the other.
    3. Repeat inside each group. Each side gets its own best question.
    4. Stop when a group is pure, too small, or the tree hits a depth limit.

    "Pure" just means unmixed. A group that is 50% pass and 50% fail is as mixed as it gets. A group that is 100% pass is pure. For predicting a number (like a price), "pure" means the values in the group are close together.

    How to read a tree

    • The top question is the single most useful split in the data.
    • Each leaf (end box) gives an answer and usually a share, such as "Pass: 85% of 40 students". The share is the tree's confidence, and the count tells you how much to trust it.
    • A leaf with only 3 students is close to a guess. Lots of tiny leaves means the tree has memorised the training data.
    • Depth is the number of questions from top to bottom. Depth 3 means at most 3 questions, and at most 8 boxes.
    Part 3

    The problem with one tree

    • Deep trees overfit. As you just saw, they memorise the training data.
    • Trees are unstable. Change a few rows of data and the top question can change, giving a completely different tree.
    • Small trees are too crude. A handful of boxes can't capture much.

    Two fixes. You can hold the tree back: limit its depth or demand at least, say, 50 examples per leaf. Or, much more powerfully, you can use many trees together. There are two main ways to do that.

    Part 4

    Random forest: many trees vote

    In one sentenceGrow hundreds of trees, each on a slightly different random version of the data, and let them vote.

    How it makes the trees different:

    • Each tree learns from a random resample of the rows: some rows twice, some not at all.
    • At each question, each tree may only choose from a random handful of the features.

    Why it works: each tree makes mistakes, but different mistakes. When they vote, the mistakes cancel out and the shared pattern remains. It's the wisdom of crowds: one person guessing the weight of a cow is often way off, but the average of 500 guesses is usually close.

    Settings that matter: number of trees (more is safer, just slower; a few hundred is typical), and how many features each question may choose from. Forests work well with almost no tuning.

    Part 5

    Gradient boosting: trees fix each other's mistakes

    In one sentenceBuild small trees one after another. Each new tree focuses on the errors the previous trees still make. Add them all up.

    Picture a golfer. The first shot gets close to the hole. Each later putt corrects whatever distance is left. Boosting works the same way: tree 1 makes a rough guess, tree 2 predicts what tree 1 got wrong, tree 3 predicts what is still wrong, and so on.

    Settings that matter:

    • Learning rate: how much each new tree's correction counts. Smaller is more careful and usually more accurate, but needs more trees.
    • Number of trees: too many and it starts fitting noise. Use early stopping: keep adding trees until accuracy on held-out data stops improving.
    • Tree depth: keep trees small, usually 3 to 8 levels.

    Popular libraries: XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost. They differ in speed and details, not in the core idea.

    Part 6

    Which one to use

    ModelHow it thinksStrong atWeak at
    Single treeOne flowchartAnyone can read and check itUnstable, not very accurate
    Random forestMany independent trees voteStrong results with little tuning, hard to breakBig and slower; usually a bit behind boosting
    Gradient boostingTrees in a row, each fixing errorsUsually the most accurate on tables of dataNeeds tuning and early stopping, or it overfits
    Linear / logistic regressionAdds up weighted featuresSmall data, smooth effects, easy to explainMisses "only if" interactions unless you add them by hand

    Tree models are strong at:

    • Spreadsheet-style data with a mix of numbers and categories. This is where boosting usually beats neural networks.
    • Finding interactions and curves on their own, without you designing them.
    • Little preparation: no need to rescale features, and odd extreme values in the inputs don't throw them off.

    They are weak at:

    • Going beyond the data they've seen. A tree trained on houses up to $1m will never predict $2m. Its boxes stop at the edge of the data.
    • Smooth relationships: they approximate a smooth curve with a staircase.
    • Images, text and sound, where neural networks win clearly.

    In practice: for table data, start with gradient boosting (LightGBM or XGBoost) with early stopping. Use a random forest as a quick, safe baseline. Use a single shallow tree when a human must be able to follow the rule.

    Show the formulas

    Gini impurity (how mixed a group is) = 1 − (share of class A)² − (share of class B)². A 50/50 group scores 0.5 (most mixed). A pure group scores 0.

    Boosting prediction = tree 1 + learning rate × tree 2 + learning rate × tree 3 + … Each tree is fitted to the errors left by all the trees before it.

    In banking, gradient boosting is the workhorse for credit scoring and fraud detection, often with a simple scorecard alongside it for regulators.

    Part 7

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting one deep tree

      It looks great on training data and falls apart on new data. Limit depth, or use a forest or boosting.

    • Boosting without early stopping

      Keep adding trees and it will memorise the noise. Always watch a held-out set.

    • Reading feature importance as cause

      "Used in many splits" means useful for predicting, not "causes the outcome". The default importance score also favours features with many distinct values. Permutation importance (shuffle a feature and see how much accuracy drops) is more honest.

    • Expecting predictions outside the data

      Trees can't predict beyond the range they were trained on. If the future will look different from the past, be careful.

    1. In the box picture, what does each question in a tree look like?

    A straight cut through the data, across one feature. The tree chops space into boxes, and each box gets one answer.

    2. A tree gets 100% on training data and 70% on new data. What happened, and what would you do?

    It overfitted: it memorised the training data. Limit its depth or leaf size, or switch to a random forest or boosting.

    3. What is the difference between a random forest and boosting, in one line each?

    Forest: many independent trees vote. Boosting: trees built one after another, each fixing the last ones' errors.

    4. Your model must predict prices higher than anything in the training data. Is a tree model a good choice?

    No. Trees can't go beyond the range they've seen. A linear model, or a tree model on a transformed target, would cope better.

    5. You need the best accuracy on a big spreadsheet of customer data. What do you try first?

    Gradient boosting with early stopping, with a random forest as a quick baseline to compare against.
