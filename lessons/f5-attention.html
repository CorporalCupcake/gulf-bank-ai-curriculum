    F5.5 · F5.6 Day one ★ About 45 minutes

    Attention and Embeddings: How Models Look Things Up and Where Meaning Lives

    Attention is a soft, learned lookup that lets every word read every other word. An embedding is the vector that makes a word, a merchant or a customer something a model can compare. Between them they explain most of what a modern language model does, and most of what it costs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn embedding turns each item into a point whose position carries meaning, and attention lets each item build a new, context-aware point by taking a weighted average of the other items, with the weights decided by how well a question from one matches a label on another.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Read this sentence from a customer complaint: "The transfer to the supplier failed, so I called the branch and asked them to reverse it." What does "it" mean? The transfer. You know because your mind jumped back across seven words, ignored "supplier", "branch" and "called", and picked the one that fits. That jump is what attention does. Every word asks a question ("what do I refer to?"), every other word offers a label ("I am a thing that can be reversed"), and the best matches get the most weight.

    The older approach to language models was to read left to right and squeeze everything seen so far into one fixed-size memory. Long sentences lost their beginnings. Attention removed that bottleneck: instead of one summary, the model keeps every word available and, at each step, looks up what it needs. The 2017 paper that built a whole model from nothing else was called Attention Is All You Need, and the architecture it introduced, the transformer, now sits under almost every language, vision and speech model you will meet at the bank.

    Attention needs something to operate on. A model cannot read the string "ADNOC fuel" or the Arabic "محطة بنزين". It reads vectors, lists of a few hundred or thousand numbers. An embedding is the map from an item to its vector, trained so that items that behave alike land near each other. Petrol station and fuel retailer should be neighbours. A cash withdrawal at an ATM and a transfer to a crypto exchange should not.

    Hold onto thisEmbeddings are where meaning is stored: position in space. Attention is how meaning is combined: a weighted average over positions. Almost everything else in a transformer (layers, heads, feed-forward blocks) is machinery to apply these two ideas many times over.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Queries, keys and values

    Start with a sentence already turned into vectors: one row per token (a token is a word or word piece, see tokenisation). Stack them into a matrix X with n rows. The layer makes three different views of every token by multiplying X by three learned matrices (this is the matrix multiplication of F1.1):

    • Query Q = X WQ: what this token is looking for.
    • Key K = X WK: what this token advertises about itself.
    • Value V = X WV: what this token hands over if someone picks it.

    Why three views and not one? Because "what I am searching for", "how I can be found" and "what I contribute" are different jobs. A library works the same way: your search words (query) are matched against catalogue entries (keys), but what you carry home is the book (value). The matrices are learned by gradient descent (F1.3), so the model decides for itself what questions and labels are useful.

    The computation has three steps. Score every query against every key with a dot product, which is large when two vectors point the same way. Turn each row of scores into weights that are positive and sum to one with softmax. Then take the weighted average of the values.

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V

    Q KT is an n × n table of match scores: entry (i, j) says how much token i wants token j. Softmax turns each row into weights summing to 1. Multiplying by V gives each token a new vector that is a blend of everyone's values. dk is the key dimension; the division by its square root is explained next.

    Notice what is not in the formula: position. The sum over tokens does not care what order they came in. "Bank robbed the man" and "man robbed the bank" would look identical. Transformers fix this by adding position information to the embeddings before attention (sinusoidal patterns in the original paper; rotary embeddings, RoPE, in most current models). Remember that attention is a set operation with order bolted on.

    Here is the whole thing in a few lines of NumPy. You will not write this by hand, but it is worth seeing how little there is.

    import numpy as np

def attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, causal=False):
    Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv           # three views of every token
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(K.shape[-1])      # n x n match table
    if causal:                                    # a token may not see the future
        scores = np.where(np.tril(np.ones_like(scores)) == 1, scores, -np.inf)
    w = np.exp(scores - scores.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    w = w / w.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)         # softmax: each row sums to 1
    return w @ V, w                               # blended values, and the weights

    Why divide by √d, and what softmax does with it

    Suppose query and key entries are independent random numbers with mean 0 and variance 1. A dot product of d such pairs is a sum of d terms, so its variance is d and its typical size is √d. With d = 64 the raw scores spread about ±8; with d = 1,000, about ±32. Softmax exponentiates its inputs, so a gap of 8 means one key gets e8 ≈ 3,000 times the weight of another. The output is almost one-hot, and the gradient of a nearly one-hot softmax is nearly zero, so the layer stops learning. Dividing by √d puts the scores back at a spread of about 1 whatever the dimension. It is a small trick that keeps training stable, and it is in the formula for that reason alone.

    Multi-head attention and what heads learn

    One attention pattern per layer is limiting. A word may need to look at its grammatical subject, at a number three words back and at a previous mention of the same name, all at once. Multi-head attention runs several attention operations side by side, each with its own WQ, WK, WV, each working in a smaller slice of the dimension (a 4,096-wide model might use 32 heads of 128). Their outputs are joined and mixed by one more matrix. The cost is about the same as a single wide head, but the model gets several independent "views".

    Interpretability work has found that individual heads do recognisable jobs: some attend to the previous token, some track syntax, and a famous kind, induction heads, spot that "A B ... A" has appeared and predict "B" next. That is the circuit behind much in-context learning, the ability to follow a pattern you show in the prompt (Olsson et al., 2022). Two cautions. Many heads are redundant and can be pruned with little loss. And a head's attention weights are not a reliable explanation of why the model produced an answer (Jain and Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019 argue the question is subtler). Do not present a heat map of attention to a validator as "the model's reasoning".

    A transformer layer pairs attention with a feed-forward network applied to each token separately, with residual connections and normalisation around both. Attention moves information between tokens; the feed-forward block transforms it within each. Stack dozens of layers and early layers tend to build local features while later ones build more abstract relationships.

    Causal masks, cost, and the KV cache

    A language model is trained to predict the next token. If token 5 could look at token 6 while predicting it, it would just copy the answer. So generation models use a causal mask: scores for all later positions are set to minus infinity before softmax, giving them zero weight. Each row still sums to 1, over fewer tokens. Models that only read text, such as the encoders used for embeddings and classification, let every token see every other, in both directions.

    The cost problem is in that n × n table. Double the text and the table quadruples. At 128,000 tokens one head in one layer scores about 1.6 × 1010 pairs; a model has dozens of heads and layers. Two engineering ideas made long context affordable. FlashAttention computes the same exact result but never writes the big table to slow memory, working in small tiles that stay in fast on-chip memory; it is an exact speed-up, not an approximation (Dao et al., 2022, with later versions for newer GPUs). And the KV cache: when generating token by token, the keys and values of earlier tokens never change, so they are stored and reused rather than recomputed. That makes each new token cheap in compute but turns memory into the bottleneck, because the cache grows with every token for every layer. The longer story is in context windows and the KV cache.

    The standard cure for cache size is to share keys and values across heads. Multi-query attention uses one shared key/value set for all heads (Shazeer, 2019). Grouped-query attention uses a few, say 8 groups for 32 query heads, a middle path adopted by many open and commercial models (Ainslie et al., 2023). DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention compresses keys and values into a small latent vector and caches that, reporting a cache a small fraction of the size of standard multi-head attention at matched quality (DeepSeek-V2, 2024).

    Embeddings: turning things into points

    An embedding layer is, at heart, a table with one row per token. Token 4,817 looks up row 4,817 and gets back its vector. At the start the rows are random. Training moves them so that tokens used in similar ways end up near each other, because nearby vectors let the rest of the network treat them alike. Nobody tells the model that "petrol" and "fuel" are related; it discovers this because they appear in the same company. This is the distributional idea ("you shall know a word by the company it keeps") and it is why word2vec in 2013 could produce vectors in which king − man + woman lands near queen. That famous arithmetic is cherry-picked and fragile, but the underlying geometry is real: directions encode relations (R5 returns to this).

    There are three kinds you will meet:

    KindOne vector for…ExampleCatch
    Static word embeddingseach word, whatever the contextword2vec, GloVe, fastText"bank" (river) and "bank" (Gulf Bank) share a vector. Largely replaced.
    Contextual embeddingseach word in its sentence: the output of the transformer layersBERT, hidden states of any LLMThe vector now depends on the whole input, so it is not reusable on its own.
    Sentence and document embeddingsa whole text, as one vectorEmbedding models behind semantic search and RAGOne vector must hold everything: topic, language, tone. Information is lost.
    Entity embeddingsa merchant, customer, account, device or productLearned from transaction sequences or a customer-merchant graphOnly as good as the behaviour it was trained on; new entities have no history (cold start).

    How a sentence embedding model is made. Take a pretrained encoder, then train it with a contrastive objective (F5.7): for each text, pull its known match (a paraphrase, the answer to a question, the translation) closer, and push everything else in the batch further away. Sentence-BERT (2019) did this with sentence pairs; modern models train on billions of pairs, often with instructions such as "represent this query for retrieving passages", and many are built by converting a large language model into an encoder. Multilingual ones are trained with translation pairs so that English "cash withdrawal" and Arabic "سحب نقدي" land close together, which is the property that makes cross-lingual search possible.

    Measuring closeness. Three choices, which are not the same:

    • Cosine similarity: the angle between vectors, ignoring length. The default for text, because length often reflects accidents such as word frequency.
    • Dot product: angle and length. Favours long vectors. If a model was trained with the dot product (many recommenders are), length carries real meaning such as popularity, and you must use it.
    • Euclidean distance: straight-line distance. If vectors are normalised to length 1, it ranks neighbours exactly as cosine does, so all three agree. That is why many systems normalise once and then use whichever is fastest.

    The rule is to use what the model was trained with. Mixing them up silently returns the wrong neighbours, with no error message.

    Entity embeddings in tabular work. A categorical column with 3,000 merchant categories or 200,000 merchants is awkward to one-hot encode. A neural net can instead learn a small vector per category as part of training (Guo and Berkhahn, 2016), so that similar merchants share statistical strength and rare ones borrow from neighbours. The learned vectors can also be exported as features for a gradient-boosted model, a pattern worth trying when you have a high-cardinality field. The risk is leakage: if embeddings are learned from data that includes the test period or the label, the features carry the answer (F3.1).

    One last mechanical point links the two halves. The embedding you start with is a fixed lookup. After the first attention layer each token's vector has been rewritten using its neighbours, and after the last layer it is a contextual representation. That is why you can read a sentence embedding off a model's last layers, and why you can think of a transformer as a machine that repeatedly refines embeddings by letting them consult each other.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This lesson is the hinge between classic deep learning and everything about large language models. The links, in both directions:

    F1.1 – F1.2 · Vectors, matrices, geometryThe dot product is the engine

    Attention scores are dot products; embeddings are positions in a vector space. If cosine and projection are clear, this lesson is mostly bookkeeping.

    F5.1 – F5.4 · Neurons, layers, convolutionsWhere this sits

    The weight matrices WQ, WK, WV are ordinary layers trained by backpropagation. Compare convolutions: a fixed local neighbourhood, where attention chooses its neighbourhood on the fly.

    F5.7 · Self-supervised and contrastive learningHow embeddings get their meaning

    Predict-the-next-token and pull-matches-together are the training signals that shape the embedding space, with no hand-made labels.

    M1.1 · TokenisationWhat a "token" is

    The rows of the embedding table. Arabic often splits into more tokens than English for the same meaning, which makes every attention cost higher for Arabic text.

    M1.2 · M1.3 · Pretraining and contextContext windows and the KV cache

    The practical consequence of the n × n table and the cache bill. See also pretraining for how these layers get trained at scale.

    M3 · RetrievalEmbeddings are the retrieval engine

    Dense retrieval embeds queries and passages and finds nearest neighbours. It pairs with BM25 in hybrid search, and chunking decides what each vector has to summarise.

    R10 · Matryoshka embeddingsTruncatable vectors

    Train so the first 256 numbers of a 1,024-number embedding are already a good embedding. Cuts storage and search cost.

    R1 · A5 · Efficient inferenceWhy serving got cheaper

    Grouped and latent attention, FlashAttention and mixture of experts are the reasons token prices fell.

    A8 · Foundation models for transaction dataAttention over a customer's history

    Treat each transaction as a token and a customer's year as a sentence, and the same machinery learns behaviour embeddings.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Semantic search over policies and contracts. A staff copilot embeds credit policy paragraphs and the employee's question, retrieves the closest, and passes them to an LLM. Quality is mostly an embedding and chunking question.
    • Name and entity matching. Sanctions and KYC screening compare names across scripts (Mohammed, محمد, Muhammad). Embeddings capture sound and script variation that string distance misses, but cannot replace exact identifiers; see sanctions screening.
    • Fraud and behaviour. Merchant and device embeddings learned from transaction sequences feed fraud models; a customer's recent transactions embedded together describe "what normal looks like for this person".
    • Complaints and call-centre triage. Embed English and Arabic complaints into one space, cluster them, and spot an emerging issue before a manager reads a single ticket.
    • Recommendation. Customer and product embeddings are the basis of next-best-offer systems (F9).
    • Document AI. Trade-finance and KYC documents are read by models whose layers are attention; image-based retrieval embeds page images directly (A6).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The core formula from 2017 is still what runs in nearly every frontier model. The activity is in making it cheaper at long context, in choosing what each head must remember, and in embedding models that work across languages and tasks.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with two light searches. Items marked "from memory" were not re-verified. Leaderboard positions change monthly and different trackers disagree, so none are quoted as fact.

    1. 2013–14
      Word embeddings

      word2vec and GloVe showed that vectors trained only to predict neighbouring words encode meaning and analogy.

      Mikolov et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2014 (from memory for GloVe)
    2. 2014–15
      Attention in translation

      Bahdanau and colleagues let a translation model look back over the source sentence while writing each word, which fixed the long-sentence problem. Luong et al. simplified it.

      Bahdanau et al., 2014; Luong et al., 2015
    3. 2017
      The transformer

      Attention with no recurrence, trained in parallel across the whole sequence. The reason models scaled.

      Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017
    4. 2018–19
      Contextual and sentence embeddings

      BERT produced word vectors that depend on the sentence. Sentence-BERT made sentence-level embeddings cheap to compare, which is the starting point of modern semantic search.

      Devlin et al., 2018; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019
    5. 2019–23
      Shrinking the cache

      Multi-query attention, then grouped-query attention as the compromise most model families adopted.

      Shazeer, 2019; Ainslie et al., 2023
    6. 2022
      FlashAttention and induction heads

      An exact, memory-aware implementation made long context practical, and a mechanistic account of in-context learning named induction heads.

      Dao et al., 2022; Olsson et al., 2022
    7. 2023
      Attention sinks

      Models dump a lot of attention on the first token regardless of meaning, a harmless-looking quirk that matters when you drop old tokens from the cache; keeping a few "sink" tokens makes streaming possible.

      Xiao et al., StreamingLLM, 2023
    8. 2024
      Latent attention

      DeepSeek-V2 compresses keys and values into a low-rank latent, reporting a KV cache of roughly 5–13% of standard multi-head attention at matched or better quality in one commentary's reading of its benchmarks.

      DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-V2, 2024
    9. 2025–26
      Sparse and hybrid attention at long context

      Rather than every token scoring every token, newer designs pick a subset. DeepSeek Sparse Attention appeared in the V3.2 line and was reported in a later model family too; Native Sparse Attention is a separate DeepSeek design combining sliding-window, compressed and selected blocks. Several 2025–26 models also mix a few full-attention layers with cheaper linear or state-space layers. Details vary by model and are not always disclosed; treat claims about specific models as needing the technical report.

      Search results, Nov 2026; DeepSeek technical reports (check the originals)
    10. 2025–26
      General-purpose embedding models

      Strong text embedders are now built from large language models and trained with instructions, many open under permissive licences (for example the Qwen3-Embedding family) and Google's Gemini Embedding. Multilingual coverage is a headline feature. Public leaderboard (MTEB) averages across many tasks and languages; they do not tell you how a model does on Arabic banking text, so measure per language on your own data.

      Lee et al., Gemini Embedding, 2025; Zhang et al., Qwen3 Embedding, 2025 (from memory); MTEB leaderboard
    11. 2025
      Limits of single-vector retrieval

      A theoretical and empirical argument that a fixed-size vector cannot represent every possible combination of relevant documents; hard-to-retrieve cases appear even in simple tasks. This supports hybrid search and multi-vector or re-ranking designs.

      Weller et al., 2025 (from memory; check the title and numbers)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Reading attention maps as explanations

      "The model looked at these words, so these words caused the output." Weights show where information flowed in one layer and head, not what the answer depended on.

      Spot itAsk whether the claim was tested by removing or altering the highlighted words and watching the output. If not, treat the map as illustration only (F8).
    • Quadratic cost surprises

      A prototype on five-page documents works; the production job on 300-page loan files is slow and costly.

      Spot itTime the system at three input lengths. Cost that more than doubles when length doubles is the n² table or a cache that no longer fits.
    • Lost in the middle

      The facts the answer needs sit in the middle of a long prompt, and the model fails to use them even though they fit in the window.

      Spot itMove the key passage to the start, middle and end of the same prompt and compare answers. A long window is not a guarantee of equal use.
    • Wrong similarity measure

      An index built with dot product on a model trained for cosine, or the reverse. Nothing crashes; retrieval is quietly worse.

      Spot itRead the model card. Normalise vectors and check that cosine, dot and Euclidean give the same neighbours; if they do not, vectors are not normalised.
    • Mixing embedding models or versions

      Queries embedded with one model, documents with another, or an index not rebuilt after an upgrade. The spaces are unrelated.

      Spot itStore model name and version with every vector, and refuse to compare vectors with different tags. Budget for re-embedding everything on upgrade.
    • Trusting a leaderboard for Arabic or domain text

      A model that tops an English average does poorly on Gulf-dialect complaints or bilingual contracts.

      Spot itBuild 100 to 200 real query-and-correct-passage pairs in each language, and measure recall at 5 for each candidate. Few hours of work, decisive.
    • Near means similar, not true

      The closest passage to "can staff waive the fee?" may be a policy saying the opposite, because negation barely moves a vector. Antonyms and exceptions embed close.

      Spot itInclude negated, "except when" and numerically different cases in the test set. Use a re-ranker or an LLM check on retrieved text.
    • Embeddings leak and encode bias

      Text can often be partly reconstructed from its embedding (Morris et al., 2023), so vectors of customer data are personal data. Embeddings also inherit associations from training text, such as between a name or nationality and a risk word.

      Spot itApply the same access controls to a vector store as to the source documents, and test whether scores differ across names or nationalities for otherwise identical text.
    • Entity embeddings built with the future

      Merchant vectors learned over the whole year and used to predict fraud in March.

      Spot itAsk when each embedding was trained and what data it saw. It must predate the prediction date.
    • Cold start

      A new merchant or a new customer has no history, so no useful embedding; it gets a default vector that looks like "average".

      Spot itReport performance separately for entities seen fewer than N times.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Language models read text by turning every word into a list of numbers where similar meanings sit close together, and then letting each word look at all the others to decide which matter for the task at hand. That second step is called attention, and it is why these models handle context so well. It is also why long documents get expensive: the work grows with the square of the length. So we choose the amount of text we feed in deliberately, and we test the search side on our own English and Arabic documents instead of trusting a public ranking.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Attention computes softmax of QKᵀ over root d, times V: each token takes a weighted average of values, weights set by query-key dot products. The scaling keeps softmax out of saturation. Multi-head runs several in parallel; decoders mask the future. Cost is quadratic in length for compute, and linear per token but large for the KV cache, which grouped-query or latent attention shrink. For retrieval I would pick an embedding model by recall at k on our own bilingual query set, normalise vectors, use the similarity the model was trained with, version the index with the model, and pair it with keyword search and a re-ranker.”
    “What is the difference between attention and an embedding?”

    An embedding is a vector for one item, a position in a space where closeness means similarity. Attention is an operation on a set of vectors: each one builds a new vector as a weighted average of the others. Embeddings store meaning; attention mixes it according to context.

    “Why queries, keys and values? Why not just compare the vectors directly?”

    Because the roles differ. Matching, being matched and being passed on are separate jobs; three learned projections let the model use different aspects of the same token for each. Comparing raw vectors would force "what I am looking for" to equal "what I am", which cannot express relations such as a verb looking for its subject.

    “Why is long context expensive, and what has changed?”

    The score table grows with the square of the length, and the KV cache grows linearly but with a big constant: for an illustrative 32-layer model with full multi-head attention it is about 0.5 MB per token, 64 GB at 128,000 tokens. Grouped-query attention cuts that fourfold, latent attention much further, and FlashAttention removes the memory cost of the table without changing the answer. The compute is still quadratic for full attention, which is why sparse and hybrid designs are active.

    “Can we see which words the model paid attention to and use that as the explanation?”

    We can see it, but it is not a faithful explanation. Weights are one step in a deep computation and later layers recombine them. If we need a reason for a decision, we should use methods that test the output directly, such as altering the input, and I would not put an attention map in a model risk document as evidence of reasoning.

    “Which embedding model should we use?”

    I would shortlist two or three, open and commercial, then measure recall at 5 and 10 on a few hundred real question-and-passage pairs in Arabic and English, with exceptions and negations included. Cost, latency, data residency and whether we can host it matter as much as a point of score. I would also re-test when we upgrade, because every vector must be recomputed.

    “Is the vector database a privacy risk?”

    Yes, treat it like the documents. Research shows text can be partly reconstructed from embeddings, so access controls, retention and residency rules should match the source data. Also keep permissions on retrieval, so a user never gets a passage they could not open directly.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. Three keys give a query scores of 2, 1 and 0. What are the attention weights?

    Exponentials: e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1. Sum 11.11. Weights: 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0%. A gap of 1 in score is a factor of 2.7 in weight.

    2. Why is the score divided by √d, and by how much does that change a model with d = 64?

    A dot product of d unit-variance terms has standard deviation √d, so raw scores grow with dimension and softmax saturates. For d = 64, √d = 8: raw scores spread about ±8, which become about ±1 after dividing. Without it the weights go almost one-hot and gradients vanish.

    3. You double the context from 8,000 to 16,000 tokens. By what factor do the score table and the KV cache grow?

    The score table has n² entries, so it grows 4 times. The KV cache is proportional to n, so it doubles.

    4. A model has 40 layers, 8 KV heads of size 128, and stores 16-bit numbers. How big is the cache for a 100,000-token conversation?

    Per token: 2 × 40 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 163,840 bytes. Times 100,000 = 16.4 GB (about 15.3 GiB), before counting the model weights.

    5. Why does a decoder need a causal mask in training but an embedding encoder does not?

    The decoder is trained to predict the next token; if it could see that token the task would be trivial. An encoder that produces a representation of a text is not predicting the future, and every word benefits from seeing context on both sides.

    6. Two vectors have cosine similarity 0.9 but one is five times longer than the other. Which is larger, the dot product or the cosine, and when does the difference matter?

    Cosine is 0.9 by definition; the dot product is 0.9 × length₁ × length₂, so with a 5× length gap it is far larger. It matters whenever neighbours are ranked: dot product favours long vectors, cosine ignores length. Use the one the model was trained with.

    7. Your English-language benchmark says model A beats model B for retrieval. You deploy for Arabic complaints and it does poorly. What happened and what do you do?

    An average across tasks and mostly English data does not predict Arabic performance, particularly for dialect and mixed Arabic and English text. Build a labelled query set from real Arabic complaints, measure recall at 5 per language for each candidate, and test whether Arabic text is split into many more tokens by the model's tokeniser.

    8. Why can a retrieval system return a policy that says the opposite of what the user needs?

    Embeddings capture topic far better than polarity: "fees may be waived" and "fees may not be waived" are nearly identical in meaning-space. Add a re-ranker or an LLM check on the retrieved text, and include negated queries in your evaluation set.
