    Embeddings and Attention

    Part 1

    What an embedding is

    In one sentenceAn embedding is a list of numbers that places a word (or any item) as a point on a map, so that things with similar meanings sit close together.

    A computer cannot do sums with the word "loan". An embedding turns it into numbers the network can work with, and does so in a way that keeps its meaning.

    Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of directions, not two. The picture is a flattened view, but the idea is the same: near means similar.

    How it differs from other ways to encode words

    • One-hot encoding gives each word its own slot: "loan" is 1 in slot 4,017 and 0 elsewhere. Every word is equally far from every other, so "loan" is no closer to "credit" than to "bus".
    • An embedding is a short, dense list of numbers (say 768) that is learned. Similar words end up close together, because the network is rewarded for using them in similar ways.
    • Hand-made features need a person to decide what matters. Embeddings find it from data.

    How to read embeddings

    • Distance means difference. Close points mean similar things.
    • Cosine similarity is the usual score. It compares the direction of two lists. A score near 1 means nearly the same meaning, near 0 means unrelated.
    • Directions can carry meaning too. The famous example: the step from "man" to "woman" is roughly the same as the step from "king" to "queen".
    • Single numbers mean nothing. You cannot say "the 17th number is about money". Only the positions, distances and directions matter.
    • Embeddings can describe more than words: sentences, images, customers, products, transactions.
    Part 2

    What attention is

    In one sentenceAttention lets each word look at all the other words in the sentence and pull in information from the ones that matter most.

    A word on its own is ambiguous. In "The bank approved the loan", the word "loan" tells us that "bank" means a place that lends money, not a river bank. Attention is how the network makes that link.

    Thicker arc, more attention. "bank" takes most of its new meaning from "loan", a little from "approved", and a little from itself.

    How it works, for one word:

    1. Make a question, a label and a content. Each word's embedding is turned into three lists: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what I will hand over if chosen).
    2. Score every word. Compare the word's query with every key. A good match gives a high score.
    3. Turn scores into weights. A function called softmax makes them positive and adds them up to 1.
    4. Blend. The new vector for the word is the weighted mix of all the values. Here, mostly "loan".

    Every word does this at the same time. The result is a contextual embedding: "bank" now has a different vector in "river bank" than in "bank loan".

    How to read attention

    • A high weight (0.55) means the word draws mostly from that other word.
    • Even weights (0.20 each across five words) mean no clear focus.
    • Several heads. Models run many attention patterns side by side ("multi-head"). One head might track grammar, another which pronoun refers to whom.
    • Weights are a clue, not a full explanation. They show where information came from, not why the model decided something.

    Where it fits: the transformer

    A transformer is a stack of attention layers, each followed by a small ordinary network. Because attention on its own ignores word order, the model also adds a position signal to each embedding. Large language models are transformers.

    Part 3

    How it compares and when it matters

    ApproachHow it links wordsStrong atWeak at
    AttentionEvery word looks at every other word directlyLong-range links; trains in parallelCost grows with the square of the length
    RNN / LSTMOne memory passed along step by stepSmall, streams easilyForgets far-back words; slow to train
    CNNSmall windows of neighboursLocal patternsDistant words need many layers to meet
    Bag of wordsCounts words, ignores orderSimple, quick baselineNo word order, no meaning

    Embeddings are strong at: search by meaning, grouping similar items, and feeding other models. They are weak at: rare or brand-new words, and they carry biases from the text they were trained on.

    Attention is strong at: language and any data where distant parts relate. It is weak at: very long inputs. Comparing every word with every other means 10 times the length costs about 100 times the work.

    In practice: use a ready-made embedding model for search and similarity. Use a pretrained transformer for text and adjust it, rather than training one yourself.

    Show the formula

    Attention = softmax(Q × Kᵀ ÷ √d) × V. Q, K and V hold the queries, keys and values of all the words. Q × Kᵀ gives every word's score against every other. Dividing by √d (the size of the lists) keeps the scores from growing too large. Softmax turns the scores into weights, and multiplying by V blends the values.

    Cosine similarity = (A · B) ÷ (|A| × |B|): the dot product of two lists divided by their lengths. It ranges from −1 to 1.

    In banking, embeddings power search over policies and customer queries, and attention-based models read contracts, emails and complaints.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Treating embedding numbers as readable

      No single number has a meaning. Compare whole embeddings by distance or cosine similarity.

    • Mixing embeddings from different models

      Each model has its own map. Embeddings from two models cannot be compared with each other.

    • Reading attention weights as proof of reasoning

      They show where information flowed, not why the answer came out as it did.

    • Forgetting the cost of long inputs

      Doubling the length roughly quadruples the attention work. Long documents need chunking or special designs.

    1. Why is an embedding better than one-hot encoding?

    One-hot makes every word equally different. An embedding puts similar words close together, so the model can share what it learns between them.

    2. Two embeddings have cosine similarity 0.95. What does that mean?

    They point in almost the same direction, so the items have very similar meanings.

    3. What do the attention weights for one word add up to?

    1. They split that word's attention across all the words, itself included.

    4. What do the query, key and value do?

    Query against key gives the match score. The scores become weights, and the weights blend the values.

    5. Why does "bank" get a different vector in "river bank" and "bank loan"?

    Attention mixes in information from the neighbouring words, so the result depends on context.
