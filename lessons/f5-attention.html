    Attention and Embeddings: How Models Look Things Up and Where Meaning Lives

    Attention is a soft, learned lookup that lets every word read every other word. An embedding is the vector that makes a word, a merchant or a customer something a model can compare. Between them they explain most of what a modern language model does, and most of what it costs.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn embedding turns each item into a point whose position carries meaning, and attention lets each item build a new, context-aware point by taking a weighted average of the other items, with the weights decided by how well a question from one matches a label on another.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Attention is how a word finds the other words that matter. Take: "The transfer to the supplier failed, so I called the branch and asked them to reverse it." What is "it"? The transfer. Your mind jumped back seven words and skipped "supplier", "branch" and "called". Attention does the same: every word asks a question ("what do I refer to?"), every other word shows a label ("I can be reversed"), and the best matches get the most weight.

    Before attention, models read left to right and squeezed everything into one fixed-size memory, so long sentences lost their beginnings. Attention keeps every word available and looks up what it needs at each step. The 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need built a whole model from it, the transformer, which now sits under almost every language, vision and speech model at the bank.

    Attention needs numbers to work on. A model cannot read "ADNOC fuel" or "محطة بنزين"; it reads vectors, lists of hundreds or thousands of numbers. An embedding maps an item to its vector, trained so items that behave alike land close together. Petrol station and fuel retailer should be neighbours. An ATM withdrawal and a crypto-exchange transfer should not.

    Hold onto thisEmbeddings store meaning as position in space. Attention combines meaning as a weighted average over positions. Layers, heads and feed-forward blocks just repeat these two ideas.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Queries, keys and values

    Think of a library. Your search words are the query, the catalogue entries are the keys, and the book you carry home is the value. Matching, being matched and being handed over are different jobs, so each token gets three views. Stack the token vectors (a token is a word or word piece, see tokenisation) into a matrix X with n rows and multiply by three learned matrices (F1.1):

    • Query Q = X WQ: what this token is looking for.
    • Key K = X WK: what it advertises.
    • Value V = X WV: what it hands over if picked.

    Gradient descent (F1.3) learns the matrices, so the model chooses its own questions and labels. Then three steps: score every query against every key with a dot product (large when vectors point the same way); turn each row of scores into positive weights summing to 1 with softmax; take the weighted average of the values.

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V

    Q KT is an n × n table of match scores: entry (i, j) says how much token i wants token j. Softmax turns each row into weights summing to 1. Multiplying by V gives each token a new vector that is a blend of everyone's values. dk is the key dimension; the division by its square root is explained next.

    Reading a weight. Three keys score 2, 1 and 0 for a query. Softmax gives 66.5%, 24.5% and 9.0%. Each extra point of score multiplies the weight by about 2.7. A row of weights is "how much of my new vector comes from each other token". Do not read a high weight as "this token caused the answer".

    Position is missing from the formula. "Bank robbed the man" and "man robbed the bank" would look identical, because a weighted sum ignores order. Transformers add position information to the embeddings first (sinusoidal patterns originally, rotary embeddings, RoPE, in most current models). Attention is a set operation with order bolted on.

    The whole thing in NumPy:

    import numpy as np

def attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, causal=False):
    Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv           # three views of every token
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(K.shape[-1])      # n x n match table
    if causal:                                    # a token may not see the future
        scores = np.where(np.tril(np.ones_like(scores)) == 1, scores, -np.inf)
    w = np.exp(scores - scores.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    w = w / w.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)         # softmax: each row sums to 1
    return w @ V, w                               # blended values, and the weights

    Why divide by √d, and what softmax does with it

    Without the division, big vectors make attention go all-or-nothing. A dot product of d unit-variance pairs has variance d, so scores spread about ±√d: ±8 at d = 64, ±32 at d = 1,000. Softmax exponentiates, so a gap of 8 gives one key e8 ≈ 3,000 times another's weight. The output is nearly one-hot, its gradient is nearly zero, and the layer stops learning. Dividing by √d restores a spread of about 1 at any dimension. It is a stability trick, nothing more.

    Multi-head attention and what heads learn

    One attention pattern is not enough. A word may need its subject, a number three words back and an earlier mention of the same name, all at once. Multi-head attention runs several attentions side by side, each with its own WQ, WK, WV in a smaller slice (a 4,096-wide model might use 32 heads of 128). Outputs are joined and mixed by one more matrix. Cost is about that of one wide head, but you get several independent views, like several readers highlighting a contract for different things.

    Heads do recognisable jobs: previous-token, syntax, and induction heads, which see "A B ... A" and predict "B". That circuit underlies much in-context learning, following a pattern shown in the prompt (Olsson et al., 2022). Two cautions. Many heads are redundant and can be pruned. And attention weights are not a reliable explanation of an answer (Jain and Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019 argue it is subtler). Never present an attention heat map to a validator as "the model's reasoning".

    Each layer pairs attention with a feed-forward network applied to each token alone, with residual connections and normalisation around both. Attention moves information between tokens; the feed-forward block transforms it within each. Early layers tend to build local features, later ones abstract relations.

    Causal masks, cost, and the KV cache

    Causal mask. A model trained to predict token 6 must not see token 6, or it just copies. So generation models set scores for later positions to minus infinity before softmax, giving them zero weight. Rows still sum to 1, over fewer tokens. Read-only encoders (for embeddings and classification) let every token see every other, both ways.

    Quadratic cost. The n × n table quadruples when text doubles. At 128,000 tokens one head in one layer scores about 1.6 × 1010 pairs, and a model has dozens of heads and layers. Two ideas made long context affordable:

    • FlashAttention gives the exact same result but works in small tiles in fast on-chip memory and never writes the big table out. An exact speed-up, not an approximation (Dao et al., 2022).
    • KV cache. Keys and values of earlier tokens never change, so generation stores and reuses them. Each new token is cheap in compute, but the cache grows with every token in every layer, so memory becomes the bottleneck. See context windows and the KV cache.

    To shrink the cache, share keys and values across heads. Multi-query attention uses one shared set for all heads (Shazeer, 2019). Grouped-query attention uses a few, say 8 groups for 32 query heads, and is widely adopted (Ainslie et al., 2023). DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention caches a compressed latent vector instead, reporting a cache a small fraction of standard size at matched quality (DeepSeek-V2, 2024).

    Embeddings: turning things into points

    An embedding layer is a table with one row per token: token 4,817 fetches row 4,817. Rows start random. Training moves them so tokens used alike end up close, because nearby vectors let the network treat them alike. Nobody tells the model "petrol" and "fuel" are related; they keep the same company. That is the distributional idea ("you shall know a word by the company it keeps"), and why word2vec (2013) gave king − man + woman ≈ queen. That example is cherry-picked and fragile, but the geometry is real: directions encode relations (R5).

    Kinds you will meet:

    How a sentence embedding model is made. Take a pretrained encoder and train it contrastively (F5.7): pull each text towards its match (a paraphrase, the answer, the translation) and push the rest of the batch away. Sentence-BERT (2019) used sentence pairs; modern models use billions of pairs, often with instructions such as "represent this query for retrieving passages". Multilingual ones train on translation pairs, so "cash withdrawal" and "سحب نقدي" land together. That is what makes cross-lingual search work.

    Measuring closeness. Three rulers, not interchangeable:

    • Cosine similarity: angle only, ignoring length. The text default, because length often reflects accidents such as word frequency. 1 means same direction, 0 unrelated.
    • Dot product: angle and length, so long vectors win. If the model was trained with it (many recommenders), length carries real meaning such as popularity.
    • Euclidean distance: straight-line gap. For vectors normalised to length 1 it ranks neighbours exactly as cosine does, so many systems normalise once and use the fastest.

    Use what the model was trained with. A mismatch returns wrong neighbours silently.

    Entity embeddings in tabular work. One-hot encoding 200,000 merchants is awkward. A neural net can instead learn a small vector per category (Guo and Berkhahn, 2016), so rare merchants borrow strength from similar ones. Export the vectors as features for a gradient-boosted model when you have a high-cardinality field. Risk: if embeddings are learned from data including the test period or the label, they carry the answer (F3.1).

    The two halves link here. The embedding table is a fixed lookup. After each attention layer a token's vector is rewritten using its neighbours, and after the last layer it is a contextual representation. A transformer is a machine that repeatedly refines embeddings by letting them consult each other, which is why you can read a sentence embedding off the last layers.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reading attention maps as explanations

      "The model looked at these words, so they caused the output." Weights show where information flowed in one head, not what the answer depended on.

      Spot itWas the claim tested by removing or altering the highlighted words? If not, it is illustration only (F8).
    • Quadratic cost surprises

      A prototype on five-page documents works; production on 300-page loan files is slow and costly.

      Spot itTime three input lengths. Cost that more than doubles when length doubles is the n² table or a cache that no longer fits.
    • Lost in the middle

      The needed facts sit mid-prompt and the model ignores them though they fit in the window.

      Spot itMove the key passage to start, middle and end of the same prompt and compare answers.
    • Wrong similarity measure

      Dot-product index on a model trained for cosine, or the reverse. Nothing crashes; retrieval is quietly worse.

      Spot itRead the model card. After normalising, cosine, dot and Euclidean must give the same neighbours; if not, vectors are not normalised.
    • Mixing embedding models or versions

      Queries embedded with one model and documents with another, or an index not rebuilt after an upgrade. The spaces are unrelated.

      Spot itStore model name and version with every vector and refuse mismatched comparisons. Budget for re-embedding on upgrade.
    • Trusting a leaderboard for Arabic or domain text

      A model topping an English average does poorly on Gulf-dialect complaints or bilingual contracts.

      Spot itBuild 100 to 200 real query-and-correct-passage pairs per language and measure recall at 5 for each candidate. A few hours, decisive.
    • Near means similar, not true

      The closest passage to "can staff waive the fee?" may say the opposite, because negation barely moves a vector.

      Spot itPut negated, "except when" and numerically different cases in the test set. Add a re-ranker or LLM check.
    • Embeddings leak and encode bias

      Text can often be partly reconstructed from its embedding (Morris et al., 2023), so vectors of customer data are personal data. They also inherit associations such as name or nationality with a risk word.

      Spot itGive the vector store the same access controls as the source documents, and test whether scores differ across names or nationalities for identical text.
    • Entity embeddings built with the future

      Merchant vectors learned over the whole year, used to predict fraud in March.

      Spot itAsk when each embedding was trained and on what data. It must predate the prediction date.
    • Cold start

      A new merchant or customer has no history, so it gets a default "average" vector.

      Spot itReport performance separately for entities seen fewer than N times.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Three keys give a query scores of 2, 1 and 0. What are the attention weights?

    e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1; sum 11.11. Weights 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0%. A score gap of 1 is a factor of 2.7 in weight.

    2. Why is the score divided by √d, and by how much for d = 64?

    A dot product of d unit-variance terms has standard deviation √d, so softmax saturates as d grows. For d = 64, √d = 8: raw scores spread about ±8, becoming about ±1. Without it weights go almost one-hot and gradients vanish.

    3. Context doubles from 8,000 to 16,000 tokens. By what factor do the score table and the KV cache grow?

    The table has n² entries: 4 times. The cache is proportional to n: 2 times.

    4. A model has 40 layers, 8 KV heads of size 128, 16-bit numbers. How big is the cache for 100,000 tokens?

    Per token: 2 × 40 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 163,840 bytes. Times 100,000 = 16.4 GB (about 15.3 GiB), before model weights.

    5. Why does a decoder need a causal mask in training but an embedding encoder does not?

    A decoder predicts the next token; seeing it makes the task trivial. An encoder building a representation of a text is not predicting the future, and each word benefits from context on both sides.

    6. Two vectors have cosine 0.9 but one is five times longer. Which is larger, dot product or cosine, and when does it matter?

    Cosine is 0.9 by definition; the dot product is 0.9 × length₁ × length₂, far larger with a 5× gap. It matters when ranking neighbours: dot product favours long vectors, cosine ignores length. Use what the model was trained with.

    7. An English benchmark says model A beats B for retrieval. Deployed on Arabic complaints, it does poorly. What happened, and what do you do?

    An English-heavy average across tasks does not predict Arabic performance, especially dialect and mixed-language text. Build a labelled query set from real Arabic complaints, measure recall at 5 per language per candidate, and check whether the tokeniser splits Arabic into many more tokens.

    8. Why can retrieval return a policy that says the opposite of what the user needs?

    Embeddings capture topic far better than polarity: "fees may be waived" and "fees may not be waived" sit nearly together. Add a re-ranker or LLM check and include negated queries in evaluation.
