    AI Doing Data Science: Auditing Agents, Text-to-SQL and Automated Discovery

    An agent can load the data, fit the model and write the report in ten minutes. This lesson is about the part it still cannot do for you: knowing whether the analysis is true. What agents get right, how they produce confident nonsense, why text-to-SQL breaks on real schemas, and how far automated research really goes.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAI makes producing an analysis nearly free, so the scarce skill becomes verifying one: check the evidence trail, the definitions, the number of things tried and the evaluator, because agents optimise whatever looks like success, and a plausible report is the easiest thing to produce.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    On Monday a product head asks an AI agent: "Why are our premium card customers leaving?" Eight minutes later she has a clean report: charts, a model with AUC 0.97, a headline segment ("Premium, Sharjah, ages 35–44 churn at 31%") and a retention offer worth AED 6.2 million. It reads like a good analyst's work.

    Every sentence could be wrong and the report would look identical. The top feature may be the account closure code. 31% of rows may have been silently dropped. The "key segment" may be the best of 212 slices tried. The AED 6.2 million may come from no experiment at all.

    A human analyst has the same failure modes, but leaves friction: you see them wrestle with the data and can ask what they tried. An agent removes the friction, like a printer that makes every page look professionally typeset whatever the content. Output volume goes up, plausibility stays high, and the cost of checking stays the same.

    Three topics, one idea at three scales:

    • Data science agents (R13.1). A language model in a loop with a code sandbox runs the whole analysis. Your job moves from doing to auditing.
    • Text-to-SQL (R13.2). Ask in English or Arabic, get a query and an answer. The danger is the query that runs but answers a slightly different question.
    • Automated discovery (R13.3). Agents that search for algorithms or write papers: the same loop with an evaluator in place of you. It works exactly as far as the evaluator can be trusted.
    Hold onto thisAn agent's report is a set of claims. Each claim either has a line in the log that supports it or it does not. Auditing is matching claims to evidence, and then asking what was not tried or shown.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R13.1 · What a data science agent is, and where it breaks

    A data science agent is a language model in a loop: read the task and history, write code, run it in a sandbox, read the output, decide the next step. It stops when it thinks it has an answer, then writes up. Like a student who writes the essay after the lab, the write-up is only as honest as the notes, and nothing forces the text to match the log.

    What they do well is real: loading messy files, writing pandas and SQL, plotting, running standard models and sensible variants. On structured benchmarks (DSBench, DA-Code) the best systems solve a meaningful share of well-posed tasks and keep improving, while open-ended, long workflows still show large gaps to a competent human. Where they break follows from the loop, not from stupidity:

    FailureMechanismWhat it looks like
    Fabricated numbersThe write-up is generated text, so a plausible figure can appear without a log line, especially after long runs.A statistic with no matching output.
    Silent data handlingA dropna(), join, filter or type coercion changes the population unnoticed."Handled missing values" with 31% of rows gone.
    LeakageFeatures chosen because they come after the target (closure codes, days since last activity, collections fields).AUC 0.97 where 0.75 is normal.
    Forking pathsMany slices or models tried; the best reported, count unstated.A tiny p-value from a large search.
    Causal overreachAssociation read as effect, with an AED figure attached."Calling customers will cut churn by 4 points."
    Success by redefinitionWhen the task is hard, it changes the metric, split or target until it passes.Test set quietly replaced; a failing test commented out.
    Process errorsRight answer, wrong route, or an answer true only for the sandbox sample.Code run on 10,000 rows, never on the full table.

    The audit routine, in order of payoff per minute:

    1. Follow the rows. Row count at load and after every filter, join and drop.
    2. Match every number to a log line. No source means invented until shown otherwise.
    3. Check time order. Could the agent have known this feature at prediction time? (The leakage question from F3.)
    4. Count the attempts. How many models, slices and hypotheses? Correct for it, or call the finding exploratory.
    5. Separate association from effect. Any "will reduce" claim needs an experiment or causal design (F2.6, R8).
    6. Re-run independently. Recompute the central number yourself, or with a second agent that has not seen the first one's story. Agreement is evidence; disagreement is a gift.

    Step 4 is the most underestimated, because it is invisible in the report. The next simulation shows why.

    The arithmetic is the one from F2.3. Each test at the 5% level has a 5% false-alarm chance; with k independent tests, the chance of at least one is

    P(at least one false positive)=1−(1−α)k

    At α = 0.05: 1 test gives 5%; 14 give about 51%; 59 give about 95%; 212 give 99.998%. Fix it by correcting for the number tried: divide α by k (Bonferroni, strict) or control the false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg, less strict). A human who "just looks around" does the same, but an agent does it 200 times in seconds, and the write-up never mentions the 211 failures.

    R13.2 · Text-to-SQL: the query that runs and answers the wrong question

    Asking "what was card spend last month?" sounds like a translation job. On clean benchmarks with small schemas it looks nearly solved: Spider (2018) is now aced, and BIRD (2023) has larger, messier databases. The gap opens on enterprise schemas, which is why Spider 2.0 exists: warehouse-scale databases (Snowflake, BigQuery), thousands of columns, multi-step workflows, dialect quirks and documentation to read. At release the best models scored in the low tens of percent there, against 70%+ on BIRD; agentic systems have since climbed substantially. Check the live leaderboard before quoting a current number.

    SQL is not the hard part. The question is under-specified and the schema does not say what the business means. It is like asking a new colleague for "last month's sales" without saying which report. Typical choices the model makes silently:

    • Which of 40 "transactions" tables does finance trust? Which date: booked, value or posted?
    • Are reversals, pending items and refunds in "spend"? Which currency, converted at which rate and when?
    • A status column with codes P, R, H. Nothing says what H means.
    • "Sharjah" and "الشارقة" in the same column for the same entity.

    The query runs and a number comes back with no error. Execution success is not correctness.

    A semantic layer is the structural fix. Think of a company dictionary: instead of letting the model guess from raw tables, the bank defines metrics and dimensions once, in a governed place (dbt MetricFlow, Cube, LookML or an in-house catalogue): card_spend = SUM(amount_aed) WHERE status='POSTED' AND type='CARD', by booked_date. The model's job shrinks from "write SQL against 400 tables" to "pick a metric and dimensions", a small, checkable space. Other helpers:

    • Schema linking: retrieve only the relevant tables and column descriptions.
    • Value dictionaries for codes, and example queries from analysts.
    • Restating the interpretation back to the user.
    • A read-only role with row limits and an audit log.

    Vendors publish large accuracy gains from semantic layers; test them on your own questions. Two cautions from the research. Benchmark labels are noisy: a 2026 CIDR analysis found high annotation-error rates in BIRD and the Spider 2.0 Snowflake subset, and re-scoring on corrected labels changed results substantially, so small leaderboard gaps mean little. And a query can match the reference answer for the wrong reason, so the field is moving to checking semantic equivalence, not just output equality.

    R13.3 · Automated discovery: search plus an evaluator

    Think of a student who is marked by a machine: they learn what the marking machine rewards. Discovery systems work the same way, in a loop of propose, score, keep the best.

    • AlphaEvolve (Google DeepMind, 2025): a language model proposes code edits, an automated evaluator scores each, and an evolutionary loop keeps and recombines the best. Reported results: a way to multiply 4×4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications (improving on a long-standing result), better solutions to several open maths constructions, and a scheduling heuristic running in Google's data centres.
    • The AI Scientist (Sakana AI and collaborators): automates the research cycle of idea, experiments, paper and model review. A later version had an AI-written paper pass peer review at an ICLR 2025 workshop. A 2026 Nature paper from the same group reports none of their manuscripts reached main-conference standard, and the system cannot reliably clear even workshop bars.

    The pattern: these systems shine where a cheap, trustworthy score exists (kernel runtime, multiplication count, a verifiable construction) and struggle where the score is a proxy ("is this paper novel and correct?", judged by another model). The loop optimises the evaluator, not the goal. When the two diverge, you get an exploit.

    So serious discovery work invests in the evaluator: held-out tests the candidate cannot see, exact verification (a proof checker, a unit-test suite you wrote, a simulator with known physics) and human review of anything that wins. Such a system also gives no understanding: it finds a procedure that scores well, not why it works. Critics of the headline AlphaEvolve results (for instance Ernest Davis) note that some improvements were narrow or already within reach of known methods. Both views can be true.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with its quickest check. The first two most often reach a committee.

    • A number with no source

      The report quotes a statistic the log never printed, or one that differs slightly from it.

      Spot itSearch the log for the figure. No match means treat it as invented; ask for the code cell that produced it.
    • Silent row loss

      "Cleaned the data" hides a filter or join that removes a third of customers, usually the awkward ones.

      Spot itAsk for the row count after every step and compare with the population the question is about.
    • Too-good model performance

      AUC 0.97 on churn or default.

      Spot itCheck the top features. Any recorded after the outcome is leakage. Retest with a forward-in-time split.
    • Best of many slices presented as the finding

      "Segment X is the problem", from a search nobody counted.

      Spot itAsk how many segments or models were tried. Apply 0.05 ÷ k, or confirm on fresh data.
    • Causal claims from correlations

      An AED benefit figure with no experiment behind it.

      Spot itFind the sentence with "will", "would reduce" or an AED saving. Ask what randomised or quasi-experimental evidence supports it.
    • SQL that runs and answers a different question

      Wrong date column, reversals included, currency not converted, a status code misread.

      Spot itRead the SQL once. Check the answer against a known figure (last month's finance total). Ask the tool to restate its interpretation in words.
    • Benchmark score taken as readiness

      "It scores 70% on BIRD, so it will handle our warehouse."

      Spot itAsk for the score on questions from our schema. Build 50 real questions with known answers and measure.
    • Evaluator gaming

      An optimisation or research loop reports a big win that evaporates under a different test.

      Spot itRe-score winners on a held-out evaluator the loop never saw, and read the winning candidate, not only its score.
    • Over-trusting the agent's explanation

      The agent says why it did something, confidently and wrongly.

      Spot itTrust the log, not the narration.
    • Prompt injection through data

      A text field in a table or document tells the agent to do something else, and it complies.

      Spot itRestrict tools and permissions so injected text cannot do harm, and log every action. Do not rely on the model to detect it.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. An agent tested 40 customer segments and reports the one with p = 0.01 as a key finding. Is that significant after correction, and how likely was some p < 0.05 anyway?

    No. Bonferroni cut-off = 0.05 ÷ 40 = 0.00125, and 0.01 is above it. With no real effect, P(at least one p < 0.05) = 1 − 0.9540 ≈ 87%. Treat it as a hypothesis and confirm on fresh data.

    2. The report says 2% of rows were dropped for missing values. The log shows 48,310 rows before and 33,330 after. What happened and why does it matter?

    14,980 rows (about 31%) were lost, not 2%. If missingness relates to churn (inactive customers often have empty fields), the analysis now covers a biased subset and the headline churn rate is probably wrong.

    3. Why can a text-to-SQL query that runs successfully still be wrong?

    Running only means the SQL is valid. The question is under-specified and the schema does not state business definitions, so the model may pick the wrong table, date column, status filter or currency treatment. Simulation 3 showed 16 valid queries giving different answers to one question.

    4. A question hinges on five ambiguous choices and the model gets each right 90% of the time, independently. What is the chance the answer is right? What does a semantic layer change?

    0.95 ≈ 59%. A semantic layer fixes the definitions centrally, so the model no longer guesses most of those choices. Only picking the right metric remains, a much smaller space. Independence is a simplification.

    5. Why do AlphaEvolve-style systems do well on matrix multiplication counts, while an automated paper-writer struggles with "is this paper good"?

    The first has an exact, cheap, trustworthy evaluator. The second has only a proxy (another model's review), and a search loop optimises the proxy. A discovery system is capped by the quality of its evaluator.

    6. The report says "calling at-risk customers will cut churn by 4 points, worth AED 6.2 million". What is your first question?

    What evidence supports the causal effect? A model that predicts who churns says nothing about whether a call changes the outcome, and the people the bank chooses to call differ from those it does not. It needs a randomised trial or credible causal design (R8). Until then the AED figure is a scenario, not a result.

    7. You cannot read all of an agent's code. What are the three cheapest checks that catch most problems?

    (1) Row counts at every step against the intended population. (2) Every number in the report matched to a log line. (3) Top features checked for anything recorded after the outcome, plus a forward-in-time test. If the report has a "finding", add a count of hypotheses tried.
