    AI Doing Data Science: Auditing Agents, Text-to-SQL and Automated Discovery

    An agent can load the data, fit the model and write the report in ten minutes. This lesson is about the part it still cannot do for you: knowing whether the analysis is true. What agents get right, how they produce confident nonsense, why text-to-SQL breaks on real schemas, and how far automated research really goes.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAI makes producing an analysis nearly free, so the scarce skill becomes verifying one: check the evidence trail, the definitions, the number of things tried and the evaluator, because agents optimise whatever looks like success, and a plausible report is the easiest thing to produce.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    On Monday a product head at the bank asks an AI agent: "Why are our premium card customers leaving?" Eight minutes later she has a clean report: charts, a model with AUC 0.97, a headline segment ("Premium, Sharjah, ages 35–44 churn at 31%") and a recommended retention offer worth AED 6.2 million. It reads like the work of a good analyst.

    Every sentence in it could be wrong, and the report would look exactly the same. Maybe the model's top feature was the account closure code. Maybe 31% of rows were silently dropped. Maybe the "key segment" was the best of 212 slices tried. Maybe the AED 6.2 million came from no experiment at all.

    A human analyst has the same failure modes, but a human also leaves friction: you can see them wrestle with the data, you can ask what they tried and they remember. An agent removes the friction. Output volume goes up, plausibility stays high, and the cost of checking stays the same. That asymmetry is the whole subject.

    The three topics are one idea at three scales.

    • Data science agents (R13.1). A language model in a loop with a code sandbox. It runs the whole analysis. Your job moves from doing to auditing.
    • Text-to-SQL (R13.2). The narrow, most commercially pushed case: ask a question in English or Arabic, get a query and an answer. The danger is the query that runs and answers a slightly different question.
    • Automated discovery (R13.3). Agents that search for new algorithms or write whole papers. The same loop, with an evaluator in place of you. It works exactly as far as the evaluator can be trusted.
    Hold onto thisAn agent's report is a set of claims. Each claim either has a line in the log that supports it or it does not. Auditing is matching claims to evidence, and then asking what was not tried or shown.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R13.1 · What a data science agent is, and where it breaks

    Strip away the branding. A data science agent is a language model that repeats a loop: read the task and what has happened so far, write some code, run it in a sandbox, read the output, decide the next step. It stops when it decides it has an answer, then writes up. The "analysis" is whatever the model's text says about the log. Nothing forces the text to match the log.

    What they do well is real, and it is not trivial. They are fast at loading messy files, writing pandas and SQL, plotting, running standard models and trying sensible variants. On structured benchmarks (for example DSBench and DA-Code, built from real data analysis and Kaggle-style tasks) the best systems solve a meaningful share of well-posed tasks, and they keep improving. Benchmarks that stress long, open-ended workflows still show large gaps to what a competent human does end to end (see Part 4).

    Where they break follows from the loop, not from stupidity:

    FailureMechanismWhat it looks like
    Fabricated numbersThe write-up is generated text. If a figure was not in the log, the model can still produce a plausible one, especially after a long run when it works from a summary of earlier steps.A statistic in the report with no matching output line.
    Silent data handlingA dropna(), a join, a filter or a type coercion changes the population. The agent may not look at row counts."Handled missing values" with 31% of rows gone.
    LeakageThe agent picks features that predict the target because they come after it (closure codes, days since last activity, a field filled in by collections).AUC of 0.97 on a problem where 0.75 is normal.
    Forking pathsIt tries many slices, models or specifications and reports the best, often without saying how many it tried.A "key finding" with a tiny p-value from a large search.
    Causal overreachIt reads an association as an effect and writes a recommendation with an AED figure."Calling customers will cut churn by 4 points."
    Success by redefinitionWhen the task is hard it changes the metric, the split or the target until it passes, and reports the pass.Test set quietly replaced; a failing test commented out.
    Process errors, not just wrong answersRight final answer, wrong route, or an answer that holds only for the sandbox sample.Code that ran on 10,000 rows and was never run on the full table.

    The audit routine, in order of payoff per minute:

    1. Follow the rows. Row count at load, after every filter, join and drop. Ask where the population changed.
    2. Match every number to a log line. A number with no source is treated as invented until shown otherwise.
    3. Check the target and the features for time order. Could the agent have known this at prediction time? (This is the leakage question from F3.)
    4. Count the attempts. How many models, slices and hypotheses were tried? Correct for it, or say the finding is exploratory.
    5. Separate association from effect. Any "will reduce" claim needs an experiment or a causal design (F2.6, R8).
    6. Re-run independently. Rerun the central number with your own code, or have a second agent with no access to the first one's story do it. Agreement is evidence; disagreement is a gift.

    Step 4 is the one people underestimate, because it is invisible in the report. The next simulation shows why.

    The arithmetic is the one from F2.3. Each test at the 5% level has a 5% chance of a false alarm; with k independent tests the chance of at least one is

    P(at least one false positive)=1−(1−α)k

    At α = 0.05: 1 test gives 5%; 14 tests give about 51%; 59 tests give about 95%; 212 tests give 99.998%. The fix is to correct for the number tried: divide α by k (Bonferroni, strict) or control the false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg, less strict). A human analyst who "just looks around" does the same thing. An agent does it in seconds and 200 times, and the write-up never mentions the 211 failures.

    R13.2 · Text-to-SQL: the query that runs and answers the wrong question

    The task: turn "What was card spend last month?" into SQL over the bank's warehouse. On clean benchmarks with small schemas this looks nearly solved. The standard academic ones are Spider (a 2018 benchmark that models now ace) and BIRD (2023, with larger databases and messier values). The gap opens on enterprise schemas, which is why Spider 2.0 was built: real warehouse-scale databases (Snowflake, BigQuery), thousands of columns, multi-step workflows, dialect quirks and documentation to read. At release, the best models scored in the low tens of percent there, against 70%+ on BIRD, and agentic systems have since climbed substantially. The exact current top number is not something to quote without checking the live leaderboard.

    Why is the enterprise case so much harder? Not because SQL is hard. Because the question is under-specified and the schema does not say what the business means:

    • Which of the 40 tables holding "transactions" is the one finance trusts? Which date: booked, value, posted?
    • Are reversals, pending items and refunds in "spend"? Which currency, and converted at which rate and when?
    • A column named status with codes P, R, H. Nothing in the data says what H means.
    • Arabic and English names for the same entity, "Sharjah" and "الشارقة", in the same column.

    Every one of those is a choice. The model makes it silently, the query runs, a number comes back with no error. Execution success is not correctness. The simulation below makes it concrete.

    A semantic layer is the structural fix. Instead of letting the model guess from raw tables, the bank defines metrics and dimensions once, in a governed layer (examples: dbt's MetricFlow, Cube, LookML, or a bank's own metrics catalogue): card_spend = SUM(amount_aed) WHERE status='POSTED' AND type='CARD', by booked_date. The model's job shrinks from "write SQL against 400 tables" to "pick a metric and dimensions", a much smaller, checkable space. Other helpers that work in practice: schema linking (retrieve only relevant tables and column descriptions), documented value dictionaries for codes, example queries from analysts, a requirement to restate the interpretation back to the user, and a read-only role with row limits and an audit log. Vendors publish large accuracy gains from semantic layers; treat those as claims to test on your own questions.

    Two further cautions from the research itself. Benchmark labels are noisy: a 2026 CIDR analysis found high annotation-error rates in BIRD and in the Spider 2.0 Snowflake subset, and re-scoring leaderboard systems on corrected labels changed results substantially, so small leaderboard gaps mean little. And a query can match the reference answer for the wrong reason; the field is moving toward checking semantic equivalence, not just output equality.

    R13.3 · Automated discovery: search plus an evaluator

    Two families, same skeleton. AlphaEvolve (Google DeepMind, 2025) pairs a language model that proposes code edits with an automated evaluator that scores each candidate, and an evolutionary loop that keeps and recombines the best. Reported results include a procedure for multiplying 4×4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, improving on a result that had stood since Strassen-era methods, better solutions to several open maths constructions, and a scheduling heuristic running in Google's data centres. The AI Scientist (Sakana AI and collaborators) goes further and automates the research cycle: generate ideas, write and run experiments, write the paper, and have a model review it. A later version had a fully AI-generated paper pass peer review at an ICLR 2025 workshop. A 2026 Nature paper from the same group reports that none of their submitted manuscripts reached the standard of the main ICLR conference, and that the system cannot yet reliably clear even workshop bars.

    The shape of the result is consistent. These systems shine where a cheap, trustworthy score exists: runtime of a kernel, number of multiplications, a verifiable construction. They struggle where the score is a proxy: "is this paper novel and correct?" judged by another model. The loop optimises the evaluator, not the goal. When the two diverge, you get an exploit.

    That is why serious automated-discovery work puts its effort into the evaluator: held-out tests the candidate cannot see, exact verification (a proof checker, a unit test suite you wrote, a simulator with known physics), and human review of anything that wins. Also note what such a system does not give you: understanding. It finds a procedure that scores well; whether anyone knows why it works is a separate question. Critics of the headline AlphaEvolve results (for instance Ernest Davis's notes) point out that some of the improvements were narrow or already within reach of known methods. Both views can be true.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Self-service analytics. Business teams asking questions of the warehouse in English or Arabic. The biggest text-to-SQL risk is a confident wrong number reaching a committee paper.
    • Model development acceleration. Agents drafting feature pipelines and baseline models for credit, fraud or AML. Validation (C2) must treat agent-written code like any other code, and ask for the log.
    • Regulatory and audit trails. If an agent produced an analysis behind a decision, someone must be able to reproduce it. Whether regulators (CBUAE, SAMA) have set specific expectations for agent-generated analysis is something to confirm with compliance; assume existing model risk and record-keeping expectations apply.
    • Data access. An agent with credentials can read what its role can read. Least privilege, read-only roles and masked PII are not optional.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Agent capability for data work is climbing quickly, and so is the volume of benchmarks measuring it. Three things are settled: agents are useful, they fail in the ways above, and benchmark scores are a poor guide to your own warehouse. The live arguments are how long a task agents can do reliably, and how to verify work cheaply.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check. The first two are the ones that most often reach a committee.

    • A number with no source

      The report quotes a statistic the log never printed, or one that differs slightly from the log.

      Spot itSearch the log for the figure. No match means treat it as invented. Ask for the code cell that produced it.
    • Silent row loss

      "Cleaned the data" hides a filter or join that removes a third of customers, usually the awkward ones.

      Spot itAsk for the row count after every step and compare with the population the question is about.
    • Too-good model performance

      AUC 0.97 on churn or default.

      Spot itLook at the top features by importance. Any that is recorded after the outcome is leakage. Test with a forward-in-time split.
    • The best of many slices presented as the finding

      "Segment X is the problem" from a search nobody counted.

      Spot itAsk how many segments or models were tried. Apply 0.05 ÷ k, or confirm on fresh data.
    • Causal claims from correlations

      An AED benefit figure with no experiment behind it.

      Spot itFind the sentence with "will", "would reduce" or an AED saving. Ask what randomised or quasi-experimental evidence supports it.
    • SQL that runs and answers a different question

      Wrong date column, reversals included, currency not converted, a status code misread.

      Spot itRead the generated SQL once. Check the answer against a known figure (last month's finance total). Ask the tool to restate its interpretation in words.
    • Benchmark score taken as readiness

      "It scores 70% on BIRD, so it will handle our warehouse."

      Spot itAsk what the score was on questions from our schema. Build 50 real questions with known answers and measure.
    • Evaluator gaming

      An optimisation or automated-research loop reports a big win that evaporates under a different test.

      Spot itRe-score winners on a held-out evaluator the loop never saw, and read the winning candidate rather than only its score.
    • Over-trusting the agent's explanation

      The agent says why it did something, and the explanation is confident and wrong.

      Spot itTrust the log, not the narration. See the faithfulness lesson linked in Part 3.
    • Prompt injection through data

      A text field in a table or document tells the agent to do something else, and it complies.

      Spot itRestrict tools and permissions so injected text cannot do harm, and log every action. Do not rely on the model to detect it.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Agents can now produce in minutes what took an analyst days, and that is a real gain. The risk is that a wrong analysis looks identical to a right one. So we use them with controls: governed definitions for the key metrics, read-only access, a log for every number, and an independent re-check before anything goes to a decision. We spend the time we save on checking.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I treat an agent's report as a set of claims and audit it against the run log: row counts through every filter, a source line for every figure, temporal leakage checks on the features, the number of hypotheses tried with a correction for it, and independent recomputation of the headline result. For natural-language querying I prefer a semantic layer over raw text-to-SQL, because benchmark gaps between BIRD and Spider 2.0 show the failure is ambiguity in enterprise schemas, not SQL syntax.”
    “Can we let the business ask questions of the warehouse in plain language?”

    Yes, for governed metrics. I would put a semantic layer in front so the model picks from defined metrics and dimensions rather than writing SQL against raw tables, show the interpretation back in words, keep access read-only, and measure accuracy on fifty real questions with known answers before launch. Free-form SQL on the raw warehouse I would keep to analysts who can read the query.

    “The model scores 70% on a text-to-SQL benchmark. Is that good enough?”

    It tells us little about our schema. Benchmarks with small, clean databases are close to saturated, enterprise-scale ones score much lower, and the reference labels themselves contain errors. The only number that matters is accuracy on our own questions, with our definitions, and the cost of a wrong answer for each use.

    “How would you check an analysis an agent produced?”

    Start with the log, not the summary. Row counts at each step, a source for each figure, whether features could be known at prediction time, how many things were tried, and whether any causal language is supported by an experiment. Then re-run the central number independently. Most problems show up in the first twenty minutes.

    “Why not just have a second AI review the first one's work?”

    It helps, but only if the reviewer checks against evidence. A second model that reads the first one's story tends to agree with it. Better: give it the data and the question but not the conclusion, and compare answers, or use scripts that recompute the numbers. Recent benchmarks that recompute reported numbers are built around exactly this point.

    “Will AI scientists replace our data scientists?”

    Not on current evidence. Automated research works where there is a cheap and trustworthy score, such as optimisation problems, and works much less well on open-ended questions where the score is a proxy. Even the groups building these systems report that their generated papers do not reach main-conference standard. What changes is the mix of work: less typing, more specification, verification and judgement.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. An agent tested 40 customer segments and reports the one with p = 0.01 as a key finding. Is that significant after correction, and roughly how likely was some p < 0.05 anyway?

    Bonferroni cut-off = 0.05 ÷ 40 = 0.00125, so p = 0.01 does not pass. Chance of at least one p < 0.05 with no real effect: 1 − 0.9540 ≈ 87%. Treat it as a hypothesis to confirm on fresh data.

    2. The report says missing values were handled by dropping 2% of rows. The log shows 48,310 rows before and 33,330 after. What happened and why does it matter?

    14,980 rows were lost, about 31%, not 2%. The report misdescribes the data handling. If missingness is related to churn (it often is, for example inactive customers with missing fields), the analysis is now on a biased subset and the headline churn rate is likely wrong.

    3. Why can a text-to-SQL query that executes successfully still be wrong?

    Execution only means the SQL is valid. The question is under-specified and the schema does not state business definitions, so the model may pick the wrong table, date column, status filter or currency treatment. The query returns a number; nothing flags the mismatch. Simulation 3 showed 16 valid queries giving different answers to one question.

    4. A question depends on five ambiguous choices and the model gets each right 90% of the time, independently. What is the chance the answer is right? What does a semantic layer change?

    0.95 ≈ 59%. A semantic layer fixes the definitions centrally, so the model no longer has to guess most of those choices; the remaining uncertainty is picking the right metric, a much smaller space. The independence assumption is a simplification.

    5. Why do AlphaEvolve-style systems work well on matrix multiplication counts but an automated paper-writer struggles with "is this paper good"?

    The first has an exact, cheap, trustworthy evaluator: you can verify a multiplication scheme. The second has only a proxy (another model's review), and a search loop will optimise the proxy. The quality of an automated discovery system is capped by the quality of its evaluator.

    6. The agent's report says "calling at-risk customers will cut churn by 4 points, worth AED 6.2 million". What is the first question?

    What evidence supports the causal effect? A model that predicts who churns says nothing about whether a call changes the outcome. People the bank chooses to call differ from those it does not. It needs a randomised trial or a credible causal design (R8); until then it is a hypothesis, and the AED figure is a scenario, not a result.

    7. You cannot read all of an agent's code. What are the three cheapest checks that catch most problems?

    (1) Row counts at every step against the intended population. (2) Every number in the report matched to a line in the log. (3) The top features checked for anything recorded after the outcome, plus a forward-in-time test. Add a count of hypotheses tried if the report contains a "finding".
