    Finding the Right Passage: Keyword Search (BM25), Dense Retrieval and Choosing an Embedding Model

    How a search engine scores words, why that old formula still beats fancy models on codes and names, how embeddings search by meaning, and how to pick an embedding model for English and Arabic bank text without trusting a leaderboard.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBM25 finds documents that share the query's rare words, dense retrieval finds documents that mean the same thing in different words, each fails exactly where the other works, and the only honest way to choose between them, or between embedding models, is to measure recall on your own questions.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    An internal copilot for relationship managers has 40,000 policy pages, product sheets and circulars behind it. A user asks a question. Before the language model can answer anything, something has to pick the handful of passages worth showing it. That step is retrieval, and in a retrieval-augmented system (M2.3) the answer can be no better than what retrieval finds. If the right passage is not in the top five, no prompt will rescue it.

    There are two basic ways to find passages.

    • Lexical (keyword) search. Look for the query's words in the documents. Rare words count for more than common ones. This is how search engines worked for decades, and the standard scoring recipe is BM25.
    • Dense (semantic) search. Turn the query and every passage into a vector with an embedding model, so that texts with similar meaning land close together (F1.1). Return the nearest passages. "I can't get into my app" lands near "reset your online banking password" although they share no word.

    Each has a blind spot, and they are opposite blind spots. Lexical search cannot see meaning: "my money was taken by a thief" shares no word with "report an unauthorised transaction". Dense search cannot see exact strings: the SWIFT reference TXN4471 and TXN9920 look almost the same to an embedding model, but to a settlements clerk they are different payments.

    Hold onto thisRetrieval has two jobs: match the words (names, codes, product terms, amounts) and match the meaning (paraphrase, complaint language, cross-language). BM25 does the first cheaply and predictably. Embeddings do the second. Production systems usually need both, which is the next lesson (hybrid search).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M3.1 Keyword search and BM25

    The index. A lexical engine (Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr, or the full-text feature of PostgreSQL) builds an inverted index ahead of time: for every term, a list of the documents that contain it and how many times. At query time it only touches the lists for the query's terms, so it is fast on millions of documents without any GPU. Before indexing, text goes through an analyser: lower-casing, splitting into tokens, perhaps removing stop words and stemming ("transfers" and "transfer" become one term). The analyser is where most real-world lexical failures hide (see Arabic below).

    The score. BM25 sums, over the query's terms, three ideas multiplied together.

    1. Rarity (IDF). A term found in almost every document tells you nothing. "Account" in a bank corpus is worth little; "murabaha" or "MT103" is worth a lot. IDF is roughly the log of how many documents there are per document containing the term.
    2. Term frequency with saturation. More occurrences of a term suggest relevance, but with diminishing returns. The tenth "card" adds far less than the second. Raw counts would let a document stuffed with a word win; saturation caps the benefit.
    3. Length normalisation. A long document contains more words by chance, so a match inside a 3,000-word manual is weaker evidence than the same match in a 20-word FAQ entry. Scores are reduced for documents longer than average.
    score(q,d)=∑t∈qIDF(t)·f·(k1+1)f+k1(1−b+b·|d|avgdl)

    f is how many times the term appears in the document, |d| the document's length in tokens, avgdl the average length over the collection. IDF(t) = ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5) ÷ (n + 0.5)), with N documents in total and n containing the term (the form used by Lucene). The two knobs: k1 (typically 1.2) sets how fast term frequency saturates, and b (typically 0.75) sets how strongly length is penalised. Whatever f is, the frequency part can never exceed k1 + 1.

    Two details to notice. First, stop words and the IDF interact: even without removing "the" and "of", their near-zero IDF would make them irrelevant. Second, with no stemming, "stolen" does not match "stole". Real engines add stemmers or lemmatisers, which fix some misses and create others ("organisation" and "organ").

    Where lexical search is strong. Exact strings: account and reference numbers, SWIFT message types, product codes, customer and company names, regulation article numbers ("Article 12"), error codes. Rare terms get high IDF automatically. It needs no training, no GPU, is explainable (you can say which words matched), and a failure is easy to debug. In the BEIR benchmark of 18 retrieval datasets (Thakur et al., 2021), BM25 was a surprisingly hard baseline to beat when models were tested on data they were not trained on.

    Where it is weak. Vocabulary mismatch ("thief" versus "unauthorised transaction"), different languages, spelling variants, and questions phrased as sentences rather than keywords. Also boilerplate: if every product sheet contains the same disclaimer, queries about it match everything equally.

    Arabic and mixed-language bank text

    Lexical search depends on the analyser, and Arabic is hard for it. One word can carry a prefix ("ال" the, "و" and, "ب" with) and suffixes, so "والتحويلات" (and the transfers) contains the stem of "تحويل" (transfer) but is a different token. Letter variants (أ إ آ ا), the final ى versus ي, ة versus ه, and optional diacritics mean that two spellings of the same word fail to match. Good practice: normalise letters and strip diacritics and tatweel (the elongation character), apply a light Arabic stemmer or a morphological analyser, and test on real queries. Customer text also mixes Arabic, English and Arabizi (Arabic written in Latin letters, "tahweel"), and bank names and product names appear in both scripts. Check what your engine's Arabic analyser really does; treat any claim of "supports Arabic" as a hypothesis to test.

    # The core of BM25 in a few lines (illustrative; use a library in practice)
import math
from collections import Counter

def bm25(query_terms, doc_terms, df, N, avgdl, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    tf = Counter(doc_terms)
    score = 0.0
    for t in query_terms:
        if t not in tf: continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + 0.5) / (df[t] + 0.5))
        norm = tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(doc_terms) / avgdl))
        score += idf * norm
    return score

    One more variant you will meet: BM25F scores several fields (title, headings, body) with different weights, so a match in a product's title counts for more than one buried in a footnote. Cheap and effective for policy libraries.

    M3.2 Dense retrieval: searching by meaning

    A bi-encoder is a neural network (a transformer, F5.5) that reads a text and outputs one vector, typically 384 to 3,072 numbers. The same network, or a twin, encodes the query. The score is the cosine similarity between the two vectors (F1.1): the angle between them, ignoring length. Because documents are encoded once, offline, only the query needs the model at search time.

    How the model learns "close means similar". Not from word counts. It is trained contrastively (F5.7) on huge numbers of pairs (a question and a passage that answers it, a title and its article, a sentence and its translation). The loss pulls each matching pair together and pushes it away from the other passages in the same batch. After training, "I can't log in" and "reset your password" are neighbours because they often appeared as a pair in training, not because they share words. This also means the model knows only the notions of similarity it was shown. Embeddings trained mostly on web text may not know that "CDD", "KYC refresh" and "periodic review" are the same task in your bank.

    Searching fast. With a million vectors you cannot compare the query to every one in latency-sensitive systems, so vector databases use approximate nearest neighbour indexes, most often HNSW, a layered graph you walk towards the query. They return almost, not exactly, the true nearest neighbours; the setting that trades speed for recall is worth knowing exists. Many ordinary databases (PostgreSQL with pgvector, OpenSearch, Elasticsearch) now include vector search, which often removes the need for a separate product.

    Practicalities that bite.

    • Query and document are not symmetric. Many models expect a prefix such as "query: " or an instruction; skipping it silently lowers quality. Read the model card.
    • Context length. Each model has a maximum input (512 tokens for older models, 8,192 or more for newer ones). Anything beyond is cut off. This is one reason chunking matters (M3.3).
    • One vector per passage averages the meaning. A paragraph about two different topics gets a vector in between, which matches neither well. Late-interaction models such as ColBERT keep one vector per token and are more precise at higher storage cost; learned sparse models (SPLADE) give weighted keywords that include related terms.
    • Similar is not the same as relevant. The nearest passage to "what is the daily ATM limit for a Platinum card" may be the one about Gold cards. Embeddings are weak at negation, numbers and fine distinctions ("Do not" versus "Do").

    Choosing an embedding model

    There are hundreds of models and a public leaderboard (MTEB, Muennighoff et al., 2022) that ranks them across dozens of tasks. Use the leaderboard to build a shortlist, never to make the decision. The reasons: the average mixes tasks you do not care about; the test sets are public and models can be tuned toward them; your language, vocabulary and document types are different; and vendor-reported numbers are not independently verified.

    The decision is made on your own evaluation set. Build 100 to 300 realistic questions, each with the passage(s) that answer it, written or checked by someone who knows the business (ten good questions from the compliance desk beat a thousand synthetic ones). Mix English, Arabic and mixed queries, exact-code lookups, and paraphrased complaint-style questions. Then measure, for each candidate and each setting:

    • Recall@k: in what share of questions does a correct passage appear in the top k (say 5 or 20)? This is the number that matters for RAG, because the generator only sees the top few.
    • MRR or nDCG@10 (F7.6): how high up is the first correct passage, and is the ranking good overall?
    • Report intervals. With 150 questions, a 4-point difference in recall@5 is within noise (F2.1).
    # Minimal retrieval evaluation: recall@k and MRR
def evaluate(retrieve, questions, k=5):
    hits, rr = 0, 0.0
    for q in questions:                       # q = {"text": ..., "gold": {doc_id, ...}}
        ranked = retrieve(q["text"], top=20)  # list of doc ids, best first
        if q["gold"] & set(ranked[:k]): hits += 1
        for i, d in enumerate(ranked, 1):
            if d in q["gold"]: rr += 1 / i; break
    n = len(questions)
    return {"recall@%d" % k: hits / n, "mrr": rr / n}

    Lock-in to remember. Vectors from different models are not comparable. Changing the model means re-embedding everything and rebuilding the index. Store the model name and version with each vector, and keep the source text so you can redo it.

    The cheapest accuracy gains usually do not come from the biggest model. In order of effort: fix chunking and metadata, add the lexical leg (hybrid), add a reranker over the top 50, then consider fine-tuning or a bigger embedding model. Each is measured on the same evaluation set.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Internal copilot for policies and procedures (B7.2). Staff ask in sentences (dense) but also by policy number, circular reference or product code (BM25).
    • Trade finance and document search (B7.1). Letter-of-credit numbers, HS codes, party names: lexical territory.
    • Complaint triage and call-centre knowledge. Customers describe problems in their own words, in Arabic, English or both: dense territory.
    • Name search in screening (B2.2). Closer to fuzzy string matching than to either method here; general-purpose embeddings are poor at transliterated names.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The settled view: dense retrieval is better than BM25 at meaning, BM25 is better at exact terms and out-of-domain robustness, and the best systems combine them and rerank. The live arguments are about multilingual quality, long inputs, and how much a domain-tuned small model beats a giant general one.

      BM25

      Developed in the Okapi system at TREC in the 1990s, and written up in full by Robertson and Zaragoza. Still the default scoring in Lucene-based engines.

      Sentence-BERT

      Made transformer sentence embeddings practical for comparing texts by cosine, the template for modern bi-encoders.

    3. 2020
      Dense Passage Retrieval, and ColBERT

      DPR trained a two-tower model on question-passage pairs and beat BM25 on open-domain question answering by a clear margin on its in-domain data. ColBERT introduced late interaction: one vector per token, scored by best matches.

    4. 2021
      BEIR: BM25 is hard to beat out of domain

      Eighteen datasets, zero-shot. Dense models trained on one dataset often generalised worse than BM25; reranking and late-interaction models did best but cost more.

      Thakur et al., NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2021
    5. 2021–22
      Learned sparse and general-purpose embedding models; MTEB

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Picking the embedding model from the leaderboard

      "It is number one on MTEB, so we use it." Your Arabic, your jargon and your documents are not in the average.

      Spot itAsk for recall@5 on a bank-specific question set, in both languages, with an interval.
    • Dense-only search that loses exact codes

      A staff member pastes a circular number and gets three vaguely related circulars.

      Spot itInclude exact-identifier queries in the test set. If recall on them is below BM25's, add the lexical leg.
    • Keyword-only search that misses paraphrase

      Zero results for a complaint written in the customer's own words.

      Spot itLog queries with no or weak results and review them weekly.
    • Arabic analyser left on default

      Alef variants, the definite article and clitics break matching; mixed Arabic and English fields are tokenised as one language.

      Spot itRun the same query with and without the hamza and the prefix, and see if results differ.
    • Missing query or document prefix

      The model needs "query:" and "passage:" or an instruction, but the pipeline omits it. Quality drops quietly.

      Spot itCompare against the model card's usage example on a few known pairs.
    • Silent truncation

      Chunks longer than the model's input limit are cut; the end of every chunk is never embedded.

      Spot itCheck chunk token lengths against the model's maximum.
    • Wrong passage, high similarity

      The Gold-card limit is returned for a Platinum-card question, because the two read almost the same.

      Spot itAdd near-duplicate pairs to the test set; consider metadata filters (product, date, version) and a reranker.
    • Stale or mixed-version indexes

      Some vectors from the old model, some from the new; or superseded policies still indexed.

      Spot itStore model name, version and document effective date with each vector, and test that only current versions are returned.
    • Testing on questions made from the chunks

      Synthetic questions generated by an LLM from a chunk copy its wording, so every method looks excellent.

      Spot itInclude real user queries; compare synthetic-set scores with real-set scores.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our copilot is only as good as the passages it finds. Keyword search is excellent when staff quote a code or a name, and weak when they describe a problem in their own words. Meaning-based search is the reverse. We'll combine both and choose the meaning-based model by testing a few hundred real questions in Arabic and English, rather than by a public ranking. That test is cheap and it's what tells us whether the system will be trusted.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd build a labelled retrieval set from real queries, including identifier lookups, paraphrases and Arabic, and report recall@k and nDCG with bootstrap intervals for BM25, a few candidate embedders, and hybrid. I'd check the Arabic analyser for BM25, the prefixes and input limits for the embedder, and store model version with each vector so re-embedding is controlled. The shortlist comes from MTEB; the decision comes from our set.”
    “Why not just use embeddings for everything? Isn't BM25 obsolete?”

    No. It is cheap, explainable, needs no training, and wins on exact strings and on data unlike an embedding model's training set. On BEIR it was a hard baseline to beat zero-shot. Dense models win on paraphrase and cross-language. I would run both and measure.

    “Which embedding model should we use?”

    I don't know until we test. I'd shortlist three or four that support Arabic and English, fit our hosting constraints and input length, and run them on 150 or so real questions with known answers. The shortlist can come from a public leaderboard; the choice comes from recall@5 on our data.

    “Can we use a vendor's embedding API with customer data?”

    That is a data-governance question before a technical one: what leaves the bank, where it is processed, retention, and what our regulator and outsourcing rules require. I'd get the answer from legal and information security. For sensitive text, open weights hosted inside our environment avoid the question, at the cost of running them.

    “Search is missing an obvious document. Where do you look?”

    Work backwards: is the document in the index at all and parsed properly; was it chunked so that the answer is in one chunk; does BM25 find it with the right words; does dense find it; where does it land before reranking? That identifies whether it's a data, chunking, lexical or semantic failure.

    “What happens if we change the embedding model next year?”

    We re-embed the whole corpus and rebuild the index, because vectors from different models can't be compared. So I'd keep source text and chunking reproducible, tag vectors with model version, and keep the evaluation set so we can prove the new model is better before switching.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A corpus has 10,000 documents. "account" appears in 6,000 and "murabaha" in 20. Which gets the higher IDF, and roughly by how much?

    IDF = ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)). For "account": ln(1 + 4000.5/6000.5) = ln 1.667 ≈ 0.51. For "murabaha": ln(1 + 9980.5/20.5) = ln 487.8 ≈ 6.19. Murabaha is worth about twelve times as much.

    2. What does k1 = 0 do? What does b = 0 do?

    k1 = 0 makes the frequency part equal 1 whenever the term appears, so only presence matters. b = 0 removes the length penalty, so long documents are not penalised for being long.

    3. A term appears 50 times in a document and 5 times in another, same length. With k1 = 1.2 and an average-length document, which frequency part is larger and by how much?

    With length equal to average the denominator is f + 1.2. For f = 50: 50 × 2.2 / 51.2 ≈ 2.15. For f = 5: 5 × 2.2 / 6.2 ≈ 1.77. Ten times the occurrences give 21% more. The ceiling is 2.2.

    4. Why can two wire confirmations with different reference numbers look the same to an embedding model but not to BM25?

    The embedding squeezes a whole text into a vector that captures its overall topic; a short identifier changes it very little, and the tokeniser splits it into meaningless fragments. BM25 indexes the whole token and gives a rare one a very high IDF.

    5. A teammate says model A has a higher MTEB average than model B, so use A. Give three reasons that is not enough.

    The average covers tasks and languages unlike ours; public test sets can be targeted by developers and vendor numbers may be unverified; it ignores cost, hosting, input length, and licence; and nothing tells us how it behaves on Arabic banking text. Test on our own set.

    6. You have 50 million chunks. Compare the vector storage of 1,024-dimension float32 and 256-dimension int8.

    50M × 1,024 × 4 bytes = 204.8 GB. 50M × 256 × 1 byte = 12.8 GB, sixteen times smaller. The smaller form will lose some recall, which you measure; a reranker over the shortlist often recovers it.

    7. Recall@5 is 62% on your evaluation set of 100 questions. How sure are you?

    Standard error ≈ √(0.62 × 0.38 / 100) ≈ 4.9 points, so a 95% interval of roughly 52% to 72%. A rival model at 66% is not distinguishable. You need more questions or a paired comparison on the same questions.

    8. A customer's question is in Gulf Arabic and the policy is in Modern Standard Arabic. Which method is likely to struggle, and what would you test?

    BM25 will, since dialect words differ from the formal terms. A multilingual embedding may bridge it, but dialect coverage varies by model. I'd build a test set of real dialect queries against the MSA documents and compare recall@k for each candidate, with and without hybrid.
