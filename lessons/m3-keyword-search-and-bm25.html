    Finding the Right Passage: Keyword Search (BM25), Dense Retrieval and Choosing an Embedding Model

    How a search engine scores words, why that old formula still beats fancy models on codes and names, how embeddings search by meaning, and how to pick an embedding model for English and Arabic bank text without trusting a leaderboard.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBM25 finds documents that share the query's rare words, dense retrieval finds documents that mean the same thing in different words, each fails exactly where the other works, and the only honest way to choose between them, or between embedding models, is to measure recall on your own questions.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Retrieval picks the few passages worth showing the language model. In a RAG system (M2.3) the answer is no better than what retrieval finds. Think of a librarian fetching books before an expert answers.

    Two ways to fetch from a 40,000-page policy library:

    • Lexical (keyword) search. Find the query's words, with rare words counting more. Like Ctrl+F with a smart ranking. The standard recipe is BM25.
    • Dense (semantic) search. An embedding model turns query and passages into vectors so similar meanings sit close (F1.1). "I can't get into my app" lands near "reset your online banking password" with no shared word.

    The blind spots are opposite. Lexical cannot see meaning: "my money was taken by a thief" shares no word with "report an unauthorised transaction". Dense cannot see exact strings: TXN4471 and TXN9920 look almost identical to an embedding, yet are different payments.

    Hold onto thisBM25 matches words (names, codes, amounts); embeddings match meaning (paraphrase, other languages). Production usually needs both (hybrid search, next lesson).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M3.1 Keyword search and BM25

    The index. Like the index at the back of a textbook, an inverted index (Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, PostgreSQL full-text) lists, for every term, the documents containing it. Search is fast with no GPU. Before indexing, an analyser lower-cases, splits into tokens and may stem ("transfers" becomes "transfer"). Most real lexical failures hide there.

    The score. For each query word found in a document, BM25 combines three ideas, then adds up over the query words.

    1. Rarity (IDF). "Account" in a bank corpus is worth little; "murabaha" or "MT103" is worth a lot, like a rare surname narrowing a search far more than "Smith". It is rarity, not importance to the user.
    2. Term frequency with saturation. More occurrences help, with diminishing returns: the tenth "card" adds far less than the second. The knob is k1 (typically 1.2).
    3. Length normalisation. A match in a 3,000-word manual is weaker evidence than the same match in a 20-word FAQ entry. The knob is b (typically 0.75).

    Scores only rank documents for one query; they are not comparable across queries. Stop words ("the", "of") have near-zero IDF, so they barely matter.

    Strong: exact strings such as account and reference numbers, SWIFT message types, product codes, names, "Article 12". No training, no GPU, and explainable: you can say which words matched.

    Weak: vocabulary mismatch ("thief" versus "unauthorised transaction"), other languages and spelling variants.

    Arabic and mixed-language bank text

    Arabic words carry prefixes and suffixes: "والتحويلات" (and the transfers) is a different token from "تحويل" (transfer). Letter variants (أ إ آ ا) and optional diacritics make two spellings of one word fail to match. Fix: normalise letters, strip diacritics, add a light stemmer, and test on real queries. Treat "supports Arabic" as a hypothesis to test.

    M3.2 Dense retrieval: searching by meaning

    A bi-encoder is a transformer (F5.5) that reads a text and outputs one vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers. The query is encoded the same way. The score is cosine similarity (F1.1): it compares direction only, so 1 means pointing the same way, 0 means unrelated. Think of GPS coordinates for meaning: nearby points mean similar topics. The number only ranks results within one model; it is not a "70% match".

    How it learns. Contrastively (F5.7): the model sees huge numbers of matching pairs (question and answer, title and article) and learns to pull them together. "I can't log in" and "reset your password" become neighbours because they appeared as a pair, not because they share words. The catch: it knows only the similarity it was shown. It may not know that "CDD", "KYC refresh" and "periodic review" are one task at your bank.

    Searching fast. Vector databases use approximate nearest neighbour indexes (usually HNSW): fast search that trades a little recall for speed.

    Practicalities that bite:

    • Prefixes. Many models expect "query: " or similar. Skipping it silently lowers quality. Read the model card.
    • Context length. Input beyond the model's maximum is cut off, one reason chunking matters (M3.3).
    • Similar is not relevant. One vector averages meaning, and the nearest passage to "daily ATM limit for a Platinum card" may be the Gold card one. Embeddings are weak at negation, numbers and fine distinctions.

    Choosing an embedding model

    Use the public leaderboard (MTEB, Muennighoff et al., 2022) to build a shortlist, never to decide. It is like choosing a car by overall magazine score when you need one that tows: the average mixes tasks you do not care about, and your language and documents differ.

    Decide on your own evaluation set. Build 100 to 300 realistic questions, each with its answering passage, checked by someone who knows the business. Mix English, Arabic, exact-code lookups and paraphrased complaints. Then measure each candidate:

    • Recall@k: the share of questions where a correct passage is in the top k (5 or 20). Going from 62% to 70% at k=5 means 8 more questions in 100 give the generator what it needs.
    • MRR or nDCG@10 (F7.6): how high the first correct passage sits, and how good the ranking is overall.
    • Intervals. With 150 questions, a 4-point recall@5 gap is noise (F2.1).

    Practical criteria:

    CriterionWhat to checkWhy it matters at a bank
    LanguagesArabic (MSA and Gulf dialect), English, mixed.Many models are English-first. Test your own text.
    Where it runsVendor API, or open weights you host.External APIs raise data-residency questions (P4.2); open weights can run on-premises.
    Input length and dimension512 to 8,192+ tokens; 384 to 3,072 numbers (R10 Matryoshka).Caps chunk size; drives index size and memory (Simulation 4).

    Lock-in. Vectors from different models are not comparable, like prices in different currencies. A model change means re-embedding everything, so store the model version with each vector.

    The cheapest gains rarely come from the biggest model. In order: fix chunking and metadata, add the lexical leg (hybrid), add a reranker, then fine-tune or go bigger. Measure each on the same evaluation set.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Picking the model from the leaderboard

      "Number one on MTEB, so we use it." Your Arabic, jargon and documents are not in the average.

      Spot itAsk for recall@5 on a bank-specific, bilingual question set, with an interval.
    • Dense-only loses exact codes; keyword-only misses paraphrase

      A pasted circular number returns vaguely related circulars; or a complaint in the customer's own words returns nothing.

      Spot itTest identifier and paraphrase queries; log no-result queries. Add the other leg (hybrid).
    • Arabic analyser left on default

      Alef variants, the definite article and clitics break matching.

      Spot itRun one query with and without the hamza and the prefix; results should not change.
    • Missing prefix or silent truncation

      A missing "query:" prefix, or chunks longer than the input limit, quietly degrade results.

      Spot itCompare with the model card; check chunk lengths against the maximum.
    • Wrong passage, high similarity

      The Gold-card limit comes back for a Platinum-card question; they read almost the same.

      Spot itTest near-duplicate pairs; add metadata filters and a reranker.
    • Stale indexes or flattering test questions

      Mixed model versions or superseded policies still indexed; or LLM-generated test questions that copy the chunk's wording, so every method looks excellent.

      Spot itStore model version and date per vector; use real user queries.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. "Account" appears in 6,000 of 10,000 documents and "murabaha" in 20. Which gets the higher IDF, and what does that mean for a query containing both?

    Murabaha, by a large margin (roughly twelve times). A document containing "murabaha" outranks one that only repeats "account", because the rare word carries the signal.

    2. What does k1 = 0 do? What does b = 0 do?

    k1 = 0 counts presence only: a word appearing once or fifty times scores the same. b = 0 removes the length penalty, so long documents are not discounted.

    3. A term appears 50 times in one document and 5 times in another. Why does the first not score ten times higher?

    Saturation. Extra repeats add very little, which stops keyword-stuffed documents winning.

    4. Why can two wire confirmations with different reference numbers look the same to an embedding model but not to BM25?

    An embedding captures overall topic, so a short identifier barely moves it, and the tokeniser splits it into meaningless fragments. BM25 indexes the whole token and gives a rare one a very high IDF.

    5. A teammate says model A has a higher MTEB average than model B, so use A. Give reasons that is not enough.

    The average covers tasks and languages unlike ours, ignores cost, hosting and input length, and says nothing about Arabic banking text. Test on our own set.

    6. You have 50 million chunks. Why might you choose 256-dimension int8 vectors over 1,024-dimension float32, and what must you check?

    They are sixteen times smaller (about 13 GB against 205 GB), which can decide whether the index fits in memory. Some recall is lost, so measure it on your own set; a reranker often recovers it.

    7. Recall@5 is 62% on 100 evaluation questions. How sure are you?

    Not very: the true figure could plausibly be anywhere from about 52% to 72%. A rival at 66% is indistinguishable. Use more questions or a paired comparison on the same questions.

    8. A customer asks in Gulf Arabic and the policy is in Modern Standard Arabic. Which method struggles, and what would you test?

    BM25, since dialect words differ from formal terms. A multilingual embedding may bridge it, but dialect coverage varies. Test real dialect queries against MSA documents and compare recall@k per candidate, with and without hybrid.
