    Finding the Right Passage: Keyword Search (BM25), Dense Retrieval and Choosing an Embedding Model

    How a search engine scores words, why that old formula still beats fancy models on codes and names, how embeddings search by meaning, and how to pick an embedding model for English and Arabic bank text without trusting a leaderboard.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceBM25 finds documents that share the query's rare words, dense retrieval finds documents that mean the same thing in different words, each fails exactly where the other works, and the only honest way to choose between them, or between embedding models, is to measure recall on your own questions.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Retrieval picks the few passages worth showing the language model. In a RAG system (M2.3) the answer is no better than what retrieval finds: if the right passage is not in the top five, no prompt rescues it. Think of a librarian fetching books before an expert answers.

    Example: a copilot for relationship managers sits on 40,000 policy pages, product sheets and circulars. Two ways to fetch from it:

    • Lexical (keyword) search. Find the query's words in documents; rare words count more. Like Ctrl+F with a smart ranking. The standard recipe is BM25.
    • Dense (semantic) search. An embedding model turns query and passages into vectors so similar meanings sit close (F1.1). Like filing by topic rather than by title. "I can't get into my app" lands near "reset your online banking password" with no shared word.

    The blind spots are opposite. Lexical cannot see meaning: "my money was taken by a thief" shares no word with "report an unauthorised transaction". Dense cannot see exact strings: SWIFT references TXN4471 and TXN9920 look almost identical to an embedding, yet are different payments.

    Hold onto thisRetrieval has two jobs: match the words (names, codes, amounts) and match the meaning (paraphrase, complaint language, cross-language). BM25 does the first, embeddings the second. Production usually needs both (hybrid search, next lesson).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    M3.1 Keyword search and BM25

    The index. Like the index at the back of a textbook, an inverted index (Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr, PostgreSQL full-text) lists, for every term, the documents containing it and how often. Queries touch only the query terms' lists, so search is fast on millions of documents with no GPU. First, an analyser lower-cases, splits into tokens, and may drop stop words and stem ("transfers" becomes "transfer"). Most real lexical failures hide in the analyser (see Arabic below).

    The score. For each query term, BM25 multiplies three ideas, then sums over terms.

    1. Rarity (IDF). A word in nearly every document says nothing. "Account" in a bank corpus is worth little; "murabaha" or "MT103" is worth a lot, like a rare surname narrowing a search far more than "Smith". A higher IDF means a rarer term. Misreading to avoid: IDF is not importance to the user, only rarity in the corpus.
    2. Term frequency with saturation. More occurrences suggest relevance, with diminishing returns: the tenth "card" adds far less than the second. Saturation stops a keyword-stuffed document winning.
    3. Length normalisation. A match inside a 3,000-word manual is weaker evidence than the same match in a 20-word FAQ entry, so longer-than-average documents are discounted.
    score(q,d)=∑t∈qIDF(t)·f·(k1+1)f+k1(1−b+b·|d|avgdl)

    f = occurrences of the term in the document, |d| = document length in tokens, avgdl = average length. IDF(t) = ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5) ÷ (n + 0.5)), with N documents and n containing the term (Lucene's form). Two knobs: k1 (typically 1.2) sets how fast frequency saturates; b (typically 0.75) sets how hard length is penalised. The frequency part never exceeds k1 + 1. Scores only rank documents for one query; they are not comparable across queries.

    Two details. Stop words ("the", "of") have near-zero IDF, so they are irrelevant even if kept. Without stemming, "stolen" does not match "stole"; stemmers fix some misses and create others ("organisation" and "organ").

    Strong: exact strings such as account and reference numbers, SWIFT message types, product codes, names, "Article 12", error codes. Rare terms get high IDF automatically. No training, no GPU, and explainable: you can say which words matched. In BEIR, 18 retrieval datasets (Thakur et al., 2021), BM25 was a hard baseline to beat on data models were not trained on.

    Weak: vocabulary mismatch ("thief" versus "unauthorised transaction"), other languages, spelling variants, and sentence-style questions. Boilerplate also hurts: a disclaimer in every product sheet makes queries about it match everything equally.

    Arabic and mixed-language bank text

    Arabic is hard for analysers because words carry prefixes and suffixes: "والتحويلات" (and the transfers) contains the stem of "تحويل" (transfer) but is a different token. Letter variants (أ إ آ ا; ى versus ي; ة versus ه) and optional diacritics make two spellings of one word fail to match. Fix: normalise letters, strip diacritics and tatweel (the elongation character), add a light stemmer or morphological analyser, and test on real queries. Customers also mix Arabic, English and Arabizi (Arabic in Latin letters, "tahweel"). Treat "supports Arabic" as a hypothesis to test.

    # The core of BM25 in a few lines (illustrative; use a library in practice)
import math
from collections import Counter

def bm25(query_terms, doc_terms, df, N, avgdl, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    tf = Counter(doc_terms)
    score = 0.0
    for t in query_terms:
        if t not in tf: continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + 0.5) / (df[t] + 0.5))
        norm = tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(doc_terms) / avgdl))
        score += idf * norm
    return score

    BM25F weights fields differently (title, headings, body), so a title match beats a footnote match. Cheap and effective for policy libraries.

    M3.2 Dense retrieval: searching by meaning

    A bi-encoder is a transformer (F5.5) that reads a text and outputs one vector of 384 to 3,072 numbers. The query is encoded the same way. The score is cosine similarity (F1.1): the angle between the vectors, ignoring length. Think of GPS coordinates for meaning: nearby points mean similar topics. A cosine near 1 is very similar; near 0 is unrelated. The number is only meaningful for ranking within one model, not as a "70% match". Documents are encoded once, offline; only the query needs the model at search time.

    How it learns. Contrastively (F5.7), on huge numbers of pairs (question and answering passage, title and article, sentence and translation). The loss pulls matching pairs together and pushes them away from other passages in the batch. "I can't log in" and "reset your password" become neighbours because they appeared as a pair, not because they share words. The catch: the model knows only the similarity it was shown. Web-trained embeddings may not know that "CDD", "KYC refresh" and "periodic review" are one task at your bank.

    Searching fast. Comparing a query to millions of vectors is slow, so vector databases use approximate nearest neighbour indexes, usually HNSW (a layered graph you walk towards the query). Results are almost, not exactly, the true nearest; a setting trades speed for recall. PostgreSQL (pgvector), OpenSearch and Elasticsearch now include vector search, often removing the need for a separate product.

    Practicalities that bite.

    • Query and document differ. Many models expect a prefix such as "query: " or an instruction; skipping it silently lowers quality. Read the model card.
    • Context length. Maximum input is 512 tokens for older models, 8,192 or more for newer. The rest is cut off, one reason chunking matters (M3.3).
    • One vector averages meaning. A paragraph on two topics gets a vector between them, matching neither well. ColBERT-style late interaction keeps one vector per token (more precise, more storage); SPLADE gives weighted keywords including related terms.
    • Similar is not relevant. The nearest passage to "daily ATM limit for a Platinum card" may be the Gold card one. Embeddings are weak at negation, numbers and fine distinctions ("Do not" versus "Do").

    Choosing an embedding model

    Use the public leaderboard (MTEB, Muennighoff et al., 2022) to build a shortlist, never to decide. It is like choosing a car by overall magazine score when you need one that tows. The average mixes tasks you do not care about, test sets are public and can be tuned toward, your language and documents differ, and vendor numbers are unverified.

    Decide on your own evaluation set. Build 100 to 300 realistic questions, each with its answering passage(s), written or checked by someone who knows the business (ten good compliance-desk questions beat a thousand synthetic ones). Mix English, Arabic, mixed queries, exact-code lookups and paraphrased complaints. Then measure each candidate:

    • Recall@k: the share of questions where a correct passage is in the top k (5 or 20). Going from 62% to 70% at k=5 means 8 more questions in 100 give the generator what it needs. It is the key RAG number because the generator sees only the top few. It says nothing about order within the top k.
    • MRR or nDCG@10 (F7.6): how high the first correct passage sits (MRR 0.5 means about rank 2 on average) and how good the ranking is overall.
    • Intervals. With 150 questions, a 4-point recall@5 gap is noise (F2.1).
    # Minimal retrieval evaluation: recall@k and MRR
def evaluate(retrieve, questions, k=5):
    hits, rr = 0, 0.0
    for q in questions:                       # q = {"text": ..., "gold": {doc_id, ...}}
        ranked = retrieve(q["text"], top=20)  # list of doc ids, best first
        if q["gold"] & set(ranked[:k]): hits += 1
        for i, d in enumerate(ranked, 1):
            if d in q["gold"]: rr += 1 / i; break
    n = len(questions)
    return {"recall@%d" % k: hits / n, "mrr": rr / n}

    Lock-in. Vectors from different models are not comparable, like prices in different currencies. A model change means re-embedding everything and rebuilding the index. Store model name and version with each vector, and keep the source text.

    The cheapest gains rarely come from the biggest model. In order: fix chunking and metadata, add the lexical leg (hybrid), add a reranker over the top 50, then fine-tune or go bigger. Measure each on the same evaluation set.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Picking the model from the leaderboard

      "Number one on MTEB, so we use it." Your Arabic, jargon and documents are not in the average.

      Spot itAsk for recall@5 on a bank-specific, bilingual question set, with an interval.
    • Dense-only search loses exact codes

      A staff member pastes a circular number and gets three vaguely related circulars.

      Spot itPut identifier queries in the test set. If recall is below BM25's, add the lexical leg.
    • Keyword-only search misses paraphrase

      Zero results for a complaint in the customer's own words.

      Spot itLog no-result and weak-result queries; review weekly.
    • Arabic analyser left on default

      Alef variants, the definite article and clitics break matching; mixed Arabic and English fields are tokenised as one language.

      Spot itRun one query with and without the hamza and the prefix; results should not change.
    • Missing query or document prefix

      The model wants "query:" and "passage:" or an instruction; the pipeline omits it and quality drops quietly.

      Spot itCompare with the model card's example on a few known pairs.
    • Silent truncation

      Chunks beyond the input limit are cut; their ends are never embedded.

      Spot itCheck chunk token lengths against the model maximum.
    • Wrong passage, high similarity

      The Gold-card limit comes back for a Platinum-card question; they read almost the same.

      Spot itAdd near-duplicate pairs to the test set; use metadata filters (product, date, version) and a reranker.
    • Stale or mixed-version indexes

      Vectors from old and new models mixed, or superseded policies still indexed.

      Spot itStore model name, version and effective date per vector; test that only current versions return.
    • Testing on questions made from the chunks

      LLM-generated questions copy the chunk's wording, so every method looks excellent.

      Spot itUse real user queries; compare synthetic and real scores.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A corpus has 10,000 documents. "account" appears in 6,000 and "murabaha" in 20. Which gets the higher IDF, and roughly by how much?

    "Account": ln(1 + 4000.5/6000.5) ≈ 0.51. "Murabaha": ln(1 + 9980.5/20.5) ≈ 6.19. Murabaha is worth about twelve times as much.

    2. What does k1 = 0 do? What does b = 0 do?

    k1 = 0 makes the frequency part 1 whenever the term appears: presence only. b = 0 removes the length penalty.

    3. A term appears 50 times in one document and 5 times in another, same average length. With k1 = 1.2, which frequency part is larger and by how much?

    f = 50: 50 × 2.2 / 51.2 ≈ 2.15. f = 5: 5 × 2.2 / 6.2 ≈ 1.77. Ten times the occurrences give 21% more; the ceiling is 2.2.

    4. Why can two wire confirmations with different reference numbers look the same to an embedding model but not to BM25?

    An embedding captures overall topic, so a short identifier barely moves it, and the tokeniser splits it into meaningless fragments. BM25 indexes the whole token and gives a rare one a very high IDF.

    5. A teammate says model A has a higher MTEB average than model B, so use A. Give reasons that is not enough.

    The average covers tasks and languages unlike ours; public test sets can be targeted and vendor numbers may be unverified; it ignores cost, hosting, input length and licence; and it says nothing about Arabic banking text. Test on our own set.

    6. You have 50 million chunks. Compare the vector storage of 1,024-dimension float32 and 256-dimension int8.

    50M × 1,024 × 4 bytes = 204.8 GB. 50M × 256 × 1 byte = 12.8 GB, sixteen times smaller. Some recall is lost, which you measure; a reranker often recovers it.

    7. Recall@5 is 62% on 100 evaluation questions. How sure are you?

    Standard error ≈ √(0.62 × 0.38 / 100) ≈ 4.9 points, so a 95% interval of roughly 52% to 72%. A rival at 66% is indistinguishable. Use more questions or a paired comparison on the same questions.

    8. A customer asks in Gulf Arabic and the policy is in Modern Standard Arabic. Which method struggles, and what would you test?

    BM25, since dialect words differ from formal terms. A multilingual embedding may bridge it, but dialect coverage varies. Test real dialect queries against MSA documents and compare recall@k per candidate, with and without hybrid.
