    Keyword and Semantic Search

    Part 1

    What search does in a RAG system

    In one sentenceRetrieval is the step that fetches the few passages worth showing a language model, and there are two ways to do it: keyword search matches the words you typed, and semantic search matches what you meant.

    Our running example is a bank help-centre with a few thousand short passages, such as "Reset your online banking password", "Report a lost or stolen card" and "Report an unauthorised transaction". A customer types a question, and we want the right passage back.

    How the two differ

    • Keyword search looks for the query's words in each passage. Think of Ctrl+F with a smart ranking. The standard recipe is called BM25.
    • Semantic search (also called dense retrieval) turns the query and every passage into a vector, a list of numbers, so that similar meanings sit close together. "I can't get into my app" lands near "Reset your online banking password" although they share no word.

    Their blind spots are opposite. Keyword search cannot see meaning. Semantic search cannot see exact strings: two payment references like TXN4471 and TXN9920 look almost identical to it.

    Part 2

    Keyword search (BM25)

    How it works

    1. Prepare the text. Lower-case it, split it into words and sometimes trim endings ("transfers" becomes "transfer"). Do this identically for passages and queries.
    2. Build an index. Like the index at the back of a textbook, it lists for each word the passages that contain it. Lookup is fast and needs no GPU.
    3. Score each passage for every query word it contains, using the three ideas below.
    4. Add up and rank. The highest total comes first.
    • Rarity (called IDF) is how few passages contain the word. In a bank, "account" appears everywhere and is worth little. "MT103" (a type of wire message) appears rarely and is worth a lot. Words like "the" are worth almost nothing.
    • Repeats (term frequency) help with diminishing returns, which stops a passage winning by repeating "card" twenty times. A setting called k1, usually about 1.2, controls how quickly gains flatten.
    • Length matters because a match in a 20-word answer is stronger evidence than the same match in a 3,000-word manual. A setting called b, usually about 0.75, controls the discount.

    How to read the score

    • It only ranks. A BM25 score of 12 means little alone. It tells you the order for this one query.
    • Do not compare across queries. A long query with rare words scores higher than a short one, whatever the quality.
    • Zero means no query word matched. That is the "my money was taken by a thief" case: no results.
    • Check the text preparation first when a match you expect fails. Most keyword failures hide there. In Arabic, for instance, "والتحويلات" (and the transfers) is a different token from "تحويل" (transfer) unless you strip prefixes and normalise letter variants.
    Part 3

    Semantic search

    How it works

    1. Choose an embedding model. It reads a text and outputs one vector, typically a few hundred to a few thousand numbers.
    2. Embed every passage once, ahead of time, and store the vectors.
    3. Embed the query with the same model when the customer asks.
    4. Find the nearest vectors by cosine similarity, which compares the direction of two arrows.

    The model learned its sense of "close" by seeing huge numbers of matching pairs, such as a question with its answer, and pulling them together. So "I can't log in" and "reset your password" became neighbours because they appeared as a pair, not because they share words.

    How to read the score

    • Cosine runs from −1 to 1. Near 1 is the same direction, near 0 is unrelated.
    • It is not a percentage. 0.7 does not mean "70% match", and what counts as "close" differs between models. Use it to rank, not as an absolute pass mark.
    • Similar is not the same as relevant. The nearest passage to "daily ATM limit for a Platinum card" may be the Gold card one, because they read almost the same. Embeddings are weak at negation, numbers and fine distinctions.

    Practical points

    • Use the same model throughout. Vectors from different models are not comparable, like prices in different currencies. Changing the model means re-embedding everything.
    • Check the model card. Many models expect a prefix such as "query: ". Skipping it quietly lowers quality. Text beyond the model's input limit is cut off.
    • Test on your own questions. Public leaderboards such as MTEB help to build a shortlist, but they average tasks and languages you may not care about. Build 100 to 300 realistic questions with known answer passages, then measure how often the right passage appears in the top 5 (called recall@5). Many models are English-first, so test your Arabic and mixed-language text.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    Keyword (BM25)Semantic (embeddings)
    MatchesWordsMeaning
    Needs trainingNoUses a pre-trained model
    Needs a GPUNoYes, to embed (once per passage)
    ExplainableYes, you can show the matched wordsHard
    Paraphrase and other languagesPoorGood
    Codes, names, amountsGoodPoor
    • Keyword is strong at exact strings: account numbers, product codes, SWIFT message types, "Article 12". It is weak at vocabulary mismatch ("thief" against "unauthorised transaction").
    • Semantic is strong at paraphrase and multilingual questions. It is weak at identifiers and near-duplicates.
    • In practice production systems usually run both and merge the results. That is called hybrid search, and it is the usual next step.
    Show the BM25 idea as a formula

    For each query word, score = rarity × repeat term. The repeat term is roughly count × (k1 + 1) ÷ (count + k1 × length penalty). Because count sits on both top and bottom, it flattens towards a ceiling of k1 + 1 instead of growing without limit. The length penalty is larger for long passages, scaled by b.

    In banking, a policy search needs both legs: staff paste circular numbers and product codes, while customers describe problems in their own words.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Picking the model from a leaderboard

      "Number one on MTEB" says nothing about your Arabic text and your documents. Test on a question set of your own.

    • Dense-only or keyword-only

      Dense-only loses pasted reference numbers. Keyword-only returns nothing for a complaint in the customer's own words. Test both kinds of query.

    • Default text preparation for Arabic

      Letter variants, the definite article and attached prefixes break matching. Search once with and without the prefix; results should not change.

    • Missing prefix or silent truncation

      A skipped "query:" prefix, or passages longer than the model's limit, quietly degrade results. Compare with the model card.

    1. "Account" appears in 6,000 of 10,000 passages and "murabaha" in 20. Which counts for more in a query with both?

    "Murabaha", by a wide margin. The rare word carries the signal, so a passage containing it outranks one that merely repeats "account".

    2. A word appears 50 times in one passage and 5 in another. Why is the first not ten times better?

    Repeats saturate: extra occurrences add very little. This stops keyword-stuffed passages winning.

    3. Why can two wire confirmations with different reference numbers look the same to embeddings but not to BM25?

    An embedding captures overall topic, so a short code barely moves it. BM25 indexes the whole code as one rare word with a high score.

    4. A cosine of 0.72 came back for a query. Is that a good match?

    You cannot tell alone. Compare it with other passages for the same query and the same model; it is a rank, not a percentage.

    5. You switch embedding model. What must you do to the stored vectors?

    Re-embed everything, because vectors from different models are not comparable. Store the model version with each vector.
