    The Model Lifecycle, and Knowing Exactly Which Model Is Running

    The stages a bank model passes through from idea to retirement, why decay and review cadence are an engineering question, how experiment tracking stops you fooling yourself, and what a registry must be able to answer when an auditor asks.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is not a file you ship once but a controlled asset with a life: it has stages, owners and gates, it decays, and at every moment the bank must be able to say which version is running, what data and code built it, who approved it, and how to go back.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist builds a card-fraud model in a notebook. It scores well, and someone copies its output into a scoring service. Nine months later alerts are worse, and a regulator asks: which model is this, who approved it, what data trained it, when was it last checked? Nobody can say. The notebook was edited, the training table was overwritten, the approver moved teams, and fifty experiments ran with no record of which became production. The model may be fine. The bank cannot show it.

    The lifecycle is the fix. Treat a model like an aircraft part, not a document: design, tests, certificate, serial number, scheduled inspections, retirement date. None of this adds accuracy. It makes the model trustworthy, reproducible and reversible.

    • Models decay, so the work is never finished. Fraud tactics, customers and the economy move, and a model drifts from right to wrong silently. The lifecycle builds in a monitoring and review loop (Simulation 2 shows what the interval costs).
    • Identity is the foundation. You cannot audit, roll back or retire "the model" unless it is a precise versioned thing: this code, this data snapshot, these parameters, this environment, this approval. The tracker records how it was made; the registry records what is live and why.
    Hold onto thisEvery question about a production model is one of four: what is it exactly, how was it made, who allowed it, how do we undo it. Stages, trackers and registries answer those.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The model lifecycle, stage by stage

    Nine stages appear in model-risk policies worldwide. US SR 11-7 (2011) set the pattern: development, validation, governance, ongoing monitoring. The CBUAE Model Management Standards (2022) cover a similar span, from development through use, monitoring, independent validation and changes such as recalibration. Saudi banks follow SAMA's expectations. Read your bank's current text, not a summary, including this one.

    Step through the stages: what happens, what artefact it leaves, who signs, and what breaks if it is skipped.

    • It is a loop. Monitoring feeds change, and change re-enters development and validation. A recalibration is a new version needing its own approval, scaled to how material the change is.
    • Tiering sets effort. A fraud model that can block thousands of customers, or an IFRS 9 model that moves provisions by millions of dirhams, gets full independent validation and frequent review. A model that routes internal emails does not.
    • Independence is structural. Builders cannot validate their own model, like marking your own exam. The business owner stays accountable for use. G3 covers this; here the point is that the lifecycle must leave evidence a third party can use.

    Decay and review cadence

    Models go stale like milk, not like a light bulb: quality slides slowly with noise (new fraud tactics, a changed customer mix, a rate change), or drops suddenly when an upstream feed breaks. F7.7 covers detecting drift. The lifecycle question is: how often do you look, and how stale is the answer?

    Say quality falls steadily and the bank has agreed a floor below which the model is unfit. With a review every c months, a breach at month m* is found only at the next review, and only once labels have matured (F3.5, B1.2):

    detected at=⌈m*+δc⌉·c

    m*: month true quality crosses the floor. δ: label delay in months. c: review interval. Brackets mean "round up to the next review". On average you wait about half an interval plus the label delay; at worst a full interval. Each month below the floor is harm: missed fraud, wrong provisions, mispriced loans.

    Two points. First, review cadence is a risk decision with a price you can estimate. A quarterly review of a high-tier model is the interval at which you accept being wrong. Second, automation shrinks the interval term, not the label delay. A weekly dashboard cannot see true performance if fraud labels take eight weeks. So input-drift monitors (PSI, KS, F7.7) and proxy metrics run alongside: earlier warning, weaker evidence, confirmed later by labelled metrics.

    Experiment tracking: counting what you tried

    Development is hundreds of runs, not one model. An experiment tracker (MLflow, Weights & Biases, or a bank-built tool) records for each run:

    • Code version: git commit, and whether the working tree was clean.
    • Data version: snapshot ID or content hash of the exact training table, plus the point-in-time cut-off (F3.1).
    • Parameters: hyperparameters, random seed, feature list.
    • Environment: library versions, hardware, container image.
    • Metrics and artefacts: validation scores, plots, the fitted model file.

    It is a lab notebook that cannot be edited afterwards. Its first use is reproduction (F10.4). Its second is honesty about selection. Roll a die 100 times and report only the highest roll: it looks lucky because you chose it. Likewise, the best of N comparable configurations is biased upward, because the winner was partly lucky. Only the tracker tells you N: "we tried a few things" can mean 5 or 500.

    If each score is true quality plus noise of standard deviation σ, the best of N is inflated by about

    optimism≈σ2τ2+σ2·eN

    τ: how much configurations genuinely differ. σ: validation noise (shrinks with more positive cases, F3.5). eN: expected maximum of N standard normal draws: about 0 for N = 1, 1.5 for 10, 2.5 for 100, 3.2 for 1,000. If all are truly equal (τ = 0), optimism is σ·eN and the winner has no real edge. Read it as: more tries, noisier scores and smaller real differences all mean a bigger gap between reported and true.

    The remedy is not fewer experiments. Keep a test set that selection never touched, report N beside the best score, and use nested validation or repeated splits when it matters (F4.5, F4.9). A tracker makes all three possible, and catches the lost winner: a great notebook result nobody logged and nobody can reproduce.

    The model registry: what is live, and why

    The tracker records attempts. The model registry records decisions. Think of a car registration: it ties a plate to one vehicle, its owner and its inspections. The registry is a catalogue of named models with numbered versions, each linked to the run that made it, plus three kinds of state:

    • Alias or stage: a movable label such as champion, challenger, staging, archived. Serving code asks for "the champion of fraud-card", never a file path, so promotion and rollback are label moves.
    • Metadata: owner, risk tier, intended use, validation status, approval record, documentation (a "model card"), data snapshot and code commit.
    • History: an append-only log of who moved which label, when, under what ticket.

    The rule that makes it a control: a label moves only if the evidence is attached. A version without independent validation and approval cannot become champion. Banks enforce this with access control (only the release process may write the alias) and pipeline checks.

    Registries expose the traps. A registered model whose training data was overwritten is a name without an identity. A rollback target whose libraries no longer install is not a rollback. 400 versions called "final_v7_REAL" is a landfill. Good practice:

    • Immutable versions: never edit, only add.
    • Pinned environments: a container image digest, not "latest".
    • Data snapshots by reference (P1.5 on lineage).
    • A retention policy that keeps whatever a regulator might ask about.

    For generative models the "model" also includes the prompt, retrieval index, tools and guardrails. P2.5 extends tracking and registries to these.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • The notebook that became production

      The model runs from a copied notebook with hand-set paths and no environment spec. Nobody can rebuild it.

      Spot itAsk for the command that retrains the live model from scratch. Run it on a clean machine.
    • The unlogged winner and the unmentioned fifty

      A headline score is the best of many runs and the count is never reported. It does not survive new data.

      Spot itAsk how many configurations were tried and whether the test set helped choose. Check the tracker's run count.
    • A data version that is a table name

      The record says "training_table_v2", but the table refreshes nightly, so the same name holds different rows.

      Spot itCheck that data references are immutable snapshots or hashes with an as-of date.
    • Promotion by hand, outside the registry

      Someone copies a file to the serving host to fix an urgent issue. The registry now disagrees with reality.

      Spot itCompare the production artefact's hash with the champion's registered hash.
    • Rollback that has never been tried

      The previous model exists on paper, but its dependencies no longer install or its feature table has changed.

      Spot itAsk when the last rollback drill ran. If never, the rollback is unproven.
    • Review cadence set by habit, not risk

      Every model is reviewed annually, including a fast-decaying fraud model that is wrong within months.

      Spot itCompare the review interval with the observed decay rate and label delay. If it is much longer than the time to breach, it is too slow.
    • Metrics measured on immature labels

      Monitoring reports last month's performance before outcomes arrive, so results look better or worse than they are.

      Spot itAsk how old the labels are in each window and whether numbers are restated later.
    • Approval without a link to the version

      The sign-off names "the fraud model", not a version ID. Later versions inherit approval they never earned.

      Spot itCheck each approval names an immutable version and the validation report it relied on.
    • Shadow models and zombies

      Old models still scoring in a corner of a batch job, uninventoried and unmonitored. Or retired models whose outputs another process still reads.

      Spot itReconcile the inventory against what is running and what downstream systems read.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Name the four questions a lifecycle and registry should answer about any production model.

    What is it exactly (version, artefact hash)? How was it made (code, data snapshot, parameters, environment)? Who allowed it (validation and approval records)? How do we undo it (a rollback target that still works)?

    2. A model's AUC falls 0.008 a month from 0.82; the floor is 0.75. Reviews are every 6 months and labels take 3 months to mature. When is the breach detected, and how long was the model below the floor?

    Breach at (0.82 − 0.75) ÷ 0.008 = 8.75 months. Detection: ⌈(8.75 + 3) ÷ 6⌉ × 6 = ⌈1.96⌉ × 6 = 12 months. Below the floor for 12 − 8.75 = 3.25 months.

    3. Why does weekly automated monitoring not remove the label-delay term?

    Outcome-based metrics need outcomes. Weekly AUC checks are useless if labels arrive after eight weeks. Drift and proxy metrics warn earlier but are weaker evidence, so they trigger a review and do not replace it.

    4. You tried 200 equal configurations and the best has validation AUC 0.84 on 200 positives. Is that a better model?

    No. Noise is about 0.27 ÷ √200 ≈ 0.019. The expected best of 200 draws is about 2.7 standard deviations up, so luck alone predicts about 0.80 + 0.052 = 0.85. A reported 0.84 is what chance gives. The winner's true AUC is about 0.80.

    5. What is the difference between an experiment tracker and a model registry?

    The tracker records every attempt and what produced it. The registry records decisions: which versions exist, their state, who approved them, which is live. Each registry entry links back to a tracker run.

    6. Why should a registry store a data snapshot ID, not a table name?

    A table name points at whatever the table holds today, and tables are refreshed. A snapshot ID or hash, with the as-of date, points at exactly the rows the model saw. Without it the model cannot be rebuilt or validated.

    7. A recalibration of a live credit model is proposed. Is it a new model version?

    Yes. It changes predictions, so it gets a new version and, under most policies, validation and approval in proportion to materiality. A minor recalibration may get a lighter review; skipping the record is the failure.
