    The Model Lifecycle, and Knowing Exactly Which Model Is Running

    The stages a bank model passes through from idea to retirement, why decay and review cadence are an engineering question, how experiment tracking stops you fooling yourself, and what a registry must be able to answer when an auditor asks.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA model is not a file you ship once but a controlled asset with a life: it has stages, owners and gates, it decays, and at every moment the bank must be able to say which version is running, what data and code built it, who approved it, and how to go back.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A data scientist at a Gulf bank builds a card-fraud model in a notebook. It scores well. Someone copies the notebook's output into a scoring service. Nine months later the fraud team says alerts have got worse. A regulator asks: which model is this, who approved it, what data trained it, and when was it last checked?

    Nobody can answer. The notebook was edited after the copy. The training table was overwritten by a refresh. The person who approved it has moved teams. Fifty experiments were run, and nobody remembers which one became the production model or how many were tried before it looked good. The model may be fine. The bank cannot show that it is.

    The lifecycle is the fix. Think of a model as an aircraft part, not a document. It has a design stage, test stages, a certificate, a serial number, scheduled inspections and a retirement date. None of this makes the model more accurate. It makes the model trustworthy, reproducible and reversible, which in a regulated bank matters as much.

    Two ideas carry the lesson.

    • Models decay, so the work is never finished. The world moves: fraud tactics change, customers change, the economy changes. A model that was right at deployment becomes wrong slowly and silently. The lifecycle has a monitoring and review loop built in, and Simulation 2 shows what the review interval costs.
    • Identity is the foundation. You cannot monitor, audit, roll back or retire "the model" unless "the model" is a precise, versioned thing: this code, this data snapshot, these parameters, this environment, this approval. Experiment tracking records how a model was made; the registry records what is live and why.
    Hold onto thisEvery serious question about a production model reduces to one of four: what is it exactly, how was it made, who allowed it, and how do we undo it. Lifecycle stages, trackers and registries are the machinery for answering those four.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The model lifecycle, stage by stage

    Banks differ in naming, but the same nine stages appear in model risk policies worldwide. The US supervisory guidance SR 11-7 (2011) set the pattern: development and implementation, validation, and governance, with ongoing monitoring in between. The Central Bank of the UAE's Model Management Standards, issued in 2022, describe a similar span of stages: development, validation before implementation, implementation, use and monitoring, independent validation, and changes such as recalibration or redevelopment. Saudi banks work under SAMA's own expectations; read the current text for your institution rather than rely on a summary, including this one.

    Click through the stages below. For each one: what happens, what artefact it leaves behind, who must sign, and what goes wrong if the stage is skipped.

    Three details separate real practice from the diagram.

    • It is a loop, not a line. Monitoring feeds back into change, and change re-enters development and validation. A recalibration is not "just a refit": it is a new model version, and under most policies it needs its own approval. How much validation depends on how material the change is.
    • Tiering sets the effort. A fraud model that can block thousands of customers, or an IFRS 9 model that moves provisions by millions of dirhams, is high tier: full independent validation, frequent review. A model that routes internal emails is low tier. The bank spends scarce validator time where harm is largest.
    • Independence is structural. The builder cannot validate their own model. The validators report separately, and the model owner (usually the business) remains accountable for use. G3 covers independent validation and review in depth; here the point is that the lifecycle must leave behind evidence a third party can use.

    Decay and review cadence

    The reason the loop exists is decay. Performance after deployment usually falls, not in a cliff but along a slope with noise: new fraud tactics, a changed customer mix, a rate change that alters who defaults. Sometimes it falls suddenly, because a rule upstream changed or a feed broke. F7.7 covers how to detect drift; the lifecycle question is different: how often do you look, and how stale will the answer be?

    Suppose a model's measured quality falls steadily, and the bank has agreed that below some floor the model is unfit. If you review every c months, a breach at time m* is not found until the next review, and the metric itself is only trustworthy once labels have matured (the delay problem from F3.5 and B1.2). So

    detected at=⌈m*+δc⌉·c

    m* is the month the true quality crosses the floor, δ the label delay in months, c the review interval. The ceiling brackets mean "round up to the next review". On average you wait about half an interval plus the label delay after the real breach, and in the worst case a full interval. Months spent below the floor are months of harm: missed fraud, wrong provisions, mispriced loans.

    Two lessons hide here. First, review cadence is a risk decision, with a price you can estimate. A quarterly review for a high-tier model is not bureaucracy; it is the interval at which you accept being wrong. Second, automation shrinks the first term but not the second. A dashboard can check weekly, but if fraud labels take eight weeks to mature, true performance is invisible for eight weeks. That is why input-drift monitors (PSI, KS, F7.7) and proxy metrics run alongside: they warn early, with weaker evidence, and the labelled metrics confirm later.

    Experiment tracking: counting what you tried

    Development is not one model. It is hundreds of runs: different features, algorithms, hyperparameters, splits and seeds. An experiment tracker (MLflow, Weights & Biases, or a bank-built equivalent) records for every run:

    • Code version: the git commit, and whether the working tree was clean.
    • Data version: a snapshot ID or content hash of the exact training table, plus the point-in-time cut-off (F3.1).
    • Parameters: every hyperparameter, random seed and feature list.
    • Environment: library versions, hardware, container image.
    • Metrics and artefacts: validation scores, plots, the fitted model file, sometimes sample predictions.

    The first use is reproduction: rebuild any number you reported (F10.4). The less obvious use is honesty about selection. If you run N comparable configurations and report the best validation score, that score is biased upward, because the winner was selected partly for good luck. The tracker is the only record that tells you N. Without it, "we tried a few things" can mean 5 or 500.

    The size of the effect has a clean form. If each configuration's validation score is its true quality plus noise with standard deviation σ, then the best of N is inflated by about

    optimism≈σ2τ2+σ2·eN

    τ is how much the configurations genuinely differ, σ the validation noise (which shrinks with the number of positive cases, as in F3.5), and eN the expected maximum of N standard normal draws: about 0 for N = 1, 1.5 for N = 10, 2.5 for N = 100, 3.2 for N = 1,000. If all configurations are really equal (τ = 0), the optimism is σ·eN and the winner has no real edge at all.

    The remedy is not to run fewer experiments. It is to keep a test set that selection never touched, report N beside the best score, and, when it matters, use the corrections in F4.5 and F4.9 (nested validation, repeated splits). A tracker makes all three possible. It also catches the quieter failure of a lost winner: someone gets a great result in a notebook, never logs it, and nobody can reproduce it.

    The model registry: what is live, and why

    The tracker records attempts. The model registry records decisions. It is a catalogue of named models, each with numbered versions, and each version links back to the run that produced it. On top sit three kinds of state:

    • Stage or alias: a movable label such as champion, challenger, staging, archived. Serving code asks for "the champion of fraud-card" and never for a file path, so promotion and rollback are label moves, not redeployments of mystery files. Modern tools lean on aliases and tags rather than the older fixed stages, but the idea is the same.
    • Metadata: owner, risk tier, intended use, validation status, approval record, links to documentation (a "model card"), the data snapshot and code commit.
    • History: an append-only log of who changed which label, when, and under what ticket.

    The rule that makes it a control and not a convenience: the label can only move if the evidence is attached. A version that has not passed independent validation and approval cannot become champion. Many banks enforce this with access control (only the release process may write the champion alias) and automated checks in the deployment pipeline.

    Registries are where the lifecycle becomes enforceable. They also expose the traps. A model that is registered but whose training data has since been overwritten is a name without an identity. A rollback target that was deleted or whose library versions no longer install is not a rollback. And a registry full of 400 unlabelled versions, "final_v7_REAL", is a landfill. Good practice: immutable versions (never edit, only add), pinned environments (a container image digest, not "latest"), data snapshots by reference (see P1.5 on lineage), and a short retention policy that keeps everything a regulator might ask about.

    For generative models, the "model" is more than weights. It includes the prompt, the retrieval index, the tools and the guardrails. P2.5 shows how the same tracking and registry ideas extend to prompt versions and traces.

    How it connects

    Tracking is that habit made systematic: the run record is the recipe.

    Shadow, champion-challenger and canary releases are the mechanisms that move a registry alias safely.

    For an assistant, prompts, retrieval settings and tool definitions are versioned artefacts too.

    Label delay is the second term in the detection formula and a hard floor under any monitoring system.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataFew positives, noisy scores

    The noise σ in Simulation 3 comes from the positive count. Rare events make selection bias worse.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • IFRS 9 and credit models. Provisions depend on PD, LGD and EAD models. Any change must be traceable to a version, a validation and an approval, because it moves reported numbers.
    • Fraud and AML. High-frequency retraining, fast decay, delayed labels. Champion-challenger runs constantly, and rollback must be a minute, not a day.
    • Marketing and churn. Lower tier, lighter gates, but the same need to know which model produced which campaign list.
    • Staff copilots and document AI. The "model" includes prompt, index and tools. Version them together or you cannot explain an answer from last Tuesday.
    • Vendor models. A model you bought is still your responsibility. The registry should hold the vendor's version, their documentation and your validation of it.

    Where the field is now

    The principles are stable. What moves is tooling, scope (from models to AI systems and agents) and regulators writing down expectations that used to be informal.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure has a quick check you can ask for in a review.

    • The notebook that became production

      The model runs from a copy of a notebook, with hand-set paths and no environment spec. Nobody can rebuild it.

      Spot itAsk for the command that retrains the live model from scratch, and try running it on a clean machine.
    • The unlogged winner and the unmentioned fifty

      A headline score comes from the best of many runs, and the count is never reported. The score does not survive new data.

      Spot itAsk how many configurations were tried and whether the test set was used to choose among them. Look at the tracker's run count.
    • A data version that is a table name

      The record says "training_table_v2", but the table is refreshed nightly. The same name now holds different rows.

      Spot itCheck that data references are immutable snapshots or content hashes with an as-of date.
    • Promotion by hand, outside the registry

      Someone copies a file to the serving host to fix an urgent issue. The registry now disagrees with reality.

      Spot itCompare the hash of the artefact in production against the champion's registered hash. They should match.
    • Rollback that has never been tried

      The previous model exists on paper, but its dependencies no longer install or its feature table has changed shape.

      Spot itAsk when the last rollback drill was run. If never, treat the rollback as unproven.
    • Review cadence set by habit, not risk

      Every model is reviewed annually, including a fast-decaying fraud model that is wrong within months.

      Spot itCompare the review interval with the observed decay rate and the label delay. If the interval is much longer than the time to breach, the cadence is too slow.
    • Metrics measured on immature labels

      Monitoring reports last month's performance before outcomes have arrived, so every month looks worse than it is, or better.

      Spot itAsk how old the labels are in each monitoring window and whether the numbers get restated later.
    • Approval without a link to the version

      The sign-off refers to "the fraud model", not to a version ID. Later versions inherit approval they never earned.

      Spot itCheck that every approval record names an immutable version and the validation report it relied on.
    • Shadow models and zombies

      Old models still scoring in a corner of a batch job, uninventoried and unmonitored. Or retired models whose outputs another process still reads.

      Spot itReconcile the inventory against what is actually running and what the downstream systems read.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A model is like a licensed asset, not a one-off project. We give each one a version number, a record of exactly how it was built, a named approver and a review date set by how fast it goes stale. That lets us answer a regulator in minutes, switch back to the previous version if something goes wrong, and know that the model we approved is the one that is running. The cost is some discipline up front; the saving is that we never have an incident we cannot explain.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Every run is tracked with code commit, immutable data snapshot and as-of date, parameters, seed and environment image, and I report the number of configurations tried. Only a version that has passed independent validation and approval can take the champion alias, and the release pipeline, not individuals, moves it. Review frequency is set from tier, observed decay and label delay, with drift monitors giving early warning. Rollback is rehearsed, and the registry points to everything needed to rebuild the model from scratch.”
    “We already have notebooks and a shared drive. Why do we need a tracker and a registry?”

    Because the questions that matter come later and from outside: which model made this decision, can you rebuild it, who approved it. A shared drive cannot answer them reliably. A tracker and a registry make the answer a query, not an archaeology project. It also speeds up the team, since nobody redoes lost work.

    “How often should we review the model?”

    Set it from risk, not habit. Tier sets the minimum under policy, but also look at how fast the model decays and how long labels take. A fraud model that loses accuracy within months and has eight-week label delays needs automated drift monitoring weekly and a labelled review quarterly. A stable low-tier model may be fine annually.

    “The new model scores higher in validation. Why not just ship it?”

    Three reasons. How many models did we try to find that score, since the best of many is inflated? Has it passed independent validation and been approved? And has it been tested against the current champion on live data, in shadow or a controlled rollout? A higher offline number is a reason to test it, not to release it.

    “What do we need to log so a model is reproducible?”

    The code commit, an immutable data snapshot plus the as-of date and feature definitions, all parameters and the random seed, the environment (library versions or container digest), and the metrics and artefacts. For LLM systems add the prompt, retrieval index version, tool definitions and model version. Exact bit-level reproduction is often unrealistic on GPUs; the standard is to rebuild an equivalent model and match its metrics within noise.

    “Can the model retrain and deploy itself automatically?”

    For low-risk models, with guardrails: automatic retraining, then automatic checks against a fixed evaluation set and the current champion, then a canary rollout with an automatic rollback. For high-tier models, retraining can be automated but promotion should keep a human approval and, where the policy requires it, validation sign-off. Automation does not remove accountability; it changes where evidence is generated.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Name the four questions a lifecycle and registry should let you answer about any production model.

    What is it exactly (version, artefact hash)? How was it made (code, data snapshot, parameters, environment)? Who allowed it (validation and approval records)? How do we undo it (rollback target that still works)?

    2. A model's AUC falls 0.008 a month from 0.82; the floor is 0.75. Reviews are every 6 months and labels take 3 months to mature. When is the breach detected, and how long was the model below the floor?

    Breach at (0.82 − 0.75) ÷ 0.008 = 8.75 months. Detection needs a review at least 3 months after the breach: ⌈(8.75 + 3) ÷ 6⌉ × 6 = ⌈1.96⌉ × 6 = 12 months. It ran below the floor for 12 − 8.75 = 3.25 months.

    3. Why does adding weekly automated monitoring not remove the label-delay term?

    Monitoring can only compute outcome-based metrics once outcomes exist. Weekly runs are useless for AUC if labels arrive after eight weeks. Input drift and proxy metrics warn earlier but are weaker evidence, so they trigger a review and do not replace it.

    4. You tried 200 configurations, all really equal, and the best has validation AUC 0.84 on a set with 200 positives. Is that evidence of a better model?

    No. Noise is about 0.27 ÷ √200 ≈ 0.019, and the expected best of 200 draws is about 2.7 standard deviations above the mean, so luck alone predicts a best near 0.80 + 0.052 = 0.85. A reported 0.84 is exactly what chance gives. The winner's true AUC is the common value, about 0.80.

    5. What is the difference between an experiment tracker and a model registry?

    The tracker records every attempt and what produced it. The registry records the decisions: which versions exist for a named model, what state each is in, who approved it, and which one is live. The registry entry links back to the tracker run.

    6. Why should a registry store a data snapshot ID, not a table name?

    A table name points at whatever the table holds today, and tables are refreshed. A snapshot ID or content hash, with the as-of date, points at exactly the rows the model saw. Without it, the model cannot be rebuilt, and validators cannot test the data it learned from.

    7. A recalibration of a live credit model is proposed. Is it a new model version?

    Yes. It changes predictions, so it gets a new version, and most policies treat it as a model change requiring validation and approval in proportion to its materiality. A minor recalibration might have a lighter review; skipping the record altogether is the failure.
