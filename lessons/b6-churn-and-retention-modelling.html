    Churn, Propensity and Uplift: Who Leaves, Who to Call, and Whether the Call Changes Anything

    How a bank defines "gone" when nobody ever says goodbye, why a good churn score can still produce a useless campaign, and why the question that pays is not "who will leave?" but "whose behaviour will change if we act?"

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA churn model predicts who will leave, but money is made by acting on the customers whose decision your action can change, so define churn carefully, predict early enough to act, and target by expected uplift in value rather than by risk or propensity alone.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank customer almost never says goodbye. Think of a gym member who stops turning up: the membership runs on, but they left months ago. The salary moves elsewhere, card use drifts, the balance falls to a few hundred dirhams, and the account sits open for two years.

    So retention is two jobs: define "gone" and spot it early enough to act, and decide whom to call.

    Say you can make 5,000 calls and a model ranks 100,000 customers by chance of leaving. The obvious plan is to call the top 5,000. Who is in that list?

    • Lost causes have already signed up elsewhere. A call changes nothing.
    • Persuadables are at risk and would respond to a better rate or fee waiver. They are the only people worth the call.
    • Sure things will stay whatever you do. Calling them wastes money, and a discount costs margin.
    • Sleeping dogs react badly. The call reminds them to compare fees, and they leave.

    A churn model sees risk, like a smoke alarm that signals heat but not whether the fire brigade can help. It never learned what happens when you intervene, so it cannot tell the four groups apart. Uplift modelling closes that gap. Churn, propensity and uplift form one chain: who might leave, what to do, and did it work.

    Hold onto thisPrediction asks "what happens to this customer if we do nothing?" Decisions need "what happens if we act versus do nothing?" That is a causal question (F9.3). Most retention programmes stop at the first and wonder why the campaign report disappoints.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Defining churn when nobody says goodbye (B6.1)

    Most retail products are non-contractual: a current account, debit card or savings account has no end date. Cards and loans end with a visible event, but the customer often goes quiet long before. Churn is therefore something you define from behaviour, and the definition shapes everything after it.

    • Account closure. Clean and certain, but late. The money has already left.
    • Inactivity. No transactions for d days. Easy, but it mislabels customers who simply transact rarely.
    • Balance or salary migration. Balance down by over half, or salary credit stopped. Early and commercially meaningful, because revenue sits in balances and salary relationships.
    • Product-level churn. Card unused for 90 days, mortgage refinanced elsewhere, term deposit not rolled over. Often the actionable unit.
    • Relationship-level churn. Gone from the bank across all products. Hardest to see, but it is the loss that matters.

    Gulf banking adds three wrinkles. Many retail customers are expatriates on employment visas; when the job ends or they leave the country, the account closes or goes dormant. That is real attrition but not retainable, and a model that cannot separate it wastes calls. Salary transfer anchors the relationship in many UAE and Saudi banks, so a salary switch is among the strongest early signs. Islamic and conventional products behave differently, so fit per product family or add the family as a feature. Ask the business what applies at your bank.

    The inactivity trap shows up with arithmetic. If a customer transacts at random, λ times a month on average, the chance of a silent d-day window is e−λd/30. A heavy card user (20 a month) is essentially never silent for 30 days by chance. An occasional user (0.4 a month) is silent for 30 days 67% of the time while perfectly happy. One fixed window is too jumpy for the second and too slow for the first. The fix is to compare silence with the customer's own usual gap: flag when silence exceeds k times that gap, which happens by chance with probability e−k for everyone. The first simulation shows both.

    Model performance depends on this definition. A model trained to predict "silent for 90 days" partly learns to find rare transactors: AUC looks fine, usefulness is poor. Validate the label against something firmer (closure within a year, salary gone) before building on it.

    Windows, snapshots and lead time (B6.1)

    Churn modelling is a snapshot problem, like judging a runner from their splits so far and then checking the finish. Pick a date, say 31 March. Describe each customer using only what was known on or before it (the observation window, typically 3 to 12 months). Label each by what happens after (the outcome window, say 90 days). Then slide the date forward and stack snapshots. This is point-in-time correctness (F3.1): a feature computed over the outcome window, such as "balance at month-end after churn", leaks the answer.

    -- one row per customer per snapshot date
SELECT c.customer_id, s.snapshot_date,
       COUNT(t.txn_id) FILTER (WHERE t.txn_date > s.snapshot_date - 90)  AS txns_90d,
       COUNT(t.txn_id) FILTER (WHERE t.txn_date BETWEEN s.snapshot_date - 180
                                                    AND s.snapshot_date - 91) AS txns_prev_90d,
       MAX(CASE WHEN a.salary_credit_date <= s.snapshot_date THEN a.salary_credit_date END) AS last_salary,
       -- the label looks FORWARD only
       CASE WHEN NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM salary_credits x
                             WHERE x.customer_id = c.customer_id
                               AND x.credit_date > s.snapshot_date
                               AND x.credit_date <= s.snapshot_date + 90) THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS churn_90d
FROM customers c CROSS JOIN snapshots s ...

    The strongest features are changes, not static traits like age or tenure: transactions this quarter against last, balance trend, salary credits stopping, a new complaint or failed dispute, a rate rise, a call about closing or fees, falling logins, a card never activated. Also useful: number of products held (more products, stickier) and, where permitted, signs of another bank via open-banking data.

    How far ahead should the model look? Predicting the next 7 days is accurate because the customer is already half out of the door, and useless because acting takes time. Call that time the action lag L: a relationship manager has to call, an offer has to be approved, a transfer takes a payday or two to reverse. Anyone who leaves within L days of being flagged cannot be saved. Look too far ahead (180 days) and the signal is weak, because today's behaviour barely separates future leavers from stayers. The sweet spot between is set by your action lag as much as by your data.

    useful recall≈recall(h,c)·h−Lh

    Recall is the share of eventual leavers in the contacted list; it falls as horizon h grows and ranking worsens. The second factor is the share whose exit is still at least L days away (exits assumed evenly spread); it rises with h. The product peaks.

    Two rules follow. Choose the horizon from the action lag and the intervention, not from what flatters AUC. And report precision and recall at the contact budget. AUC says how well the whole list is ordered; a retention team works only the top few percent (F7.1 to F7.4, and imbalanced data for why a 4% churn rate behaves like a rare event).

    Modelling choices, and judging a churn model by money (B6.1)

    Modelling is the least surprising part. A gradient-boosted tree (F4.3) on snapshot features is the strong baseline; logistic regression (F4.2) is the interpretable one, often close behind. Three alternatives:

    • Survival models (F2.8) model time to leaving and the hazard at each age, not a yes/no within 90 days. They handle customers who have not churned yet (censoring), give a risk curve, and let you pick the horizon later.
    • Sequence models. Feed the raw timeline of transactions and contacts to a recurrent or transformer model. It sometimes beats hand-built features on rich data, but rarely by enough to justify the cost, and it is harder to explain to a validator.
    • Segment first (B6.3). Salaried retail, affluent, small business and Islamic customers leave for different reasons. One model per segment, or segment as a feature, usually beats one global model.

    Judge the model by money, not just AUC:

    • Lift or precision at the contact budget. "The top 5% has 6 times the base churn rate" is a sentence an executive understands. Lift 1 means no better than random; higher is better.
    • Calibration (F7.4). If a score of 0.2 means 20% of such customers leave, you can multiply by value at risk and add. Rebalancing training data distorts this.
    • Value-weighted ranking. A customer with AED 400 and one with AED 400,000 do not deserve equal attention. Rank by probability × value at risk (B6.3), or train with value weights. Verbeke and colleagues (2012) argued for profit-driven evaluation for this reason.
    • Time-ordered testing (F4.5). Train on early snapshots, test on later ones, since drivers shift with pricing and competitors.

    SHAP reasons (F8.2) explain the score, not the cause. "Salary credits stopped" is a signal you cannot fix; "fee increased last month" might be a cause you can. Test before turning a feature-importance chart into strategy.

    Propensity and next-best-action (B6.2)

    A propensity model is the same machinery pointed at another outcome: the probability a customer takes an action, such as a personal-finance product, a savings plan, card activation or the app. Churn is propensity to leave. The label is "did X happen within N days of a snapshot"; point-in-time correctness, time-ordered testing and calibration all carry over.

    Next-best-action (NBA) is the layer above, like a waiter who picks one suggestion per table rather than reading out the whole menu. It chooses one action per customer per moment (offer a product, ask for a document, send a service message, do nothing), in four steps:

    1. Eligibility and suitability. Hard-filter out anything not eligible, not suitable, without marketing consent, in collections or hardship, or unavailable to the segment.
    2. Score each remaining action by expected value: probability of outcome × its value − cost of contact − cost of annoying the customer.
    3. Apply contact policy. Cap messages per month, space them, respect channel preferences, quiet hours and call-centre capacity.
    4. Choose, log and learn. Record what was shown, the alternatives and the result.

    Step four makes NBA a learning system. If you always send the model's favourite, you never learn how the others would have done. The fix is deliberate exploration: randomise a small share of decisions, the bandit idea of F9.7. Ranking many products is also covered by recommender systems, but steps one and three make banking very different from recommending films.

    The weakness: a customer with a high propensity to take a savings product would probably have done it anyway. Targeting by propensity concentrates budget on people who need persuading least, and the campaign looks successful because you picked people who were going to convert. Uplift modelling exists to fix this.

    Uplift: modelling the change, not the outcome (B6.2)

    Each customer has two potential outcomes: contacted and not contacted. What you want is the difference, the treatment effect or uplift. If a 70% chance of staying becomes 74% after a call, uplift is 4 points.

    τ(x)=P(retained∣x,contacted)−P(retained∣x,not contacted)

    x is what you know about the customer. You never observe both outcomes for one person, so uplift is estimated by comparing alike groups, which needs contacted and not-contacted customers drawn at random. Rule: contact when τ(x) × V > c, with V the value of a retained customer and c the cost of contact. Negative τ (a sleeping dog) is never contacted, however high the risk. Reading it: τ = 0.04 means the call adds 4 retained customers per 100 called; τ near 0 means the call is wasted. Common misreading: high τ does not mean high risk.

    The data must come from a randomised experiment (or, carefully, a very clean natural one) where a random subset is held out. Otherwise the contacted differ from the rest in ways the model cannot see, and "uplift" is selection bias (F2.6) in disguise. Common estimators:

    • Two models (T-learner). Fit one on the treated, one on controls; uplift is the difference of predictions. A sound first try, but a difference of two noisy numbers is noisier than either.
    • One model with treatment as a feature (S-learner). It can shrink the effect to zero if the tree never splits on the treatment flag.
    • Uplift trees and forests split to maximise the difference in outcome between treated and control in each leaf (Rzepakowski and Jaroszewicz, 2012). Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018) and the R-learner (Nie and Wager, 2021) are on firmer statistical footing and give confidence intervals.
    from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier as GB

# df: one row per customer, 'treated' is RANDOMISED, 'retained' is the outcome
m1 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 1], df.retained[df.treated == 1])
m0 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 0], df.retained[df.treated == 0])
uplift = m1.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] - m0.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

# Evaluate on held-out randomised data: rank by uplift, then compare
# retention of treated vs control within each decile (a Qini or uplift curve).

    No customer has a ground-truth uplift, so evaluation uses the Qini curve: rank by predicted uplift, then plot cumulative extra retained customers (treated minus control) as you contact more. A good model rises steeply, then flattens or falls. The simulation draws this, with true uplift known because it is simulated.

    Three lessons sit in that picture. The uplift ranking differs from the churn ranking: the most at-risk group is not the best to call. Past a point, calling more hurts, by wasting money or reaching customers who react badly. The budget is separate from the model: call until τ × V drops below the cost of a call.

    Two cautions. Estimates are noisy and real effects are small (a few points at best), so you need large experiments and wide intervals (F2.1, F2.7). And a model is specific to the action it learned from: a fee-waiver experiment says nothing about a phone call.

    Constraints that sit above the model (B6.2)

    A few things are fixed before the model gets a say. Build them into the system rather than leaving them to the campaign team.

    • Consent and data use. Marketing contact and profiling need a lawful basis. The UAE (federal PDPL, 2021) and Saudi Arabia (PDPL) have data-protection laws, and CBUAE and SAMA set conduct rules. Check with compliance and legal which data may feed a model and which channels are allowed.
    • Suitability. Offering high-cost borrowing to someone stretched is a conduct risk, however high the propensity. Suitability comes before ranking. Sharia-compliant products have their own eligibility rules, and offer wording must match the contract type (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq), which the product team owns.
    • Fairness. Retention offers are an economic benefit. Check the pattern does not track nationality, gender or another sensitive attribute (G2). Proxies such as postcode or employer can do this quietly.
    • Contact fatigue. Every message costs attention. Caps and cool-offs belong in the objective.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A churn definition that measures something else

      "Inactive for 90 days" quietly predicts low-frequency customers or expatriates who left the country.

      Spot itCheck flagged customers a year later. If many are still active or closed for non-retainable reasons, the label is wrong.
    • Leakage through the outcome window

      A feature like "balance last month" computed after the snapshot gives an AUC of 0.97.

      Spot itDistrust any churn AUC above about 0.9. Check every feature's date against the snapshot date.
    • Predicting too late to act

      Excellent at 7 days, but the money has gone by the time the call is made.

      Spot itAsk for action lag and horizon together. Count flagged customers who would leave within the lag.
    • Calling the persuadable and the doomed alike

      A "save rate" among the contacted includes many who would have stayed anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the control group's retention. A save rate without a randomised control is not evidence.
    • A holdout that is not random

      Controls are customers with no valid phone number, or those managers skipped.

      Spot itCompare groups on pre-campaign behaviour. A gap before treatment means the uplift is partly selection.
    • Optimising propensity and expecting incremental sales

      The model ranks people who already intend to buy. Take-up is high; incremental take-up is small.

      Spot itCheck conversion in the untreated high-propensity group. If it is nearly as high as the treated, the campaign adds little.
    • The feedback loop

      The model learns only from customers it chose, so it keeps choosing the same type.

      Spot itCheck whether any decisions are randomised. If none, you cannot learn what else would have worked.
    • Over-reading an uplift model

      A small experiment and flexible model give a "top decile uplift of 12 points" that is noise.

      Spot itAsk for experiment size, intervals on decile effects, and a fresh-holdout result.
    • Discounts that train customers to threaten

      Offers to those who complain teach everyone to complain, and cut margin.

      Spot itWatch complaints, closure requests and net margin after launch, not only the save count.
    • Compliance blind spots

      A data source or channel outside consent, or a product that fails suitability.

      Spot itAsk where consent and suitability filters sit in the pipeline and who signed off the feature data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A customer makes about 1.5 card transactions a month at random. What is the chance of a 60-day silence by pure chance?

    e−λd/30 with λ = 1.5, d = 60 gives e−3 ≈ 5%. One in twenty such customers is wrongly called churned by a 60-day rule though nothing changed.

    2. A model flags 5,000 customers, of whom 400 will leave within 90 days. 100,000 customers have a 3% churn rate overall. What are precision, recall and lift?

    Precision = 400 ÷ 5,000 = 8%. Leavers total 3,000, so recall = 400 ÷ 3,000 ≈ 13%. Lift = 8% ÷ 3% ≈ 2.7 times the base rate. Useful, not spectacular, and a reminder of how little churn is predictable.

    3. Why is a 7-day prediction horizon a bad idea even if its AUC is 0.95?

    If acting takes two weeks, every flagged customer leaves before any action lands. Useful recall = recall × (h − L) ÷ h, which is zero when h ≤ L.

    4. Customers are in four groups: persuadable, sure thing, lost cause, sleeping dog. Which does a churn model rank highest, and which should you call?

    A churn model ranks lost causes highest, then persuadables, then sleeping dogs, with sure things last. Call persuadables only: contacting the others wastes money or does harm.

    5. An offer costs AED 40 to make. A retained customer is worth AED 5,000. Which uplift justifies contact?

    Contact when τ × 5,000 > 40, so τ > 0.8 percentage points. Below that, skip the customer however high their risk.

    6. You have only observational data where relationship managers chose whom to call. Can you estimate uplift?

    Not reliably. Their choices correlate with things the model cannot see, so the estimate mixes the action with the selection. Adjustment needs every driver of the decision to be measured, a strong assumption. The clean path is a randomised holdout.

    7. The T-learner's predicted uplifts look wildly noisy. What are two sensible responses?

    Get more experimental data, since uplift needs large samples. Use a method that estimates the effect directly with regularisation (causal forest, R-learner), a simpler model, or shrinkage toward the average. Also aggregate: rank by predicted uplift, then check treated-versus-control differences by decile on fresh data.
