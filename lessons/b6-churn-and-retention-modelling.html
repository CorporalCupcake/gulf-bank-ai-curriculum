    Churn, Propensity and Uplift: Who Leaves, Who to Call, and Whether the Call Changes Anything

    How a bank defines "gone" when nobody ever says goodbye, why a good churn score can still produce a useless campaign, and why the question that pays is not "who will leave?" but "whose behaviour will change if we act?"

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA churn model predicts who will leave, but money is made by acting on the customers whose decision your action can change, so define churn carefully, predict early enough to act, and target by expected uplift in value rather than by risk or propensity alone.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A telecom customer who leaves cancels a contract. There is a date, a form, a clear event. A bank customer almost never does that. They move their salary to another bank, then the card use drifts away, then the balance falls to a few hundred dirhams, and the account sits open for two years. Nobody told you. The customer was "lost" months before any system noticed.

    So the first job in retention is not modelling. It is deciding what "churned" means, and noticing it early enough to do something. The second job is separate and often forgotten: deciding whom to call. Those are different questions.

    Picture a retention team with a budget for 5,000 calls and a model that ranks 100,000 customers by chance of leaving. The obvious plan is to call the top 5,000. Now think about who is in that list.

    • Some have already decided and signed up elsewhere. A call changes nothing. These are lost causes.
    • Some are at risk and would respond to a better rate or a fee waiver. These are the persuadables, the only people worth the call.
    • Some will stay whatever you do. Calling them wastes money, and giving them a discount costs margin. These are sure things.
    • A few will react badly. A call about their account reminds them to compare the fees. These are sleeping dogs, and the call makes them leave.

    A churn model sees risk. It cannot tell the four groups apart, because it never learned what happens when you intervene. That gap is what uplift modelling closes, and it is why this lesson covers churn, propensity and uplift together: they are one chain from "who might leave" to "what do we do, and did it work".

    Hold onto thisPrediction answers "what will happen to this customer if we do nothing?" Decisions need "what will happen if we do this versus nothing?" The second is a causal question (F9.3). Most retention programmes stop at the first and wonder why the campaign report is disappointing.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Defining churn when nobody says goodbye (B6.1)

    Retail banking is a non-contractual setting for most products. A current account, a debit card or a savings account has no end date. Credit cards and loans end through a visible event (closure, settlement), but the customer often goes quiet long before. So churn is something you define, using behaviour, and the definition shapes everything after it. Common choices:

    • Account closure. Clean and certain, but late. By then the money has left.
    • Inactivity. No transactions for d days. Easy to compute, but it mislabels customers who simply transact rarely.
    • Balance or salary migration. Balance down by more than half, or salary credit stopped. Early and more commercially meaningful, because the revenue sits in balances and salary relationships. This is the "move balances" case in the module description.
    • Product-level churn. Card unused for 90 days, a mortgage refinanced elsewhere, a term deposit not rolled over. Often the actionable unit.
    • Relationship-level churn. The customer is gone from the bank, across all products. Harder to see, but it is the loss that matters.

    Gulf banking adds its own shapes. A large share of retail customers are expatriates on employment visas. When a job ends or the person leaves the country, the account closes or goes dormant. That is real attrition, but not retainable, and a model that cannot tell it apart wastes calls and will look worse than it is. Salary transfer is also the anchor of the relationship in many UAE and Saudi banks (employers pay through a nominated account), so a salary switch is among the strongest early signs. Islamic accounts and profit-sharing deposits behave differently from conventional ones, and a model should usually be fit per product family or with the family as a feature. Treat all of this as a prompt to ask the business rather than as a fact I can give you for your bank.

    The inactivity trap is easy to see with a little arithmetic. If a customer makes transactions at random with an average of λ per month, the chance of a silent window of d days with no transactions is e−λd/30. A heavy card user (20 a month) will essentially never be silent for 30 days by chance. An occasional user (0.4 a month) will be silent for 30 days 67% of the time while still being a perfectly happy customer. A single fixed window is too jumpy for them and too slow for the heavy user. The fix is to compare silence to the customer's own usual gap: flag when the silence is longer than k times their normal interval, a chance event with probability e−k for everyone. The first simulation shows both.

    Notice what the model's performance will depend on: this definition. A model trained to predict "silent for 90 days" learns, in part, to predict customers who transact rarely. Its AUC may look fine and its usefulness be poor. Validate the label itself against something firmer (closure within a year, salary gone) before building on it.

    Windows, snapshots and lead time (B6.1)

    Churn modelling is a snapshot problem. Pick a date, say 31 March. Describe every customer using only what was known on or before that date (the observation window, typically 3 to 12 months of behaviour). Label each by what happens after it (the outcome window, say the next 90 days). Then slide the date forward and stack snapshots. This is point-in-time correctness from F3.1 in its most common form: a feature that is computed over the outcome window, such as "balance at month-end after churn", leaks the answer.

    -- one row per customer per snapshot date
SELECT c.customer_id, s.snapshot_date,
       COUNT(t.txn_id) FILTER (WHERE t.txn_date > s.snapshot_date - 90)  AS txns_90d,
       COUNT(t.txn_id) FILTER (WHERE t.txn_date BETWEEN s.snapshot_date - 180
                                                    AND s.snapshot_date - 91) AS txns_prev_90d,
       MAX(CASE WHEN a.salary_credit_date <= s.snapshot_date THEN a.salary_credit_date END) AS last_salary,
       -- the label looks FORWARD only
       CASE WHEN NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM salary_credits x
                             WHERE x.customer_id = c.customer_id
                               AND x.credit_date > s.snapshot_date
                               AND x.credit_date <= s.snapshot_date + 90) THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS churn_90d
FROM customers c CROSS JOIN snapshots s ...

    The features that carry the most signal are rarely the static ones (age, tenure, segment). They are changes: transactions this quarter against last, balance trend, salary credits stopping, a new complaint, a failed dispute, a rate rise on a product, a call-centre contact about closing or about fees, logins falling, a card replaced and never activated. Also useful: how many products the customer holds (more products, stickier), and whether the customer has another bank visible through open-banking style data where permitted.

    Now the less obvious part: how far ahead should the model look? A model that predicts the next 7 days is accurate, because by then the customer is already half out of the door. It is also useless, because retention work needs time: a relationship manager has to call, an offer has to be approved, a transfer takes a payday or two to reverse. Call the time this takes the action lag L. A customer who leaves within L days of being flagged cannot be saved. Look too far ahead (180 days) and the signal is weak: little in today's behaviour separates the leavers from the stayers yet. Between them lies a sweet spot, and it is set by your action lag as much as by your data.

    useful recall≈recall(h,c)·h−Lh

    Recall is the share of eventual leavers who are in the contacted list, which falls as the horizon h lengthens and the model's ranking gets worse. The second factor is the share of those whose exit date is still at least L days away, assuming exits are spread evenly across the horizon. It rises with h. Their product has a peak.

    Two practical rules follow. First, choose the horizon from the action lag and the intervention, not from what makes AUC look best. Second, report precision and recall at the contact budget, not just AUC. AUC says how well the whole list is ordered. A retention team only works the top few percent, and the top of the list is what matters (F7.1 to F7.4, and imbalanced data for why a 4% churn rate behaves like a rare event).

    Modelling choices, and judging a churn model by money (B6.1)

    The modelling itself is the least surprising part. A gradient-boosted tree (F4.3) on snapshot features is the standard strong baseline; logistic regression (F4.2) is the interpretable one and often close behind. Three alternatives are worth knowing.

    • Survival models (F2.8). Instead of "leaves within 90 days, yes or no", model the time to leaving and the hazard at each age. They use customers who have not churned yet properly (censoring), give a risk curve rather than one number, and let you choose the horizon after the fact. Good when horizons vary by product.
    • Sequence and event models. Feed the raw timeline of transactions and contacts to a recurrent or transformer model. This sometimes beats hand-built features on rich data. On tabular summaries it rarely wins by enough to justify the cost, and it is harder to explain to a validator.
    • Segment first, then model (B6.3). Salaried retail, affluent, small business and Islamic products leave for different reasons. One model per segment, or the segment as a feature, usually beats a single global one.

    Judging the model is where banking teams can do better than most. Do not stop at AUC.

    • Lift or precision in the top decile and at the actual contact budget. "The top 5% contains 6 times the base churn rate" is a sentence an executive understands.
    • Calibration (F7.4). If scores are real probabilities, you can multiply by value at risk and add. Remember that rebalancing training data distorts them.
    • Value-weighted ranking. A customer with AED 400 in the account and a customer with AED 400,000 do not deserve the same attention. Rank by probability × value at risk (the lifetime-value ideas of B6.3), or train with value weights. Verbeke and colleagues (2012) argued for profit-driven evaluation, not a purely statistical one, for exactly this reason.
    • Time-ordered testing (F4.5). Train on early snapshots, test on later ones. Churn drivers shift with pricing, competitors and the economy.

    And a caution on explanation. SHAP reasons (F8.2) tell you what drives the score. "Salary credits stopped" is a signal, not a cause you can fix; "fee increased last month" might be a cause you can. Do not turn a feature importance chart into a retention strategy without testing it.

    Propensity and next-best-action (B6.2)

    A propensity model is the same machinery pointed at a different outcome: the probability that a customer will take an action, such as taking a personal-finance product, opening a savings plan, activating a card or adopting the app. Churn is propensity to leave. The label is "did X happen within N days of a snapshot", the features are behaviour before the snapshot, and everything about point-in-time correctness, time-ordered testing and calibration carries over.

    Next-best-action (NBA) is the layer above. A bank has many things it could say to a customer: offer a product, ask for a document, send a service message, do nothing. NBA picks one per customer per moment. In its simplest honest form it is expected value, under rules:

    1. Eligibility and suitability. Remove anything the customer should not be offered: not eligible, not suitable, no marketing consent, in collections or hardship, product not available to the segment. This is a hard filter, not a score (more in the next section).
    2. Score each remaining action by expected value: probability of the desired outcome × its value − cost of the contact − any cost of annoying the customer.
    3. Apply contact policy. Cap messages per customer per month, space them out, respect channel preferences and quiet hours, respect capacity of the call centre and branches.
    4. Choose, log and learn. Record what was shown, what the alternatives were, and what happened.

    Step four is where NBA becomes a learning system. If you always send the action the model likes best, you never learn how the others would have done, and the training data reinforces its own choices. The fix is deliberate exploration, a small share of decisions randomised, which is exactly the bandit idea of F9.7. Ranking many products per customer is also covered by recommender systems; in banking the constraints in steps one and three make it a very different problem from recommending films.

    The weakness of a propensity model is the same as for churn, just less visible. A customer with a high propensity to take a savings product is, almost by definition, someone who would probably have done it anyway. Targeting by propensity concentrates the budget on the people least in need of persuasion, and the campaign appears to work because the people you picked were going to convert. That is the trap uplift modelling exists to fix.

    Uplift: modelling the change, not the outcome (B6.2)

    Define two potential outcomes for each customer: what happens if contacted, and what happens if not. The thing you actually want is the difference, the treatment effect or uplift:

    τ(x)=P(retained∣x,contacted)−P(retained∣x,not contacted)

    x is what you know about the customer. You can never observe both outcomes for one person, so uplift is estimated by comparing groups that look alike: that is why you need contacted and not-contacted customers drawn at random. The decision rule is then simple: contact a customer when τ(x) × V > c, where V is the value of a retained customer and c is the cost of the contact. A sleeping dog has negative τ, so the rule never contacts them, however high their churn risk.

    How is it estimated? The data must come from a randomised experiment (or, with care, a very clean natural one): a random subset of customers is held out from the campaign. Without that, the people who were contacted differ from those who were not in ways the model cannot see, and "uplift" is just selection bias (F2.6) in disguise. With it, the common approaches are:

    • Two models (T-learner). Fit one model on the treated group and one on the control group. Uplift is the difference between their predictions. Simple and a sound first try. Weakness: the difference of two noisy numbers is noisier than either.
    • One model with the treatment as a feature (S-learner), or with interaction terms. Can shrink the effect toward zero if the tree never splits on the treatment flag.
    • Uplift trees and forests, which choose splits to maximise the difference in outcome between treated and control in each leaf (Rzepakowski and Jaroszewicz, 2012). Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018) and the R-learner (Nie and Wager, 2021) put this on firmer statistical footing and give confidence intervals.
    from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier as GB

# df: one row per customer, 'treated' is RANDOMISED, 'retained' is the outcome
m1 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 1], df.retained[df.treated == 1])
m0 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 0], df.retained[df.treated == 0])
uplift = m1.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] - m0.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

# Evaluate on held-out randomised data: rank by uplift, then compare
# retention of treated vs control within each decile (a Qini or uplift curve).

    Evaluating an uplift model is harder than evaluating a classifier, because there is no ground-truth uplift per customer. The standard tool is the Qini curve (or cumulative gain curve): rank customers by predicted uplift, then plot the cumulative extra retained customers, treated minus control, as you contact more of the list. A good model rises steeply, then flattens or falls. The simulation uses exactly this picture, with the true uplift known because it is simulated.

    Three lessons sit inside that picture. The ranking differs from the churn ranking: the most at-risk group is not the best group to call. Past a point, calling more makes things worse, either by wasting money or by reaching customers who react badly. And the budget decision is separate from the model: with uplift in hand, the right number of calls is wherever τ × V drops below the cost of a call.

    Two honest cautions. Uplift estimates are noisy: most real campaigns have small effects (a few percentage points at best), so you need large experiments and wide intervals (F2.1, F2.7). And an uplift model is specific to the action it learned from. A model trained on a fee-waiver experiment says nothing about what a phone call would do.

    Constraints that sit above the model (B6.2)

    None of this is allowed to run free. A few things are fixed before the model gets a say, and a good data scientist builds them into the system rather than leaving them to the campaign team.

    • Consent and data use. Marketing contact and profiling need a lawful basis. The UAE and Saudi Arabia both have personal-data protection laws (the UAE federal PDPL of 2021 and the Saudi PDPL, with its implementing rules) and banks also face central bank conduct rules (CBUAE and SAMA consumer-protection requirements). I will not state the specific articles from memory: check with your compliance and legal teams which data may feed a churn or propensity model and which contact channels are permitted.
    • Suitability. Offering a high-cost borrowing product to someone already stretched is a conduct risk, however high the propensity. Suitability checks come before ranking, not after. Product eligibility for Sharia-compliant products has its own rules, and wording of offers must match the contract type (murabaha, ijara, tawarruq and so on), which the product team owns.
    • Fairness. Retention offers are an economic benefit. If better rates go only to customers the model considers valuable or at risk, check that the pattern does not track nationality, gender or another protected or sensitive attribute (G2). Proxies such as postcode or employer can do this quietly.
    • Contact fatigue. Every message has a cost in attention. Caps and cool-offs are part of the objective, not an afterthought.

    How it connects

    F2.8 · Survival analysisTime to leaving, with censoring

    Customers who have not churned yet are unfinished, not safe. Hazard models give a risk curve instead of one 90-day number.

    F3.1 · Point-in-time and leakageSnapshots done right

    Every churn dataset is a stack of snapshots. A feature computed after the snapshot date quietly hands the model the answer.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataChurn as a rare event

    A 3–5% quarterly rate means few positives per segment, wide intervals and a threshold chosen from costs, not from rebalancing.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Metrics and calibrationPrecision at the budget

    Judge the top of the list. Calibrated probabilities let you multiply by value at risk.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceUplift is a causal estimate

    Randomised holdouts, selection bias and heterogeneous effects underlie everything in the uplift section.

    F9.5 · F9.7 · Recommenders and banditsExploration for NBA

    A system that always sends its favourite action never learns about the others. Bandits randomise a little, on purpose.

    B6.3 · Segments and lifetime valueWhat a customer is worth

    The value V in the decision rule comes from lifetime value. Segments define the models and the offers.

    F3.6 · ExperimentsRun the holdout properly

    Size, randomisation unit, duration and a pre-agreed success measure decide whether you can believe the uplift.

    P5.3 · Batch scoring and campaignsPutting it in production

    Monthly scoring, contact-policy engines, suppression lists and feeding outcomes back to training.

    F7.7 · DriftBehaviour shifts

    A rate change, a new competitor or a salary-system migration moves who leaves. Monitor decile lift as well as AUC.

    G2 · Fairness and privacyWho gets the offer

    Targeting that allocates benefits needs fairness checks, plus data-minimisation and consent rules from privacy basics.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Salary and primary-relationship defence. Detect early drift of salary credits and card spend; offer a pre-approved benefit through the relationship manager.
    • Credit-card attrition and dormancy. Card unused for 60 to 90 days, spend falling, balance transfer out. Win-back and activation campaigns.
    • Deposit retention. Maturing term deposits and rate-sensitive balances: who will roll over, who is likely to move, and what rate it takes. Highly relevant for liquidity planning too (B5).
    • Digital adoption and cross-sell. App activation, savings goals, insurance, personal finance: propensity plus contact policy.
    • Mortgage and loan refinancing risk. Customers likely to refinance elsewhere. Pricing and prepayment are tied to the lending lessons in B4.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Churn prediction is a mature, unfashionable corner: the best results come from careful definitions and good features rather than new architectures. The movement is in uplift and causal methods becoming a standard toolkit, and in decision-focused, sequential approaches to action selection.

    Checked from memory and prior reading, not by fresh searches in this session, so treat the 2024 to 2026 statements as a direction rather than citations. The dated items below are published work.

    1. 2006
      Ensembles for churn

      Early evidence that bagging and boosting beat single trees and logistic regression on customer churn. The start of treating churn as a tabular benchmark task.

      Lemmens & Croux, Journal of Marketing Research, 2006
    2. 2011
      Uplift modelling as a practice

      Radcliffe and Surry described uplift trees and the Qini measure for direct marketing, giving the field its name and its evaluation curve.

      Radcliffe & Surry, 2011; Radcliffe, 2007
    3. 2012
      Profit-driven churn evaluation

      Argued that models should be compared by the profit of the campaign they enable, with the cost of offers and the value of customers in the metric.

      Verbeke et al., European Journal of Operational Research, 2012
    4. 2012–18
      Uplift methods mature

      Information-theoretic uplift trees, then causal forests for heterogeneous treatment effects with valid confidence intervals.

      Rzepakowski & Jaroszewicz, 2012; Wager & Athey, JASA, 2018
    5. 2019–21
      Meta-learners and the R-learner

      A common language (S-, T-, X- and R-learners) for building heterogeneous-effect estimates out of ordinary machine-learning models.

      Künzel et al., PNAS, 2019; Nie & Wager, Biometrika, 2021
    6. 2018–19
      Public benchmarks for uplift

      A large randomised advertising dataset (the Criteo uplift data) and a systematic comparison of methods found that no single approach dominates, and that evaluation metrics and data size matter as much as the algorithm.

      Diemert et al., 2018; Devriendt, Moldovan & Verbeke, Big Data, 2018
    7. Now
      Decisions, not scores

      Practice is moving from "score, then threshold" towards policy learning: choose actions to maximise long-run value with constraints, using offline evaluation of logged decisions plus a small randomised exploration share. Open-source toolkits (CausalML, EconML) have made the estimators routine. Language models are being used around the edges (summarising call notes, drafting offers, extracting complaint reasons as features) rather than as the churn predictor itself. I have not verified specific bank deployments, which are rarely published.

      Direction of travel; see CausalML and EconML documentation

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A churn definition that measures something else

      "Inactive for 90 days" quietly predicts low-frequency customers, or expatriates who have left the country.

      Spot itTake a sample flagged as churned and check what happened a year later. If a large share are still active or the accounts were closed for non-retainable reasons, the label is wrong.
    • Leakage through the outcome window

      A feature such as "balance last month" computed after the snapshot. AUC of 0.97 for a churn model.

      Spot itBe suspicious of any churn AUC above about 0.9. Check the date on every feature against the snapshot date.
    • Predicting too late to act

      The model is excellent at 7 days. By the time the call is made the money has gone.

      Spot itAsk for the action lag and the horizon in the same sentence. Compute how many flagged customers would have left within the lag.
    • Calling the persuadable and the doomed alike

      Campaign reports show a "save rate" among the contacted. Many would have stayed anyway.

      Spot itAsk for the control group's retention. A save rate without a randomised control is not evidence.
    • A holdout that is not random

      Control customers are the ones with no valid phone number, or the ones the relationship managers skipped.

      Spot itCompare the two groups on pre-campaign behaviour. If they differ before treatment, the estimated uplift is partly selection.
    • Optimising propensity and expecting incremental sales

      The cross-sell model ranks people who already intend to buy. Take-up is high; incremental take-up is small.

      Spot itLook at conversion in the untreated high-propensity group. If it is nearly as high as in the treated one, the campaign adds little.
    • The feedback loop

      The model learns from outcomes of the customers it chose to contact, so it keeps choosing the same type and never learns about the rest.

      Spot itCheck whether any decisions are randomised. If every action is chosen by the model, you have no way to learn what else would have worked.
    • Over-reading an uplift model

      A small experiment, a flexible model, and a "top decile uplift of 12 points" that is noise.

      Spot itAsk for the size of the experiment, intervals on decile effects, and a result on a fresh holdout.
    • Discounts that train customers to threaten

      Retention offers given to those who complain teach everyone to complain, and lower margin.

      Spot itWatch the rate of complaints or closure requests after launch, and net margin, not only the save count.
    • Compliance blind spots

      Using a data source or channel not covered by the customer's consent, or pushing a product that fails suitability.

      Spot itAsk where consent and suitability filters sit in the pipeline, and who signed off the data used for features.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “A churn score tells us who is likely to leave. It does not tell us whom a call will keep. Some of the top-risk customers have already gone in their heads, and some of the low-risk ones would be annoyed by a call. I'd keep a random group out of every campaign, measure what the campaign really changes, and then aim at the customers where the change is worth more than the cost of the contact. That is how we move from a model that looks good to a campaign that pays.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd define churn at relationship level from salary and balance migration, validated against closure, and exclude non-retainable exits. Snapshot dataset with a 90-day outcome window and strict point-in-time features, chosen horizon against the action lag. Gradient boosting baseline, evaluated forward in time on precision and lift at the contact budget, calibrated, ranked by probability times value at risk. For targeting I'd train a T-learner or causal forest on a randomised holdout, evaluate with Qini curves and intervals, and keep a standing exploration share so the next model has data to learn from.”
    “Our churn model has an AUC of 0.88. Why did the retention campaign barely move attrition?”

    AUC measures ranking, not whether acting changes the outcome. Several things could be wrong: the model predicts too late for the action lag; the top of the list is full of lost causes or customers who would stay anyway; or the offer does not work. I'd check precision at the contact budget, then look at the randomised control group. If there is no control group, we cannot say the campaign did anything.

    “What's the difference between a propensity model and an uplift model?”

    A propensity model predicts the chance of an outcome, such as buying or leaving. An uplift model predicts the change in that chance caused by our action. The first finds people likely to do something; the second finds people whose behaviour we can move. The first can be built from observational data; the second needs randomised treatment and control groups.

    “How do you define churn for a current account?”

    I would not use a single inactivity window. I'd use an early commercial signal, such as salary credits stopped and balance down by a set fraction, validated against account closure over the next year. I'd compare silence to the customer's own usual gap, and separate customers who left the country or the employer, because those exits are real but not retainable.

    “Why not just predict churn 12 months ahead? More notice, surely.”

    Because the signal fades. A year out, little in today's behaviour separates the future leavers from the stayers, so the list is mostly wrong. Notice only helps if the model is right. We choose a horizon from the action lag: far enough that the customer is still reachable, near enough that behaviour has started to change. In practice that is often one to three months for current accounts, but it should be tested.

    “Can we run uplift modelling on last year's campaign data?”

    Only if the contacted and not-contacted customers were chosen at random. If the team called whoever looked at risk, the groups differ for reasons we cannot adjust away, and the model would learn who got called, not who responds. Without randomisation I'd start a holdout now, and in the meantime use the history for risk modelling only.

    “Is it ethical to give better offers only to customers who threaten to leave?”

    It is a legitimate commercial practice, but it has fairness and conduct implications: loyal customers pay more, and if the pattern correlates with protected characteristics, there is a problem. I'd check offers across groups, make sure rules are consistent and explainable, and involve compliance and the product owner. I would not state the legal position without them.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A customer makes about 1.5 card transactions a month at random. What is the chance of a 60-day silence by pure chance?

    e−λd/30 with λ = 1.5 and d = 60 gives e−3 ≈ 5%. About one in twenty such customers will be wrongly called churned by a 60-day rule even though nothing has changed.

    2. A model flags 5,000 customers, of whom 400 will leave within 90 days. 100,000 customers have a 3% churn rate overall. What are precision, recall and lift?

    Precision = 400 ÷ 5,000 = 8%. Total leavers = 3,000, so recall = 400 ÷ 3,000 ≈ 13%. Lift = 8% ÷ 3% ≈ 2.7 times the base rate. A useful model, though not spectacular, and a reminder of how little of churn is predictable.

    3. Why is a 7-day prediction horizon a bad idea even if its AUC is 0.95?

    If acting takes two weeks, every flagged customer will have left before any action lands. The model is accurate about what can no longer be changed. Useful recall = recall × (h − L) ÷ h, which is zero when h ≤ L.

    4. Customers are in four groups: persuadable, sure thing, lost cause, sleeping dog. Which does a churn model rank highest, and which should you call?

    A churn model ranks lost causes highest, then persuadables, then sleeping dogs, then sure things last. You want to call persuadables only. Sure things and lost causes waste the contact and sleeping dogs are harmed by it.

    5. An offer costs AED 40 to make. A retained customer is worth AED 5,000. Which uplift justifies contact?

    Contact when τ × 5,000 > 40, so τ > 0.8 percentage points. A customer whose chance of staying rises by less than 0.8 points is not worth contacting, however high their risk.

    6. You have only observational data where relationship managers chose whom to call. Can you estimate uplift?

    Not reliably. The managers' choices correlate with things the model cannot see, so the estimated effect mixes the action with the selection. Some adjustment methods exist if you can argue every driver of the decision is measured, but it is a strong assumption. The clean path is a randomised holdout.

    7. The T-learner's predicted uplifts look wildly noisy. What are two sensible responses?

    Get more experimental data, since uplift needs large samples. And use a method that estimates the effect directly with regularisation (causal forest, R-learner) or a simpler model, or shrink estimates toward the average. Also aggregate: rank by predicted uplift, then check treated-versus-control differences by decile on fresh data.
