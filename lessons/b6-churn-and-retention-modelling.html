    Churn, Propensity and Uplift: Who Leaves, Who to Call, and Whether the Call Changes Anything

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank customer almost never says goodbye. Like a gym member who stopped turning up, they left months before the account closes: the salary moves, card use drifts, the balance falls to a few hundred dirhams.

    So retention is two jobs: spot leaving early enough to act, and decide whom to call.

    Say you can make 5,000 calls and a model ranks 100,000 customers by chance of leaving. The obvious plan is to call the top 5,000. But who is in that list?

    • Lost causes have already signed up elsewhere. A call changes nothing.
    • Persuadables are at risk and would respond to a better rate or fee waiver. They are the only people worth the call.
    • Sure things will stay whatever you do. Calling them wastes money.
    • Sleeping dogs react badly. The call reminds them to compare fees, and they leave.

    A churn model sees risk, like a smoke alarm that signals heat but not whether the fire brigade can help. It never learned what happens when you intervene, so it cannot tell the four groups apart. Uplift modelling closes that gap.

    Hold onto thisPrediction asks "what happens to this customer if we do nothing?" Decisions need "what happens if we act versus do nothing?" That is a causal question (F9.3). Most retention programmes stop at the first and wonder why the campaign disappoints.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Defining churn when nobody says goodbye (B6.1)

    Current accounts and cards have no end date, so churn has to be defined from behaviour, and the definition shapes everything after it. Three common choices:

    • Account closure. Clean and certain, but late. The money has already left.
    • Inactivity. No transactions for a set number of days. Easy, but it mislabels customers who simply transact rarely.
    • Balance or salary migration. Balance down by over half, or salary credit stopped. Early and commercially meaningful.

    In the Gulf, many retail customers are expatriates on employment visas. When the job ends the account goes dormant. That is real attrition but not retainable, and a model that cannot separate it wastes calls. A salary switch is among the strongest early signs.

    The inactivity trap: a heavy card user is almost never silent for 30 days by chance, but an occasional user (0.4 transactions a month) is silent that long about two times in three while perfectly happy. One fixed window is too jumpy for the second and too slow for the first. Compare silence with the customer's own usual gap instead, for example flag at 4 times that gap. The first simulation shows both.

    A model trained to predict "silent for 90 days" partly learns to find rare transactors: AUC looks fine, usefulness is poor. Check the label against something firmer (closure within a year, salary gone) before building on it.

    Windows, snapshots and lead time (B6.1)

    Churn modelling is a snapshot problem, like judging a runner from their splits so far, then checking the finish. Pick a date. Describe each customer using only what was known on or before it, and label them by what happens in the next 90 days or so. A feature computed after the date, such as "balance at month-end after churn", leaks the answer (F3.1).

    The strongest features are changes, not static traits like age: transactions this quarter against last, balance trend, salary credits stopping, a new complaint, falling logins.

    How far ahead should the model look? Predict 7 days out and it is accurate, because the customer is already half out of the door, but useless, because acting takes time. That time is the action lag: a manager has to call, an offer has to be approved. Anyone who leaves within the lag cannot be saved. Look 180 days out and the signal is weak. The sweet spot sits between, and your action lag sets it as much as your data does. Useful recall is the share of eventual leavers you catch and still have time to reach. It peaks in the middle.

    Two rules follow. Choose the horizon from the action lag, not from what flatters AUC. And report precision and recall at the contact budget: a retention team works only the top few percent (F7.1 to F7.4, and imbalanced data).

    Modelling choices, and judging a churn model by money (B6.1)

    Modelling is the least surprising part. A gradient-boosted tree (F4.3) on snapshot features is the strong baseline; logistic regression (F4.2) is the interpretable one. Two extras:

    • Survival models (F2.8) model time to leaving, handle customers who have not churned yet, and let you pick the horizon later.
    • Segment first (B6.3). Different customer types leave for different reasons, so one model per segment usually beats one global model.

    Judge the model by money, not just AUC:

    • Lift at the contact budget. "The top 5% has 6 times the base churn rate" is a sentence an executive understands. Lift 1 means no better than random.
    • Calibration (F7.4). If a score of 0.2 means 20% of such customers leave, you can multiply by value at risk and add up.
    • Value-weighted ranking. A customer with AED 400 and one with AED 400,000 do not deserve equal attention. Rank by probability times value at risk (B6.3).

    Test on later snapshots than you trained on (F4.5), since drivers shift with pricing and competitors. And SHAP reasons (F8.2) explain the score, not the cause: "salary credits stopped" is a signal you cannot fix; "fee increased last month" might be a cause you can.

    Propensity and next-best-action (B6.2)

    A propensity model is the same machinery pointed at another outcome: the probability a customer takes an action, such as buying a product or activating a card. Churn is propensity to leave.

    Next-best-action (NBA) is the layer above, like a waiter who picks one suggestion per table rather than reading out the whole menu. For each customer it:

    1. Filters out anything ineligible, unsuitable, or without marketing consent.
    2. Scores each remaining action by expected value, net of contact cost and the cost of annoying the customer.
    3. Applies contact limits: message caps, channel preferences, call-centre capacity.
    4. Logs what was shown and what happened.

    If you always send the model's favourite, you never learn how the others would have done. The fix is to randomise a small share of decisions (the bandit idea, F9.7).

    The weakness: a customer with a high propensity to take a savings product would probably have done it anyway. Targeting by propensity spends the budget on people who need persuading least, and the campaign looks successful because you picked people who were going to convert.

    Uplift: modelling the change, not the outcome (B6.2)

    Each customer has two possible outcomes: contacted and not contacted. The difference is the uplift. If a 70% chance of staying becomes 74% after a call, uplift is 4 points. You never see both outcomes for one person, so you estimate it by comparing alike groups.

    The decision rule is simple: contact when uplift times the value of a retained customer beats the cost of contact. Negative uplift (a sleeping dog) is never contacted, however high the risk. High uplift does not mean high risk.

    The data must come from a randomised experiment with a random holdout. Otherwise the contacted differ from the rest in ways the model cannot see, and "uplift" is selection bias (F2.6) in disguise. Common estimators:

    • Two models (T-learner). Fit one on the treated, one on controls; uplift is the difference of predictions. A sound first try, though noisy.
    • One model with treatment as a feature (S-learner). It can shrink the effect to zero if the tree never splits on the treatment flag.
    • Uplift trees and causal forests split to maximise the difference between treated and control, and give confidence intervals.
    from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier as GB

# 'treated' is RANDOMISED
m1 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 1], df.retained[df.treated == 1])
m0 = GB().fit(X[df.treated == 0], df.retained[df.treated == 0])
uplift = m1.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] - m0.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

    No customer has a known uplift, so evaluation uses the Qini curve: rank by predicted uplift, then plot cumulative extra retained customers (treated minus control) as you contact more. A good model rises steeply, then flattens or falls.

    Three lessons sit in that picture. The uplift ranking differs from the churn ranking: the most at-risk group is not the best to call. Past a point, calling more hurts, by wasting money or reaching customers who react badly. The budget is separate from the model: call until the gain drops below the cost of a call.

    Real effects are small (a few points at best), so you need large experiments (F2.1, F2.7). And a model is specific to the action it learned from: a fee-waiver experiment says nothing about a phone call.

    Constraints that sit above the model (B6.2)

    A few things are fixed before the model gets a say. Build them into the system rather than leaving them to the campaign team. Contact fatigue belongs here too: caps and cool-offs go in the objective.

    • Consent and data use. Marketing contact and profiling need a lawful basis (the UAE and Saudi PDPLs, plus CBUAE and SAMA conduct rules). Check with compliance.
    • Suitability. Offering high-cost borrowing to someone stretched is a conduct risk, however high the propensity. Suitability comes before ranking, and Sharia-compliant products have their own eligibility rules.
    • Fairness. Retention offers are an economic benefit. Check the pattern does not track nationality, gender or another sensitive attribute, including through proxies such as postcode or employer (G2).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • A churn definition that measures something else

      "Inactive for 90 days" quietly predicts low-frequency customers or expatriates who left the country.

      Spot itCheck flagged customers a year later. If many are still active or closed for non-retainable reasons, the label is wrong.
    • Leakage through the outcome window

      A feature like "balance last month" computed after the snapshot gives an AUC of 0.97.

      Spot itDistrust any churn AUC above about 0.9. Check every feature's date against the snapshot date.
    • Predicting too late to act

      Excellent at 7 days, but the money has gone by the time the call is made.

      Spot itAsk for action lag and horizon together.
    • No real control group

      A "save rate" among the contacted includes many who would have stayed anyway, and a holdout of customers managers skipped is not random.

      Spot itAsk for the control group's retention, and compare groups on pre-campaign behaviour. A gap before treatment means selection.
    • Optimising propensity and the feedback loop

      The model ranks people who already intend to buy, learns only from the customers it chose, and keeps choosing the same type. Take-up is high; incremental take-up is small.

      Spot itCheck conversion in the untreated high-propensity group, and whether any decisions are randomised.
    • Over-reading an uplift model

      A small experiment and flexible model give a "top decile uplift of 12 points" that is noise.

      Spot itAsk for experiment size, intervals on decile effects, and a fresh-holdout result.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A customer makes about 1.5 card transactions a month at random. Is a 60-day silence strong evidence that they have left?

    No. By pure chance about one such customer in twenty goes silent for 60 days with nothing changed, so a 60-day rule wrongly calls them churned. Rarer transactors are worse.

    2. A model flags 5,000 customers, of whom 400 will leave within 90 days. Overall churn among 100,000 customers is 3%. Is this good?

    Precision is 400 ÷ 5,000 = 8%, against a 3% base rate: lift of about 2.7 times. Recall is 400 ÷ 3,000 ≈ 13%. Useful, not spectacular.

    3. Why is a 7-day prediction horizon a bad idea even if its AUC is 0.95?

    If acting takes two weeks, every flagged customer leaves before any action lands. The prediction is accurate and worthless.

    4. Customers are in four groups: persuadable, sure thing, lost cause, sleeping dog. Which does a churn model rank highest, and which should you call?

    A churn model ranks lost causes highest, then persuadables, then sleeping dogs, with sure things last. Call persuadables only: contacting the others wastes money or does harm.

    5. An offer costs AED 40 to make and a retained customer is worth AED 5,000. How do you decide whether to contact someone, and why might you stop long before running out of high-risk customers?

    Contact only when expected gain (uplift times AED 5,000) beats AED 40, which needs an uplift of about 0.8 percentage points. Risk alone does not matter. Once the remaining customers fall below that uplift, further calls lose money.

    6. You have only observational data where relationship managers chose whom to call. Can you estimate uplift?

    Not reliably. Their choices correlate with things the model cannot see, so the estimate mixes the action with the selection. The clean path is a randomised holdout.

    7. The T-learner's predicted uplifts look wildly noisy. What are two sensible responses?

    Get more experimental data, since uplift needs large samples, and use a method that estimates the effect directly with regularisation (causal forest) or shrinks toward the average. Also aggregate: rank by predicted uplift, then check treated-versus-control differences by decile on fresh data.
