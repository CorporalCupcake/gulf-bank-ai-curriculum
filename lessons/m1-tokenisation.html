    Tokens and the Transformer: How an LLM Reads, Thinks and Writes

    Why Arabic can cost twice the tokens of English, why a model that writes a good essay cannot count the letters in a word, and the whole journey from text to token to vector to attention to the next word.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn LLM never sees text: it sees integer ids from a fixed vocabulary, turns each into a vector, lets the vectors read each other through attention, and outputs a probability for every possible next id, so cost, context, language quality and many odd failures all trace back to how text was cut into tokens.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank's innovation team pilots an assistant that summarises customer emails. In English the pilot costs a few thousand dirhams a month. They switch on Arabic, same volume, same summary length, and the bill is close to double. Nobody changed the model. The text was simply cut into more pieces.

    A model does not read letters or words. Before anything else, a program called the tokeniser chops text into pieces from a fixed list of, typically, 100,000 to 250,000 pieces (the vocabulary). Each piece has an id number. "transfer" might be one piece. A rarer word might be three. The model's whole world is a stream of these ids. You pay per token, the context window is measured in tokens, and speed depends on tokens.

    Then comes the second half of the story. Each id is looked up in a table and becomes a vector of a few thousand numbers (F1.1). A stack of identical layers, the transformer, lets each vector gather information from the vectors before it using attention (F5.3 introduced it). At the end, the last vector is turned into a score for every token in the vocabulary. The scores become probabilities. One token is chosen, appended, and the whole thing runs again. That loop is all of text generation.

    Three ideas to hold.

    • The tokeniser is learned from data, once, and then frozen. It gives short pieces to text that was common in its training sample and many small pieces to text that was rare. English was common. Arabic, especially dialects and fully vowelled text, was less so. That is a cost story and a quality story.
    • The model is a very large next-token predictor. Everything it seems to "know" or "reason" is produced by repeatedly predicting a plausible next piece given everything so far. Understanding how that is computed removes much of the mystery, and explains many failure modes.
    • Attention is the only place tokens talk to each other. Everything else in a layer works on one position at a time. That is why context length, memory and cost behave the way they do, and why M1.3 (context windows and the KV cache) matters.
    Hold onto thisWhen a model does something odd with text (miscounts letters, garbles a long number, costs more in Arabic, loses the middle of a long document) ask first: what did the tokeniser hand it, and what can attention reach? Most "mysteries" are answered there.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Byte-pair encoding: how a vocabulary is learned

    The obvious choices both fail. If the vocabulary is whole words, any new word (a product name, a typo, an Emirati surname) has no id. If it is single characters, every sentence becomes very long, and long sequences are expensive. The compromise used by almost every modern LLM is subword tokenisation, most often byte-pair encoding (BPE), borrowed from a 1994 compression idea and brought to language models by Sennrich and colleagues in 2016.

    The training procedure is short enough to say in four lines.

    1. Start with every word cut into single characters (or bytes). The vocabulary is just those characters.
    2. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across the whole training text, weighted by how often each word occurs.
    3. Take the most frequent pair, say "t" followed by "h", and add a new symbol "th" to the vocabulary. Rewrite the corpus using it. Record the merge.
    4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the size you want. Each loop adds one entry.

    To tokenise new text, you replay the recorded merges in the same order. Nothing is "understood": it is frequency counting. Frequent strings end up as single tokens ("the", " transfer", " account"). Rare strings stay in pieces. In practice there are details: the text is first split by a pattern so merges do not cross word boundaries, spaces are attached to the following word, and the base alphabet is the 256 possible bytes rather than characters, so any input at all can be encoded and nothing is ever "unknown". Variants differ in these details: WordPiece (used by BERT) picks merges by likelihood rather than raw count, and the Unigram model in SentencePiece prunes down from a large vocabulary instead of building up.

    Notice what the slider did. Merges are a budget. Each one is spent on whatever is most frequent in the training sample. If English dominates the sample, English wins the early merges, and Arabic words are still pieces when the budget is spent. A bigger vocabulary helps, which is why vocabularies grew: GPT-2 had about 50,000 entries, Llama 2 32,000, and recent models 100,000 to 250,000 (check the exact figure for any model you use, it is in the model card or tokeniser file).

    Why Arabic costs more, and what it does to money, context and speed

    Several things stack up.

    • Training mix. As shown above, the tokeniser spends its merges where the data is. Researchers measure this with fertility: the average number of tokens per word. A good fit gives English near 1.3 tokens per word. Studies of commercial and open tokenisers have found large gaps between languages, in some cases several times (Ahia et al., 2023; Petrov et al., 2023). A March 2026 study of Arabic tokenisers uses fertility as a proxy for both cost and quality problems and found one widely used OpenAI tokeniser had the highest fertility on Arabic among those tested. Models built for Arabic, such as Jais, use a tokeniser trained on Arabic and have the lowest fertility in comparisons (Arabic Stable LM report).
    • Morphology. Arabic words carry a lot. A single written word can be "and they will write it" (a conjunction, a tense marker, a verb stem built on a three-letter root, and a pronoun attached). The same meaning in English is four words. So per word comparisons mislead: compare tokens for the same message, as the simulation does.
    • Bytes. In UTF-8, English letters take 1 byte and Arabic letters 2. When a tokeniser has not learned an Arabic string, it falls back to bytes, so each letter costs about two tokens rather than one. Optional vowel marks (tashkeel) add more characters. Dialect spellings, Arabizi (Arabic in Latin letters with numbers, such as "3" for ع) and code-switching between English and Arabic mid-sentence are rarer still in training text.

    The consequence reaches three places at once. Money: you pay per token. Context: the window is counted in tokens, so an Arabic document fills it faster, which matters for retrieval (M3.3, chunk sizes in tokens, not characters). Compute and speed: twice the tokens means about twice the generation time and, for the attention part, up to four times the compute on the input, since attention compares every token with every other (Part 2, attention). Quality can also suffer: when a word is shredded into byte-like pieces the model has to reassemble meaning from fragments it saw rarely.

    What to do about it? Measure first: count tokens of a few hundred real bank documents in each language with the tokeniser of each candidate model, and put the ratio in the business case. Then the levers are: choose a model whose tokeniser handles Arabic well (M2.3), trim the prompt, cache repeated instructions, retrieve less but better (M3), or, for open models, extend the vocabulary with Arabic tokens and continue training. A 2026 preprint on a small Arabic model reports about 17% fewer tokens per word after adding Arabic tokens to an existing vocabulary, though from a very small test sample (see Part 4). Arabic-first models are covered in A14.

    What tokens explain: miscounted letters, numbers and odd words

    "How many r's in strawberry?" The model does not receive ten letters. It receives a few ids, for example a piece like "straw" and a piece like "berry" (the exact split depends on the tokeniser). Inside the model, each id is a vector. Whether that vector carries the spelling of its piece is something the model had to learn indirectly, from the few places in the training text where words are spelled out. So counting letters is a task about information the input format hides. Models do better when asked to spell the word out letter by letter first (each letter then becomes its own token), or when they can call a tool; reasoning models (A5.1) often do this on their own. The same logic explains trouble with reversing strings, rhyming and acrostics.

    Numbers. A long figure such as 12,345,678.90 may be cut into inconsistent chunks, so digit alignment, which arithmetic needs, is hard to learn. Some tokenisers split numbers into single digits or fixed groups for exactly this reason. For banking, the rule is simple: never let a model do the arithmetic that matters in its head. Have it call a calculator or write code, and check the output.

    Whitespace, case and formatting. "Account", " account" and "ACCOUNT" are different tokens with different vectors. A prompt that changes spacing can change behaviour a little. Indented code and tables use many tokens.

    Glitch tokens. A token can enter the vocabulary because it was frequent in the tokeniser's data (a Reddit username, say) yet be almost absent from the model's training data. Its vector is then barely trained and the model behaves strangely when it appears (Rumbelow and Watkins, 2023). It is a reminder that the tokeniser and the model are trained separately and can be mismatched.

    Security angle. Because filters and models can tokenise the same text differently, unusual spacing, look-alike Unicode characters and mixed scripts can slip past keyword guardrails (see A3 on injection and G1). Normalise and test text in the form the model sees.

    import tiktoken                      # OpenAI's open tokeniser library
enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
ids = enc.encode("حول المبلغ الى حسابي")
print(len(ids), [enc.decode([i]) for i in ids])
# Compare len(ids) with the English equivalent. Other vendors publish
# their own token counters; use the one for the model you will pay for.

    Embeddings: from id to vector, and where order comes from

    The first layer of the model is a lookup table with one row per vocabulary entry. With a 128,000-token vocabulary and 4,096 numbers per row, that table alone holds about 525 million numbers. Token id 4,127 is replaced by row 4,127: a vector. These vectors are learned during training, starting random, and end up arranged so that tokens used in similar ways sit near each other. This is the geometry of F1.1 and F1.2 at work: directions come to mean things, and distance means similarity. It is also the same idea as in the embedding models used for search (M3), although those are trained for a different goal.

    One problem: attention, as we are about to see, treats its inputs as an unordered set. "Bank approved customer" and "customer approved bank" would look the same. Order has to be injected. The original transformer added fixed sine and cosine patterns to the vectors. Most current models instead use rotary position embeddings (RoPE), which rotate the query and key vectors by an angle that depends on position, so that attention scores depend on the distance between two tokens. How well a model copes with positions beyond those it trained on is a big part of the long-context story (M1.3).

    Attention: letting tokens read each other

    After embedding, "bank" is the same vector in "river bank" and "bank account". Attention is how context changes that. For every position, the model computes three vectors from its current vector by multiplying with learned matrices: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I hand over if chosen?). Position i scores every position j by comparing its query with j's key (a dot product, F1.1), turns the scores into weights that add to one (softmax), and returns the weighted average of the values. The result replaces its vector. In symbols:

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V

    Each row of QKT holds one token's scores against all tokens. Dividing by √dk (the key length) stops the scores growing with vector size, which would push softmax into near one-hot, saturated weights that train badly. Softmax makes each row positive and sum to one. Multiplying by V takes the weighted average of values. For a prompt of n tokens the score matrix has n² entries, which is the source of the quadratic cost.

    Two refinements matter. First, multi-head: the model runs many attention computations side by side (a few dozen), each with its own matrices, so different heads can track different relations (a verb's subject, the previous token, a matching bracket) and their outputs are joined. Second, the causal mask: in a model that generates text, position i may only attend to positions up to i. Otherwise it could look at the answer. The next simulation shows both what attention does and why the mask matters.

    The transformer block, stacked

    A transformer layer is attention plus a second component and some plumbing. Per position:

    1. Normalise the vector (layer norm or RMSNorm) to keep numbers in a stable range.
    2. Attention mixes in information from earlier positions. Its output is added to the original vector, not substituted. This residual connection means each layer makes a small edit to a running message, and gradients flow straight back through the stack when training (F1.3).
    3. Normalise again, then a feed-forward network (MLP), which works on each position separately: expand the vector (commonly to about four times its width, or a gated variant such as SwiGLU), apply a nonlinearity, shrink it back. Added to the running vector again. Roughly two thirds of the parameters of a dense model live here, and research on interpretability suggests this is where much stored factual knowledge sits (R5 on features and superposition).

    Stack that block 24 to 100 or so times with different weights in each. The original 2017 transformer had 6 layers of width 512; a model of around 8 billion parameters has about 32 layers of width 4,096 (from published model cards, check yours). A handy rule: one block has roughly 12 × width² parameters (4 for attention, 8 for the MLP), so width 4,096 gives about 200 million per layer; 32 layers is about 6.4 billion, plus the embedding tables, which gets you near the headline figure. Early layers tend to build local, surface features such as grammar; later layers build more abstract ones. Interpretability work (Elhage et al., 2021) describes the residual stream as a shared channel that attention heads and MLPs read from and write to.

    The output: probabilities, sampling and the generation loop

    After the last layer, the vector at the final position is multiplied by a matrix with one row per vocabulary entry (often the embedding table reused). That gives one raw score, a logit, per token. Softmax turns the logits into a probability distribution over the next token. With a temperature T:

    pi=exp(zi/T)∑jexp(zj/T)

    zi is the logit of token i. At T = 1 this is the model's own distribution. Below 1 the distribution sharpens towards the top token; as T approaches 0 it becomes "always pick the top one" (greedy). Above 1 it flattens. Example: logits 2, 1, 0 give probabilities 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0% at T = 1, but 86.7%, 11.7%, 1.6% at T = 0.5.

    Then one token is picked. Pure sampling from the full distribution sometimes selects a very unlikely token, which can derail the sentence. Two common guards: top-k (keep only the k most likely tokens) and top-p, or nucleus sampling (keep the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p; Holtzman et al., 2020). Both then renormalise and sample. The chosen token is appended to the input and the model runs again for the next one. Training used the same machinery, with the right next token known: the loss is the cross-entropy of F1.7, the negative log probability the model gave to the actual next token (F6.1).

    The loop in code, stripped to the idea (real systems add the KV cache so that earlier positions are not recomputed, the subject of M1.3):

    ids = tokenizer.encode(prompt)                 # text -> integers
while True:
    logits = model(ids)[-1]                    # scores for the next token only
    probs  = softmax(logits / temperature)     # optionally apply top-k / top-p
    nxt    = sample(probs)                     # pick one id
    ids.append(nxt)                            # feed it back in
    if nxt == tokenizer.eos_id: break
text = tokenizer.decode(ids)                   # integers -> text

    Even at temperature 0, outputs from hosted models are not always identical from run to run: batching and floating-point order can change tiny numbers, and that can flip a near-tie. For regulated work, record the model version and settings, and test for stability rather than assume it.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The basic recipe, a subword tokeniser plus a decoder-only transformer, is the same one that powered the first GPT models, with many refinements. The live arguments are whether we can get rid of tokenisers altogether, how to make vocabularies fairer across languages, and which parts of the transformer to replace for long and cheap inference.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026. Searches were light (two); items marked "from memory" were not re-checked.

    1. 2016
      BPE comes to neural language models

      Sennrich and colleagues applied byte-pair encoding to translation so that rare words could be built from known pieces. It became the default idea in LLM tokenisers.

      Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, ACL 2016
    2. 2017
      The transformer

      Attention replaced recurrence, which allowed training in parallel across a sequence and scaling to billions of parameters. The core block described in Part 2 has survived largely intact.

      Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017
    3. 2018
      SentencePiece

      A tokeniser that works on raw text with no language-specific pre-splitting, supporting BPE and the Unigram model. Used by many open models.

      Kudo & Richardson, EMNLP 2018
    4. 2023
      Tokenisers and language fairness

      Two studies measured how the same text costs different amounts across languages in commercial and open tokenisers, with large gaps, and argued this is a fairness and cost issue because users pay per token and get shorter effective context.

      Ahia et al., EMNLP 2023; Petrov et al., NeurIPS 2023
    5. 2024
      Tokenizer-free models: Byte Latent Transformer

      Meta researchers group raw bytes into dynamically sized patches (longer for predictable text, shorter for surprising text) and report matching tokeniser-based models at up to 8 billion parameters, with some efficiency and robustness gains. The released weights are research-licensed and, when checked, no inference provider hosted them, so this remains research rather than production.

      Pagnoni et al., arXiv 2412.09871; Hugging Face model card
    6. 2026
      Arabic tokenisers under the microscope

      "Morphemes Without Borders" evaluates how well Arabic tokenisers respect root-and-pattern morphology, uses fertility as a cost and quality proxy, and reports that alignment with morphology does not by itself predict good generation. A May 2026 preprint on a sub-1B Arabic model reports about 17% fewer tokens per word after injecting 27,032 Arabic tokens into an existing vocabulary, on a 368-word sample only.

      arXiv 2603.15773; arXiv 2605.28827 (preprint, small test)
    7. 2023–26
      Architecture refinements (from memory)

      Rotary positions (RoPE), grouped-query attention (fewer key/value heads, smaller cache), RMSNorm, gated MLPs, FlashAttention kernels and sparse mixture-of-experts layers are now standard in leading open models. Vocabularies have grown (Llama 3 uses about 128,000 entries; some models use 250,000 or more), partly to improve non-English efficiency. I did not re-verify per-model figures; check the model card.

      Su et al. 2021; Ainslie et al. 2023; Dao et al. 2022

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor proposals, pilots and AI-written business cases. Each comes with a quick check.

    • Costing Arabic with English token ratios

      A business case budgets "one token per 0.75 English words" for Arabic traffic, and the bill arrives double.

      Spot itAsk which tokeniser and which sample of Arabic text the estimate came from. Re-count a few hundred real messages.
    • Comparing "per word" across languages

      "Arabic uses 2.6 tokens per word against 1.3." Arabic words carry more meaning, so the gap per message differs.

      Spot itAsk for tokens for the same translated message, not per word.
    • Trusting letter counts, character limits and exact string tasks

      An assistant "checks" that an IBAN has 23 characters or counts digits and is wrong.

      Spot itAny rule on exact characters belongs in code. Test the model on a few hundred cases to see how often it fails.
    • Letting the model do the arithmetic

      Sums, percentages and currency conversions produced in prose.

      Spot itLook for a calculator tool or code step. Recompute five outputs by hand.
    • Chunking and limits in characters

      A retrieval pipeline cuts 2,000-character chunks; Arabic ones overflow the embedding model's token limit and are silently truncated.

      Spot itLog the token count of every chunk against the model's limit.
    • Guardrails that do not see what the model sees

      A keyword filter passes text with look-alike characters or odd spacing that the model reads perfectly well.

      Spot itRed-team with Unicode variants, diacritics, zero-width characters and Arabizi.
    • Setting temperature to 0 and calling it deterministic

      A regulated process assumes identical output every run.

      Spot itRun the same input 50 times; record the version and settings. Pin the model version.
    • Switching tokenisers or models and keeping old limits

      A prompt that fit before is truncated after a model upgrade because the new tokeniser counts differently.

      Spot itRe-measure token counts after any model change and check the gateway's limits.
    • Explaining the model as "understanding words"

      It predicts tokens. A confident, fluent answer is not evidence of correct content.

      Spot itAsk what was verified against a source, not how good the prose sounds.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These models read and are billed in small chunks called tokens, and the chunking was built mostly around English. The same message in Arabic can be close to twice as many tokens, so it costs more, fills the memory sooner and runs slower. I wouldn't quote you one budget for both languages. I'd measure a few hundred real messages with each candidate model, and we'd pick on cost per correct answer in Arabic, not the English price list.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Subword tokenisation, typically byte-level BPE with a vocabulary of 100 to 250 thousand, then embeddings with rotary positions, a stack of pre-norm blocks of causal multi-head attention and gated MLPs with residual connections, and a softmax over the vocabulary. Attention is quadratic in length, mitigated by KV caching and grouped-query attention. For Arabic I'd compare fertility on our own documents, test dialect and mixed-script inputs, and keep arithmetic and exact string checks outside the model.”
    “Why does Arabic cost more than English?”

    The tokeniser was learned from text where English dominated, so common English strings are single tokens while Arabic words are cut into more pieces. Arabic morphology (prefixes and suffixes attached to words) and two-byte characters in the fallback make it worse. How much worse depends on the model: Arabic-trained tokenisers narrow the gap. So the answer to “how much” is: measure it.

    “Why can't it count letters if it's so smart?”

    It never sees letters, only token ids that represent chunks of letters. Spelling is something it learns indirectly. It does better when it spells the word out first or uses a tool, which is why we move exact checks into code.

    “What is attention, simply?”

    Each word asks a question (query), every earlier word advertises what it offers (key), and the best matches hand over their content (value) in proportion to the match. It is a learned, weighted lookup that lets “bank” pick up that the sentence is about an account rather than a river.

    “Is it really just predicting the next word?”

    Mechanically yes: one token at a time, from a probability distribution. But to predict well across vast text, the model has to build internal representations of grammar, facts and procedures, so “just” undersells it. What it does not do is check its output against reality, which is why we add retrieval, tools and verification.

    “Is a bigger context window the fix for long documents?”

    Only partly. Longer windows cost more (attention grows with the square of length, and the cache grows with length), Arabic fills them faster, and models often use information in the middle of long inputs less reliably. Retrieval or summarising steps can be cheaper and more accurate. M1.3 covers this.

    “Should we fine-tune a tokenizer for Arabic?”

    Not as a first step. Changing a tokeniser means new embedding rows and continued pretraining, so it is a research-grade project. First try a model whose tokeniser is already efficient in Arabic, and compare quality as well as cost. Revisit only if volumes justify it and we run an open model in-house.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. You will process 10,000 Arabic documents of 500 words a month at 2.4 tokens per word. Input price is USD 3 per million tokens. What is the monthly input cost in AED (3.6725 per USD)?

    Tokens = 10,000 × 500 × 2.4 = 12 million. Cost = 12 × 3 = USD 36, and 36 × 3.6725 ≈ AED 132. The same documents at 1.3 tokens per word would be 6.5 million tokens, about AED 72.

    2. An Arabic prompt is 2 times longer in tokens than the English one. By roughly how much does the attention score computation grow, and how big is the KV cache compared with English?

    The score matrix has n² entries, so about 4 times the attention compute. The KV cache holds keys and values for each token, so it is about 2 times as large. The MLP and the embeddings scale linearly, so the whole forward pass grows by between 2 and 4 times depending on length.

    3. Logits are 2, 1, 0 for three tokens. What are the probabilities at temperature 1, and what happens at temperature 0.5?

    At T = 1: e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1, sum 11.107, so 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0%. At T = 0.5 the logits double to 4, 2, 0: 54.6, 7.39, 1, sum 63.0, giving 86.7%, 11.7%, 1.6%. Lower temperature sharpens the distribution without changing the order.

    4. Why is the dot product of query and key divided by √dk?

    A dot product of two vectors with dk random entries has a spread that grows like √dk. Without the division, large vectors give extreme scores, softmax goes near one-hot and gradients through it vanish. The division keeps scores at a stable scale.

    5. A model has a 128,000-token vocabulary and width 4,096. How many numbers are in its embedding table, and why does adding Arabic tokens to the vocabulary cost more than the table alone?

    128,000 × 4,096 ≈ 524 million (525 million for 128,256). Each added token needs a new embedding row and, usually, a new output row, but the real cost is that the new vectors start untrained, so you must continue pretraining on Arabic text for them to mean anything, and check the old abilities have not degraded.

    6. Why does a decoder-only model use a causal mask, and what is the downside for understanding a word from later context?

    During training, the model predicts every position in parallel; without the mask each position could read the very token it must predict. At generation time tokens do not exist yet. The downside, seen in Simulation 3: a word can only be refined by what came before it. Later words can use the earlier ones, so at the end of the sentence the information is present, but the vector for "bank" itself stays ambiguous. Encoder models used for classification and embeddings read both directions.

    7. Your chatbot passes tests in English but truncates some Arabic answers at the output limit. Give two likely causes and checks.

    The output limit is counted in tokens and Arabic needs more tokens per answer, so the same cap gives a shorter Arabic reply. Also the input prompt may fill part of the window. Check tokens used per request in each language against the caps, then raise the output limit for Arabic or shorten the prompt.

    8. A teammate says “set temperature to 0 and we get the same answer every time, so we can skip testing for consistency.” What is wrong?

    Temperature 0 picks the top token each step, but hosted systems may still vary slightly from batching and floating-point effects, and any change in model version or prompt changes everything. Consistency has to be measured with repeated runs and pinned versions.
