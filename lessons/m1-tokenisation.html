    Tokens and the Transformer: How an LLM Reads, Thinks and Writes

    Why Arabic can cost twice the tokens of English, why a model that writes a good essay cannot count the letters in a word, and the whole journey from text to token to vector to attention to the next word.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn LLM never sees text: it sees integer ids from a fixed vocabulary, turns each into a vector, lets the vectors read each other through attention, and outputs a probability for every possible next id. Cost, context, language quality and many odd failures trace back to how text was cut into tokens.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank pilots an assistant that summarises customer emails. In English it costs a few thousand dirhams a month. Switch to Arabic, same volume and summary length, and the bill nearly doubles. The model did not change. The text was cut into more pieces.

    Think of Lego. A model reads bricks, not letters or words. A tokeniser chops text into bricks from a fixed catalogue of 100,000 to 250,000 pieces (the vocabulary), each with an id number. You pay per token, the context window is measured in tokens, and speed depends on tokens.

    Then the model loops. Each id becomes a vector (F1.1). A stack of layers, the transformer, lets each vector gather information from earlier ones using attention (F5.3). The last vector becomes a probability for every token in the vocabulary, one token is chosen and appended, and the loop runs again. That is all of text generation.

    • The tokeniser is learned from data once, then frozen. Text common in its sample gets few, long pieces; rare text gets many small ones. English was common. Arabic, especially dialect, less so.
    • The model is a next-token predictor. Like phone autocomplete, but vastly bigger. Everything it seems to "know" comes from predicting a plausible next piece.
    • Attention is the only place tokens talk to each other. That is why context length and cost behave as they do (M1.3).
    Hold onto thisWhen a model does something odd with text (miscounts letters, garbles a long number, costs more in Arabic, loses the middle of a long document), ask: what did the tokeniser hand it, and what can attention reach?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Byte-pair encoding: how a vocabulary is learned

    Whole-word vocabularies fail on new words (a product name, a typo). Single-character ones make sequences long and expensive. Modern LLMs compromise with byte-pair encoding (BPE), like a shorthand writer who invents a symbol for each phrase they write most often: start from single characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new entry, and stop at the target size.

    Nothing is "understood"; it is frequency counting. Frequent strings become single tokens, rare ones stay in pieces. The simulation runs real BPE on a tiny bilingual banking corpus.

    Merges are a budget, spent on whatever is most frequent in the training sample. If English dominates, it wins the early merges and Arabic is still in pieces when the budget runs out. Bigger vocabularies help, which is why they grew from about 32,000 to 250,000.

    Why Arabic costs more, and what it does to money, context and speed

    • Training mix. The tokeniser spends merges where the data is. Fertility is average tokens per word; English is near 1.3. Studies find gaps of several times between languages (Ahia et al., 2023; Petrov et al., 2023).
    • Morphology. One Arabic word can mean "and they will write it"; English needs four. So compare tokens for the same message, not per word.
    • Bytes. Arabic letters take 2 bytes against 1 for English. An unlearned Arabic string falls back to bytes, about two tokens per letter.

    The effect lands in three places. Money: you pay per token. Context: Arabic documents fill the window faster, so size retrieval chunks in tokens, not characters (M3.3). Speed: twice the tokens is about twice the generation time and up to four times the attention work. Quality can suffer too, since shredded words are reassembled from pieces the model rarely saw.

    What to do: measure first. Count tokens on a few hundred real documents per language with each candidate model's tokeniser, and put the ratio in the business case. Then pick a model whose tokeniser handles Arabic well (M2.3), trim prompts and cache repeated instructions. Arabic-first models are in A14.

    What tokens explain: miscounted letters, numbers and odd words

    "How many r's in strawberry?" It is like asking someone to count letters on a single Scrabble tile. The model receives a few ids (say "straw" + "berry"), not ten letters. Spelling the word out first or calling a tool helps. Reversing strings fails for the same reason.

    Numbers. A figure like 12,345,678.90 may be cut into inconsistent chunks. Never let a model do arithmetic that matters in its head. Have it call a calculator or write code, and check the output.

    Odd tokens. "Account", " account" and "ACCOUNT" are different tokens, so spacing and case can shift behaviour.

    Security. Filters and models can tokenise the same text differently, so odd spacing or look-alike Unicode can slip past keyword guardrails (A3, G1).

enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
ids = enc.encode("حول المبلغ الى حسابي")
print(len(ids), [enc.decode([i]) for i in ids])

    Embeddings: from id to vector, and where order comes from

    The first layer is a lookup table: token id 4,127 becomes row 4,127, a vector of a few thousand numbers. Rows are learned, ending up so that tokens used in similar ways sit near each other (F1.1, F1.2).

    Attention treats inputs as an unordered set, so "bank approved customer" and "customer approved bank" would look identical. Position information is injected separately, and handling positions beyond training length is a big part of the long-context story (M1.3).

    Attention: letting tokens read each other

    After embedding, "bank" is the same vector in "river bank" and "bank account". Attention fixes that using context. Think of a search engine inside the sentence. Each position makes a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I hand over if chosen?). It matches its query against every key, turns the match scores into weights that sum to one, and replaces its vector with the weighted blend of the values.

    Multi-head: many attentions run side by side, each tracking a different relation (a verb's subject, the previous token). Causal mask: a text generator may only look at earlier positions, otherwise it could read the answer. Every token is compared with every other, so cost grows with the square of length.

    The transformer block, stacked

    A layer is attention plus a feed-forward network (MLP) that processes each position on its own and holds much of the model's stored knowledge. Each part adds to the running vector rather than replacing it, so each layer is a small edit.

    Stack this 24 to 100 or so times. Early layers tend to build surface features such as grammar, later ones more abstract features.

    The output: probabilities, sampling and the generation loop

    The final vector gives one score per vocabulary entry, turned into probabilities. Temperature is a sharpness dial, like contrast. At 1 you get the model's own distribution. Below 1 it sharpens towards the top token; near 0 it always picks the top one. Above 1 it flattens, and unlikely tokens get a real chance. It is not "creativity" or accuracy.

    Pure sampling can pick a very unlikely token and derail the sentence. Two guards: top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens; top-p keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to p. The chosen token is appended and the model runs again.

    The loop in code (real systems add a KV cache so earlier positions are not recomputed; see M1.3):

    ids = tokenizer.encode(prompt)                 # text -> integers
while True:
    logits = model(ids)[-1]                    # scores for the next token only
    probs  = softmax(logits / temperature)     # optionally apply top-k / top-p
    nxt    = sample(probs)                     # pick one id
    ids.append(nxt)                            # feed it back in
    if nxt == tokenizer.eos_id: break
text = tokenizer.decode(ids)                   # integers -> text

    Even at temperature 0, hosted models are not always identical run to run. For regulated work, record the model version and settings, and test for stability.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor proposals, pilots and AI-written business cases.

    • Costing or comparing Arabic with English token ratios

      A business case budgets "one token per 0.75 English words" for Arabic, and the bill arrives double. Or it quotes tokens per word, when Arabic words carry more meaning.

      Spot itAsk which tokeniser and Arabic sample the estimate came from. Re-count tokens for the same translated message on a few hundred real documents.
    • Trusting the model with letters, digits and arithmetic

      An assistant "checks" that an IBAN has 23 characters, counts digits, or converts currencies in prose, and is wrong.

      Spot itExact-character rules and sums belong in code or a calculator tool. Recompute five outputs by hand.
    • Sizing in characters, or keeping old limits after a model change

      A retrieval pipeline cuts 2,000-character chunks; Arabic ones overflow the embedding model's token limit and are silently truncated. A prompt that fit before is cut after an upgrade because the new tokeniser counts differently.

      Spot itLog every chunk's token count against the limit, and re-measure after any model change.
    • Guardrails that do not see what the model sees

      A keyword filter passes look-alike characters or odd spacing that the model reads perfectly.

      Spot itRed-team with Unicode variants, diacritics, zero-width characters and Arabizi.
    • Setting temperature to 0 and calling it deterministic

      A regulated process assumes identical output every run.

      Spot itRun the same input 50 times; record version and settings; pin the model version.
    • Explaining the model as "understanding words"

      It predicts tokens. A fluent, confident answer is not evidence of correct content.

      Spot itAsk what was verified against a source, not how good the prose sounds.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. They test meaning and judgement, not arithmetic.

    1. A vendor quotes the same price per token for English and Arabic. Does that mean Arabic costs the same? How would you estimate the real cost?

    No. The same message needs more Arabic tokens, so the bill is higher. Count tokens on a few hundred real documents per language and use that ratio.

    2. An Arabic prompt is twice as long in tokens as the English one. Why does the cost of attention grow by more than the cost of everything else?

    Every token is compared with every other, so doubling length roughly quadruples the attention work, while most other parts grow only in line with length.

    3. What does lowering the temperature do to the next-token probabilities, and does it make answers more accurate?

    It sharpens the distribution: more probability to the top token, less to the tail. That makes output more repeatable, not more correct.

    4. In the search-engine picture of attention, what are the query, key and value?

    The query is what a position is looking for, the key is what each position advertises, the value is what it hands over. Matching gives weights, and the output is a weighted blend of values.

    5. Why does adding Arabic tokens to an existing model's vocabulary cost more than just adding rows to a table?

    The new vectors start untrained. You must continue pretraining on Arabic text, then check old abilities have not degraded.

    6. Why does a decoder-only model use a causal mask, and what is the downside for understanding a word from later context?

    Without the mask each position could read the very token it must predict, and at generation time later tokens do not exist. Downside: a word is refined only by what came before it. Encoder models read both directions.

    7. Your chatbot passes English tests but truncates some Arabic answers at the output limit. Give two likely causes and checks.

    The output limit is in tokens and Arabic needs more per answer, so the same cap gives a shorter reply. Check tokens per request in each language against the caps.

    8. A teammate says "set temperature to 0 and we get the same answer every time, so we can skip consistency testing." What is wrong?

    Hosted systems can still vary slightly, and any model or prompt change alters everything. Measure consistency with repeated runs and pinned versions.
