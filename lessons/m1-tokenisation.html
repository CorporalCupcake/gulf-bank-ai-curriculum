    Tokens and the Transformer: How an LLM Reads, Thinks and Writes

    Why Arabic can cost twice the tokens of English, why a model that writes a good essay cannot count the letters in a word, and the whole journey from text to token to vector to attention to the next word.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn LLM never sees text: it sees integer ids from a fixed vocabulary, turns each into a vector, lets the vectors read each other through attention, and outputs a probability for every possible next id. Cost, context, language quality and many odd failures trace back to how text was cut into tokens.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank pilots an assistant that summarises customer emails. In English it costs a few thousand dirhams a month. Switch on Arabic, same volume and summary length, and the bill nearly doubles. The model did not change. The text was cut into more pieces.

    Think of Lego. A model cannot read letters or words; it reads bricks. A tokeniser chops text into bricks from a fixed catalogue of 100,000 to 250,000 pieces (the vocabulary), each with an id number. "transfer" might be one brick, a rarer word three. You pay per token, the context window is measured in tokens, and speed depends on tokens.

    Then the model works in a loop. Each id becomes a vector of a few thousand numbers (F1.1). A stack of identical layers, the transformer, lets each vector gather information from earlier ones using attention (F5.3). The last vector becomes a score for every token in the vocabulary, the scores become probabilities, one token is chosen and appended, and the loop runs again. That is all of text generation.

    • The tokeniser is learned from data once, then frozen. Text that was common in its sample gets few, long pieces; rare text gets many small ones. English was common. Arabic, especially dialect and fully vowelled text, less so. That is both a cost and a quality story.
    • The model is a next-token predictor. Like phone autocomplete, but vastly bigger. Everything it seems to "know" comes from repeatedly predicting a plausible next piece.
    • Attention is the only place tokens talk to each other. Everything else works on one position at a time. That is why context length and cost behave as they do (M1.3).
    Hold onto thisWhen a model does something odd with text (miscounts letters, garbles a long number, costs more in Arabic, loses the middle of a long document), ask: what did the tokeniser hand it, and what can attention reach?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Byte-pair encoding: how a vocabulary is learned

    Whole-word vocabularies fail on new words (a product name, a typo, an Emirati surname). Single-character vocabularies make sequences very long and expensive. Nearly all modern LLMs compromise with subword tokenisation, usually byte-pair encoding (BPE) (Sennrich et al., 2016, from a 1994 compression idea). It works like a shorthand writer who invents a symbol for each phrase they write most often.

    1. Start with every word cut into single characters (or bytes).
    2. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across the training text, weighted by word frequency.
    3. Merge the most frequent pair (say "t"+"h" becomes "th") into a new vocabulary entry. Record the merge.
    4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

    To tokenise new text, replay the merges in order. Nothing is "understood"; it is frequency counting. Frequent strings ("the", " transfer") become single tokens, rare ones stay in pieces. Real tokenisers start from the 256 possible bytes, so any input can be encoded and nothing is "unknown". Variants: WordPiece (BERT) picks merges by likelihood, and SentencePiece's Unigram prunes down from a large vocabulary.

    The simulation runs real BPE on a tiny bilingual banking corpus. It is a toy, but the mechanism is the one that sets real costs.

    Merges are a budget, spent on whatever is most frequent in the training sample. If English dominates, English wins the early merges and Arabic is still in pieces when the budget runs out. A bigger vocabulary helps, which is why vocabularies grew: GPT-2 about 50,000, Llama 2 32,000, recent models 100,000 to 250,000 (check the model card).

    Why Arabic costs more, and what it does to money, context and speed

    • Training mix. The tokeniser spends merges where the data is. Fertility is average tokens per word; English is near 1.3 with a good fit. Studies find gaps of several times between languages (Ahia et al., 2023; Petrov et al., 2023). A March 2026 study of Arabic tokenisers found one widely used OpenAI tokeniser had the highest Arabic fertility of those tested. Arabic-built models such as Jais have the lowest (Arabic Stable LM report).
    • Morphology. One Arabic word can mean "and they will write it" (conjunction, tense, verb root, pronoun attached). English needs four words. So compare tokens for the same message, not per word.
    • Bytes. In UTF-8 English letters take 1 byte, Arabic letters 2. An unlearned Arabic string falls back to bytes, so each letter costs about two tokens. Vowel marks (tashkeel) add characters. Dialect spellings, Arabizi ("3" for ع) and mid-sentence code-switching are rarer still in training text.

    The effect lands in three places. Money: you pay per token. Context: Arabic documents fill the window faster, so size retrieval chunks in tokens, not characters (M3.3). Speed: twice the tokens is about twice the generation time and up to four times the input attention compute, since attention compares every token with every other. Quality can suffer too: a word shredded into byte-like fragments must be reassembled from pieces the model rarely saw.

    What to do: measure first. Count tokens of a few hundred real bank documents per language with each candidate model's tokeniser, and put the ratio in the business case. Then pick a model whose tokeniser handles Arabic well (M2.3), trim the prompt, cache repeated instructions, retrieve less but better (M3), or, for open models, add Arabic tokens to the vocabulary and continue training. A 2026 preprint reports about 17% fewer tokens per word this way, but on a very small test sample. Arabic-first models are in A14.

    What tokens explain: miscounted letters, numbers and odd words

    "How many r's in strawberry?" It is like asking someone to count the letters in a word printed on a single Scrabble tile. The model receives a few ids (say "straw" + "berry", depending on the tokeniser), not ten letters. Whether each vector carries its spelling was learned only indirectly. Spelling the word out first (each letter becomes its own token) or calling a tool helps; reasoning models (A5.1) often do this unprompted. Reversing strings, rhyming and acrostics fail for the same reason.

    Numbers. A figure like 12,345,678.90 may be cut into inconsistent chunks, so the digit alignment that arithmetic needs is hard to learn. Some tokenisers split into single digits for this reason. Rule for banking: never let a model do arithmetic that matters in its head. Have it call a calculator or write code, and check the output.

    Whitespace and case. "Account", " account" and "ACCOUNT" are different tokens with different vectors, so spacing changes can shift behaviour. Indented code and tables use many tokens.

    Glitch tokens. A token can enter the vocabulary because it was frequent in tokeniser data (a Reddit username) yet be almost absent from model training. Its vector is barely trained and the model behaves strangely (Rumbelow and Watkins, 2023). Tokeniser and model are trained separately and can mismatch.

    Security. Filters and models can tokenise the same text differently, so odd spacing, look-alike Unicode and mixed scripts can slip past keyword guardrails (A3, G1). Normalise and test text in the form the model sees.

    import tiktoken                      # OpenAI's open tokeniser library
enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
ids = enc.encode("حول المبلغ الى حسابي")
print(len(ids), [enc.decode([i]) for i in ids])
# Compare len(ids) with the English equivalent. Other vendors publish
# their own token counters; use the one for the model you will pay for.

    Embeddings: from id to vector, and where order comes from

    The first layer is a lookup table, one row per vocabulary entry. With 128,000 tokens and 4,096 numbers per row, that is about 525 million numbers. Token id 4,127 becomes row 4,127. Rows start random and are learned, ending up so that tokens used in similar ways sit near each other: directions mean things, distance means similarity (F1.1, F1.2). Search embedding models (M3) use the same idea with a different training goal.

    Attention treats inputs as an unordered set, so "bank approved customer" and "customer approved bank" would look identical. Order must be injected. The original transformer added sine and cosine patterns. Most current models use rotary position embeddings (RoPE), which rotate query and key vectors by an angle depending on position, so scores depend on the distance between tokens. Coping with positions beyond training length is a big part of the long-context story (M1.3).

    Attention: letting tokens read each other

    After embedding, "bank" is the same vector in "river bank" and "bank account". Attention fixes that using context. Think of a search engine inside the sentence. Each position makes three vectors from its own, via learned matrices: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I hand over if chosen?). Position i scores every position j by query-times-key (a dot product, F1.1), softmaxes the scores into weights summing to one, and returns the weighted average of the values. That replaces its vector.

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V

    Each row of QKT is one token's scores against all tokens. Dividing by √dk (key length) stops scores growing with vector size, which would saturate softmax into near one-hot weights that train badly. Softmax makes each row positive and sum to one; multiplying by V averages the values. For n tokens the score matrix has n² entries: the source of quadratic cost.

    Multi-head: many attention computations run side by side (a few dozen), each with its own matrices, so different heads track different relations (a verb's subject, the previous token, a matching bracket); outputs are joined. Causal mask: in a text generator, position i may only attend to positions up to i, otherwise it could read the answer. The simulation shows both what attention does and why the mask matters.

    The transformer block, stacked

    A layer is attention plus a second component and some plumbing. Per position:

    1. Normalise (layer norm or RMSNorm) to keep numbers stable.
    2. Attention mixes in earlier positions. Its output is added to the original vector, not substituted. This residual connection makes each layer a small edit to a running message, and lets gradients flow straight back in training (F1.3).
    3. Normalise, then a feed-forward network (MLP) per position: expand the vector (about 4x its width, or a gated variant such as SwiGLU), apply a nonlinearity, shrink back, add again. Roughly two thirds of a dense model's parameters live here, and interpretability research suggests much stored factual knowledge does too (R5).

    Stack this 24 to 100 or so times, with different weights in each. The 2017 transformer had 6 layers of width 512; an 8-billion-parameter model has about 32 layers of width 4,096 (check model cards). Rule of thumb: a block has about 12 × width² parameters (4 attention, 8 MLP). Width 4,096 gives about 200 million per layer; 32 layers is about 6.4 billion, and the embedding tables take you near the headline figure. Early layers tend to build surface features such as grammar, later ones more abstract features. Elhage et al. (2021) describe the residual stream as a shared channel that heads and MLPs read from and write to.

    The output: probabilities, sampling and the generation loop

    The final position's vector is multiplied by a matrix with one row per vocabulary entry (often the embedding table reused), giving one raw score, a logit, per token. Softmax turns logits into probabilities. With temperature T:

    pi=exp(zi/T)∑jexp(zj/T)

    zi is token i's logit. Temperature is a sharpness dial, like turning up the contrast. At T = 1 you get the model's own distribution. Below 1 it sharpens towards the top token, and as T approaches 0 it is greedy (always the top one). Above 1 it flattens. Logits 2, 1, 0 give 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0% at T = 1, but 86.7%, 11.7%, 1.6% at T = 0.5. Common misreading: temperature is not "creativity" or accuracy, only how much probability the tail gets.

    Pure sampling sometimes picks a very unlikely token and derails the sentence. Two guards: top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens; top-p (nucleus sampling, Holtzman et al., 2020) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to p. Both renormalise, then sample. The chosen token is appended and the model runs again. Training uses the same machinery with the true next token known: the loss is cross-entropy, the negative log probability given to the actual next token (F1.7, F6.1).

    The loop in code (real systems add the KV cache so earlier positions are not recomputed; see M1.3):

    ids = tokenizer.encode(prompt)                 # text -> integers
while True:
    logits = model(ids)[-1]                    # scores for the next token only
    probs  = softmax(logits / temperature)     # optionally apply top-k / top-p
    nxt    = sample(probs)                     # pick one id
    ids.append(nxt)                            # feed it back in
    if nxt == tokenizer.eos_id: break
text = tokenizer.decode(ids)                   # integers -> text

    Even at temperature 0, hosted models are not always identical run to run: batching and floating-point order change tiny numbers and can flip a near-tie. For regulated work, record the model version and settings, and test for stability.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor proposals, pilots and AI-written business cases.

    • Costing Arabic with English token ratios

      A business case budgets "one token per 0.75 English words" for Arabic, and the bill arrives double.

      Spot itAsk which tokeniser and Arabic sample the estimate came from. Re-count a few hundred real messages.
    • Comparing "per word" across languages

      "Arabic uses 2.6 tokens per word against 1.3." Arabic words carry more meaning, so the per-message gap differs.

      Spot itAsk for tokens for the same translated message.
    • Trusting letter counts, character limits and exact string tasks

      An assistant "checks" that an IBAN has 23 characters, or counts digits, and is wrong.

      Spot itExact-character rules belong in code. Test the model on a few hundred cases.
    • Letting the model do the arithmetic

      Sums, percentages and currency conversions produced in prose.

      Spot itLook for a calculator tool or code step. Recompute five outputs by hand.
    • Chunking and limits in characters

      A retrieval pipeline cuts 2,000-character chunks; Arabic ones overflow the embedding model's token limit and are silently truncated.

      Spot itLog every chunk's token count against the model's limit.
    • Guardrails that do not see what the model sees

      A keyword filter passes look-alike characters or odd spacing that the model reads perfectly.

      Spot itRed-team with Unicode variants, diacritics, zero-width characters and Arabizi.
    • Setting temperature to 0 and calling it deterministic

      A regulated process assumes identical output every run.

      Spot itRun the same input 50 times; record version and settings; pin the model version.
    • Switching models and keeping old limits

      A prompt that fit before is truncated after an upgrade because the new tokeniser counts differently.

      Spot itRe-measure token counts after any model change and check gateway limits.
    • Explaining the model as "understanding words"

      It predicts tokens. A fluent, confident answer is not evidence of correct content.

      Spot itAsk what was verified against a source, not how good the prose sounds.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. You process 10,000 Arabic documents of 500 words a month at 2.4 tokens per word. Input price is USD 3 per million tokens. What is the monthly input cost in AED (3.6725 per USD)?

    10,000 × 500 × 2.4 = 12 million tokens. 12 × 3 = USD 36, and 36 × 3.6725 ≈ AED 132. At 1.3 tokens per word: 6.5 million tokens, about AED 72.

    2. An Arabic prompt is 2 times longer in tokens than the English one. By roughly how much does attention score computation grow, and how big is the KV cache compared with English?

    The score matrix has n² entries, so about 4 times the attention compute. The KV cache holds keys and values per token, so about 2 times as large. MLP and embeddings scale linearly, so the whole forward pass grows 2 to 4 times depending on length.

    3. Logits are 2, 1, 0 for three tokens. What are the probabilities at temperature 1, and what happens at 0.5?

    T = 1: e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1, sum 11.107, so 66.5%, 24.5%, 9.0%. T = 0.5 doubles the logits to 4, 2, 0: 54.6, 7.39, 1, sum 63.0, giving 86.7%, 11.7%, 1.6%. Lower temperature sharpens the distribution without changing the order.

    4. Why is the query-key dot product divided by √dk?

    A dot product of two vectors with dk random entries has a spread growing like √dk. Undivided, large vectors give extreme scores, softmax goes near one-hot and gradients vanish. The division keeps scores at a stable scale.

    5. A model has a 128,000-token vocabulary and width 4,096. How many numbers are in its embedding table, and why does adding Arabic tokens cost more than the table alone?

    128,000 × 4,096 ≈ 524 million (525 million for 128,256). Each added token needs a new embedding row and usually a new output row. The real cost is that new vectors start untrained, so you must continue pretraining on Arabic text and check old abilities have not degraded.

    6. Why does a decoder-only model use a causal mask, and what is the downside for understanding a word from later context?

    In training, every position is predicted in parallel; without the mask each could read the very token it must predict. At generation time later tokens do not exist yet. Downside, as in Simulation 3: a word is refined only by what came before it, so the vector for "bank" itself stays ambiguous, though later words can use it. Encoder models for classification and embeddings read both directions.

    7. Your chatbot passes English tests but truncates some Arabic answers at the output limit. Give two likely causes and checks.

    The output limit is in tokens, and Arabic needs more tokens per answer, so the same cap gives a shorter reply. The input prompt may also fill part of the window. Check tokens used per request in each language against the caps, then raise the Arabic output limit or shorten the prompt.

    8. A teammate says "set temperature to 0 and we get the same answer every time, so we can skip consistency testing." What is wrong?

    Temperature 0 picks the top token each step, but hosted systems can still vary slightly from batching and floating-point effects, and any model or prompt change alters everything. Measure consistency with repeated runs and pinned versions.
