    Pretrained Forecasters and Graphs That Respect Time

    What Chronos and TimesFM are, what zero-shot forecasting really buys you against a good seasonal baseline, and why graph models for transaction networks must treat time as structure.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA time-series foundation model is a forecaster pretrained on a huge variety of series, so it can forecast without fitting, and a temporal graph model treats "who paid whom, and when" as structure; both are worth trying, neither may skip the simple baseline.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a 14-day cash forecast for each of 1,800 ATMs. The classical route fits one model per machine, and gives you little for the 40 machines installed last month. A time-series foundation model is trained once on billions of observations from thousands of unrelated series, then asked for the next 14 values of any new series with no fitting. That is zero-shot forecasting, like a doctor who has seen thousands of patients and recognises a new weekend-dip case.

    The graph half is the same idea applied to payments: accounts are nodes and transfers are edges. In a bank the edges carry timestamps, and order matters. A to B at 09:00 then B to C at 09:05 is a relay. The same two payments in the opposite order are unrelated, and a model that ignores order sees a chain that is not there.

    Hold onto this"Zero-shot" means no training on your series, not no judgement. These models do best on short, generic series and worst where patterns depend on things they cannot see: salary days, Ramadan, a product launch. Judge them against the cheapest strong baseline, out of time, with intervals.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Chronos: treat numbers as a language

    Chronos (Amazon) scales a series so it is centred around 1, then rounds each value into one of a few thousand bins and reads the bin IDs like words. A language-model network predicts the next ID as a probability distribution. To forecast, it samples many paths, so quantiles of the paths give intervals for free, and it can express "normal Thursday or salary-day spike" instead of averaging the two.

    The cost is precision. Scaling uses the average size of the context, so one outlier inflates the scale and makes every bin coarser for all the normal days.

    In more detail, there are two steps. Scale: divide the context by its mean absolute value, so a series in AED millions and one in single units both sit around 1. Quantise: cut a fixed range of scaled values (about −15 to +15) into a few thousand equal bins, and clip anything outside to the end bin. The network is a standard T5 language model trained with next-token classification, not squared error. That is why it can be bimodal and why sampling gives intervals.

    Chronos was trained on a large public collection plus synthetic series drawn from random Gaussian-process kernels (KernelSynth), which teach trend, seasonality and level shifts that real datasets under-represent. Later variants changed the machinery: Chronos-Bolt uses patches and a direct quantile output for much faster inference, and Chronos-2 adds related series and known-future covariates. The field has moved from tokenised values towards patches and quantile heads.

    TimesFM and patches: read in chunks, write in chunks

    One token per value makes long histories and horizons expensive, and each forecast value fed back in as if it were data lets errors compound. TimesFM (Google) groups consecutive values into patches, say 32 per token, and writes its forecast in patches too. A 512-value history becomes 16 tokens, and a long horizon needs a handful of passes instead of hundreds.

    The trade is detail for speed and range: less position detail inside a patch, but cheaper attention, longer usable context and fewer chances for errors to feed on themselves.

    TimesFM is a decoder-only transformer (the GPT family). The input patch length sets how much history one token summarises. The output patch length sets how many values are written per pass, and it is the main control on error compounding, because each pass feeds its forecast back as if it were data. It was pretrained on roughly 100 billion time points from sources such as Google Trends, Wikipedia page views, public datasets and synthetic series. The first release gave a point forecast; later releases add quantile heads. Moirai (Salesforce) is also patch-based, but accepts several related variables at once and outputs a mixture of distributions.

    Zero-shot against honest baselines

    "Good" means beating the cheapest strong method on your data, out of time. For daily banking series that is a seasonal average with drift: average each weekday over recent weeks and add the trend. It is free, needs no GPU and is hard to beat on clean, stable series. The simulation runs only the baselines you must beat.

    Score with quantile loss and interval coverage: do the 10-90% intervals contain about 80% of outcomes? Models that accept known-future covariates such as holiday calendars (Chronos-2, Moirai) matter for treasury, since salary days and Eid are known in advance.

    The baseline is not a straw man. Zeng et al. (2023) showed a single linear layer beating several elaborate transformer forecasters, and benchmarks have since become more careful. Sharp but over-confident intervals are worse for liquidity planning than wider honest ones, so check coverage as well as accuracy.

    Order of attack: zero-shot first, then light fine-tuning on your own data if a real gap to your baseline remains. Fine-tuning often closes the gap to a tuned local model. If it does not, the series probably depends on information the model cannot see.

    Graphs that change over time

    A graph neural network updates each account's representation from its neighbours, like judging someone by the company they keep. It catches guilt by association that a flat table cannot, such as a fresh account whose first five counterparties were all flagged. Temporal versions (TGN, TGAT) process timestamped events and may use only events before the prediction time. Graph transformers let every node see every other, at higher cost; graph foundation models are still early on noisy bank data.

    Banking adds three difficulties. First, time on the edges: temporal GNNs give each node a memory updated by its events, and time encodings let attention favour recent interactions. Prediction at time t may use only events before t, which is point-in-time correctness applied to a graph. Second, limited reach: message passing sees only nearby nodes, and stacking many layers blurs everything together (over-smoothing) or squeezes distant information through a few links (over-squashing). Graph transformers, with structural encodings such as distances or random-walk features, avoid this, but their quadratic cost means you run them on sampled neighbourhoods rather than a bank-wide graph. Third, every graph is different: a GNN trained on one bank's graph does not transfer to another's. Graph foundation models try to pretrain across many graphs, for example using relation structure alone. They are strongest on knowledge-graph link prediction and weakest on noisy, high-volume, heterogeneous payment graphs.

    The most useful temporal idea is the time-respecting path: A to B to C counts only if the payment into B came before the payment out, and soon enough for mule layering.

    The hard rule also makes a strong feature: count time-respecting paths into each account in the last 24 hours and feed the count to a gradient-boosted tree. That is a high bar for a deep temporal GNN, so try the cheap version first.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Cold starts. A new ATM or product has weeks of history, too little to fit a model. A pretrained forecaster has already seen many series with similar shapes, so it extrapolates trend and weekly seasonality from short context.
    • Breadth with little effort. One model serves thousands of series with no per-series fitting or tuning. That saves engineering time when you have a long tail of small series.
    • Honest uncertainty, for the better models. Chronos samples whole paths and Chronos-Bolt and recent TimesFM releases emit quantiles directly, so you get intervals and multimodal outcomes rather than a single line.
    • Time-respecting graph features. Honouring order and hold limits cuts false leads in mule tracing, which saves investigator time.

    Weak at, or do not use when

    • Calendar-driven series. Salary days, Eid and Ramadan shift demand. A univariate model cannot see them, so it needs covariates or fine-tuning.
    • Long, clean, stable histories. With years of data, a seasonal average with drift or a tuned local model usually matches or beats a pretrained one at a fraction of the cost.
    • Regime breaks and outliers. A level shift is invisible in the history, and one data error can rescale a tokenised input. Clean inputs first.
    • Heavy compute at scale. Running a transformer over millions of series every day costs far more than a seasonal average. Sampling-based Chronos is the slowest.
    • Audit and explainability needs. Regulators and treasury teams prefer a model whose drivers they can read. A pretrained black box is harder to defend.

    In practice

    Start with seasonal average plus drift, then run one pretrained model zero-shot against it on held-out periods after the model's data cutoff, over many forecast origins, scoring MAE and 10-90% interval coverage. The knobs that matter most are context length (how much history you pass in), whether you supply calendar covariates, and whether you fine-tune.

    In banking this shows up in ATM cash forecasting, branch and call-centre volumes, card-spend and liquidity planning, where the long tail of new machines or products is the real gain. In fraud and AML, the time-respecting-path feature, fed to a gradient-boosted tree, is the practical way to use graph structure. Deep temporal GNNs and graph foundation models are research-grade for payment networks today: use engineered graph features plus a tree now, and revisit when a graph model beats that on your data out of time.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Benchmark leakage

      The test series was in pretraining, so "zero-shot" was not.

      Spot itAsk what was in the pretraining corpus. Judge on periods after the data cutoff or on private series.
    • Weak baselines

      A glossy comparison against one weak rival, such as the last value.

      Spot itDemand seasonal naive and seasonal average with drift in the table, over several forecast origins.
    • Outliers and calendar blindness

      One data error rescales a tokenised series, and a univariate model cannot see salary days, Eid or Ramadan.

      Spot itCap the outlier and see if the forecast changes. Plot errors by day-of-month and around holidays.
    • Over-confident intervals and regime breaks

      Sharp 80% intervals that contain only 60% of outcomes, and level shifts no pretrained model can know about.

      Spot itCount coverage on held-out data. Alert on a run of same-sign errors.
    • Graph leakage and label bias

      Features built on the full graph include transfers made after the prediction date, and known mules are only those the old rules caught.

      Spot itCheck neighbours are filtered by timestamp, and hold out cases found by other means.
    • Context too short or too stale

      The model sees only the window you pass in. Too little misses the yearly cycle, and old history from before a product change misleads it.

      Spot itVary the context length and plot error against it. If error keeps falling as context grows, you are truncating a real pattern.
    • Silent drift after deployment

      The series changes after launch and no one retrains or rechecks, because zero-shot feels maintenance-free.

      Spot itTrack rolling error against the seasonal baseline and interval coverage each week, and alert when the gap closes.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can Chronos give a forecast distribution without an extra uncertainty model?

    It outputs a probability distribution over the next token. Sample many paths and their quantiles are the intervals, though calibration still needs testing.

    2. Why does one data error hurt a tokenised model?

    It inflates the scale, so every bin becomes coarser for all the normal days.

    3. Why do patches help?

    Fewer tokens make attention cheaper and fewer passes mean fewer chances for fed-back errors to compound.

    4. A foundation model beats "repeat last week" on 14 ATMs. Should you adopt it?

    Not yet. Compare with seasonal average and drift, over many origins and series, after the model's data cutoff, with interval coverage.

    5. In Simulation 4, funds reach E at hour 5 and an edge E to F occurs at hour 1. Is F linked in each view?

    Static: yes. Time-respecting: no, the transfer happened before the money arrived. This is the false lead the time-aware view removes.

    6. You forecast cash for 1,800 ATMs. 1,700 have two years of history, 100 are new. Which would you choose and why?

    Seasonal average with drift, or a tuned local model, for the 1,700: it is cheap, explainable and hard to beat on stable series. A pretrained model for the 100 new machines, where there is too little history to fit anything. Check both out of time against the baseline, and review calendar effects such as salary days.

    7. For mule detection, would you start with a deep temporal GNN or time-respecting path counts into a gradient-boosted tree? Why?

    Path counts into a tree. They are quick to build, easy to explain to investigators and often hard for a deep model to beat. Move to a temporal GNN only if it wins out of time on your data, with a point-in-time-correct pipeline.
