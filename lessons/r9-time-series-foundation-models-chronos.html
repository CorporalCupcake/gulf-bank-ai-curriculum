    Pretrained Forecasters and Graphs That Respect Time

    How Chronos and TimesFM turn a numeric series into something a language-model-style network can read, what zero-shot forecasting really buys you against a good seasonal baseline, and how graph models for transaction networks have to treat time as part of the structure.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA time-series foundation model is a forecaster pretrained on a very large and varied collection of series, so it can give a usable forecast from a few hundred past values with no fitting, and a temporal graph model is a network learner that treats "who paid whom, and when" as the structure; both are worth trying, neither has earned the right to skip the simple baseline.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine the treasury team wants a 14-day forecast of cash withdrawals for each of 1,800 ATMs. The classical route (F9.1) is to fit a model per machine: seasonality, trend, holidays, an error model. It works, but it means 1,800 fits, a pipeline to keep running, and nothing to say about the 40 ATMs installed last month, which have only three weeks of history.

    A time-series foundation model takes the language-model route. Train one large network once, on billions of observations from thousands of unrelated series (electricity, web traffic, retail sales, weather, synthetic curves). Then hand it any new series as context and ask for the next 14 values. No fitting on your data. It has already seen a thousand daily series with a weekend dip, and it recognises yours as another one. That is zero-shot forecasting, and the bet is the same one language models made: broad pretraining beats narrow fitting when data per task is small.

    The second half of the lesson is about a different shape of data. A payments network is a graph: accounts are nodes, transfers are edges. Graph neural networks learn from that structure, for example by passing messages between neighbouring accounts. The trouble in a bank is that the edges carry timestamps, and the order matters. Money moving A to B at 09:00 and B to C at 09:05 is a relay. B to C at 08:00 and A to B at 09:00 is two unrelated payments. A model that ignores order will see a chain where there is none. Temporal graph models, graph transformers and the early graph foundation models are all attempts to learn from structure without losing the clock.

    Hold onto this"Zero-shot" means no training on your series, not no judgement. A foundation model is a very well-read generic forecaster. It is strongest where data per series is short and patterns are generic, and weakest where your series depend on things it cannot see: a salary-day calendar, a Ramadan shift, a product launch, a fraud ring changing tactics. Judge it the way you judge any model, against the cheapest strong baseline, out of time, with intervals.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Chronos: treat numbers as a language

    Chronos (Ansari et al., Amazon, 2024) makes one move that sounds almost silly: turn the series into a sequence of tokens from a small fixed vocabulary, and train a standard language-model architecture (T5) on it with the usual next-token cross-entropy loss. Two steps do it:

    1. Scale. Divide the context by its mean absolute value, so series measured in AED millions and series measured in single units look alike. Every series now lives around 1.
    2. Quantise. Cut a fixed range of scaled values (roughly −15 to +15) into several thousand equal bins. Each value becomes the ID of its bin, like a word. Anything beyond the range is clipped to the end bin.

    The model reads token IDs and predicts the next ID as a probability distribution over the vocabulary. To forecast, you sample: draw a next token, append it, repeat. Run that many times and you get many plausible futures, which is a forecast distribution for free. Quantiles of the samples give the intervals. The loss is classification, not squared error, so the model can be bimodal ("either a normal Thursday or a salary-day spike") without being forced to average the two.

    This has a cost, and the simulation shows it. Scaling by a mean makes the system sensitive to outliers in the context, and the bin width in original units is fixed by the scale, so there is a floor on how fine a difference the model can represent.

    s=1n∑t=1n|xt|,token(xt)=⌊clip(xt/s,−15,15)+1530/B⌋

    s is the scale, B the number of bins. The smallest difference the model can tell apart is one bin: 30·s/B in original units. I use this simplified form in the simulation; the published tokenizer has a few extra details (special tokens, the exact bin count near 4,096).

    Chronos was trained on a large public collection plus synthetic series generated from random Gaussian-process kernels (the paper calls it KernelSynth), with augmentations that mix real series. Synthetic data matters here: it teaches trend, seasonality and level shifts in forms that real datasets under-represent. Variants followed: Chronos-Bolt, which swaps the token sampling for patches and a direct quantile output for much faster inference, and Chronos-2 (October 2025), which adds multivariate series and covariates in one model. The general pattern is that the tokenised-language idea was the first step and the field then moved to patches and direct quantile heads (below).

    TimesFM and patches: read in chunks, write in chunks

    Tokenising one value at a time makes long contexts and long horizons expensive. Attention cost grows with the square of the sequence length, and generating a 720-step forecast one token at a time needs 720 passes through the network, with each guess fed back in so errors can compound.

    TimesFM (Das et al., Google, ICML 2024) is a decoder-only transformer (the same family as GPT) that works on patches: a block of consecutive values, say 32, is embedded as one token by a small network. Context of 512 values becomes 16 tokens. Crucially, the output side is also patched: from each position the model predicts a longer block, 128 values in the first release, so a 512-step horizon needs 4 passes, not 512. It was pretrained on a corpus reported at around 100 billion time points mixing Google Trends, Wikipedia page views, public datasets and synthetic series. It outputs a point forecast, and later releases add quantile heads. Moirai (Salesforce) takes a related patch-based route but is built to accept several related variables at once with an "any-variate" attention design, and outputs a mixture of distributions rather than one number.

    Two ideas to keep. First, patching is a speed and range trade: fewer tokens means cheaper attention and longer usable context, at the price of coarser positional detail inside a patch. Second, output patch length controls compounding: each autoregressive pass feeds its own forecast back as if it were data, so fewer passes means fewer chances for error to feed on itself.

    Zero-shot against honest baselines

    What does "good" mean here? Not "looks plausible". The foundation model has to beat the cheapest strong method on your data, out of time. For daily banking series with weekly seasonality, that method is a seasonal average with a drift term: take the last C weeks, average each weekday, add the trend. It costs nothing, needs no GPU and is hard to beat on clean, stable series. A good deal of research shows how often fancy models lose to simple ones: Zeng et al. (2023) beat several elaborate transformer forecasters with a single linear layer, and the benchmarks that followed made evaluation more careful.

    The simulation below does not run a foundation model; that would need the real weights. It builds the baseline set you must beat and lets you see when each simple method wins, so you know what you are asking a foundation model to improve on. Two features of the data are deliberately outside what any zero-shot model can see: a month-end bump in the forecast window (a calendar fact) and an optional regime shift.

    If you do want to test one on your own series, the code is short. Check the repository for the current interface, since these packages change quickly:

    # Chronos-Bolt (Amazon), via the chronos-forecasting package; from memory, check the README
import torch
from chronos import BaseChronosPipeline

pipe = BaseChronosPipeline.from_pretrained("amazon/chronos-bolt-base")
quantiles, mean = pipe.predict_quantiles(
    context=torch.tensor(atm_history),     # 1-D tensor of past values
    prediction_length=14,
    quantile_levels=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9],
)
# then score against a seasonal baseline on a held-out period, with pinball loss and MAE

    Evaluate with quantile loss and coverage (see F7.5 and conformal prediction): do the 10-90% intervals contain about 80% of outcomes? A foundation model that gives sharp but over-confident intervals is worse for liquidity planning than a wider honest one.

    How these models can use extra information is the live frontier. Early models were univariate: one series in, one out. Chronos-2 and Moirai take related series and covariates (known-future inputs such as a holiday calendar or planned campaigns) through group attention, which is the direction treasury cares about, since salary days, Eid and Ramadan are known in advance. Fine-tuning on your data is possible, and often a few steps of it close the gap to a tuned local model. The honest order of attack is zero-shot first, then light fine-tuning if the gap to your baseline is real.

    Graphs that change over time

    A graph neural network (GNN) updates each node's representation by gathering messages from its neighbours: your account's embedding is mixed with those of accounts you pay or are paid by, over a few rounds. This captures guilt-by-association patterns a flat table of per-account features cannot: a fresh account whose first five counterparties were all previously flagged.

    Three things make the banking version harder, and each has a model family.

    • Time matters on the edges. Temporal GNNs such as TGN (Rossi et al., 2020) and TGAT (Xu et al., 2020) process a stream of timestamped events. Each node keeps a memory that updates when it is involved in an event, and time encodings let attention weight recent interactions more than old ones. Prediction at time t may only use events before t, which is point-in-time correctness (F3.1) applied to the graph.
    • Message passing sees only nearby nodes. Stacking many layers blurs ("over-smoothing") and squeezes far-away information through a few connections ("over-squashing"). Graph transformers (Graphormer, GPS) let every node attend to every other, adding positional or structural encodings (distances, random-walk features) so the model still knows the graph's shape. The cost is attention across all pairs, which limits them to modest graphs, so for a bank-wide payments graph you run them on sampled neighbourhoods.
    • Each graph is different. A GNN trained on one bank's graph does not transfer to another's. Graph foundation models try to pretrain across many graphs, for example by using relation-structure alone so features need not match (ULTRA does this for knowledge graphs), or by turning graph tasks into text prompts for a language model. This area is early: results are strongest on knowledge-graph link prediction and weakest on the noisy, high-volume, heterogeneous graphs banks have.

    The most useful single idea from temporal graph work for a bank is the time-respecting path: a path A → B → C only counts if the edge into B happened before the edge out of B, and, for mule-style layering, soon enough after it. The simulation makes that exact.

    This hard rule is also a strong feature for a conventional model: count the time-respecting two- and three-hop paths into each account in the last 24 hours and feed the count to a gradient-boosted tree. In practice, teams find that carefully engineered graph features plus a tree model are a high bar for a deep temporal GNN to clear, and benchmarks have shown simple recency heuristics (such as EdgeBank, which just remembers past edges) beating several learned temporal models on some link-prediction tasks. Try the cheap version first.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Both halves reuse ideas you already have; the novelty is the scale of pretraining and the use of time.

    F9.1 · Time series and forecastingThe classical toolkit

    Seasonality, trend, ARIMA and ETS. Every foundation-model result is measured against these, and the seasonal average in Simulation 3 is their simplest form.

    F7.5 · Forecast metricsMAE, MASE and quantile loss

    How to score point and interval forecasts, and why a scale-free metric is needed when you forecast many series.

    F5.5 · AttentionThe block both families use

    Patching changes what a token is, not how attention works. Graph transformers apply the same operation to nodes.

    F5.7 · Self-supervised learningPretraining without labels

    Predicting the next value needs no labels, so time series can be pretrained on huge unlabelled collections just as text can.

    R9.1 · R9.2 · Relational and tabular modelsThe neighbouring lesson

    Relational deep learning turns a database into a temporal graph, the same object as this lesson's second half. Tabular foundation models are the "pretrained on many tables" parallel to pretrained forecasters.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionIntervals you can trust

    Foundation models give intervals but not guarantees. Conformal calibration on your own recent errors is a cheap way to repair coverage.

    B5.1 · Liquidity forecastingThe treasury use case

    Cash, ATM and funding forecasts across many entities, many with short histories, are the natural target for zero-shot models.

    A9.2 · Mule detection with graph MLThe temporal graph at work

    Fast relays through freshly opened accounts are exactly the time-respecting paths of Simulation 4.

    A8.1 · Transaction foundation modelsPretraining on payments

    A foundation model for sequences of transactions is the same bet as Chronos, with categorical events rather than one numeric series.

    B1.1 · Fraud and B2 · AML monitoringWhere the graph signal pays

    Shared devices, shared beneficiaries and layering chains. See also rules versus ML for how graph scores fit alongside rule scenarios.

    F3.1 · Point-in-timeTime travel in a graph

    Using an edge that happened after the prediction date is leakage, and graphs make it easy to do by accident.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Treasury and operations. ATM cash, branch footfall, call-centre volume, payment-rail load. Many series, uneven history, forecasts needed per entity.
    • New products and new entities. Cold-start series (a new card product, a new branch) where a local model has nothing to learn from.
    • Balance and deposit monitoring. Zero-shot forecasts as a quick expected range for anomaly flags, to be backed by a proper model if it matters.
    • AML and scam networks. Layering chains, mule rings, shared devices; temporal graph features and, where data supports it, GNN scores.
    • Regulated use. Capital, IFRS 9 and liquidity-risk numbers go through model-risk governance. A third-party pretrained model whose training data you cannot inspect is a harder validation case; I would not assume an answer to how a CBUAE or SAMA reviewer treats it, and would raise it early with model risk.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The live questions: how much do pretrained forecasters beat tuned local models, can they use covariates well, and how much of the benchmark progress is real given overlapping data. For graphs: whether learned temporal models beat engineered features, and whether a graph foundation model can work outside knowledge graphs.

    Research checked on 6 November 2026 with a few searches. The leaderboard changes often; newer items below come from papers and secondary write-ups and should be checked against the live pages.

    1. 2020
      Temporal graph networks

      TGN keeps a memory per node updated by timestamped events; TGAT adds functional time encodings to attention. They set the template for continuous-time graph learning.

      Rossi et al., 2020; Xu et al., ICLR 2020
    2. 2021–22
      Graph transformers

      Graphormer showed that adding structural encodings to a transformer wins on molecule benchmarks. GPS gave a recipe combining local message passing with global attention.

      Ying et al., NeurIPS 2021; Rampášek et al., NeurIPS 2022
    3. 2022
      Simple memory beats learned temporal models, sometimes

      EdgeBank, a non-learning method that remembers past edges, matched or beat several temporal GNNs on link prediction, prompting a push for harder evaluation.

      Poursafaei et al., NeurIPS 2022
    4. 2023
      Simple linear forecasters

      A one-layer linear model outperformed several transformer forecasters on common benchmarks, which reset standards for evaluation.

      Zeng et al., AAAI 2023
    5. 2023
      Temporal Graph Benchmark; a graph foundation model for knowledge graphs

      TGB provided large, standardised datasets and a realistic evaluation protocol for temporal link and node prediction. ULTRA showed one pretrained model transferring to dozens of unseen knowledge graphs.

      Huang et al., NeurIPS 2023; Galkin et al., ICLR 2024
    6. 2024
      Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai

      Three pretrained forecasters from Amazon, Google and Salesforce: tokenised values with a T5 model; decoder-only patches; any-variate patch attention. GIFT-Eval followed as a benchmark for comparing them.

      Ansari et al.; Das et al., ICML 2024; Woo et al., ICML 2024; Aksu et al., 2024
    7. 2025
      Chronos-2 and the covariate turn

      Chronos-2 handles multivariate series, covariates and cross-series information in one pretrained model of around 119 million parameters, and led GIFT-Eval at launch. The authors note some overlap between their training data and GIFT-Eval's training splits, but not its test portions. A survey of graph foundation models also appeared.

      Ansari et al., arXiv 2510.15821; Wang et al., arXiv 2505.15116
    8. 2026
      Rankings keep moving

      Later comparisons report TimesFM-3.0 ahead of Chronos-2 on GIFT-Eval, but those tables come from third-party papers rather than the official leaderboard, and independent tests show first place changing with the dataset. For temporal graphs and fraud, recent preprints propose continuous-time graph transformers and conformal wrappers, with caution that public benchmark results may not transfer to real bank fraud.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Benchmark leakage and "zero-shot" that wasn't

      The test series (or a near copy, or the same period of the same public dataset) was in pretraining. The model looks remarkable, then disappoints on your private data.

      Spot itAsk what was in the pretraining corpus. Judge only on a period after the model's data cutoff, or on series that are private to you.
    • Not beating a seasonal baseline

      A glossy comparison to one weak competitor, such as the last value.

      Spot itDemand seasonal naive and a seasonal average with drift in the table, scored with a scale-free metric such as MASE over several forecast origins.
    • Outliers wrecking the scale

      Tokenised models scale by a context statistic. A data error or a one-off settlement in the context rescales everything.

      Spot itCheck the forecast's behaviour when you remove or cap the outlier. Clean obvious errors before forecasting.
    • Missing calendar knowledge

      Salary days, Eid, Ramadan, school fees, quarter-end. A univariate model sees only past data, and the Islamic calendar shifts about 11 days a year against the Gregorian one.

      Spot itPlot errors by day-of-month and around holidays. If they cluster there, add covariates or a calendar adjustment.
    • Over-confident intervals

      Sharp 80% intervals that contain 60% of outcomes.

      Spot itCount coverage on a held-out period. Recalibrate with conformal methods if it is off.
    • Regime breaks

      A new product, merger or rule change moves the level. No pretrained model can know.

      Spot itRun the change-point check on the history; track forecast bias over rolling windows and alert on a run of same-sign errors.
    • Graph leakage through future edges

      Features or neighbours computed on the full graph include transfers made after the prediction date. Offline AUC is high; live performance is not.

      Spot itCheck that the neighbourhood sampler filters by timestamp. Rebuild features as of each date and compare.
    • Label bias in graph training sets

      Known mules are those the old rules caught. The model learns to find what the rules found.

      Spot itLook at how labels were produced. Hold out cases found by other means (customer complaints, external tip-offs) as a test.
    • Static graph, time-blind reading

      A chain of payments that never was a chain because of ordering. Investigators chase false leads.

      Spot itAsk whether paths respect timestamps and a plausible hold window; compare time-respecting counts with static ones as in Simulation 4.
    • Cost and opacity

      A large pretrained model is a vendor-style dependency: weights, licence, versioning, GPU for large volumes.

      Spot itPin the model version and licence, store its outputs for audit, and keep the baseline running as a fallback.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These are forecasting models trained once on a huge variety of series, so they can give a reasonable forecast for a new ATM or branch with almost no history of its own. I would use them as a challenger and for cold starts, and keep our simple seasonal model as the benchmark. If the pretrained model does not beat it on our own data, over a period it has not seen, we do not adopt it.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Chronos scales by mean absolute value and quantises to a few thousand tokens; TimesFM works on patches with a decoder-only transformer and writes longer output blocks. I'd evaluate zero-shot against seasonal naive and seasonal average with drift, scoring MASE and quantile loss over rolling origins after the model's data cutoff, and check interval coverage. For graphs I'd ask first whether engineered time-windowed path features in a boosted tree already capture the signal, and make sure neighbour sampling respects timestamps. Only then would I justify a temporal GNN.”
    “Can we replace our forecasting models with Chronos?”

    Not on faith. It is a strong generic forecaster and a good fit for short or new series. For long, stable series with good calendar features, tuned local models are often as good. Run it as a challenger on rolling out-of-time windows, compare with a seasonal baseline and with interval coverage, and decide per segment. Model risk will also want the training-data and versioning questions answered.

    “What does zero-shot mean here, exactly?”

    The model weights are not updated on your series; you pass recent history as context and read off the forecast. It still conditions on that context, so the quality of the history matters. Light fine-tuning on your data is a middle path if zero-shot falls short.

    “Why not just use an LLM for forecasting?”

    General language models can be prompted with numbers, but they are slow, costly and poorly calibrated on numeric series. Purpose-built forecasters are far smaller and trained for the task. Chronos borrows the language-model recipe, but is not a chatbot.

    “Is a graph neural network better than our rules and features for mule detection?”

    It can add signal where relationships matter, such as ring structure and relay speed. But I'd start with time-windowed graph features in a gradient-boosted model, which is simpler to validate and explain, and add a GNN if it improves precision at our alert capacity on a time-ordered test set. Timestamps must be respected throughout, or we will measure leakage.

    “Could we use one graph foundation model across all our products?”

    It's an attractive idea, but the evidence is early and mostly on public or knowledge-graph benchmarks. Our graphs are large, noisy and have sparse labels. I'd treat it as research to track, and test it as a challenger rather than rely on it.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A context has mean absolute value AED 50m. With 4,096 bins over ±15 times the scale, what is the finest difference represented? What if one data error pushes the mean to AED 100m?

    30 × 50 ÷ 4,096 ≈ AED 0.37m. After the outlier, 30 × 100 ÷ 4,096 ≈ AED 0.73m. The resolution doubled in coarseness for every normal day, and the outlier itself is clipped if it is above 15 × the new scale.

    2. Why can Chronos give a forecast distribution without an extra model of uncertainty?

    Its output is a probability distribution over the next token. Sampling many paths, each token fed back in, gives many futures. Quantiles of those paths are the intervals. Whether the intervals are well calibrated is a separate question to test.

    3. A context of 512 values is split into patches of 32. How many tokens, and how many attention pairs compared with patch length 1?

    16 tokens and 256 pairs, against 512 tokens and 262,144 pairs: a thousand-fold reduction.

    4. You need a 400-step horizon. How many passes with output patch 128, and with one value per pass?

    ceil(400/128) = 4 passes, against 400. Fewer passes mean fewer chances for fed-back errors to compound.

    5. A foundation model beats "repeat last week" on 14 ATMs. Should you adopt it?

    Not yet. Compare with a seasonal average and drift too, over many forecast origins, on more series, after the model's data cutoff, with a scale-free metric and interval coverage. Fourteen ATMs and one weak baseline is anecdote.

    6. Month-end salary spikes are known in advance. Why does that matter for model choice?

    A univariate model sees them only as past pattern, and they sit on a calendar it cannot infer reliably. A model that accepts known-future covariates, or a local model with calendar features, uses them directly. Covariate support is a key differentiator in newer pretrained forecasters.

    7. In Simulation 4, funds reach E at hour 5 and an edge E to F occurs at hour 1. Is F linked in the static graph? In the time-respecting one?

    Static: yes, there is an edge. Time-respecting: no, the transfer happened before the money arrived, so it cannot be part of the relay. This is the false lead the time-aware view removes.

    8. What is over-squashing, and how do graph transformers address it?

    In message passing, information from distant nodes must pass through a narrow set of intermediate connections and gets compressed into fixed-size vectors. Graph transformers let any node attend to any other directly, with structural encodings so the model knows where nodes sit. The price is quadratic attention cost.
