    Pretrained Forecasting Models

    Part 1

    What a pretrained forecaster is

    In one sentenceA pretrained forecasting model (a time-series foundation model) has already learned from billions of data points across thousands of unrelated series, so you can hand it the history of a new series and get a forecast back without training anything.

    Our running example is a café with six weeks of daily sales and a need for the next fortnight. A new café has too little history to fit a good model of its own. A pretrained forecaster has seen many series with weekly dips and gentle trends, so it recognises the shape and carries it forward.

    "Zero-shot" is the name for this: no training on your series. It does not mean no judgement is needed.

    How it differs from other forecasters

    • Classical models (ARIMA, ETS): fit one model per series. Good with long history, awkward when you have thousands of series or new ones.
    • Gradient-boosted trees on lag features: you build the features, and train on your own data. Strong, but needs effort per problem.
    • Simple baselines: "repeat last week" or "average each weekday". Free and surprisingly hard to beat.
    Part 2

    How it works

    These models borrow the language-model idea: read a sequence, predict what comes next. The only trick is turning numbers into something a language model can read.

    1. Scale. Divide the history by its average size, so a café selling 400 a day and a bank moving millions both sit near 1.
    2. Turn into tokens. Chronos (Amazon) rounds each value into one of a few thousand bins and treats the bin number like a word. TimesFM (Google) instead groups values into patches, say 32 days per token.
    3. Predict the next token. A transformer, the same kind of network as in chatbots, predicts what comes next, trained on a huge mixed collection of series.
    4. Sample or read off ranges. Chronos draws many possible futures, and the spread gives an interval. Newer models output quantiles directly.
    5. Undo the scaling to get back to real units.

    Patches make long histories and long horizons much cheaper: a 512-value history becomes 16 tokens at 32 per patch. The price is less detail inside each patch. Chronos pays a different price: rounding to bins costs precision, and one wild outlier inflates the scale, making every bin coarser for the normal days.

    Later versions moved on. Chronos-Bolt uses patches and a direct quantile output for faster forecasts, and Chronos-2 can take related series and known future inputs such as a holiday calendar.

    How to read the output

    • Median line: the single most likely path. Use it as your point forecast.
    • 10%-90% range: about 8 times in 10 the real value should land inside. If it lands inside only half the time, the range is too narrow and over-confident.
    • A wide range is honest. For cash or stock planning, a sharp but wrong range is worse than a wider true one.
    • Judge against a baseline. If "average each weekday over recent weeks, plus the trend" matches the model on your data, the model is not earning its keep.

    A worked reading: the café forecast says Saturday median 520 with a range of 440 to 600. That means roughly 80% confidence that Saturday lands between 440 and 600, not that it will hit 520.

    Part 3

    When to use it

    ApproachTraining neededBest when
    Pretrained modelNone (optional light fine-tune)Many series, short or new histories
    Seasonal average + driftNoneLong, clean, stable series; your first baseline
    Classical (ARIMA, ETS)One fit per seriesA few important series with long history
    Boosted treesYes, plus featuresRich known inputs: prices, promotions, calendar
    • Strong at: new series with little history, one model for thousands of series, and (for the better models) honest ranges.
    • Weak at: patterns driven by things it cannot see, like pay days, Ramadan or a product launch. It also cannot foresee a sudden level jump, and one data error can distort the scaling.
    • Heavy at scale: running a transformer over millions of series daily costs far more than a seasonal average. Sampling-based Chronos is the slowest.

    Usable today? Yes, as a quick first forecast and a benchmark, with open weights for Chronos and TimesFM. Treat it as one candidate to test, not a replacement for baselines. If a gap to your baseline remains, light fine-tuning on your own data is the next step.

    A reminder from the research: Zeng et al. (2023) showed a single linear layer beating several elaborate transformer forecasters, so always keep a simple model in the race.

    In banking, the natural uses are cash demand per ATM (including the newly installed machines), branch footfall and daily card volumes, checked for salary-day effects first.

    Part 4

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Skipping the baseline

      A fancy model that loses to "repeat last week" is a cost, not progress. Always score against a seasonal average first.

    • Ignoring known calendar effects

      Salary days and Eid are known in advance, but a series-only model cannot see them. Use a model that accepts covariates, or fine-tune.

    • Judging by accuracy alone

      Also check whether the 10%-90% range covers about 80% of outcomes. Over-confident ranges cause bad plans.

    • Feeding it dirty data

      One typo or outage spike can rescale the whole input. Clean the history first.

    1. What does "zero-shot" mean for a forecaster?

    It forecasts your series without any training on it, using what it learned from other series.

    2. How does Chronos turn numbers into tokens?

    It scales the series, then rounds each value into one of a few thousand bins and uses the bin number as a token.

    3. Why do patches help?

    Several values share one token, so long histories and horizons need far fewer steps. The cost is less detail inside each patch.

    4. A new ATM has three weeks of history. Which approach suits?

    A pretrained model, since it can carry in the weekly shape from similar series. Compare it with a seasonal average anyway.

    5. The model's 80% range holds the real value only half the time. What is wrong?

    The range is over-confident. It is too narrow, so plans built on it would be too tight.
