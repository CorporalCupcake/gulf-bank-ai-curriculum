    What a Model Can Read, Why It Makes Things Up, and Which Kind of Model Fits the Job

    Why a context window is a memory bill and not just a number on a spec sheet, why fluent language models state falsehoods and which defences actually cut the rate, and how encoders, decoders and embedding models divide the work.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA language model can only use what is in its window, the window costs memory and money that grow with every token, and because the model is trained to produce plausible text rather than checked truth, you control hallucination by grounding it, letting it abstain and verifying outputs, and by picking the cheapest kind of model that does the job.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture an analyst with a desk. Everything she can reason about right now is on the desk: the customer's question, the policy extracts, the chat so far. Nothing else exists for her. That desk is the context window. A bigger desk lets you lay out a whole loan agreement. But every sheet you add has to be stored, reread and paid for, and the more cluttered the desk, the more likely she misses the one paragraph that matters.

    Now give her a peculiar habit. She was trained, over years, on an enormous library, to continue any sentence in the most plausible way. She is excellent at it. Ask her for the penalty clause in a contract she has never seen, and the most plausible continuation is a clause that sounds right. She does not feel a difference between recalling and inventing. That is hallucination, and it follows from the training goal, not from a bug.

    Finally, not every job needs her. Some jobs need a reader who only labels and matches (an encoder), some need a writer (a decoder), and finding the right paragraph among two million needs a librarian who files everything by meaning (an embedding model). Using the writer for every job is expensive and often worse.

    Hold onto thisThree questions to ask of any LLM design. What is in the window, and what did it cost to put it there? Where could the model be guessing, and what stops the guess reaching a customer? Is a generator the right tool at all, or would a smaller encoder or an embedding model do it better and cheaper?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Context windows: what limits how much a model can read

    A model reads tokens, not words. Its context window is the maximum number of tokens it can handle in one go, usually counting your prompt, any retrieved documents, the conversation so far and the answer it writes. Past the limit, text must be cut or summarised. Nothing carries over between calls unless you put it back in.

    Why is there a limit? Three reasons, and they stack.

    • Attention compares every token with every other. In attention, each of n tokens looks at all earlier tokens. The work to read a prompt of n tokens grows with n squared. Double the prompt, roughly four times the attention work. Techniques such as FlashAttention make this much faster and avoid storing the huge n-by-n table, but the arithmetic is still there.
    • Positions must be learned. The model knows where a token sits through a positional scheme (most modern models use rotary embeddings). A model trained on 8,000-token documents has never seen position 100,000. Providers extend the range with scaling tricks and further training on long documents (for example YaRN), and quality at the far end is usually weaker than near the start.
    • Memory. While generating, the model keeps a record of everything it has read. That record, the KV cache, is next.

    A feel for sizes: about 500 English words fit in 650 to 700 tokens. A 100-page loan agreement is on the order of 60,000 to 80,000 tokens in English. Arabic usually costs more tokens per word than English in most tokenisers, so the same agreement in Arabic can need noticeably more; measure with the tokeniser you will actually use rather than assume.

    The KV cache: remembering instead of recomputing

    Inside every attention layer, each token produces three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I hand over if chosen?). When the model writes token 501, it needs the keys and values of tokens 1 to 500. Those never change, because the model only looks backwards. So it stores them. That store is the KV cache. Generating a new token then means computing one new query, key and value, and reading the cache.

    Without the cache the model would rerun the whole sequence at every step. Simulation 1 puts numbers on that.

    The saving is real, but it is bought with memory. The cache holds, for every layer and every token, one key vector and one value vector per key-value head:

    bytes per token=2×L×hkv×dhead×b

    The 2 is key plus value. L is the number of layers, hkv the number of key-value heads, dhead the size of each head, b the bytes per number (2 at 16-bit). For a 70-billion-parameter-class shape (80 layers, 8 key-value heads, head size 128) at 16-bit: 2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 327,680 bytes, about 320 KiB per token. Multiply by tokens and by simultaneous users.

    At 128,000 tokens that is about 40 GiB for one conversation. The model's weights are loaded once and shared by everyone; the cache is private to each conversation. So at long context the cache, not the weights, decides how many users one GPU can serve. Two more facts matter for reading speeds. Prefill (reading the prompt) is compute-heavy and parallel, so it is fast per token. Decode (writing) is one token at a time and mostly limited by how fast the GPU can stream weights and cache from memory, so a long cache slows every output token.

    The industry has attacked the cache bill in four ways, all of which you will hear named:

    • Fewer key-value heads. Multi-query attention (one shared head) and grouped-query attention (a few shared heads, now standard) shrink the cache several-fold with small quality loss. DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention goes further and stores a compressed latent instead.
    • Smaller numbers. Store the cache at 8-bit or 4-bit (a cousin of quantisation).
    • Smarter memory management. PagedAttention (the idea behind vLLM) stores the cache in small blocks like virtual memory, wasting little. Prefix caching reuses the cache of a shared opening, such as a long system prompt, across requests, so it is read once.
    • Keeping less. Sliding windows, token eviction and compression keep only the tokens judged important. Active research (Part 4); the risk is evicting the one token you needed.

    One more thing about big windows: fits is not the same as works. Several findings agree. "Lost in the middle" (Liu et al.) showed that models answer best when the relevant passage is at the start or end of the input and worse when it sits in the middle. The RULER benchmark showed that models which pass a simple needle-in-a-haystack test often degrade on harder long-context tasks well before the advertised window, giving a shorter "effective" length. Practitioners now call the general effect context rot. The honest design rule: put the most important material at the edges, send only what the task needs, and test your own task at your own lengths. More context is a cost and a risk as well as a capability. Context engineering and chunking are how you act on this.

    Cost follows tokens. Providers charge per input and output token, typically more for output, and discount input that hits a cached prefix. Check the current price sheet; the structure matters more than any figure. Put stable text (instructions, policy, schema) first and variable text last, and the cache works for you. See model choice, cost and latency.

    Why LLMs hallucinate, and which mitigations actually work

    A hallucination is output that is fluent and confident but not supported by reality or by the source you gave. Two kinds are worth separating (Ji et al.): unfaithful output contradicts or ignores the document in the prompt (the summary says 4.5% when the contract says 5.4%); unfactual output states something false about the world (a fee that does not exist). Different causes, different fixes.

    Why it happens

    • The goal is plausibility. Pretraining rewards predicting the next token of real text. A correct fee and a plausible fee are both likely continuations; nothing marks which is true.
    • Some facts are seen once. For arbitrary facts (a person's birthday, a regulation's article number) a model that matches its training data must make mistakes on those it saw rarely. Kalai and Vempala showed the error rate is at least about the share of facts that appeared once in training. Bigger models see fewer facts only once, but never none.
    • The scoring rewards guessing. Most benchmarks give 1 for right and 0 for wrong or blank. Then guessing never loses. Kalai and colleagues (2025) argue that post-training on such scores teaches models to bluff, like a student on a multiple-choice exam. A model that says "I don't know" scores worse on the leaderboard than one that guesses.
    • Errors compound. Each token is conditioned on the previous ones, including the model's own earlier mistakes, so one invented detail becomes the premise for the next.
    • No access to the facts. The model has a training cutoff, has never seen your internal policy, and cannot look anything up unless you give it a tool.
    • Sampling and pleasing. Randomness (temperature) adds variation; post-training for helpfulness can reward agreeable answers over accurate ones (sycophancy).

    One result is comforting. Base models are often well calibrated: when they say "70% sure", they are right about 70% of the time (Kadavath et al.). Post-training tends to damage that calibration, which is why abstaining sensibly has to be built back in.

    Abstention deserves a closer look because it turns "how often is it wrong?" into a business decision. Suppose a model sets a confidence for each answer. You choose a threshold: above it the model answers, below it a human takes over. A wrong answer costs W (a mis-sold product, a complaint, a regulator's attention). A hand-over costs H (staff time). If the confidence is honest, answer when the expected cost of being wrong is below the cost of a hand-over:

    answer if(1−c)·W<H⇔c>1−HW

    c is the probability that the answer is right. With W = AED 500 and H = AED 15, answer only above 97% confidence. If the model's stated confidence is inflated (says 97% but is right 94% of the time), the threshold you set on its stated number must be higher than the formula says. That gap is why calibration is measured, not assumed (F7).

    Encoders, decoders and embedding models: which kind fits which job

    The transformer comes in three arrangements. The difference is one rule about attention: may a token look forward, or only back?

    TypeHow it readsTrained byGood atExamples
    EncoderEvery token sees every other, both directions. Output: a vector per token.Masked-word filling (hide words, predict them).Understanding: classification, entity extraction, similarity. Cannot write text.BERT, RoBERTa, ModernBERT; Arabic: AraBERT, CAMeLBERT
    DecoderEach token sees only earlier ones (the causal mask). Output: the next token.Next-token prediction.Generation, following instructions, reasoning in text, extraction as text.GPT, Claude, Llama, Qwen, Jais, Falcon, Gemini
    Encoder-decoderAn encoder reads the input fully; a decoder writes the output while attending to it.Text-to-text tasks.Translation, summarisation, fixed input-to-output mappings.T5, BART
    Embedding model (bi-encoder)An encoder (or a decoder adapted to the purpose) turns a whole text into one vector.Contrastive learning: pull matching pairs together, push others apart.Search by meaning, deduplication, clustering, retrieval for RAG.Sentence-BERT lineage; BGE-M3; multilingual-e5
    Cross-encoder (reranker)Reads the query and one candidate together and outputs a relevance score.Labelled relevance pairs.Precise re-ordering of a short list. Too slow to run on millions.MS MARCO-style rerankers

    Three ideas make the table usable.

    • Bidirectional understands, causal generates. Seeing the whole sentence helps classify it, so a small encoder often beats a much larger decoder on a narrow labelling task per unit of cost. But an encoder has no way to produce the next word. Decoders won the general field because one decoder can do almost any task from instructions, which is why they sit behind chatbots and agents.
    • Two-stage search. A bi-encoder embeds every document once, ahead of time. At query time you embed the question and find the nearest vectors (F1), which is fast over millions. But the document never saw the query, so ranking is approximate. A cross-encoder reads query and document together and ranks better, but costs one model call per pair. So you retrieve a few dozen with the embedding model and rerank them with the cross-encoder. See hybrid search.
    • The lines are blurring. Modern embedding models are often built on decoder LLMs and given a pooling step. Newer encoders such as ModernBERT bring longer windows (8,192 tokens) and recent training tricks. And embeddings can be truncated to shorter vectors (Matryoshka embeddings). The training objective (contrastive learning) is what makes an embedding model, more than the architecture.

    Practical constraints you should remember. An embedding model's vectors only make sense with that model: change the model and you must re-embed the entire index. Embedding and encoder models have their own token limits (often 512 to 8,192), much smaller than a chat model's window, which is why documents are chunked. And language coverage matters: a model trained mostly on English may match Arabic dialects and transliterated names poorly. Test on your own Arabic and English queries.

    As a rule: if the job is to label, match or retrieve, start with an encoder or embedding model, and use a decoder when you need to write, reason in text, or handle variety you cannot enumerate. Try the sorter below.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Customer-facing assistants. A wrong fee, rate or Shariah-compliance statement from a chatbot is a conduct problem. (A well-known case: in 2024 a Canadian tribunal held Air Canada responsible for wrong refund policy stated by its chatbot; I cite it from memory.) Grounding, abstention and handover are the design.
    • Document review. Long loan agreements, trade-finance sets and KYC files push against window limits. The temptation to dump everything into a million-token window should be tested against retrieval of the relevant clauses, on accuracy and on cost.
    • Internal copilots. Policy search is mostly embedding and reranking; the generator writes the final summary from a handful of passages.
    • Complaint and KYC text classification. High volume, fixed labels, strict latency: often a fine-tuned encoder, kept as a baseline against an LLM.
    • Cost and capacity planning. Concurrent users times context length sets GPU memory in an on-premises or sovereign deployment, where you cannot simply buy more.
    • Data governance. The cache and any prefix caching hold customer text in memory on the serving side. Ask the provider, or your platform team, how long it is kept and where.

    Where the field is now

    Three conversations dominate. How far can windows grow before the KV cache and context rot stop it? Can hallucination be reduced by changing how models are scored rather than only how they are built? And do encoders still matter in an LLM world?

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • "It fits, so it works"

      A 300-page document is pasted in because the window allows it, and the answer misses a clause in the middle.

      Spot itRun your task with the key passage at the start, middle and end of the input. If accuracy moves, you have a position effect.
    • Ignoring the memory bill

      A pilot with five users works. Production with five hundred concurrent long conversations needs many times the GPUs.

      Spot itAsk for concurrent users times context length times bytes per token. If nobody can say, nobody has sized it.
    • Truncating silently

      Long chat histories are cut at the limit and the model forgets the customer's earlier instruction, or the system prompt gets pushed out.

      Spot itLog the token count of every call and what was dropped. Check the cut keeps instructions and the latest turn.
    • Believing temperature 0 or "be accurate" fixes it

      Output is stable and still wrong.

      Spot itAsk for the error rate on a labelled domain sample, with and without the control. If no one measured, the claim is unsupported.
    • Citations that do not support the claim

      RAG answers carry a reference, but the passage says something else, or the model cites a source it was not given.

      Spot itSample answers and check each citation against its passage by hand. Measure how often the cited passage actually entails the claim.
    • Unfaithful summaries of numbers

      A summary changes 5.4% to 4.5%, or AED 1.2 million to AED 12 million.

      Spot itProgrammatically check that every number and name in the output appears in the source. Cheap and catches a lot.
    • Using stated confidence as a probability

      "The model is 95% confident" taken at face value.

      Spot itBin answers by stated confidence and compare to actual accuracy on a labelled set. If the 95% bin is right 80% of the time, thresholds need moving.
    • An LLM for every job

      Paying for a large decoder to tag 40 million short texts into twelve categories.

      Spot itAsk for a baseline of a small fine-tuned encoder or logistic regression on embeddings, at the same accuracy target.
    • Swapping the embedding model without re-indexing

      Queries embedded with the new model are compared with documents embedded by the old one. Retrieval quietly collapses.

      Spot itStore the embedding model name and version beside every index, and refuse to query on mismatch.
    • Testing in English only

      The retrieval and model look fine on English questions; Arabic questions and mixed-script names fail.

      Spot itSplit the evaluation by language and dialect and look at each score separately.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “These models write the most plausible text, not the verified text, so some wrong answers are built in. We manage that three ways: we make the model answer only from our own approved documents and show its source, we let it say 'I'm not sure' and pass to a person when it isn't, and we check a sample every week. A longer memory window doesn't remove that risk, and it raises the cost on every call, so we send only what each question needs.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Context cost has two parts: attention work growing with the square of prompt length at prefill, and a KV cache growing linearly per token per conversation, which at long context limits concurrency more than the weights do. GQA, cache quantisation and paged or prefix caching are the standard mitigations. For hallucination I separate faithfulness from factuality, ground with retrieval and cite, constrain outputs, verify numbers against the source, and set abstention thresholds on measured calibration rather than stated confidence. For labelling I'd baseline a fine-tuned encoder before reaching for a decoder.”
    “Why not just use the million-token model and skip retrieval?”

    Sometimes you can, for a one-off analysis. At volume it loses on cost (you pay for every token every call, less any cache discount), latency (long prefill), and often accuracy (position effects and context rot). Retrieval also lets you show exactly which passage supported the answer and apply access controls per document. I'd benchmark both on our own questions and choose on accuracy per dirham.

    “Will a bigger model stop the hallucinations?”

    It reduces them, especially on well-known facts, but not to zero. Rare facts and facts it has never seen (our internal policies) stay risky, and scoring that rewards guessing keeps pushing models to answer. So we design for the residual error: grounding, abstention, verification and review sized to the risk of the use case.

    “Is RAG enough to prevent hallucinations?”

    No. It removes the most common cause, missing knowledge, but the model can still misread the passage, cite loosely, or answer when retrieval returned nothing relevant. I'd add an explicit 'not found' path, citation checks and a numeric match against the source, and measure the rate on a labelled sample.

    “What does the KV cache have to do with our GPU budget?”

    Each live conversation keeps a cache proportional to its length. For a 70B-class model with grouped-query attention at 16-bit it's around 320 KiB per token, so about 40 GiB for a 128k-token conversation. Weights are shared; caches are not. Concurrency times context length therefore sets how many users a GPU serves, which is why cache quantisation and shorter prompts are budget levers.

    “Do we need an LLM to classify complaints?”

    Not necessarily. For fixed categories at high volume, a fine-tuned encoder (or even logistic regression on embeddings) is cheaper, faster and easier to validate. An LLM is a good baseline and handles new categories without retraining. I'd compare them on a labelled Arabic and English sample, on accuracy, latency and cost per thousand.

    “How do you decide when the assistant should hand over to a person?”

    From the cost of a wrong answer against the cost of a handover, applied to a confidence that has been checked for calibration on our own questions. The break-even is answer when confidence exceeds one minus H over W. If confidence is inflated, the threshold moves up. Then we monitor the wrong-answer rate among those it answered.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A prompt of 2,000 tokens produces a 200-token answer. How many token positions are processed without a KV cache, and with one?

    Without: n×P + n(n−1)/2 = 200×2,000 + 200×199/2 = 400,000 + 19,900 = 419,900. With: P + n − 1 = 2,199. About 190 times fewer.

    2. A model has 32 layers, 8 key-value heads, head size 128, and a 16-bit cache. How much cache per token, and for a 32,768-token conversation?

    2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes = 128 KiB per token. Times 32,768 tokens = 4 GiB.

    3. You halve the number of key-value heads and quantise the cache from 16-bit to 8-bit. What happens to cache size?

    Each change halves it, so a quarter of the original. Quality effects are small but not zero, so test on your own tasks.

    4. Why does doubling the prompt length roughly quadruple the attention work at prefill, but only double the KV cache?

    Attention compares every token with every earlier one, so pairs grow with n squared. The cache stores one key and one value per token, so it grows with n.

    5. Wrong answers cost AED 800 and a handover costs AED 20. With honest confidence, above what confidence should the model answer?

    1 − 20/800 = 0.975. Answer when it is more than 97.5% likely to be right.

    6. Give two reasons a model fine on a 5-passage test might fail on a 200-passage version of the same task even though both fit in the window.

    Position effects (the relevant passage now lands in the middle) and context rot more generally (more distractors dilute attention). Also more cost and latency. Test at realistic lengths.

    7. Why does a benchmark that scores right = 1, wrong = 0, blank = 0 encourage hallucination?

    Guessing has a chance of scoring and no penalty, so answering always beats abstaining in expectation, however unsure the model is. Training and selecting models on such scores rewards confident guesses. A penalty for wrong answers, or credit for abstaining, changes that.

    8. Which model type for: (a) finding the closest 20 policy paragraphs in a corpus of 3 million; (b) re-ordering those 20; (c) drafting a reply in Arabic?

    (a) Embedding model (bi-encoder) with a vector index. (b) Cross-encoder reranker. (c) Decoder LLM.

    9. You upgrade the embedding model and only re-embed new documents. What breaks?

    Old and new vectors live in different spaces, so similarities between a new-model query and old-model documents are meaningless. Re-embed the whole index or keep separate indexes per model version.

    10. A summary says "interest of 4.5%" where the source says "5.4%". Which kind of hallucination is that, and what cheap check catches it?

    Unfaithful (contradicts the source), not unfactual. Extract all numbers from the output and confirm each appears in the source text; flag any that do not.
