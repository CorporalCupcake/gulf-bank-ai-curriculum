    What a Model Can Read, Why It Makes Things Up, and Which Kind of Model Fits the Job

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    The context window is an analyst's desk. Everything she can reason about is on it: the customer's question, policy extracts, the chat so far. A bigger desk fits a whole loan agreement, but every sheet is stored, reread and paid for, and the more cluttered it gets, the likelier she misses the one paragraph that matters.

    She was trained to continue any sentence in the most plausible way. Ask for the penalty clause in a contract she has never seen, and the most plausible continuation is a clause that sounds right. She feels no difference between recalling and inventing. That is hallucination: it follows from the training goal, not from a bug.

    Not every job needs her. Labelling needs a reader (an encoder), writing needs a writer (a decoder), and finding one paragraph among two million needs a librarian who files by meaning (an embedding model). Using the writer for everything is expensive and often worse.

    Hold onto thisAsk of any LLM design: What is in the window, and what did it cost? Where could the model be guessing, and what stops that reaching a customer? Would a smaller encoder or embedding model do the job better and cheaper?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Context windows: what limits how much a model can read

    A model reads tokens, not words. The context window is the maximum tokens it handles in one go: prompt, retrieved documents, conversation so far and its answer. Past the limit, text is cut or summarised, and nothing carries over between calls unless you put it back. Rough sizes: 500 English words is about 650 tokens; a 100-page loan agreement is 60,000 to 80,000. Arabic usually costs more tokens per word, so measure with your own tokeniser.

    Why is the window limited? Mainly two reasons:

    • Attention compares every token with every other. Double the prompt and the work grows about four times, like a meeting where everyone must shake everyone's hand (attention).
    • Memory. The model keeps a record of everything it has read, the KV cache, next.

    Also, a model trained on short documents has never seen far-away positions, so quality at the far end of a stretched window is usually weaker.

    The KV cache: remembering instead of recomputing

    Think of a student taking notes. Rereading the whole textbook for every new sentence is absurd; she reads her notes. In each attention layer every token produces a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I hand over if chosen?). To write token 501 the model needs the keys and values of tokens 1 to 500. They never change, so the model stores them: the KV cache. Without it, the model reruns the whole sequence at every step (Simulation 1).

    The saving is paid for in memory. The cache holds a key and a value for every token, in every layer. For a 70-billion-parameter-class model that is roughly 320 KiB per token, so about 40 GiB for one conversation at 128,000 tokens. Weights are loaded once and shared, like one textbook for a class; the cache is each student's private notes. At long context the cache, not the weights, decides how many users a GPU serves.

    Reading has two phases. Prefill (reading the prompt) is parallel and fast per token. Decode (writing) is one token at a time and limited by memory speed, so a long cache slows every output token.

    Three ways the industry shrinks the bill:

    • Fewer key-value heads. Grouped-query attention (now standard) shares keys and values across several heads, shrinking the cache several-fold at small quality cost.
    • Smaller numbers. Store the cache at 8-bit or 4-bit (a cousin of quantisation).
    • Prefix caching. Reuse the cache of a shared opening, such as a long system prompt, across requests.

    Fits is not the same as works. Like a long meeting, models remember the start and end better than the middle ("lost in the middle", Liu et al.). Models that pass simple needle-in-a-haystack tests often degrade on harder long tasks well before the advertised window (RULER); this is called context rot. Put key material at the edges, send only what the task needs, and test at your own lengths. See context engineering and chunking.

    Cost follows tokens. Output tokens cost more than input, and input that hits a cached prefix is discounted. Put stable text (instructions, policy) first and variable text last so the cache works for you. See model choice, cost and latency.

    Why LLMs hallucinate, and which mitigations actually work

    A hallucination is fluent, confident output that the world or your source does not support. Two kinds with different fixes: unfaithful output contradicts the document you supplied (summary says 4.5%, contract says 5.4%); unfactual output states something false about the world (a fee that does not exist).

    Why it happens

    • The goal is plausibility. Training rewards predicting the next token. A correct fee and a plausible fee are both likely continuations; nothing marks which is true.
    • Scoring rewards guessing. Most benchmarks give 1 for right and 0 for wrong or blank, so guessing never loses, like a student on a multiple-choice exam. Models trained against such scores learn to bluff.
    • No access to the facts. It has a training cutoff, has never seen your internal policy, and cannot look anything up without a tool. Facts seen only once in training are the shakiest, and one invented detail becomes the premise for the next.

    Base models are often well calibrated ("70% sure" is right about 70% of the time). Post-training tends to damage this, so sensible abstention must be built back in.

    Abstention turns "how often is it wrong?" into a business decision, like a call-centre agent deciding when to escalate. Let a wrong answer cost W (mis-sale, complaint) and a hand-over cost H (staff time). With honest confidence c, answer only when:

    answer ifc>1−HW

    With W = AED 500 and H = AED 15, answer only above 97% confidence. If the model's stated confidence is inflated, the threshold on the stated number must be higher. That is why calibration is measured, not assumed (F7).

    Encoders, decoders and embedding models: which kind fits which job

    The arrangements differ by one rule: may a token look forward, or only back?

    TypeHow it readsGood atExamples
    EncoderEvery token sees every other, both directions. Output: a vector per token.Understanding: classification, entity extraction, similarity. Cannot write text.BERT, ModernBERT; Arabic: AraBERT, CAMeLBERT
    DecoderEach token sees only earlier ones. Output: the next token.Generation, following instructions, reasoning in text.GPT, Claude, Llama, Qwen, Jais
    Encoder-decoderAn encoder reads the input fully; a decoder writes the output.Translation, summarisation, fixed input-to-output mappings.T5, BART
    Embedding model (bi-encoder)Turns a whole text into one vector, trained so matching texts land close together.Search by meaning, deduplication, clustering, retrieval for RAG.BGE-M3; multilingual-e5
    Cross-encoder (reranker)Reads the query and one candidate together and scores relevance.Precise re-ordering of a short list. Too slow for millions.MS MARCO-style rerankers
    • Bidirectional understands, causal generates. Seeing the whole sentence helps you label it, so a small encoder often beats a much larger decoder on a narrow labelling task, per unit of cost. One decoder can do almost any task from instructions, which is why decoders sit behind chatbots and agents.
    • Two-stage search, like a library. A bi-encoder files every document by meaning once, ahead of time; at query time you find the nearest vectors (F1), fast over millions. But the document never saw the query, so ranking is rough. A cross-encoder reads query and document together and ranks better, at one model call per pair. So retrieve a few dozen with the embedding model, then rerank them. See hybrid search.
    • Practical limits. Vectors only make sense with the model that made them: change the model and you re-embed the whole index. Embedding models have token limits far below a chat model's window, which is why documents are chunked. A mostly-English model may match Arabic dialects poorly, so test on your own queries.

    Rule: to label, match or retrieve, start with an encoder or embedding model. Use a decoder to write, reason in text, or handle variety you cannot enumerate.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "It fits, so it works"

      A 300-page document is pasted in because the window allows it, the answer misses a clause in the middle, or a long chat is silently truncated and the system prompt falls out.

      Spot itPut the key passage at the start, middle and end; if accuracy moves, you have a position effect. Log token counts and what was dropped.
    • Ignoring the memory bill

      A pilot with five users works. Five hundred concurrent long conversations need many times the GPUs.

      Spot itAsk for users times context length times cache size per token. If nobody can say, nobody has sized it.
    • Believing temperature 0 or "be accurate" fixes it

      Output is stable and still wrong.

      Spot itAsk for the error rate on a labelled domain sample, with and without the control. No measurement, no claim.
    • Citations and numbers that do not match the source

      A RAG answer carries a reference that says something else, or a summary turns 5.4% into 4.5%.

      Spot itSample and check citations by hand. Check programmatically that every number and name in the output appears in the source.
    • Using stated confidence as a probability

      "The model is 95% confident" taken at face value.

      Spot itBin answers by stated confidence and compare with actual accuracy. If the 95% bin is right 80% of the time, move the threshold.
    • The wrong model for the job, tested in one language

      A large decoder tags 40 million short texts into twelve categories; English questions look fine while Arabic and mixed-script names fail.

      Spot itAsk for a baseline of a small encoder or logistic regression on embeddings at the same accuracy target, and split evaluation by language.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why does a KV cache save so much work, and what does it cost in return?

    Without it, the model reprocesses the whole sequence for every new token; with it, each new token needs only one new position. The price is memory: a key and value for every token, per layer, per conversation.

    2. Why can a GPU that holds the model comfortably still serve only a few long conversations?

    The weights are loaded once and shared, but each conversation has its own cache, which grows with its length. At long context the caches, not the weights, fill the memory.

    3. You halve the key-value heads and quantise the cache from 16-bit to 8-bit. What happens to cache size?

    Each change halves it, so a quarter of the original. Quality loss is small but not zero, so test on your own tasks.

    4. Why does doubling the prompt roughly quadruple prefill attention work but only double the KV cache?

    Attention compares every token with every earlier one, so pairs grow with n squared. The cache stores one key and one value per token, so it grows with n.

    5. If a wrong answer is very costly and a hand-over is cheap, should the model's answer threshold be high or low? What if its stated confidence is inflated?

    High: only answer when nearly certain, and hand over the rest. If stated confidence is inflated, raise the threshold further, because "97% sure" may really mean 94%. Measure calibration on your own questions.

    6. Give two reasons a model fine on a 5-passage test might fail on a 200-passage version, though both fit the window.

    Position effects (the relevant passage lands in the middle) and context rot (more distractors dilute attention). Also more cost and latency. Test at realistic lengths.

    7. Why does a benchmark scoring right = 1, wrong = 0, blank = 0 encourage hallucination?

    Guessing can score and never loses, so answering beats abstaining however unsure the model is. Training on such scores rewards confident guesses. A penalty for wrong answers, or credit for abstaining, changes that.

    8. Which model type for: (a) the closest 20 policy paragraphs in a corpus of 3 million; (b) re-ordering those 20; (c) drafting a reply in Arabic?

    (a) Embedding model (bi-encoder) with a vector index. (b) Cross-encoder reranker. (c) Decoder LLM.

    9. You upgrade the embedding model and re-embed only new documents. What breaks?

    Old and new vectors live in different spaces, so new-query-to-old-document similarities are meaningless. Re-embed the whole index, or keep an index per model version.

    10. A summary says "interest of 4.5%" where the source says "5.4%". Which kind of hallucination, and what cheap check catches it?

    Unfaithful (contradicts the source), not unfactual. Extract every number from the output and flag any that is absent from the source.
