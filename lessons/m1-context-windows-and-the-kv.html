    Context Windows, Hallucination and Model Types

    Part 1

    Three ideas, one picture

    In one sentenceThe context window is the amount of text a model can read at once, a hallucination is a fluent answer the facts don't support, and the model type decides which jobs a model suits.

    Our running example is a bank's help chatbot answering questions about a fee policy. Think of the model as an analyst at a desk.

    How they differ from other things you know

    • Memory vs window. A model keeps nothing between calls. Anything it should "remember" must be put back in the window.
    • Hallucination vs a bug. It is not a fault in the code. The model was trained to continue text plausibly, and a correct fee and an invented fee are both plausible continuations.
    • A chat model vs other models. Not every job needs a text-writing model. Labelling needs a reader, searching needs a librarian.
    Part 2

    The context window

    A model reads tokens, small pieces of words, not words. The window is the maximum tokens it handles in one go: prompt, documents, chat history and its own answer. 500 English words is about 650 tokens, and a 100-page loan agreement is roughly 60,000 to 80,000. Arabic usually costs more tokens for the same meaning.

    Why the window is limited

    • Attention is pairwise. Every token is compared with every other. Double the prompt and the work grows about four times, like a meeting where everyone shakes everyone's hand.
    • Memory. The model stores a record of everything it has read: the KV cache.

    The KV cache: remembering instead of recomputing

    To write token 501, the model needs a "key" and a "value" for each of tokens 1 to 500. They never change, so the model saves them: a student reading her notes instead of rereading the textbook.

    How to think about the cost

    • Memory per user. For a 70-billion-parameter-class model the cache is roughly 320 KiB per token, about 40 GiB for one 128,000-token conversation. The model's weights are loaded once and shared; each conversation has its own private cache.
    • Prefill and decode. Reading the prompt (prefill) is parallel and fast. Writing (decode) is one token at a time, and a long cache slows every output token.
    • Shrinking the bill. Grouped-query attention shares keys and values across heads (now standard). The cache can be stored in 8-bit or 4-bit numbers. Prefix caching reuses the cache of a shared opening, such as a long system prompt.
    • Fits is not works. Models recall the start and end of a long text better than the middle ("lost in the middle", Liu et al.). Performance often degrades well before the advertised limit on hard tasks (RULER); this is called context rot. Put key material at the edges and send only what the task needs.
    Part 3

    Hallucination

    A hallucination is fluent, confident output that the world or your source does not support. There are two kinds, with different fixes:

    • Unfaithful: contradicts the document you gave it. The policy says 5.4%, the answer says 4.5%.
    • Unfactual: states something false about the world, such as a fee that does not exist.

    Why it happens

    • The goal is plausibility. Nothing in next-token training marks which continuation is true.
    • Scoring rewards guessing. Most benchmarks give 1 for right and 0 for wrong or blank, so guessing never loses, like a multiple-choice exam. Models trained against such scores learn to bluff.
    • No access to the facts. It has a training cut-off, has never seen your internal policy, and can't look anything up without a tool. One invented detail becomes the premise for the next.

    Base models are often well calibrated ("70% sure" is right about 70% of the time). Post-training tends to damage this, so sensible abstention must be built back in.

    What actually helps

    MitigationWhat it doesHonest verdict
    Retrieval (RAG): "answer only from these sources, cite them"Puts the real policy in the windowBiggest single gain for factual questions. Not a cure: it can misread or cite a source that doesn't support the claim
    Abstain"I can't answer that", or hand to a person when unsureWorks only if confidence is trustworthy; measure it on your own questions
    Tools for exact thingsCalculator, database lookup, rate tableRemoves whole classes of error
    Constrain the outputForce a schema, a fixed list or a quote that must existStops invented field names, doesn't make a value true
    Verify, then reviewSecond pass checks claims against the source; a person approves high-stakes answersCatches a useful share at extra cost
    Temperature 0, or "do not hallucinate"Repeatable output; a polite requestRepeatable is not correct. A concrete fallback ("if the sources don't say, reply NOT FOUND") works better

    Abstaining is a business decision. Say a wrong answer costs AED 500 (mis-sale, complaint) and a hand-over to staff costs AED 15. Answer only when the model's honest confidence is above 97%. If its stated confidence is inflated, the threshold on the stated number must be higher, which is why calibration is measured, not assumed.

    Show the formula

    Answer only if confidence > 1 − H ÷ W, where W is the cost of a wrong answer and H is the cost of a hand-over. With W = 500 and H = 15 that is 1 − 0.03 = 97%. The costlier a wrong answer, the closer to 100% you need.

    Part 4

    Which kind of model fits the job

    The kinds differ by one rule: may a token look forward as well as back?

    TypeGood atExamples
    EncoderUnderstanding: classifying, extracting entities, similarity. Cannot write text.BERT, ModernBERT; Arabic: AraBERT, CAMeLBERT
    DecoderWriting, following instructions, reasoning in textGPT, Claude, Llama, Qwen, Jais
    Encoder-decoderTranslation, summarising, fixed input-to-output tasksT5, BART
    Embedding model (bi-encoder)Turns a whole text into one vector so similar texts land close: search by meaning, deduplication, RAG retrievalBGE-M3, multilingual-e5
    Cross-encoder (reranker)Reads a query and one candidate together and scores relevance. Precise but too slow for millions.MS MARCO-style rerankers

    Strong at: a small encoder often beats a much larger decoder on a narrow labelling task, at a fraction of the cost. One decoder can do almost anything from instructions, which is why chatbots and agents use them.

    Two-stage search works like a library. An embedding model files every document by meaning once, so finding the nearest few dozen is fast. A cross-encoder then reranks those few for precision.

    • Vectors belong to their model. Change the embedding model and you must re-embed the whole index.

    Rule: to label, match or retrieve, start with an encoder or embedding model. Use a decoder to write, reason in text or handle variety you can't list.

    In banking, ask of any LLM design: what is in the window, where could it be guessing and what stops that reaching a customer, and would a smaller model do the job?

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • "It fits, so it works"

      A 300-page document is pasted in and a clause in the middle is missed. Test with the key passage at the start, middle and end, and log what was dropped.

    • Treating stated confidence as a probability

      If the "95%" answers are right 80% of the time, move the threshold. Check against a labelled set.

    • The wrong model for the job

      A large decoder labelling 40 million short texts looks fine in English and fails in Arabic. Compare with a small encoder and split results by language.

    1. Why does a KV cache save so much work, and what does it cost?

    Without it the model reprocesses the whole text for every new token; with it, each new token needs one new position. The price is memory per conversation.

    2. Why can a GPU that holds the model still serve only a few long conversations?

    The weights are shared, but each conversation has its own cache, which grows with its length. At long context the caches fill the memory.

    3. Why does a benchmark scoring right = 1, wrong = 0, blank = 0 encourage hallucination?

    Guessing can score and never loses, so it beats abstaining. A penalty for wrong answers, or credit for abstaining, changes that.

    4. A summary says 4.5% where the source says 5.4%. Which kind of hallucination, and what cheap check catches it?

    Unfaithful. Extract every number from the output and flag any that is absent from the source.

    5. Which model type for: the closest 20 policy paragraphs in 3 million, re-ordering those 20, and drafting an Arabic reply?

    Embedding model, then cross-encoder reranker, then decoder LLM.
