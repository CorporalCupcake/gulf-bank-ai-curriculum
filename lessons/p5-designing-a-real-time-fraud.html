    P4.1 Day one ★ Priority About 10 minutes

    Designing a Real-Time Fraud Scorer and a RAG Assistant

    Part 1

    Two systems, two kinds of problem

    In one sentenceA fraud scorer decides in milliseconds whether to approve a card payment, and a RAG assistant answers staff questions from the bank's own documents. Fraud is a speed and reliability problem; RAG is an accuracy and access-control problem.

    Both come up in almost every AI design interview. Before drawing any box, state four things: the decision, the budget (time, cost, error), the worst failure, and what the system does then.

    Part 2

    The fraud scorer

    A card is tapped at Dubai Mall. The bank has a fraction of a second to approve, decline or step up (ask for a one-time code or in-app confirmation).

    What it must guarantee

    • Latency: judged at p99, the time that 99% of requests beat, not the average.
    • Availability: payments cannot stop if the scorer is down, so it needs a defined fallback.
    • Audit trail: store inputs, score, model version and reason codes for every decision.

    How it works

    1. Features come first. The model needs counts like "transactions on this card in the last 10 minutes", kept fresh by a streaming job in a low-latency store. Training must use the same definitions, or the live model sees different inputs (online/offline skew).
    2. A boosted-tree model scores. It runs in milliseconds, is strong on tables and is easy to explain with reason codes. Heavier models run only on the unclear middle.
    3. A separate policy layer decides. It turns the score into approve, step-up or decline using costs, amount and review capacity. Changing a cut-off takes minutes; retraining takes weeks.
    4. Fallbacks are tested. If features time out, use defaults under a stricter policy. If the model is down, use rules or the last good model.
    5. Everything is logged. Labels arrive weeks late and only for what was flagged, so review a small random slice of the rest.

    How to read the latency budget

    Customers feel the slow requests, not the median. At 3,000 payments a second, 1 in 100 slow means 30 late payments every second.

    • Fan-out hurts. Ten lookups that are each slow 1% of the time make nearly one request in ten slow, even if every service looks healthy.
    • So count the calls. Pre-join features into one record per card, set hard timeouts with defaults, and place the scorer next to its store.
    • Step-up beats a hard decline for the uncertain middle. A genuine customer confirms in seconds; a fraudster usually cannot. Measure the friction and drop-off.
    • Fail open or closed? Open keeps payments flowing but raises fraud; closed blocks customers. Decide by amount and merchant risk, and write it down.

    Fraudsters probe the system, so never return the score or reason to the channel. Instant payments are hard to recall, so decide correctly before the money moves.

    Part 3

    The RAG assistant

    RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means: look up relevant passages in your own documents, hand them to a language model, and have it answer from them. It is an open-book exam. A relationship manager asks about a policy; the answer must be right, cited and limited to documents they may see.

    Ask first

    • Who asks? Staff with different permissions, writing in Arabic, English or a mix.
    • What must never happen? Showing a document the user may not read, or stating a policy that does not exist.
    • How will you know it works? Collect 100 to 300 real questions with approved answers and source passages first, including some the documents cannot answer.

    How it works

    1. Parse, chunk and tag. Most failures start here: a flattened table, a scanned Arabic page read in the wrong order, a clause cut from its heading. Store title, version, effective date and access list with every chunk.
    2. Retrieve. Use keyword and vector search together, then a reranker. Keywords catch product codes and clause numbers; vectors catch paraphrases and cross-language matches.
    3. Generate. Answer only from the passages, cite them, and say when they lack the answer. Treat retrieved text as data, never as instructions.
    4. Check. Verify that each citation supports its claim.

    How to think about it

    • Permissions belong in retrieval. "Do not reveal confidential documents" is a request, not a control. Once a restricted passage is in the model's context, a clever question can extract it.
    • Pre-filter beats post-filter. Post-filtering takes the top 10 matches and drops what the user cannot see. For a junior who may read 20% of documents, that leaves about 2 passages, and none in about one case in nine. Pre-filtering ranks only permitted chunks, so all 10 are useful.
    • A chain is as strong as its weakest link. Five stages at 90% each give only about 59% overall, so "the demo worked" is weak evidence. Spend effort on the weakest stage first.
    • Evaluate stages separately. Was the right passage in the top 10? Given that, did the model answer correctly? Add refusal, permission and Arabic tests, and run the set on every change.

    Permission changes must reach the index within an agreed time, and summaries or caches from restricted sources inherit the restriction. Agents act with the asking user's rights, not the service account's.

    Part 4

    Design choices at a glance

    QuestionFraud scorerRAG assistant
    Who calls it?Payment switch, thousands per secondA person, a few times a day
    Time budgetTens of milliseconds, whole decisionA few seconds to first words
    Wrong answer costsMissed fraud or a false declineA wrong policy, or a leaked document
    If a part failsDegrade to rules, never hangSay "I could not find that"
    Kept honest byDelayed labels, drift checks, shadow runsFixed question set, citation checks, access-log audits
    • Fraud is strong at speed and a clear audit trail. It is weak at labels, which arrive weeks late and only for what was flagged.
    • RAG is strong at answering from fresh, owned documents with citations. It is weak when documents are stale, duplicated or scanned badly.
    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Quoting the average latency

      "The model scores in 5 ms" says nothing. Ask for the p99 of the whole path and how many calls are on the hot path.

    • Cut-offs nobody can change or test

      Ask what happens at each timeout, who approved fail-open or fail-closed, and how fast a cut-off can move during an attack.

    • Access control by prompt

      Ask where permissions are checked. A test user outside the group must retrieve an empty list.

    • Judging RAG by a demo

      Ten hand-picked questions prove little. Ask for the question-set size, retrieval and answer accuracy separately, and refusal and Arabic cases.

    1. Why do more feature lookups hurt the tail even when each looks healthy?

    A request is slow if any one lookup is slow, so each extra lookup is another chance. Pre-joining features into one record cuts the chances.

    2. Why keep the decision policy separate from the model?

    Cut-offs follow costs and the current attack, so they must change in minutes. The model takes weeks to retrain.

    3. What is a step-up outcome for?

    It handles the uncertain middle cheaply: a genuine customer confirms in seconds, a fraudster usually cannot. The cost is friction and drop-off.

    4. Why does post-filtering starve a junior employee's answer?

    Most top matches are documents they may not read, so few remain after dropping them. Pre-filtering ranks only permitted chunks.

    5. Five RAG stages are each "pretty good". Why is the answer still often wrong?

    Every stage must work, so losses multiply: five stages at 90% give about 59%. Invest first in the weakest stage.
