    Designing the Two Classic AI Systems: A Real-Time Fraud Scorer and an Enterprise RAG Assistant

    How to answer "design a system that scores every card payment in under 100 milliseconds" and "design a staff assistant that answers from our documents" with numbers: latency budgets, tail latency, fallbacks, permission-aware retrieval, and the weakest-link arithmetic of a multi-stage pipeline.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceAn AI system design is a model plus a budget (time, money, risk), a data path, a safe behaviour for when something fails, and a feedback loop; the model is usually the smallest part.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two systems come up in almost every AI interview.

    • Fraud scorer. A card is tapped at Dubai Mall; the bank has a fraction of a second to approve, decline or ask for more proof. Like an airport security gate: fast, with a safe default when the scanner breaks.
    • RAG assistant. A relationship manager asks about a policy; the system finds the right paragraph, shows only documents they may see, and cites. Six seconds is fine; an invented policy is not. Like a librarian who checks your badge first.

    Fraud is a latency and reliability problem around a modest model. RAG is an accuracy and access-control problem around a large one. Knowing which dominates is most of design.

    Hold onto thisBefore drawing a box, state the decision, the budget (time, cost, error), the worst failure and what the system does then.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Fraud scoring: requirements first (P5.1)

    Write down what the system must guarantee before naming tools.

    • Latency. The fraud decision gets a slice of tens of milliseconds, judged at the 99th percentile (p99: the time 99% of requests beat), not on average.
    • Availability. Payments cannot stop if the scorer is down, so it needs a defined fallback.
    • Three outcomes and an audit trail. Approve, decline, or step-up (one-time code or in-app confirmation). Store inputs, score, model version and reason codes for every decision (F8, G3). Labels arrive weeks late (B1.2).

    Fraud scoring: the architecture

    Like triage in an emergency room: cheap, certain decisions first; the expensive specialist sees only the unclear cases. A fast path runs inside the payment; a slow path never blocks it.

    • Features are the hard part. The model needs counts like "transactions on this card in the last 10 minutes", kept fresh by a streaming job in a low-latency store. Training must use the same definitions, or the model sees different inputs live (online/offline skew, F3.1, P1.2).
    • Model choice. Boosted trees are the tabular workhorse: scoring in milliseconds, strong, easy to explain with reason codes. Heavier models run on the ambiguous middle only, or offline to make features.
    • Decision policy is separate from the model. The model gives a score; a policy layer turns it into approve, step-up or decline using costs, amount and review capacity. A threshold change takes minutes; retraining takes weeks (B1.1).

    Fraud scoring: the latency budget and the tail

    Customers feel the slow requests, not the median. At 3,000 payments a second, 1 in 100 slow means 30 late payments every second. Fan-out makes it worse: like a relay team, the race is as slow as its slowest runner. Ten lookups that are each slow 1% of the time make nearly one request in ten slow, even though every service looks healthy at p99. So the number of hot-path calls is a design variable.

    Four ways to cut the tail: fewer calls (pre-join features into one record per card), hard timeouts with defaults for missing features, co-location of scorer and store (P4.2), and hedging (ask a replica too, take the first answer).

    Fraud scoring: three outcomes, fallbacks and the feedback loop

    Two cut-offs make three zones: approve, step-up, decline. Like a bag check: most walk through, a few get a second look. A genuine customer pays a small friction cost; a fraudster usually cannot pass. So step-up beats a hard decline for the uncertain middle, though friction and drop-off are real: measure them.

    Fallbacks. Decide and test what happens when each part fails: defaults under a stricter policy if features time out; a rules-only or last-known-good model if the model service is down; fail open or closed by amount and merchant risk if everything is down. Failing open keeps payments flowing but raises fraud; failing closed blocks customers. That is a business decision: write it down.

    Feedback and drift. Log every decision with its features and model version. Labels come weeks later and only for what was alerted on (B1.2), so review a small random slice or the next model learns the old one's blind spots. Watch drift daily (F7.7). Run new models in shadow first (scoring live traffic without acting), with rollback as one configuration change.

    Adversaries. Fraudsters probe the system. Never return the score or reason to the channel, and cap attempts per device. Instant payment rails are hard to recall, so the decision must be right before the money moves (A9.1).

    Enterprise RAG: requirements first (P5.2)

    RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means: look up relevant passages in your own documents, hand them to a language model, and have it answer from them. Like an open-book exam. Ask first:

    • Who asks, and what must never happen? Staff with different documents, languages (Arabic, English, often mixed) and tolerance for error. Never: showing a document the user may not read, or stating a policy that does not exist.
    • What is the document estate? Many documents are stale, duplicated or scans (M3.5). Whether text may leave the network is a compliance question; record the answer.
    • How will you know it works? Collect 100 to 300 real questions with approved answers and source passages first. Without them you cannot compare designs.

    Enterprise RAG: ingestion and the pipeline

    What a designer decides at each stage:

    • Parse, chunk and tag. Most RAG failures start before the model: a flattened table, an Arabic scan read in the wrong order, a clause cut from its heading. Chunk by document structure, and store title, version, effective date and access list with every chunk, so you never quote last year's rule (M3.3).
    • Retrieve. Use keyword and vector search together, then a reranker. Keywords catch product codes and clause numbers; vectors catch paraphrases and cross-language matches (M3.2).
    • Generate and check. Answer only from the supplied passages, cite them, say when they lack the answer. Treat retrieved text as data, never as instructions (A3.1), and verify each citation supports its claim (A4.2).

    Enterprise RAG: permissions belong inside retrieval

    Enforce access control at retrieval, not by telling the model to behave. "Do not reveal confidential documents" is a request, not a control, like asking a guest not to look in the drawer instead of locking it. Once a restricted passage is in the model's context, a clever question can extract it. Two ways to filter:

    • Post-filter: take the top k matches, then drop what the user may not see. Simple, but you may be left with few or no passages.
    • Pre-filter: pass the user's groups into the search so only permitted chunks are ranked. The user gets k useful passages, at the cost of good, fresh permission metadata.

    Three more rules. Permission changes must reach the index within an agreed time, so ACL sync is a monitored job. Summaries, caches and chat history from restricted sources inherit the restriction. Agents act with the asking user's rights, not the service account's (A6.2).

    Enterprise RAG: a chain is as strong as its weakest link

    A RAG answer is right only if every step worked: parsed, retrieved, reranked, used faithfully, cited correctly. Like a relay race, one dropped baton loses it. Five stages at 90% each give only about 59% overall, so "the demo worked" is weak evidence. Effort pays most at the weakest stage.

    Evaluate each stage separately. For every test question: was the right passage in the top k? Given it, did the model answer correctly? Without this split you cannot tell where to work. Add refusal tests (questions the documents cannot answer), permission tests and Arabic tests, and run the set on every change (M5.2, P2.3).

    Cost and latency. The language model dominates, not retrieval. Use the smallest model that passes your test set and cache document embeddings, not answers that depend on who asked (M2.3).

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    The failures that sink designs, each with a quick check.

    • Quoting the average latency

      "The model scores in 5 ms." The p99 of the whole path, with all its lookups, is what customers feel.

      Spot itAsk for p99 and p99.9 of the end-to-end decision and how many calls are on the hot path.
    • Fallbacks and cut-offs nobody can change or test

      The feature store slows down and payments queue up behind it, or the threshold is baked into the model and needs a retrain.

      Spot itAsk what happens at each timeout, who approved fail-open versus fail-closed, and how fast the cut-off can change during an attack.
    • Online and offline features that differ

      The offline "transactions in 10 minutes" counts differently from the stream job. Offline results look great; live results do not.

      Spot itLog live feature values and compare their distribution with the training table's.
    • Learning only from what you alerted on

      The next model inherits the last one's blind spots.

      Spot itAsk whether a random slice of non-alerted traffic is reviewed.
    • Access control by prompt

      "The assistant is told not to share restricted documents."

      Spot itAsk where permissions are checked. Log the documents retrieved for a test user outside the group; the list must be empty.
    • Judging RAG by a demo

      Ten hand-picked questions work, so the system ships, while stale or contradictory documents quietly produce confident wrong answers.

      Spot itAsk for the size of the question set, retrieval recall separately from answer accuracy, and refusal, Arabic and recently-changed-policy cases.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer each before opening it.

    1. Why does adding more feature lookups to the hot path hurt the tail, even if each lookup looks healthy at p99?

    A request is slow if any one lookup is slow, so each extra lookup is another chance. Ten lookups that are each slow 1% of the time make nearly one request in ten slow. Pre-joining features into one record cuts the chances.

    2. Why is the decision policy kept separate from the model?

    Thresholds follow costs, capacity and the current attack, so they must change in minutes. The model takes weeks to retrain and validate. Separation also lets you add a step-up band or segment rule without touching the model.

    3. What is the point of a step-up outcome, and what does it cost?

    It handles the uncertain middle cheaply: a genuine customer confirms in seconds, a fraudster usually cannot. It avoids both the loss from approving and the lost goodwill from declining. The cost is friction, drop-off and the fraud that passes the check; measure all three.

    4. Name three ways the fraud system keeps working when a dependency fails.

    Hard timeouts with defaults for missing features; an embedded fallback model or rules set; a written fail-open or fail-closed policy by amount and risk. Also hedged requests, replicas and tested failover.

    5. Why does post-filtering starve the answer for a junior employee, and what fixes it?

    The top matches are ranked across all documents, and most are ones this person may not read. After they are dropped, few or no passages are left. Pre-filtering ranks only permitted chunks, so the user still gets a full set.

    6. Why can a RAG system whose stages are each "pretty good" still be wrong often, and where do you invest first?

    Every stage must work for the answer to be right, so the losses stack up: five stages at 90 to 95% leave only around 70% fully correct. Five extra points help most at the weakest stage, because every other stage only passes on what it delivers.

    7. Why is "tell the model not to reveal restricted documents" not an access control?

    The text is already in the model's context, and instructions can be bypassed by rephrasing, injection or extraction. Real control keeps restricted text out of the context, by filtering at retrieval with the user's identity.

    8. What do you collect before building a RAG assistant, and why?

    A few hundred real questions with approved answers and source passages, including some the documents cannot answer. They let you compare designs, separate retrieval from generation errors, and catch regressions when documents, prompts or models change.
