    From RLHF to DPO to Verifiable Rewards

    Part 1

    What post-training is

    In one sentenceAfter pretraining, a model is shaped by a loop: it writes answers, something scores them, and it is nudged towards the high scorers. RLHF, DPO and RLVR differ only in what does the scoring.

    A pretrained model can continue almost any text, but it is not yet an assistant. Our running example is a model answering questions, from "How do I reset my card PIN?" to "What is 17 × 24?".

    The three scorers

    • RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback): people say which of two answers is better, a reward model learns their taste, and it scores the answers.
    • DPO (direct preference optimisation): the same preference pairs, but used directly in an ordinary training run. No reward model, no sampling loop.
    • RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards): a program checks the answer. "17 × 24 = 408" is right or wrong. Usually run with an algorithm called GRPO.

    Preference scoring works for anything (tone, safety) but is a judgement that can be gamed. Verifiable scoring is exact but exists only where answers can be checked. Today's reasoning models are mostly RLVR on a strong base model.

    Part 2

    How each one works

    RLHF: learn a judge from preferences

    1. Start with a model fine-tuned on human-written answers (SFT). Freeze a copy as the reference.
    2. Collect preferences: labellers pick the better of two answers.
    3. Train a reward model to give the winner a higher score.
    4. Optimise: the model writes answers, the reward model scores them, and a method called PPO raises the score.

    A KL penalty acts as a leash: it punishes the model for drifting far from the reference. A tight leash is safe but timid. A loose one lets the model chase the reward model's blind spots. RLHF is heavy and needs labellers.

    DPO: skip the reward model

    DPO folds the reward model and the loop into one loss. For each pair, it asks: compared with the reference, did the model make the winner likelier and the loser less likely? Its knob beta plays the leash's role: higher beta keeps it closer to the reference.

    • Fixed data: it never sees its own new mistakes, whereas RLHF samples fresh answers every step.
    • Still needs pairs: it removes the reward network, not the judge.

    RLVR and GRPO: a program is the judge

    Where a checker exists (maths result, code with unit tests, SQL compared with a reference), it replaces the reward model like an answer key: 1 for right, 0 for wrong. It is hard to flatter, scales with compute rather than labellers, and rewards only the outcome. So any habit that raises the chance of a right ending gets reinforced: re-checking, trying alternatives, backing up after an error. That is where the long "thinking" of reasoning models comes from.

    GRPO (group relative policy optimisation) grades on a curve:

    1. Sample a group of answers to the same question (commonly 8 to 64).
    2. Score each with the checker.
    3. Give each an advantage: how much better or worse than the group average.
    4. Push the better-than-average answers up and the rest down, with a leash as before.

    How to read it

    • Rare success, big push. If only 1 in 8 is right, that answer gets a large positive advantage. If 7 in 8 are right, the one failure gets a large negative one.
    • All right or all wrong means zero signal. Every advantage is zero. Choose problems the model solves sometimes, roughly a few percent to 90% of the time.
    • Pass@k is the chance that at least one of k tries is right. Pass@1 is reliability; pass@256 is whether the model can do it at all.

    Does RL teach new reasoning, or sharpen what is there? A 2025 paper (Yue et al.) found RL models beat their base at small k, but the base catches up at large k, which suggests sharpening. Other work reports real gains on tasks needing composed skills. The evidence is mixed. Either way, turning "sometimes right" into "usually right" is valuable, and a stronger base model matters.

    Part 3

    Why the judge matters

    Grade a student on how confident the essay sounds and they learn to sound confident. This is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes the target stops measuring. A reward model is trained on finite data and is accurate only near answers it has seen. A strong optimiser drifts towards long, flattering, confidently wrong answers.

    • Gao et al. (2023) measured this with a large "gold" reward model standing in for human truth. Bigger reward models delay and soften the drop but do not remove it.
    • Verifiers do not have this gap, within the limits of the check. But a model will special-case weak tests if it can, so weak tests become the new thing to hack.
    Part 4

    Which to use

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    DPOYou have preference pairs and a small team; the goal is style, tone or safetyThe task needs exploration, or pair data is thin or noisy
    RLHF (PPO)The goal is fuzzy, and you can afford a reward model and watch for hackingYou lack labelling or engineering capacity
    RLVR with GRPOAnswers are checkable: maths, code, SQL, structured extractionNothing can verify the answer, or tests are easy to special-case
    Prompting, then sampling many times and votingA strong model already does the task sometimesYou need a small, cheap model to do it in one pass

    In banking, RLVR fits text-to-SQL checked against a test database and extraction checked against a schema. Preference methods fit chat assistants, where tone and compliance wording matter. Fraud, credit and forecasting models use ordinary supervised learning.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Trusting rising training reward

      With a learned judge, reward can climb while true quality falls. Hold out an independent test set and stop early.

    • Weak or incomplete tests

      A model will special-case them. A checker is only as good as its coverage.

    • Problems that are too hard or too easy

      All-wrong and all-right groups give zero signal. Match difficulty to what the model sometimes gets right.

    1. What is the one difference between RLHF and RLVR?

    The scorer. RLHF uses a reward model learned from human preferences; RLVR uses a program that checks the answer.

    2. What does DPO remove compared with RLHF?

    The separate reward model and the sampling loop. It still needs preference pairs.

    3. In GRPO, a group of 8 answers are all wrong. What is learned?

    Nothing. Every advantage is zero, so the problem teaches nothing that step.

    4. Pass@1 improved after RL but pass@256 did not. What does that suggest?

    Sharpening: the model became more reliable on problems it could already solve, rather than learning to solve new ones.
