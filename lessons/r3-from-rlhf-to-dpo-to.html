    From RLHF to DPO to Verifiable Rewards: How Reasoning Models Are Trained

    How a model learns from "this answer is better" and from "this answer is correct", why checkable rewards changed everything, what GRPO actually computes, and whether all that reinforcement learning teaches new reasoning or only sharpens what the model could already do.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePost-training turns a text predictor into an assistant by rewarding good answers, and the reward comes either from human preference (RLHF, DPO) or from a program that checks the answer (verifiable rewards with GRPO); the checkable kind scales best and produces reasoning models, but whether it creates new ability or concentrates existing ability is still open.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A pretrained model (M1.3) can continue almost any text but is not yet an assistant: ask a question and it may write three more questions. Post-training is everything after pretraining that shapes behaviour. This lesson covers learning from feedback: the model writes answers, something scores them, and the model is nudged towards the high scorers.

    Think of a trainee analyst. You can show finished reports (imitation), say which of two drafts is better (preference), or, for a ledger reconciliation, simply check whether the totals match (verification). The last two are the feedback types here:

    • Preference feedback ("A is better than B") is cheap for almost anything: tone, helpfulness, safety. But it is a judgement, and judgements can be gamed. This is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) and its simpler cousin DPO.
    • Verifiable feedback ("the code passes the tests") is exact, free at scale and hard to fool, but exists only where an answer can be checked. This is RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards), usually run with GRPO or a relative.

    Reasoning models (the ones that think before answering) are mostly verifiable-reward RL on a strong base model: given a problem, free to write any working, rewarded only if the final answer is right. Over thousands of steps, checking work, trying another route and backing up after a mistake earn more reward, so the model does more of them. Nobody wrote those habits as examples.

    GRPO's trick: older RL needed a second network, a "value model", to estimate what reward to expect. GRPO drops it and samples a group of answers to the same question, using the group as the yardstick. Better than your siblings, push up; worse, push down. It is cheap in memory and works well for clean right-or-wrong rewards.

    Hold onto thisEverything here is one loop: sample answers, score them, make high scorers likelier, and do not drift far from the start. The two real questions are who or what scores (a person, a learned reward model, or a checker) and what limits discovery (the scoring, or what the base model could already do).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    RLHF: learn a reward from preferences, then optimise it

    The classic recipe (Christiano et al. 2017; InstructGPT 2022) has three stages.

    1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Train on human-written demonstrations. The result is the reference model, πref.
    2. Reward model. Labellers pick the better of two answers. A separate network learns a scalar score so the preferred one scores higher, via the Bradley-Terry loss: P(A beats B) is the sigmoid of the score difference (F6, F1.3). It is a learned stand-in for human taste.
    3. Reinforcement learning. The model generates answers, the reward model scores them, and PPO (a policy-gradient method, F9.7) raises the score. A penalty keeps it near πref:
    maxπ Ex,y∼π[r(x,y)]−β·DKL(π‖πref)

    Maximise expected reward r of the model's answers y to prompts x, minus β times the KL divergence (F1.4) from the reference, which measures how far behaviour has moved. β is the dog's leash: large keeps the model near the SFT model, small lets it chase reward. With no leash it finds answers the reward model likes and humans do not (Simulation 3).

    Every major assistant has used some form of RLHF. It is heavy: four large models in memory (policy, reference, reward, value), unstable training and much tuning. That motivated simpler routes.

    DPO: skip the reward model and the reinforcement learning

    Rafailov et al. (2023) saw that the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum: the reference model re-weighted by exp(reward/β). Rearranged, the reward equals β times the log-ratio of new to reference probability of an answer. Substitute that into the Bradley-Terry loss and the reward model disappears, leaving a loss trained directly on (preferred, rejected) pairs:

    ℒDPO=−log σ(β[logπ(yw)πref(yw)−logπ(yl)πref(yl)])

    yw is the preferred answer, yl the rejected one. The bracket asks: against the reference, did the model make the winner likelier and the loser less likely? The loss falls as that gap widens. Like a coach who says "do more of this, less of that" relative to your old habits.

    DPO is an ordinary supervised-style loss: no sampling, no reward model, two models in memory (policy and frozen reference). That is why it spread fast. Trade-offs:

    • It learns from a fixed dataset, so it never sees its own new mistakes. RLHF samples fresh answers each step ("on-policy"), and well-tuned on-policy methods often still win on hard tasks.
    • It can lower the probability of both answers as long as the gap widens, quietly hurting fluency. Variants patch this.
    • It still needs pairs, so someone or something still judges. It removes the reward network, not the judge.

    RLVR: when a program, not a person, is the judge

    Some answers can be checked automatically: a maths result, code with unit tests, a SQL query compared with a reference, a schema-matching extraction. Like marking a multiple-choice paper with an answer key, a verifier returns 1 if right and 0 if not, replacing the learned reward model. "RL with verifiable rewards" was popularised by Tülu 3 (AI2, late 2024); DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed it at scale.

    • Hard to game. A reward model is a fallible opinion with blind spots; a unit test is much harder to fool (though a model may special-case the tests, so the verifier must be good).
    • Scales with compute, not labellers. Generate millions of attempts and check them automatically.
    • Rewards the outcome, not the route. Only the end is checked, so any intermediate behaviour that raises the chance of a correct end is reinforced: re-checking, trying alternatives, self-correction. This is where "thinking" tokens come from. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained with RL directly on a base model with no SFT, reportedly lengthened its reasoning and began to reflect and verify on its own.

    The weakness is the mirror image: no checker, no RLVR. Customer-service tone, a credit-memo summary or restructuring advice have no unit test. Labs combine verifiable rewards where available with reward models or model judges (R3.3, M5.2) elsewhere. The next lesson, process reward models and verifiers, covers partial credit for steps.

    GRPO: the algorithm, step by step

    Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeepSeekMath, 2024; the engine of DeepSeek-R1) grades on a curve, like a teacher marking each student against the class. For each question:

    1. Sample a group of G answers from the current model (commonly 8 to 64).
    2. Score each with the verifier: reward ri.
    3. Compute each answer's advantage, how much better than the group it was:
    Ai=ri−mean(r)std(r)

    Subtract the group average to judge against siblings; divide by the spread so scales compare across easy and hard problems. Positive means better than the group, so push up; the further from zero, the bigger the push. A rare success gets a big push: one correct among eight is about +2.6, each of the seven wrong ones about −0.38. The sum is zero by construction.

    1. Raise the probability of every token in positive-advantage answers and lower it in negative ones, in proportion to the advantage. A PPO-style clipped ratio stops any update moving the model too far, and a KL term keeps it near the reference.

    The group average replaces the value model. Two practical details:

    • Zero-signal groups. If all G answers are right (too easy) or all wrong (too hard), every advantage is zero. At a 5% pass rate and G = 8, about two thirds of groups are wasted (Simulation 1). So the difficulty mix matters more than the problem count. DAPO (2025) drops these groups ("dynamic sampling") and keeps sampling until the batch is informative.
    • Length and scaling biases. The original averages loss over each answer's length and divides by the std. Dr. GRPO and DAPO argue this makes wrong answers longer and over-weights very easy or very hard questions. Many variants exist and none is agreed best, so check which one a paper or library means.

    Core sketch (libraries such as TRL and verl handle the rest):

    rewards = np.array([verifier(a) for a in answers])      # 1.0 correct, 0.0 wrong
adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / (rewards.std() + 1e-6)
# adv is all zeros if every answer scored the same: nothing to learn from
loss = -(adv[:, None] * clipped_ratio).mean() + beta * kl   # per-token, simplified

    Does RL teach new reasoning, or sharpen what is there?

    This is the live debate (R3.2). Its measuring stick is pass@k: the chance that at least one of k sampled answers is correct. Think of a student allowed 256 tries at an exam question. Pass@1 is ordinary accuracy, how reliably the model solves it. Pass@256 is whether it can at all, the boundary of its ability. For per-try success q, pass@k = 1 − (1 − q)k. Going from pass@1 of 30% to 60% means it got more consistent; going from pass@256 of 80% to 90% means it can solve problems it previously could not. Misreading to avoid: a pass@1 gain does not show new capability.

    The finding that started the argument (Yue et al., 2025; NeurIPS 2025): RLVR models beat their base models at small k, but at large k the base catches up and can pass them. So RL made the model likelier to hit the right answer on problems it could already sometimes solve, and added none that were out of reach. On this view RL sharpens: it moves probability towards good paths already in the base, and may narrow the range of paths. "The Invisible Leash" (2025) argues RLVR is tied to the base model's support: it cannot reward what it never samples, since a zero pass rate gives a zero-signal group.

    The counter-view: this is partly an artefact. On maths, a base model sampled hundreds of times can stumble on the right number through flawed reasoning, inflating its pass@k; check the reasoning and RL looks better. Other work reports real gains far from the base model's strengths, especially with long training or tasks needing composed skills. The evidence as of late 2026 is mixed and depends on domain, base model and k.

    Read the toy as logic, not data. RL follows the reward gradient, the gradient is proportional to how often the model already succeeds, so progress concentrates where success is already possible. Whether skills transfer to the edge depends on what is learned. Habits that generalise (verifying, backtracking) support the right-hand side; re-weighting towards a few dominant solution styles supports the left. Real systems are probably a mixture, varying by task.

    Whichever side is right:

    • The base model's ceiling matters. If RL mainly sharpens, a stronger, more diverse base (M1.3) is worth more than clever RL.
    • Reliability gains are valuable. "Sometimes right" to "usually right" is what a bank wants, even if the ceiling stays put.
    • Sampling many times and voting or verifying recovers base ability. Test-time compute and RL are partly substitutes.
    • Distillation goes the other way. Training a small model on a stronger teacher's traces (P3.3) can add ability it could not find alone.

    Why the judge matters: reward over-optimisation

    Grade a student on how long and confident the essay sounds, and they will learn to write long, confident essays. That is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes the target stops measuring. A reward model is trained on finite data and is accurate only near answers it has seen. A strong optimiser drifts to where it is confidently wrong: long, flattering, confident answers. Gao et al. (2023) measured this using a large "gold" reward model as stand-in for human truth: as the policy moves from the reference, the proxy reward climbs without limit while the gold score rises, peaks, then falls. Bigger reward models delay and soften this but do not remove it. When the check is exact, the proxy is the truth, which is why verifiable rewards were a relief.

    When a team says training reward is still climbing, ask what the independent check says. Rising reward proves nothing if the reward is a learned opinion. Standard defences: early stopping, a strong KL leash, held-out human or gold evaluation, ensembles of reward models.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reward hacking

      The model scores without doing the task: special-casing unit tests, exploiting a format check, padding with phrases a judge likes.

      Spot itRead the highest-reward outputs, not just the curve. Ask whether the verifier could pass a wrong answer.
    • Training reward up, real quality flat or down

      The Goodhart pattern in Simulation 3.

      Spot itAsk for an evaluation independent of the training reward: held-out human ratings or a different judge.
    • Right answer, wrong reasoning

      Outcome-only reward cannot tell a sound derivation from a lucky one.

      Spot itSample traces on solved problems and check the steps support the answer. See R4.2.
    • Sycophancy and verbosity from preferences

      People and judges prefer agreement, confidence and length, so tuning learns them.

      Spot itTest with a false premise or "are you sure?" and watch for caving; check whether length tracks score.
    • Comparing pass@1 of a tuned model with a base sampled once

      "RL taught it new skills" claimed from pass@1 only.

      Spot itAsk for pass@k at large k for both models, and how many problems are newly solved at all.
    • Wasted compute on useless problems

      Problems always or never solved give zero advantage.

      Spot itAsk what share of groups had non-zero reward variance and how difficulty was chosen.
    • Collapsing diversity

      The model settles on a few solution styles; samples look alike; pass@k stops improving with k.

      Spot itCompare pass@k curves and the spread of distinct solutions before and after.
    • Assuming a verifiable-reward model is good at fuzzy tasks

      Strength on maths and code does not guarantee tone, nuance or Arabic fluency.

      Spot itEvaluate on your own tasks (M5.4). Maths benchmarks say little about your complaint replies.
    • Benchmark contamination

      Maths and code test sets leak into pretraining data, so RL gains may be partly memorised.

      Spot itAsk whether the benchmark is post-cutoff and results hold on freshly written problems.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A group of 8 attempts has 2 correct. What are the GRPO advantages of a correct and of a wrong answer?

    Mean = 0.25, std = √(0.25 × 0.75) ≈ 0.433. Correct: 0.75 ÷ 0.433 ≈ +1.73. Wrong: −0.25 ÷ 0.433 ≈ −0.58. Check: 2 × 1.73 + 6 × (−0.58) ≈ 0.

    2. A problem has a 10% pass rate and you sample 16 attempts. What share of groups give no learning signal?

    All wrong: 0.916 ≈ 0.185. All right: 0.116, negligible. So about 18.5% are wasted. At G = 8 it is 0.98 ≈ 43%, which is why larger groups help on hard problems.

    3. Why can RL with verifiable rewards not learn from a problem the model never solves?

    Every attempt scores zero, so all advantages and the gradient are zero. There is no correct example to push up. It can only learn indirectly, from skills that transfer from other problems. This is the "leash" argument.

    4. A base model has pass@1 of 30% and pass@256 of 80%. After RL, pass@1 is 60% and pass@256 is 78%. What does that suggest?

    Sharpening: reliability doubled, the boundary did not move and may have narrowed slightly (a small gap, so check sampling error and problem count). This is the sceptical papers' pattern. Reliability is still what users feel.

    5. What does the KL term in the RLHF objective do, and what happens with β = 0?

    It penalises drifting from the reference model; β sets how tight the leash is. With β = 0 nothing stops the optimiser exploiting the reward model's blind spots, so true quality typically falls while reward rises (Simulation 3).

    6. DPO removes the reward model. What does it still need?

    A dataset of preference pairs (someone or some model still judges) and a frozen reference model for the log-ratios. It is also off-policy: it cannot learn from the new model's own mistakes unless pairs are regenerated.

    7. Name two tasks at a bank where a verifiable reward exists and two where it does not.

    Exists: text-to-SQL checked against a test database; extracting amounts and dates from trade documents against validated values; reconciliations; expected-loss calculations with a known result. Does not: tone of a reply to a distressed customer; usefulness of a credit-memo summary; quality of Arabic phrasing; advice on a restructuring. These need preference data or judges.
