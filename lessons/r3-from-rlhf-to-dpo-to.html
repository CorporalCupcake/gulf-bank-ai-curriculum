    From RLHF to DPO to Verifiable Rewards: How Reasoning Models Are Trained

    How a model learns from "this answer is better" and from "this answer is correct", why checkable rewards changed everything, what GRPO actually computes, and whether all that reinforcement learning teaches new reasoning or only sharpens what the model could already do.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePost-training turns a text predictor into an assistant by rewarding good answers, and the reward comes either from human preference (RLHF, DPO) or from a program that checks the answer (verifiable rewards with GRPO); the checkable kind scales best and produces reasoning models, but whether it creates new ability or concentrates existing ability is still open.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A pretrained model (M1.3) has read a great deal of text and can continue almost anything. It is not yet an assistant. Ask it a question and it may answer, or it may write three more questions in the same style. Post-training is everything after pretraining that shapes how it behaves. Part of that is supervised fine-tuning on good example answers. The part this lesson covers is learning from feedback: the model produces answers, something scores them, and the model is nudged towards the high scorers.

    Think of a trainee analyst. You can teach by showing finished reports (imitation). Or you can let them write drafts and tell them which was better (preference). Or, if the task is reconciling a ledger, you can simply check whether the totals match (verification). The three give different kinds of signal, with very different cost and reliability:

    • Preference feedback ("answer A is better than B") is cheap to give for almost anything: tone, helpfulness, safety. But it is a judgement from a person or a model, and judgements can be gamed. This is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) and its simpler cousin DPO.
    • Verifiable feedback ("the code passes the tests", "the final number equals the known answer") is exact, free to compute at scale and hard to fool. But it exists only for tasks where an answer can be checked. This is RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards), usually run with an algorithm called GRPO or one of its relatives.

    The reasoning models you now hear about (the ones that think for a while before answering) are mostly made by running verifiable-reward reinforcement learning on a strong base model. The model is given a problem, free to write as much working as it likes, and rewarded only if the final answer is right. Over thousands of steps it discovers that checking its work, trying another route and backing up after a mistake earn more reward, and it does more of them. Nobody wrote those habits as examples.

    The picture holds the central trick. Older reinforcement learning methods needed a second neural network, a "value model", to estimate how good a state was so that rewards could be compared against expectation. GRPO drops it. It samples a group of answers to the same question and uses the group itself as the yardstick: better than your siblings, push up; worse, push down. It is simple, cheap in memory and works well when the reward is a clean right-or-wrong.

    Hold onto thisEverything in this lesson is a variation on one loop: sample answers, score them, make high-scoring answers more likely, and do not drift too far from where you started. The two real questions are who or what does the scoring (a person, a learned reward model, or a checker) and what limits what the model can discover (the scoring, or what the base model could already do).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    RLHF: learn a reward from preferences, then optimise it

    The classic recipe, from Christiano et al. (2017) and applied to language models in InstructGPT (2022), has three stages.

    1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Train the pretrained model on human-written demonstrations so it behaves like an assistant. The result is the reference model, πref.
    2. Reward model. Show labellers two answers to the same prompt and ask which is better. Train a separate network to give a scalar score, so that the preferred answer scores higher. The usual loss is the Bradley-Terry one: the probability that A beats B is the sigmoid of the score difference (F6, F1.3). The reward model is a learned stand-in for human taste.
    3. Reinforcement learning. Let the model generate answers, score them with the reward model, and update it with PPO (a policy-gradient method, see F9.7) to raise the score. A penalty keeps it near πref.

    The objective in stage 3 is:

    maxπ Ex,y∼π[r(x,y)]−β·DKL(π‖πref)

    Maximise the expected reward r of the model's own answers y to prompts x, minus β times the KL divergence (F1.4) between the new policy π and the reference. KL measures how far the new model's behaviour has moved. β is the leash: large β keeps the model close to the SFT model, small β lets it chase the reward. Without the leash the model finds answers the reward model likes and humans do not (Simulation 3).

    RLHF works, and every major assistant has used some form of it. It is also heavy: four large models in memory at once (policy, reference, reward model, value model), unstable training, and a lot of tuning. That motivated simpler routes.

    DPO: skip the reward model and the reinforcement learning

    Rafailov et al. (2023) noticed that the RLHF objective above has a known closed-form optimum: the best policy is the reference model re-weighted by exp(reward/β). Rearranged, the reward can be written in terms of the policy itself, as β times the log-ratio of the new and reference probabilities of an answer. Substitute that into the Bradley-Terry preference loss and the reward model disappears. What remains is a loss you can train directly on pairs of (preferred, rejected) answers:

    ℒDPO=−log σ(β[logπ(yw)πref(yw)−logπ(yl)πref(yl)])

    yw is the preferred ("winning") answer and yl the rejected ("losing") one. The bracket asks: relative to the reference, has the model made the preferred answer more likely and the rejected one less likely? The loss falls as that gap widens. In words: increase the probability of what people liked, decrease what they did not, measured against where you started.

    DPO is an ordinary supervised-style loss: no sampling during training, no reward model, two models in memory (policy and frozen reference). That is why it spread so fast, especially in open-source work and in smaller teams. The trade-offs are real:

    • It learns from a fixed dataset of pairs, so it never sees its own new mistakes. RLHF samples fresh answers every step ("on-policy"). Many studies find well-tuned on-policy methods still win on hard tasks.
    • It can lower the probability of both answers in a pair, as long as the gap widens, which can quietly hurt fluency. Several variants patch this.
    • It needs preference pairs, which still need someone or something to judge. It does not remove the judge; it removes the separate reward network.

    RLVR: when a program, not a person, is the judge

    Some tasks have answers you can check automatically: a maths problem with a known result, code with unit tests, a SQL query whose output can be compared with a reference, a logic puzzle, a structured extraction that must match a schema. For these you can replace the learned reward model with a verifier, a function that returns 1 if the answer is right and 0 otherwise. The term "reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards" was popularised by the Tülu 3 work at AI2 (late 2024), and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed what it could do at scale.

    Why this is such a big deal:

    • Hard to game. A learned reward model is a fallible opinion, and a strong optimiser will find its blind spots. A unit test is much harder to fool. (It can still be fooled: a model may special-case the tests. The verifier must be good.)
    • It scales with compute, not labellers. Generate millions of attempts, check them automatically. No human in the loop per sample.
    • It rewards the outcome, not the route. The model may write long working before the answer. Only the end is checked. So whatever intermediate behaviour raises the chance of a correct end gets reinforced: re-checking, trying alternatives, explicit self-correction. This is where "thinking" tokens come from. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained with RL directly on a base model and no SFT, reportedly lengthened its reasoning on its own and began to reflect and verify.

    The weakness is the mirror image. If you cannot write a checker, you cannot use it. Customer-service tone, a good summary of a credit memo, or advice on a restructuring have no unit test. Frontier labs therefore combine: verifiable rewards where available, learned reward models or model-based judges (R3.3, M5.2) where not. The next lesson, process reward models and verifiers, covers how to give partial credit for steps rather than only for the end.

    GRPO: the algorithm, step by step

    Group Relative Policy Optimization appeared in DeepSeekMath (2024) and was the engine of DeepSeek-R1. For each question:

    1. Sample a group of G answers from the current model (commonly 8 to 64).
    2. Score each with the verifier: reward ri.
    3. Compute each answer's advantage, how much better than the group it was:
    Ai=ri−mean(r)std(r)

    Subtract the group average so that an answer is judged against its siblings, then divide by the group's spread so that the scale is comparable between easy and hard problems. In Simulation 1, one correct answer among eight has advantage about +2.6; each of the seven wrong ones about −0.38. The sum is zero by construction.

    1. Raise the probability of every token in positively-scored answers and lower it in negatively-scored ones, in proportion to the advantage. A clipped ratio (borrowed from PPO) stops any one update from moving the model too far, and a KL term keeps it near the reference.

    That is the whole method. The group average replaces the value model. Two details matter in practice.

    • Zero-signal groups. If all G answers are right (an easy problem) or all are wrong (too hard), every advantage is zero and the problem contributes nothing. Simulation 1 shows how often: at a 5% pass rate and G = 8, about two thirds of groups are wasted. So the difficulty mix of training problems matters more than their number. DAPO (2025) filters these groups out ("dynamic sampling") and keeps sampling until the batch is full of informative ones.
    • Length and scaling biases. The original formula averages each answer's loss over its own length and divides by the standard deviation. Follow-up work (Dr. GRPO, DAPO and others) argued these choices bias the model: wrong answers can get longer, and very easy or very hard questions get over-weighted. Dozens of variants now exist; GRPO itself is better thought of as a family than a fixed recipe. There is no single agreed best version, so check which variant a paper or library means.

    A sketch of the core in code (real libraries such as TRL and verl handle the rest):

    rewards = np.array([verifier(a) for a in answers])      # 1.0 correct, 0.0 wrong
adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / (rewards.std() + 1e-6)
# adv is all zeros if every answer scored the same: nothing to learn from
loss = -(adv[:, None] * clipped_ratio).mean() + beta * kl   # per-token, simplified

    Does RL teach new reasoning, or sharpen what is there?

    This is the live research debate (R3.2) and it has practical consequences, so it is worth understanding the measuring stick. pass@k is the chance that at least one of k sampled answers is correct. Pass@1 is ordinary accuracy. Pass@256 asks: if we let the model try 256 times, does it ever get there? For a problem on which the model succeeds with probability q per try, pass@k = 1 − (1 − q)k. Averaged over a problem set, it measures the boundary of what the model can do at all, while pass@1 measures how reliably it does it.

    The finding that started the argument (Yue et al., 2025; NeurIPS 2025): models trained with RLVR beat their own base models at small k, but at large k the base model catches up and can pass them. Read plainly: RL made the model much more likely to produce the right answer on problems it could already sometimes solve, and did not add problems that were previously out of reach. On this view RL sharpens: it moves probability mass towards good paths that already existed in the base model, and may even narrow the range of paths by concentrating on the winners. Follow-up theory ("The Invisible Leash", 2025) argues that RLVR is tied to the base model's support: it cannot reward what it never samples, because a problem with a zero pass rate gives a zero-signal group.

    The counter-view is that this is partly an artefact. On maths, a base model sampled hundreds of times can stumble on the right number through flawed reasoning, which inflates its pass@k; once you check the reasoning rather than just the answer, RL looks better. Other work reports genuine gains on tasks far from the base model's strengths, particularly where training is long enough or the problem requires composing skills. The evidence as of late 2026 is mixed and depends on the domain, the base model and the k used.

    Do not over-read the toy. It shows the logic of the argument: reinforcement learning follows the gradient of reward, the gradient is proportional to how often the model already succeeds, and so progress concentrates where success is already possible. Whether skills transfer to problems at the edge depends on what the model actually learns. Evidence that RL on reasoning improves habits that generalise (verifying, backtracking) supports the right-hand side. Evidence that models re-weight to a few dominant solution styles supports the left. Real systems are probably a mixture, varying by task.

    Practical consequences, whichever side is right:

    • Your base model's ceiling matters. If RL mainly sharpens, a stronger or more diverse base model (and good pretraining data, see M1.3) is worth more than clever RL.
    • Reliability gains are real and valuable. Turning "sometimes right" into "usually right" is exactly what a bank wants from a model, even if the ceiling does not move.
    • Sampling many times and voting or verifying can recover base-model ability. Test-time compute and RL are partly substitutes.
    • Distillation moves the other way. Training a smaller model on a stronger teacher's reasoning traces (P3.3) can add ability the small model could not discover alone.

    Why the judge matters: reward over-optimisation

    Return to the leash in the RLHF objective. A reward model is trained on finite data and is accurate only near the kinds of answers it saw. Push a strong optimiser against it and the policy drifts to places where the reward model is confidently wrong: long, flattering, confident-sounding answers, for example. Gao et al. (2023) measured this cleanly using a large "gold" reward model as a stand-in for human truth: as the policy moves further from the reference, the proxy reward climbs without limit while the gold score rises, peaks and then falls. Bigger reward models delay and soften the problem but do not remove it. This is Goodhart's law, and it is why verifiable rewards were such a relief: when the check is exact, the proxy is the truth.

    Practically: when a team reports that training reward is still climbing, ask what the independent check says. A rising training reward is not evidence of a better model if the reward is a learned opinion. Early stopping, a strong KL leash, held-out human or gold evaluation and ensembles of reward models are the standard defences.

    How it connects

    Post-training is where several earlier ideas meet. These are the links to follow.

    M1.3 · M1.4 · Pretraining then post-trainingWhere this stage sits

    This lesson is the deep dive on the "post" half: SFT, preference tuning and reinforcement learning on a pretrained base.

    F9.7 · Bandits and reinforcement learningThe policy-gradient idea

    A language model is a policy; a sampled answer is an action; the reward arrives at the end. The explore-exploit trade-off returns as "diversity versus sharpening".

    F1.3 · F1.4 · Likelihood, entropy, KLThe leash is a KL divergence

    The penalty that keeps the policy near its reference, and the DPO log-ratios, are all likelihood ideas.

    F6.1 · Loss functionsPreference loss

    The reward-model and DPO losses are logistic losses on score differences.

    R3.3 · Verifiers and process rewardsGiving credit for steps

    When only the final answer is checked, wrong routes to right answers are rewarded. Process rewards and learned verifiers address that.

    R3.4 · Constitutional AI and RLAIFReplacing human labellers with a model

    The preference signal can come from a model following written principles, a middle route between human feedback and programmatic checks.

    R4 · Sycophancy and reasoning faithfulnessWhat optimising for approval does

    Sycophancy is reward over-optimisation in plain sight: preference data favours agreement, so the model learns to agree. See also chain-of-thought faithfulness.

    R2 · Scaling and synthetic dataA second axis of scale

    RL compute and test-time compute now sit beside pretraining compute in the scaling story. Reasoning traces are also a source of synthetic data.

    P3 · DistillationCompressing a reasoner

    Small models are often given reasoning ability by training on a large model's verified traces rather than by running RL themselves.

    M2.3 · Model choice, cost and latencyThe bill for thinking

    Reasoning models emit many hidden tokens. Better answers on hard tasks come with higher cost and delay; that trade-off is a product decision.

    M5 · EvaluationJudges and metrics

    Everything here depends on how answers are scored. The same judge problems appear in evaluating agents.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Choosing and procuring models. When a vendor says "reasoning model", you now know it was probably trained with verifiable-reward RL, performs best on checkable problem types (code, structured maths, SQL) and costs more per answer.
    • Building verifiers for your own tasks. Much banking work is checkable: a generated SQL query against a test database, a reconciliation that must net to zero, an extraction from a trade-finance document that must match the validated fields, an IFRS 9 calculation with a known answer. These are natural targets for verifiable-reward fine-tuning or at least for automated evaluation (M5).
    • Where verifiers do not exist. Arabic customer replies, complaint tone, credit-memo summaries and advice need preference signals or model judges, with their known biases. Plan for human review samples.
    • Open-weight models. Many open reasoning models are available in the region and can be hosted on-premises (P4.2). Knowing how they were trained helps you judge what fine-tuning them can and cannot achieve.
    • Model risk and validation. A validator will ask how the model was aligned and what was optimised. "A learned reward model, optimised to a distance of X from the base" is a legitimate thing to document (G3.2).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The broad picture is settled: preference methods for behaviour, verifiable-reward RL for reasoning. The argument is about what RL adds, which GRPO variant is best, and how to extend verification beyond maths and code.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Failures in training, and in how people talk about it. Each comes with a quick check.

    • Reward hacking

      The model finds a way to score without doing the task: special-casing unit tests, exploiting a format check, padding with the phrases a judge likes.

      Spot itRead samples of the highest-reward outputs, not just the reward curve. Ask whether the verifier could pass a wrong answer.
    • Training reward up, real quality flat or down

      The Goodhart pattern in Simulation 3.

      Spot itAsk for an evaluation that is independent of the training reward: held-out human ratings or a different judge.
    • Right answer, wrong reasoning

      Outcome-only reward cannot tell a sound derivation from a lucky one.

      Spot itSample reasoning traces on problems the model solved and check whether steps actually support the answer. See R4.2.
    • Sycophancy and verbosity from preferences

      People and judges prefer agreement, confidence and length. Preference tuning learns that.

      Spot itTest with a false premise or a pushback ("are you sure?") and watch for caving; check whether answer length tracks score.
    • Comparing pass@1 of a tuned model with a base sampled once

      Claims of "RL taught it new skills" based on pass@1 only.

      Spot itAsk for pass@k at large k for both models, and for the number of problems newly solved at all.
    • Wasted compute on useless problems

      Problems the model always solves or never solves give zero advantage.

      Spot itAsk what share of groups had non-zero reward variance and how problems were chosen for difficulty.
    • Collapsing diversity

      The model concentrates on a few solution styles; samples all look alike; pass@k stops improving with k.

      Spot itCompare pass@k curves and the spread of distinct solutions before and after.
    • Assuming a verifiable-reward model is good at fuzzy tasks

      Strength on maths and code does not guarantee good tone, nuance or Arabic fluency.

      Spot itEvaluate on your own tasks (M5.4). Benchmarks on maths say little about your complaint replies.
    • Benchmark contamination

      Math and code test sets leak into pretraining data, so RL gains on them may be partly memorised answers.

      Spot itAsk whether the benchmark is post-cutoff, and whether results hold on freshly written problems.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The newer reasoning models are trained by letting them attempt problems and rewarding the ones where a program confirms the answer is right. That works very well for things that can be checked, like code, SQL and calculations, and less well for judgement and tone. For us it means these models will be strongest on our checkable work, they cost more per answer because they think before replying, and we should build automatic checks into anything we deploy.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “RLHF learns a reward model from preferences and optimises against it with a KL leash; DPO folds that into one loss on preference pairs. RLVR replaces the learned judge with a verifier, and GRPO replaces the value model with a group baseline: advantage is reward minus the group mean over the group standard deviation. The open question is whether it expands the pass@k boundary or sharpens pass@1 within it. The evidence is mixed, so I'd evaluate pass@k at large k on our own tasks before assuming RL gives us new capability.”
    “What's the difference between RLHF and DPO?”

    Both learn from preference pairs. RLHF trains a separate reward model, then runs reinforcement learning against it, sampling fresh answers each step. DPO uses a mathematical rearrangement of the same objective to train directly on the pairs, with no reward model and no sampling loop. DPO is simpler and more stable; RLHF-style on-policy methods often do better on hard tasks because they learn from the model's own current mistakes.

    “Why does GRPO not need a value model?”

    The value model exists to give a baseline: what reward to expect, so you can tell good from average. GRPO gets the baseline by sampling a group of answers to the same prompt and using their mean (and spread). It is noisier per prompt but cheaper in memory and works well for right-or-wrong rewards.

    “Did DeepSeek-R1 teach itself to reason?”

    In a limited sense. R1-Zero was trained with RL on a base model using only outcome rewards, and behaviours like longer reasoning, self-checking and backtracking appeared during training. But the base model already contained a lot of reasoning-like text from pretraining, and the released R1 also used supervised data. "Taught itself" overstates; "RL amplified and organised skills present in the base" is closer.

    “Does RL give a model abilities it didn't have?”

    Unresolved. Several well-cited studies find RL improves pass@1 but not pass@k at large k, which suggests sharpening. Others show gains on settings far from the base's strengths, and criticise pass@k on maths as inflated by lucky guesses. My working assumption: it reliably improves reliability, sometimes extends reach, and I'd measure on our own tasks rather than rely on headline claims.

    “Could we use verifiable rewards on our own bank data?”

    Where outputs can be checked automatically, yes in principle: SQL against a test warehouse, extraction against validated fields, calculations with known answers. In practice the first step is building the verifier and test set, which pays off even if we never fine-tune: it gives us automatic evaluation (M5). I would not fine-tune with RL before we have a good verifier, enough problems at the right difficulty, and a validator's sign-off on what is being optimised.

    “The vendor says the model has been aligned with RLHF. Is that reassuring?”

    It tells you how its manners were shaped, not that it is correct. Preference tuning can make a model more helpful and also more agreeable and confident. I'd ask for evidence on our use cases: factuality on our documents, behaviour under pushback, refusal behaviour, and how it does in Arabic.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A group of 8 attempts has 2 correct. What are the GRPO advantages of a correct and of a wrong answer?

    Mean = 0.25, standard deviation = √(0.25 × 0.75) ≈ 0.433. Correct: (1 − 0.25) ÷ 0.433 ≈ +1.73. Wrong: (0 − 0.25) ÷ 0.433 ≈ −0.58. Check: 2 × 1.73 + 6 × (−0.58) ≈ 0.

    2. A problem has a 10% pass rate and you sample 16 attempts. What share of groups give no learning signal?

    All wrong: 0.916 ≈ 0.185. All right: 0.116, negligible. So about 18.5% of groups are wasted. At G = 8 it would be 0.98 ≈ 43%, which is why larger groups help on hard problems.

    3. Why can RL with verifiable rewards not learn from a problem the model never solves?

    Every attempt scores zero, so all advantages are zero and the gradient is zero. There is no correct example to push up. It can only learn indirectly, from skills picked up on other problems that happen to transfer. This is the core of the "leash" argument.

    4. A base model has pass@1 of 30% and pass@256 of 80% on a task. After RL, pass@1 is 60% and pass@256 is 78%. What does that suggest?

    Sharpening: reliability doubled, but the boundary did not move, and may have narrowed slightly (small difference, so check sampling error and problem count). It is the pattern the sceptical papers report. It still has real value: reliability is what users feel.

    5. What does the KL term in the RLHF objective do, and what happens with β = 0?

    It penalises the policy for moving away from the reference model, so β sets how tightly the model stays near its starting behaviour. With β = 0 nothing stops the optimiser exploiting the reward model's blind spots, and quality as users judge it typically falls even as the reward rises (Simulation 3).

    6. DPO removes the reward model. What does it still need?

    A dataset of preference pairs (so someone or some model still judges), and a frozen reference model to compute the log-ratios. It is also off-policy: it cannot learn from the new model's own mistakes unless pairs are regenerated.

    7. Name two tasks at a bank where a verifiable reward exists and two where it does not.

    Exists: text-to-SQL checked against a test database; extracting amounts and dates from trade documents checked against validated values; reconciliation checks; expected-loss calculations with a known result. Does not: tone of a reply to a distressed customer; usefulness of a credit-memo summary; quality of Arabic phrasing; advice on a restructuring. These need preference data or judges.
