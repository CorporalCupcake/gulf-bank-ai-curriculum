    From RLHF to DPO to Verifiable Rewards: How Reasoning Models Are Trained

    How models learn from "this answer is better" and "this answer is correct", and what that does to reasoning.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentencePost-training rewards good answers, with the reward coming from human preference (RLHF, DPO) or from a program that checks the answer (verifiable rewards with GRPO); checkable rewards scale best and give reasoning models, but whether they create new ability or only sharpen existing ability is still open.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A pretrained model can continue almost any text but is not yet an assistant. Post-training shapes its behaviour: the model writes answers, something scores them, and it is nudged towards the high scorers.

    The scorer is the key choice. Preference feedback ("A is better than B") works for almost anything, such as tone and safety, but it is a judgement and can be gamed. This is RLHF and its simpler cousin DPO. Verifiable feedback ("the code passes the tests") is exact and hard to fool, but exists only where answers can be checked. This is RLVR, usually run with GRPO.

    Reasoning models are mostly verifiable-reward RL on a strong base model. Checking work and backing up after a mistake earn more reward, so the model learns to do them.

    GRPO samples a group of answers to the same question and uses the group as the yardstick: better than your siblings, push up; worse, push down. It needs no separate value model.

    Hold onto thisEverything here is one loop: sample answers, score them, make high scorers likelier, stay near the start. The real questions are who or what scores, and what limits discovery.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    RLHF: learn a reward from preferences

    Humans pick the better of two answers, and a separate reward model learns to mimic those choices. The model is then trained to score highly on it, with a penalty (the KL leash) that stops it drifting far from the starting model. RLHF suits fuzzy goals but is heavy and unstable.

    DPO: skip the reward model

    DPO trains directly on pairs of preferred and rejected answers, making the winner likelier and the loser less likely compared with the old model. It is simple and cheap, but it learns from a fixed dataset and still needs someone to judge the pairs. It removes the reward network, not the judge.

    RLVR: a program is the judge

    When a program can check the answer (a maths result, code with unit tests, a SQL query), it replaces the reward model like an answer key. It is hard to game, scales with compute rather than labellers, and rewards only the outcome, so habits like re-checking and backtracking emerge.

    The weakness is the mirror image: no checker, no RLVR. Tone, credit-memo quality and restructuring advice have no unit test, so labs mix in reward models or judges there. The next lesson covers process reward models and verifiers.

    GRPO: how it works

    GRPO grades on a curve. For each question it samples a group of answers, scores each with the verifier, and gives each an advantage: how much better or worse than the group average. Positive is pushed up, negative down, and a rare success gets a big push.

    The catch: if every answer is right or every answer is wrong, all advantages are zero and nothing is learned. So the difficulty mix matters more than the problem count. Choose problems near the model's edge.

    Does RL teach new reasoning, or sharpen what is there?

    Pass@k is the chance that at least one of k sampled answers is correct. Pass@1 shows how reliably the model solves a problem; pass@256 shows whether it can at all. A pass@1 gain alone does not show new capability.

    The finding that started the argument (Yue et al., 2025): RL models beat their base at small k, but at large k the base catches up. On that view RL sharpens: it moves probability to good paths already there, and cannot reward what it never samples. The counter-view is that this is partly lucky guesses, and other work reports real gains. The evidence is mixed.

    Read the toy as logic: RL follows the reward gradient, so progress concentrates where success is already possible. Either way, a stronger base model matters, and "sometimes right" becoming "usually right" is valuable to a bank.

    Why the judge matters: over-optimisation

    Grade a student on how confident the essay sounds and they learn to sound confident. That is Goodhart's law. A reward model is accurate only near answers it has seen, so a strong optimiser drifts to long, flattering, confidently wrong answers: training reward keeps climbing while true quality peaks and falls.

    When a team says training reward is still climbing, ask what the independent check says.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Reward hacking

      The model scores without doing the task, such as special-casing unit tests or padding with phrases a judge likes.

      Spot itRead the highest-reward outputs, not just the curve.
    • Training reward up, real quality flat

      The Goodhart pattern.

      Spot itAsk for an evaluation independent of the training reward.
    • Right answer, wrong reasoning

      Outcome-only reward cannot tell a sound derivation from a lucky one.

      Spot itSample traces on solved problems and check the steps.
    • "RL taught it new skills" from pass@1 only

      Reliability is not reach.

      Spot itAsk for pass@k at large k for both models.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can RL with verifiable rewards not learn from a problem the model never solves?

    Every attempt scores zero, so there is nothing to push up. It can only learn indirectly, from skills that transfer from other problems.

    2. Pass@1 doubles from 30% to 60% after RL, but pass@256 stays near 80%. What does that suggest?

    Sharpening: more reliable, same reach.

    3. What does the KL term do, and what happens without it?

    It penalises drifting from the reference model. Without it the optimiser exploits the reward model's blind spots, so true quality falls while reward rises.

    4. DPO removes the reward model. What does it still need?

    Preference pairs, so someone or some model still judges, plus a frozen reference model. It also cannot learn from the new model's own mistakes.

    5. Name a bank task with a verifiable reward and one without.

    Verifiable: text-to-SQL checked against a test database. Not verifiable: the tone of a reply to a distressed customer. That needs preference data or judges.
