    Fairness: Definitions and Trade-Offs

    Part 1

    What fairness means for a model

    In one sentenceFairness means comparing how a model treats different groups, using a number you choose on purpose. There are several such numbers, and they cannot all be equal at once.

    Take a loan model that approves 73% of group A and 65% of group B (nationals and expatriates, say). Three reviewers react:

    • Reviewer 1: "Unfair. The approval rates differ."
    • Reviewer 2: "Of those approved, 94% of A and 87% of B repaid. Fine."
    • Reviewer 3: "Among people who would have repaid, both groups were approved equally often. That is fair."

    All three can be true of the same model. They are looking at different slices of one table. Here is that table for one group:

    How it differs from accuracy

    • Accuracy asks how often the model is right overall. It can be high while one group is treated much worse.
    • Fairness asks how that group's outcomes compare. It is always a comparison between groups, never a property of one number.
    • Bias in the data is a separate question, covered in Part 4.
    Part 2

    The main definitions

    Each definition differs in what it starts from:

    • Demographic parity starts from everyone: same approval rate in each group.
    • Equal opportunity and equalised odds start from the truth: among people who would repay, are both groups approved equally often?
    • Predictive parity (calibration) starts from the decision: among people approved, or given a score of 0.8, do both groups repay equally often?

    Unawareness"We never look at gender."Does not stop proxies

    How to read the numbers

    • Gap of 0 means that definition holds exactly. A few points is normal noise.
    • Always ask how many people. A 4-point gap measured on 40 good applicants in a small group is noise.
    • The four-fifths rule flags a group whose approval rate is below 80% of the highest group's. Here 65 ÷ 73 is 0.89, so it passes. It is a US rule of thumb, not a Gulf requirement.
    Show the formulas

    Equal opportunity compares "good, approved ÷ all good" across groups. Predictive parity compares "good, approved ÷ all approved". Four-fifths rule: lowest approval rate ÷ highest. Below 0.8 is a flag.

    Part 3

    Why you cannot have them all

    The catchWhen groups truly differ in how often people repay, and the model is imperfect, you cannot equalise approval rates, error rates and the meaning of a score all at once.

    A worked example. Suppose 85% of group A would repay, but only 70% of group B. Three things cannot all be equal unless the model is perfect:

    1. How often good applicants are approved.
    2. How often bad applicants are approved.
    3. How often approved applicants repay.

    Fix two and the third is forced apart. For instance, if 80% of good applicants are approved and 90% of approved applicants repay in both groups, then about half of A's bad applicants get approved against only about a fifth of B's. Equalise the error rates instead, and the repay rates among the approved must differ.

    The gap lives in the world, not in the code. A near-perfect score would dissolve the conflict, but real banking scores never get there.

    Which definition to use

    Which gap to accept is a values choice for business, risk and legal. The data scientist's job is to make the trade-off visible. The right definition depends on the decision:

    DecisionHarm to avoidLean towards
    Pricing, provisioningA risk score that means different things per groupCalibration
    Denying a benefit to qualified peopleGood applicants rejectedEqual opportunity
    Freezing an account for fraudInnocent customers flaggedEqual false-positive rates

    What you can do about a gap

    • Before training: fix the data. Reweight examples, drop proxy features, collect better labels.
    • During training: add a fairness penalty so the model trades some accuracy for a smaller gap.
    • After training: change the decision rule, such as different cut-offs per group. This is the cleanest fix, but it uses the protected attribute at decision time, which many jurisdictions treat as disparate treatment.

    Ask Legal and Compliance before using it. Many banks hold protected attributes only for auditing.

    Part 4

    Where bias comes from

    Everything above assumed the data tells the truth. Four ways it does not:

    • Historical bias. The data records an unequal world accurately. If women were offered smaller limits, they show lower balances and a thinner history, so the model expects less from them.
    • Representation bias. A model fitted mostly on salaried nationals is noisier for self-employed expatriates.
    • Label bias. "Defaulted" is seen only for people given credit, and "fraud" only where someone investigated.
    • Proxies. Remove gender and the model can rebuild it from occupation, employer, device or time of application. It is like blurring a face but leaving the name tag on.

    The proxy test. Train a quick model that predicts the group from the inputs. If it beats chance by a wide margin, the features jointly encode the group, and dropping the column was cosmetic. Proxies are not always errors, though: income genuinely predicts repayment. Ask whether the feature has a documented business reason.

    Feedback loops. In fraud, confirmed cases exist only where someone looked. If group B is reviewed more, more of its cases are found. The next model then sends more alerts to B, which creates more labels there. The remedy is process: review a small random sample of unflagged customers in every group.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • "We removed the protected attribute, so it is fair"

      Proxies rebuild it. Run the proxy test.

    • Reporting one fairness metric

      "Approval rates are within 3 points" can sit beside false-positive rates that differ by a factor of two. Ask for all the slices by group in one table.

    • Gaps without counts

      A gap on a few dozen people is noise. Ask for the number of people per group and an interval on each gap.

    • Checking once, or only one group at a time

      Fair on gender and on nationality can still be unfair for female expatriates., and fair in March may not hold in December.

    1. A model approves 73% of A and 65% of B. Is it unfair?

    Not necessarily. It fails demographic parity, but other definitions may hold. Ask which definition fits the decision.

    2. What is the difference between equal opportunity and predictive parity?

    Equal opportunity starts from people who would repay and asks if they are approved equally often. Predictive parity starts from approved people and asks if they repay equally often.

    3. Why can't a model satisfy every definition?

    When groups differ in how often they repay and the model is imperfect, equalising error rates forces repay rates among the approved apart, and the reverse.

    4. Group-specific cut-offs close a gap. Why might Legal object?

    It uses the protected attribute at decision time, which many jurisdictions treat as disparate treatment. Ask Legal and Compliance first.

    5. Fraud alerts go more often to group B, and confirmed cases follow. Does that prove B commits more fraud?

    No. Cases are found only where people look, so more reviews produce more recorded cases. Add random review of unflagged customers to get an unbiased rate.
