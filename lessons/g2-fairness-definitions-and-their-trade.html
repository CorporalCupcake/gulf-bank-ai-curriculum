    Fairness in Lending and Fraud Models: Competing Definitions and Where Bias Comes From

    Why "treat everyone the same" is not one rule but several, why a model cannot satisfy them all when groups differ, and how unfairness gets into a model through data, labels and proxies without anyone intending it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceFairness is a choice among competing, individually reasonable measurements that cannot all hold at once when groups differ in base rates, so the job is to pick and justify the one that fits the decision, and to hunt for the data, label and proxy problems that make every one of them misleading.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A model approves 73% of group A and 65% of group B (nationals and expatriates, say). Three reviewers react:

    • "Unfair: the approval rates differ."
    • "Of those approved, 94% of A and 87% of B repaid. The model is just more cautious where evidence is thinner."
    • "Among people who would have repaid, both groups were approved equally often. That is fairness."

    All three numbers can come from the same model. The dispute is over which comparison deserves the word "fair", not arithmetic.

    The core problem: when groups genuinely differ in how often the outcome happens (repayment, fraud) and the model is imperfect, you cannot equalise approval rates, error rates and the meaning of a score together. It is a theorem, not a software limit. Like a tight blanket on a bed that is too small: cover one side and another is exposed. Fairness work is naming that trade-off honestly.

    Bias also enters before any measurement. A model trained on past decisions inherits the past. A label that records "who got investigated" rather than "who did wrong" makes the model investigate the same people again. Removing the protected column does not help when twenty other columns rebuild it.

    Hold onto thisTwo questions. Measurement: which fairness numbers do we compute, and which gaps do we accept? Origin: does the data we measure on already carry the bias? Teams that argue only about the first often get the second wrong.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The main definitions, and what each one protects

    Take a lending model that approves anyone above a score cut, with groups A and B (gender, nationality, age and so on) and true outcome "would repay". Each definition below is a different slice of the same confusion table, computed per group. Think of a school exam: equal pass rates, equal chance for those who studied, and "a 70 means the same thing for everyone" are three different fairness claims.

    DefinitionIt asks that…In plain wordsIt protects / its weakness
    Fairness through unawarenessProtected attribute is not an input."We never look at gender."Easy to audit. Does not stop proxies (see Sources of bias).
    Demographic (statistical) parityP(approve | A) = P(approve | B).Same approval rate per group.Ignores real repayment differences. Forcing it can approve riskier applicants in one group and reject safer ones in the other.
    Equal opportunityEqual true positive rates: P(approve | would repay, A) = P(approve | would repay, B).Creditworthy people are approved equally often in either group.Protects the qualified. Ignores bad approvals, and needs true outcomes (rejected applicants never show theirs).
    Equalised oddsEqual true positive and false positive rates.Both kinds of error are equally likely in each group.Strong and symmetric.
    Predictive parity / calibrationAmong those approved (or given score s), the repaying share is equal across groups."A score of 0.8 means the same for everyone."What risk teams usually mean by fair pricing. Can hide very different error rates.
    Individual fairnessSimilar people get similar scores (Dwork et al., 2012).Applicants differing only irrelevantly get the same decision.Needs a defensible definition of "similar", which is the hard part.
    Counterfactual fairnessDecision unchanged if the attribute were different, causal story fixed (Kusner et al., 2017)."Approved had she been a man, all else equal?"Needs a contestable causal model (F9.3).

    The difference is the direction of conditioning. Demographic parity compares outcomes to group size. Equal opportunity and equalised odds condition on the truth (who would repay). Predictive parity conditions on the decision (who was approved). F1.4's warning applies: P(A given B) is not P(B given A).

    The four-fifths rule often appears in compliance packs: if one group's approval rate is below 80% of the highest group's, that is preliminary evidence of adverse impact. A group at 65% against 73% gives 0.89, so it passes. It is a US employment screening rule of thumb, not a Gulf requirement and not proof of discrimination. It looks only at approval rates.

    With equal score quality and one cut, equalised odds held automatically, yet demographic and predictive parity failed purely because repayment rates differ. The gap is in the world, not the code. It is not automatically acceptable, but "make the model more accurate" cannot fix it.

    Why you cannot have them all

    The heart is a short identity (Chouldechova, 2017). For a group with base rate p (share who would repay):

    FPR=p1−p·1−PPVPPV·TPR

    FPR = share of bad applicants approved, PPV = share of approved who repay, TPR = share of good applicants approved. With different p and equal PPV and TPR, the factor p/(1−p) differs, so FPR must differ. Equal predictive value, equal opportunity and equal false-positive rates cannot coexist unless p is equal or the model is perfect. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) proved the same for scores: calibration within groups and equal error rates fail together in those two cases only.

    Example. Group A: 85% would repay; group B: 70%. Demand PPV 90% and TPR 80% in both. Then FPR is (0.85/0.15) × (0.1/0.9) × 0.8 ≈ 50% for A and (0.70/0.30) × (0.1/0.9) × 0.8 ≈ 21% for B. Half of A's bad applicants are approved against a fifth of B's. Equalise the error rates instead and PPVs must differ. There is no third way.

    The next simulation moves group B's cut while A's stays put, which is what any real mitigation does: different treatment to equalise one statistic.

    • Every fairness rule has a price elsewhere. The constraints genuinely conflict. Which gap to accept is a values choice for business, risk and legal; data scientists make the trade-off visible.
    • The right definition depends on the decision. Pricing and provisioning need calibration within groups, so a stated default probability means the same for everyone (B4.1). Denying a benefit to qualified people calls for equal opportunity. Freezing an account is a harm of false positives, so equalise FPR.
    • A near-perfect score dissolves the conflict, which is why "just improve the model" feels right. Real banking scores never get there.

    Corbett-Davies et al. (2023) add that forcing a statistical equality can conflict with the decision-maker's goal and even harm the protected group. Show the trade-off curve and decide with open eyes.

    What you can actually do about it

    Mitigation methods differ by where in the pipeline they act.

    StageIdeaTypical toolsCatch
    Before trainingFix the data: reweight or relabel examples, remove features that act as proxies, collect better labels.Reweighing, feature auditsTreats symptoms if the real problem is the label. Slow and manual.
    During trainingAdd a fairness constraint or penalty to the loss, so the model trades accuracy for a smaller gap.Constrained optimisation, adversarial debiasing, Fairlearn reductionsNeeds the protected attribute at training time; harder to explain to validators.
    After trainingKeep the model, change the decision rule: group-specific thresholds chosen to hit equal opportunity or equalised odds (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016).Threshold optimisers, calibration per groupUses the protected attribute at decision time. In many jurisdictions that is itself disparate treatment. Legal review first.

    The last catch surprises engineers. Group-specific cuts (as in Simulation 2) are the cleanest fix mathematically and may be unlawful to apply, since they treat people differently by a protected attribute. Do not assume the UAE or Saudi answer: ask Legal and Compliance, and check central bank and consumer-protection rules. Many banks hold protected attributes only for auditing.

    Measurement has its own problem: you often lack the attribute (religion, ethnicity), and some cannot lawfully be inferred. Inferring membership from names adds error. State what you measured and how.

    Sources of bias: data, labels, proxies

    Everything above assumed the data tells the truth. Suresh and Guttag (2019) catalogue where it does not. Four matter most in banking.

    • Historical bias. The data records an unequal world accurately. If women were offered smaller limits, they show lower balances and thinner history, and the model expects less from them. Accurate data, still a problem.
    • Representation bias. A model fitted mostly on salaried nationals is noisier for self-employed expatriates. Thin-file customers are the standard case.
    • Label and measurement bias. The target is a record of what you care about. "Defaulted" is seen only for people given credit; "fraud" means fraud someone investigated and confirmed. Obermeyer et al. (2019): a US healthcare algorithm used past spending as a proxy for need. Less had been spent on Black patients with the same illness, so it under-ranked their need. It predicted cost well; cost was the wrong label.
    • Proxies. Remove gender and the model can rebuild it from occupation, employer, shopping categories, device or even time of application. Postcode proxied race in US redlining; in the Gulf, nationality, employer category and residential area can stand in for protected groups. Like blurring a face but leaving the name tag on.

    The proxy test: train a quick classifier from the features to the group. If it beats chance by a wide margin, the features jointly encode the group and dropping the column was cosmetic. If it cannot recover the group, no model can exploit it through those features.

    Proxies are not always errors. Income genuinely predicts repayment and also correlates with nationality. Ask whether the feature has a documented business reason and whether a less discriminatory alternative would do as well. Feature selection is a documented decision, not a statistical default.

    Labels that record who was looked at

    The quietest damage in fraud and AML: confirmed cases exist only where someone investigated. Like a speed camera on one road only, that road "has more speeders". If group B is reviewed more (old rule, branch practice, earlier model), more of its cases are found and its recorded rate is higher even if the true rate is identical. The next model sends more alerts there, creating more labels there. This is a feedback loop (Ensign et al., 2018, in predictive policing). Its credit relative is selective labels (Lakkaraju et al., 2017): repayment is seen only for those approved (reject inference, B4.5).

    The remedy is process, not model. Send a small random sample of unflagged customers to review in every group for an unbiased true rate. Track alert and confirmed-case rates by group over time. Compare against a rule-based baseline (C1.2) that does not learn from past review patterns. As F3.5 and F2.6 said: a label reflects what happened and who looked.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Common in bank and AI-generated analyses, each with its quickest check.

    • "We removed the protected attribute, so the model is fair"

      Proxies reconstruct it. The model is only blind in the narrowest sense.

      Spot itAsk for a quick model that predicts the group from the inputs. Compare its accuracy with chance. Check the top features for postcode, employer type, device or name-derived fields.
    • Reporting one fairness metric

      "Approval rates are within 3 points." Meanwhile false-positive rates differ by a factor of two.

      Spot itAsk for approval rate, TPR, FPR and PPV by group in one table. If only one appears, ask why the others were left out.
    • Believing a metric can be driven to zero for free

      "We tuned thresholds to equalise everything." The identity says that cannot hold unless the base rates match.

      Spot itAsk which fairness criteria are satisfied and which are not. A claim that all hold, with different base rates and an imperfect model, is wrong or hiding something.
    • Gaps without intervals

      A 4-point TPR gap measured on 40 positives in a small group is noise.

      Spot itAsk for the number of positives per group and a bootstrap interval on each gap (F2.2).
    • Trusting the label

      "Confirmed fraud" or "default" are records of observation, not truth.

      Spot itAsk how the label is produced, who decides what to investigate, and whether outcome rates differ by group for reasons that match the review rates, not the behaviour.
    • Feedback loops left unmonitored

      The model sends more alerts to a group, generating more confirmed cases there, which justifies the model.

      Spot itPlot alert share and confirmed-case share by group across retraining cycles. Look for a random-review baseline.
    • Averaging away a subgroup

      Fair on gender, fair on nationality, but unfair on female expatriates.

      Spot itCheck intersections, as long as the groups are big enough to measure.
    • Fairness checked once, at launch

      Customer mix, products and fraud patterns drift. The gap that was acceptable in March is not in December.

      Spot itAsk for fairness metrics in the ongoing monitoring pack (F7.7, G3.5), with thresholds that trigger a review.
    • Applying a mitigation that is itself not allowed

      Group-specific cutoffs introduced by a data science team without legal sign-off.

      Spot itAsk whether Legal has approved use of the attribute in the decision, and whether the approach appears in the model documentation.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Answer before opening. The numeric ones need only the identity in Part 2.

    1. A model approves 65% of group B and 73% of group A. Does it pass the four-fifths rule, and does that mean it is fair?

    65 ÷ 73 = 0.89, above 0.8, so it passes that screen. It does not mean fair: the rule looks only at approval rates, ignores error rates and calibration, and comes from US employment guidance. It is a flag, not a verdict.

    2. Group A has 85% who would repay, group B 70%. You require PPV = 90% and TPR = 80% in both. What are the false-positive rates?

    FPR = p/(1−p) × (1−PPV)/PPV × TPR. A: 5.667 × 0.1111 × 0.8 ≈ 50%. B: 2.333 × 0.1111 × 0.8 ≈ 21%. They differ, so equalised odds fails.

    3. Why did equalised odds hold automatically in Simulation 1?

    The score had identical quality in both groups and one cut was used, so TPR and FPR depend only on the cut and score quality, and are the same. Base rates enter approval rates and PPV but not TPR or FPR. In real data scores are rarely equally good across groups, so this would not be automatic.

    4. A team says "equal opportunity is satisfied: among those who repaid, approval is the same in both groups". What might still be wrong?

    You only see repayment for the people you approved, so the "would have repaid" group is partly unobserved (selective labels). Also, the metric ignores false positives: one group might have many more bad loans approved. And the label may be a flawed record of the truth.

    5. Why can a model that never sees nationality still approve nationals more often?

    If nationals and expatriates differ in features such as employer type, tenure, residential area or device and also in repayment history, the model will learn those features. Jointly they identify the group. Simulation 3: many weak features add up to an accurate group guess.

    6. True fraud rates are equal across two customer groups, but one group is confirmed as fraud twice as often. Give two likely causes besides a real difference.

    The group is reviewed or investigated more (more detection), or the alert rule or an older model fires more often on that group, creating more confirmed cases. A third: different reporting behaviour by customers. Fix by sampling randomly for review in both groups to estimate the true rates.

    7. Is applying a lower approval cut to a disadvantaged group acceptable because it makes the model fairer?

    It may reduce a statistical gap, but it uses the protected attribute in the decision, which in many jurisdictions is itself restricted. It also trades off other criteria (Simulation 2). I would not assume it is allowed: Legal and Compliance must approve, and the choice of criterion must be documented.
