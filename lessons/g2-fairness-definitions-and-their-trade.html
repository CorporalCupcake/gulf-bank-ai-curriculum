    Fairness in Lending and Fraud Models: Competing Definitions and Where Bias Comes From

    Why "treat everyone the same" is not one rule but several, why a model cannot satisfy them all when groups differ, and how unfairness gets into a model through data, labels and proxies without anyone intending it.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceFairness is a choice among competing, individually reasonable measurements that cannot all hold at once when groups differ in base rates, so the job is to pick and justify the one that fits the decision, and to hunt for the data, label and proxy problems that make every one of them misleading.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank builds a personal-finance approval model. A reviewer splits applicants into two groups, say UAE nationals and expatriates, or men and women, or under-30s and over-30s, and finds that the model approves 73% of one group and 65% of the other. "Unfair," says one person. Another replies: "Of those we approved, 94% in one group and 87% in the other repaid. The model is simply more cautious where the evidence is thinner." A third says: "Among people who would have repaid, we approved the same share in both groups. That is what fairness means."

    All three are measuring something real. All three numbers can come from the same model. The disagreement is not about arithmetic. It is about which comparison deserves the word "fair".

    Here is the awkward core, which this lesson builds up to. When the two groups genuinely differ in how often the outcome happens (repayment, fraud, default), and your model is not perfect, you cannot equalise approval rates, error rates and the meaning of a score all at the same time. This is a theorem, not a software limitation. Pick one and you break another. So fairness work is mostly an exercise in naming trade-offs honestly.

    The second half of the lesson is about how bias enters before any of that. A model trained on past decisions inherits the past. A label that records "who got investigated" rather than "who did something wrong" teaches the model to investigate the same people again. Removing the protected column does not help when twenty other columns quietly reconstruct it.

    Hold onto thisTwo separate questions. First, measurement: given a model and its outcomes, which fairness numbers do we compute and which gaps do we accept? Second, origin: is the data we are measuring on a trustworthy picture of reality, or does it already carry the bias? Teams that argue only about the first question often have the second one wrong.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The main definitions, and what each one protects

    Take a lending model that scores applicants and approves anyone above a cut. Call the groups A and B (the protected attribute could be gender, nationality, age, religion or anything similar). Let the true outcome be "would repay". Everything below is a statement about a different slice of the same confusion table, computed per group.

    DefinitionIt asks that…In plain wordsIt protects / its weakness
    Fairness through unawarenessThe protected attribute is not a model input."We never look at gender."Easy to audit and often legally required as a rule of conduct. Does not prevent discrimination, because other inputs act as proxies (Part 2, Sources of bias).
    Demographic (statistical) parityP(approve | A) = P(approve | B).Same approval rate in each group.Ignores whether the groups differ in real repayment. Forcing it can approve riskier applicants in one group and reject safer ones in the other.
    Equal opportunityTrue positive rates match: P(approve | would repay, A) = P(approve | would repay, B).Creditworthy people are approved equally often whichever group they are in.Protects the qualified. Says nothing about the unqualified who get approved, and needs the true outcome (hard when rejected applicants never show theirs).
    Equalised oddsTrue positive and false positive rates both match.Both kinds of error are equally likely in each group.Strong and symmetric. Both error rates fall on the same footing.
    Predictive parity / calibration within groupsAmong those the model approves (or gives a score s), the share who truly repay is the same in each group."A score of 0.8 means the same thing for everyone."What risk and finance teams usually mean by fair pricing. Can hide very different error rates between groups.
    Individual fairnessSimilar people get similar scores (Dwork et al., 2012).Two applicants who differ only in an irrelevant way get the same decision.Intuitive, but needs a defensible definition of "similar", which is the whole difficulty.
    Counterfactual fairnessThe decision would not change if the protected attribute were different, holding the causal story fixed (Kusner et al., 2017)."Would she have been approved had she been a man, everything else about her life equal?"Needs a causal model of how the attribute influences other variables (F9.3), which is contestable.

    Notice the structure. Demographic parity compares outcomes to group size. Equal opportunity and equalised odds compare outcomes to the truth, conditioning on who really would repay. Predictive parity compares the truth to the decision, conditioning on who got approved. These are different directions of conditioning. F1.4 taught that P(A given B) and P(B given A) are different numbers. Fairness definitions are the same trap, wearing a suit.

    One more number often appears in compliance packs: the four-fifths rule. If one group's approval rate is less than 80% of the highest group's, that is treated as preliminary evidence of adverse impact. It comes from US employment-selection guidance, and it is a screening rule of thumb, not a Gulf requirement and not a proof of discrimination. Teams use it because it is simple, so know where it came from.

    Read what happened. With the same score quality in both groups and one cut, equalised odds held automatically. A model that treats everyone identically and is equally accurate for everyone still fails demographic parity and predictive parity, purely because the groups differ in how often they repay. The gap is in the world, not the code. That does not mean it is acceptable. It means it cannot be fixed by "making the model more accurate".

    Why you cannot have them all

    The mathematical heart is a short identity (Chouldechova, 2017). For any group with base rate p (the share who would repay), the confusion-table rates are tied together:

    FPR=p1−p·1−PPVPPV·TPR

    FPR is the share of bad applicants approved, PPV (predictive value) the share of approved who repay, TPR the share of good applicants approved. Take two groups with different p. If you force PPV and TPR to be equal across them, the first factor p/(1−p) differs, so FPR must differ. Equal predictive value, equal opportunity and equal false-positive rates are incompatible unless p is equal or the model is perfect. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) proved the matching result for scores: calibration within groups and equal error rates cannot both hold except in those same two special cases.

    Example with numbers. Group A: 85% would repay. Group B: 70%. Suppose we insist that 90% of approvals repay (PPV) and 80% of good applicants are approved (TPR) in both groups. Then FPR for A is (0.85/0.15) × (0.1/0.9) × 0.8 ≈ 50%, and for B it is (0.70/0.30) × (0.1/0.9) × 0.8 ≈ 21%. Half the bad applicants in group A get approved but only a fifth in group B. To equalise the error rates instead, the PPVs would have to differ. The arithmetic leaves no third way.

    Three lessons from playing with it.

    • Every fairness rule has a price somewhere else. It is not that engineers have failed to find the clever fix. The constraints genuinely conflict. Which gap you accept is a values choice that belongs to the business, risk and legal functions, with data scientists making the trade-off visible.
    • The right definition depends on the decision. For credit pricing and provisioning, calibration within groups matters: a stated probability of default must mean the same thing for everybody or the capital numbers are wrong (B4.1). For a benefit that qualified people are being denied, equal opportunity speaks to the harm. For a harmful intervention such as freezing an account, the false-positive rate is the harm, so equalise that. There is no universal answer.
    • When the score is nearly perfect the conflict disappears, which is why "just improve the model" feels right. In real banking data the score never reaches that quality, so the conflict stays.

    Corbett-Davies and co-authors (2023) press a further point: forcing a statistical equality is often at odds with what the decision-maker is trying to achieve, and can harm the very group it was meant to protect. The honest position is to show the trade-off curve and decide with open eyes, not to treat any one definition as sacred.

    What you can actually do about it

    Mitigation methods fall into three groups by where in the pipeline they act.

    StageIdeaTypical toolsCatch
    Before trainingFix the data: reweight or relabel examples, remove features that act as proxies, collect better labels.Reweighing, feature auditsTreats symptoms if the real problem is the label. Slow and manual.
    During trainingAdd a fairness constraint or penalty to the loss, so the model trades accuracy for a smaller gap.Constrained optimisation, adversarial debiasing, Fairlearn reductionsNeeds the protected attribute at training time; harder to explain to validators.
    After trainingKeep the model, change the decision rule: group-specific thresholds chosen to hit equal opportunity or equalised odds (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016).Threshold optimisers, calibration per groupUses the protected attribute at decision time. In many jurisdictions that is itself disparate treatment. Legal review first.

    That last catch is the one that surprises engineers. Group-specific cuts, as in Simulation 2, are the cleanest mathematical fix and may be unlawful to apply because they treat people differently by a protected attribute. Whether and how the attribute can be used, even to measure fairness, depends on the jurisdiction and the specific regulation. Do not assume the answer for the UAE or Saudi Arabia: ask Legal and Compliance, and see how the central bank and consumer-protection rules treat it. Many banks collect protected attributes only for auditing and keep them out of decisions.

    Measurement has its own practical problem: you often do not have the attribute. Banks may not hold religion or ethnicity, and some attributes cannot lawfully be inferred. Proxy-based estimation of group membership (from names, say) introduces its own errors and sensitivities. Be explicit about what you measured and how.

    Sources of bias: data, labels, proxies

    Everything above assumed the data told the truth about who really would repay. Often it does not. Suresh and Guttag (2019) catalogue where bias enters a machine-learning pipeline. Four entry points matter most in banking.

    • Historical bias. The world itself was unequal and the data records it faithfully. If women were historically offered smaller credit limits and so show lower balances and less credit history, a model trained on that history learns to expect less from women. The data is accurate, and the pattern is still a problem.
    • Representation bias. Some groups are thin in the data. A model fitted mainly on salaried nationals has little to go on for self-employed expatriates, so its scores there are noisier and its error rates differ. Thin files and thin-file customers are a standard example.
    • Label and measurement bias. The target is often not the thing you care about but a record of it. "Defaulted" is only observed for people who were given credit. "Fraud" means "fraud that someone investigated and confirmed". Obermeyer and colleagues (2019) found a widely used US healthcare algorithm used past spending as a stand-in for health need, and because less had been spent on Black patients with the same illness, it systematically under-ranked their need. The model was accurate at predicting cost; cost was the wrong label.
    • Proxies. Remove gender and the model can still find it: occupation, employer, shopping categories, device type, the length of a name field, even the time of day an application is made. Postcode was historically a proxy for race in US redlining. In the Gulf, nationality, employer category and residential area can similarly stand in for protected groups. Dropping the column ("unawareness") does not work when the information survives in the others.

    The accuracy at which you can predict the protected attribute from the other inputs is the practical test for proxies. Train a quick classifier from features to group. If it beats chance by a wide margin, the features jointly encode the group, and removing the column was cosmetic. This test also tells you something useful in the other direction: if you can not recover the group, at least no model can exploit it through those features.

    Proxies are not always a mistake to eliminate. Income genuinely predicts repayment and also correlates with nationality. The question is whether the feature has a legitimate, documented business reason and whether a less discriminatory alternative would do the job as well. Regulators in several jurisdictions frame it that way. That makes feature selection a documented decision, not a statistical default.

    Labels that record who was looked at

    The most damaging data problem in fraud and AML work is the quiet one. Confirmed cases only exist where someone investigated. If customers in group B are reviewed more often, whether through a historic rule, a local branch practice or an earlier model, more of their genuine cases get found, and the recorded rate comes out higher even if the true rate is identical. The next model learns "group B is riskier", sends more alerts there, which produces more labels there, and so on. This is a feedback loop, studied formally by Ensign and colleagues (2018) in predictive policing, and its relative in credit is the selective labels problem (Lakkaraju et al., 2017): you observe repayment only for those you approved (see reject inference, B4.5).

    The remedy is not in the model at all. It lies in the process: send a small random sample of unflagged customers to review in every group, so you have an unbiased estimate of the true rate; track alert rates and confirmed-case rates by group over time; and compare the model against a rule-based baseline (C1.2) that does not learn from past review patterns. F3.5 made the same point about label delay, and F2.6 about selection bias: a label reflects both what happened and who looked.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Fairness uses almost everything you have already learned, in a harder setting where the numbers carry moral weight.

    F7.1 – F7.4 · Precision, recall, calibrationThe same confusion table, split by group

    Every fairness definition is a per-group precision, recall or calibration. If you can read a confusion table you can read a fairness report.

    F1.4 · Conditional probabilityDirections of conditioning

    The impossibility result is Bayes' rule in action: different base rates plus an imperfect test mean positive predictive value and error rates cannot both be equal.

    F2.6 · Selection biasLabels exist where someone looked

    Simulation 4 is selection bias with a feedback loop. Also see Simpson's paradox: an aggregate fairness gap can reverse inside each segment.

    F9.3 · Causal inferenceWhat counterfactual fairness needs

    "Would the decision differ if she were a man?" is a causal question. It cannot be answered from a table of correlations.

    F8.1 · F8.2 · ExplanationsFinding the proxy

    Global importance and per-decision attribution are how you discover which inputs carry the bias, and how you give an applicant a reason.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced data and label delayFew positives per group

    Fairness gaps are estimated on subgroups with even fewer positives. Put intervals on every gap.

    B4 · Scorecards and reject inferenceCredit models under scrutiny

    Scorecards are the most audited place for fairness in the bank. Reject inference (B4.5) is the selective-labels problem under another name.

    B1 · B2 · B3 · Fraud, AML, KYCWhere false positives hurt people

    A false positive in fraud or AML means a frozen card or a closed account. Equalising false-positive rates matters most here. See also risk rating and feedback loops.

    G2.5 · G3 · Oversight and validationContestability, documentation, review

    A fairness analysis is part of the model documentation that independent validation reviews, and customers need a way to contest a decision.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Personal finance, cards and mortgages. Approval, limit and pricing models. Gender, age, nationality and employer category are the usual segments to test. The Apple Card limits dispute of 2019 shows how fast the question escalates when customers compare outcomes publicly.
    • Fraud and AML alerts. Disproportionate friction for certain nationalities or names. Name-matching in sanctions screening can have systematically higher false-positive rates on transliterated Arabic names. Measure it.
    • KYC and eKYC. Face matching and liveness models can differ in error rates by skin tone, age or gender, which determines who gets onboarded without a branch visit.
    • Collections and marketing. Who gets the soft offer and who gets the hard call. Often outside the formal model-risk process and therefore under-tested.
    • Staff copilots and customer chatbots. Language models may answer in Arabic and English with different quality, tone or willingness. This is a fairness question that classical tables do not capture.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The definitions and impossibility results are about a decade old and stable. What is moving is how regulators turn "fairness" into obligations, how language models complicate measurement, and how sceptical researchers are of simple parity metrics.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in bank analyses and in AI-generated ones. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "We removed the protected attribute, so the model is fair"

      Proxies reconstruct it. The model is only blind in the narrowest sense.

      Spot itAsk for a quick model that predicts the group from the inputs. Compare its accuracy with chance. Check the top features for postcode, employer type, device or name-derived fields.
    • Reporting one fairness metric

      "Approval rates are within 3 points." Meanwhile false-positive rates differ by a factor of two.

      Spot itAsk for approval rate, TPR, FPR and PPV by group in one table. If only one appears, ask why the others were left out.
    • Believing a metric can be driven to zero for free

      "We tuned thresholds to equalise everything." The identity says that cannot hold unless the base rates match.

      Spot itAsk which fairness criteria are satisfied and which are not. A claim that all hold, with different base rates and an imperfect model, is wrong or hiding something.
    • Gaps without intervals

      A 4-point TPR gap measured on 40 positives in a small group is noise.

      Spot itAsk for the number of positives per group and a bootstrap interval on each gap (F2.2).
    • Trusting the label

      "Confirmed fraud" or "default" are records of observation, not truth.

      Spot itAsk how the label is produced, who decides what to investigate, and whether outcome rates differ by group for reasons that match the review rates, not the behaviour.
    • Feedback loops left unmonitored

      The model sends more alerts to a group, generating more confirmed cases there, which justifies the model.

      Spot itPlot alert share and confirmed-case share by group across retraining cycles. Look for a random-review baseline.
    • Averaging away a subgroup

      Fair on gender, fair on nationality, but unfair on female expatriates.

      Spot itCheck intersections, as long as the groups are big enough to measure.
    • Fairness checked once, at launch

      Customer mix, products and fraud patterns drift. The gap that was acceptable in March is not in December.

      Spot itAsk for fairness metrics in the ongoing monitoring pack (F7.7, G3.5), with thresholds that trigger a review.
    • Applying a mitigation that is itself not allowed

      Group-specific cutoffs introduced by a data science team without legal sign-off.

      Spot itAsk whether Legal has approved use of the attribute in the decision, and whether the approach appears in the model documentation.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There isn't one measure of fairness. We can make approval rates equal, or make mistakes equally likely, or make a score mean the same for everyone. When the groups really differ in repayment, we cannot do all three; that is mathematics, not a gap in our work. So I'd like us to decide which harm we most want to prevent for this product, and I'll show you what that costs on the others and in profit. I'd also like us to check that our records of past outcomes are not themselves skewed by who we reviewed.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd report per-group approval rate, TPR, FPR, PPV and calibration with bootstrap intervals, and say which criterion the product is held to and why. I'd test for proxies by predicting group membership from the features, review label provenance for selection and measurement bias, and keep a random review sample so label feedback loops are visible. Any mitigation that uses the protected attribute in the decision goes to Legal first. Monitoring includes these gaps by segment after launch.”
    “Why not just make the model blind to gender and nationality?”

    We should, as a rule of conduct, but it isn't sufficient. Other inputs such as employer, postcode, device or spending categories can jointly encode the attribute, and the model will use them if the groups differ in outcomes. I'd test whether the group can be predicted from the inputs, and keep the attribute in a separate, restricted dataset for auditing.

    “Which fairness metric should we use?”

    The one that matches the harm. For credit pricing and provisioning, calibration within groups. For a good thing that qualified people are denied, equal opportunity. For a burden like a frozen account, equal false-positive rates. I'd report all of them, name the one we are held to, and show the others so no one is surprised.

    “Can't we tune it until every group has the same error rates and the same approval rate?”

    Not if the groups' true outcome rates differ and the model is imperfect. There's an identity linking the rates: with unequal base rates, equal precision and equal true-positive rate force unequal false-positive rates. We can choose where the inequality falls, but not remove it.

    “Group A is approved 8 points more often. Is the model biased?”

    It shows a disparity, not yet proof of bias. I'd check whether the groups differ in true repayment, whether the model is equally accurate for both, whether labels and inputs are measured equally well, and whether a less disparate model would do as well. If the gap is driven by a feature with a weak business rationale or by a biased label, it is a problem to fix. If it reflects real differences in risk, it is a decision for the business to justify and document.

    “We have no data on religion or ethnicity. How can we test fairness?”

    With care. Test on attributes we hold and may lawfully use for auditing, such as gender, age band and nationality. For others, proxy-based estimation is imprecise and sensitive, so I'd involve Legal and Privacy and be explicit about the error in the estimate. Testing outcomes on observable segments such as region or product is still useful as a signal.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the identity in Part 2.

    1. A model approves 65% of group B and 73% of group A. Does it pass the four-fifths rule, and does that mean it is fair?

    65 ÷ 73 = 0.89, above 0.8, so it passes that screen. It does not mean fair: the rule looks only at approval rates, ignores error rates and calibration, and comes from US employment guidance. It is a flag, not a verdict.

    2. Group A has 85% who would repay, group B 70%. You require PPV = 90% and TPR = 80% in both. What are the false-positive rates?

    FPR = p/(1−p) × (1−PPV)/PPV × TPR. A: 5.667 × 0.1111 × 0.8 ≈ 50%. B: 2.333 × 0.1111 × 0.8 ≈ 21%. They differ, so equalised odds fails.

    3. Why did equalised odds hold automatically in Simulation 1?

    The score had identical quality in both groups and one cut was used, so TPR and FPR depend only on the cut and score quality, and are the same. Base rates enter approval rates and PPV but not TPR or FPR. In real data scores are rarely equally good across groups, so this would not be automatic.

    4. A team says "equal opportunity is satisfied: among those who repaid, approval is the same in both groups". What might still be wrong?

    You only see repayment for the people you approved, so the "would have repaid" group is partly unobserved (selective labels). Also, the metric ignores false positives: one group might have many more bad loans approved. And the label may be a flawed record of the truth.

    5. Why can a model that never sees nationality still approve nationals more often?

    If nationals and expatriates differ in features such as employer type, tenure, residential area or device and also in repayment history, the model will learn those features. Jointly they identify the group. Simulation 3: many weak features add up to an accurate group guess.

    6. True fraud rates are equal across two customer groups, but one group is confirmed as fraud twice as often. Give two likely causes besides a real difference.

    The group is reviewed or investigated more (more detection), or the alert rule or an older model fires more often on that group, creating more confirmed cases. A third: different reporting behaviour by customers. Fix by sampling randomly for review in both groups to estimate the true rates.

    7. Is applying a lower approval cut to a disadvantaged group acceptable because it makes the model fairer?

    It may reduce a statistical gap, but it uses the protected attribute in the decision, which in many jurisdictions is itself restricted. It also trades off other criteria (Simulation 2). I would not assume it is allowed: Legal and Compliance must approve, and the choice of criterion must be documented.
