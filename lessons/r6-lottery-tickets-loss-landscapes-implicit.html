    Why Gradient Descent Finds Good Solutions, and What a Prompt Might Be Doing Inside the Model

    Lottery tickets, the shape of the loss surface and the quiet biases of the optimiser, then the theory that a prompt's examples act like training steps run inside a single forward pass.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA modern network has far more parameters than it needs, so countless settings fit the training data, and gradient descent has built-in preferences (nearby, small, flat, simple) that explain much of why it generalises; one influential theory adds that a prompt's examples make the network run a small gradient-descent-like procedure inside one forward pass, a useful picture and an unfinished one.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a credit-risk team with 20 labelled borrowers and a model with 1,000 free numbers. Classical statistics says this fails: with more knobs than examples, countless settings match the 20 labels, and most are nonsense on borrower 21.

    Deep networks live in exactly this situation and still work. They have more parameters than examples and can even fit pure noise (Zhang et al., 2017), yet predict well on unseen data. So something other than "fits the data" chooses among the fits. What is gradient descent choosing, and why is the choice usually good?

    Think of a landscape: each parameter setting is a point and the height is training loss. In a huge model the bottom is a wide flat valley floor of zero-loss solutions. Gradient descent rolls downhill and stops at some point on it, set by the start, step size, optimiser and stopping time. Like rainwater, where it ends up depends on the route as well as the destination. Those choices act as an unwritten regulariser: implicit regularisation, preferences nobody added to the loss.

    Three ideas, each covered below.

    • The optimiser has taste. From a small start, gradient descent moves only along directions the data points in, so it lands on the smallest solution that fits (implicit regularisation).
    • Shape matters, not just height. Two solutions with equal training loss can sit in valleys of different sharpness. Large steps cannot stay in sharp valleys, so they drift to flat ones, which tend to survive small changes in the data (loss landscapes).
    • Most of the network is spare. You can often delete 80-90% of the weights after training and keep the accuracy. Was a small network hiding inside from the start? (lottery tickets).

    The second topic: put worked examples in a prompt and the weights do not change, yet behaviour does. One theory says the forward pass runs something like gradient descent on those examples (in-context learning as implicit gradient descent). It has real proofs in small settings and real doubts for large ones.

    Hold onto this"Fits the training data" does not pin down a model. The training procedure picks among the fits, and that choice carries most of the generalisation. When someone says a model "just memorises", ask which solution the optimiser would choose and why. It is a sharper question than "is it overfitting?".
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Implicit regularisation: the bias nobody wrote down

    Take a linear model with more weights than examples. Each gradient step, w ← w − η·(error)·x, adds a multiple of an example's input and nothing else. So in every direction the data never touches, the weights stay exactly where they started. Like a hiker told only to head for the road, it never wanders sideways without a reason. If the run reaches zero loss, it lands on the perfect fit closest to the start:

    w∞=w0+X⊤(XX⊤)−1(y−Xw0)

    X holds the training inputs (one example per row), y the targets, w0 the start. From w0 = 0 this is the minimum-norm interpolating solution: of all perfect fits, the one with the smallest weights. It is the limit of ridge regression (F2.5) as the penalty shrinks to zero. In Simulation 1, x = (2, 1), y = 2 gives (0.8, 0.4), the green point. Nobody asked for small weights; the algorithm delivered them.

    That is exact for linear models. Research looks for analogues in real networks:

    • Separable classification. If a line can split the classes, gradient descent on logistic loss never stops (the loss shrinks as weights grow), but the direction of the weights converges to the maximum-margin separator, the same one a support vector machine picks (Soudry et al., 2018).
    • Depth changes the preference. In deep linear networks and matrix factorisation, gradient descent from a small start finds low-rank solutions, learning strong patterns first and weak ones later (Arora et al., 2019). One layer prefers small weights; a deep one prefers simple structure.
    • The optimiser is part of the model. Adam vs SGD, step size, initial scale, momentum, batch size and stopping time each select a different point on the valley floor. Early stopping is implicit regularisation on purpose: stopping early returns something between the start and the minimum-norm answer, much like a ridge penalty (Simulation 4).
    • It is not a complete explanation. The preferences are proved for simple models and only observed for large ones. Neyshabur et al. (2015) call this the search for the "real inductive bias": size does not control generalisation, something about the search does. No theorem yet predicts a real language model's generalisation.

    This is the story of double descent and grokking: interpolating does not mean generalising badly. It also reframes bias and variance (F4.1): parameter count is the wrong complexity measure for these models.

    Loss landscapes: valleys, sharpness and step size

    A real network has millions to trillions of weights, so the surface has that many dimensions. Four facts to carry:

    • Bad local minima are rarely the problem. A true pit needs upward curvature in every one of millions of directions, so most zero-slope points are saddles (like a mountain pass), not traps. Over-parameterised networks usually reach near-zero training loss. The mystery is the quality of what is found.
    • Good regions are connected. Solutions from different random starts can often be joined by a path of low loss (Garipov et al., 2018; Draxler et al., 2018). Networks trained from the same early checkpoint land in the same basin, which is why averaging weights (model "soups", merging; R2.5) can work.
    • Sharpness is the second axis. At a minimum, the surface curves upward by different amounts in different directions. The largest curvature, h, is the sharpness. Think of a ball in a narrow ravine versus a wide bowl: nudge the weights (or the data) and the ravine's loss jumps, the bowl's barely moves. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) argued flat minima generalise better; Keskar et al. (2017) found large-batch training reaches sharper minima and generalises worse.
    • Step size acts on sharpness. Gradient descent in a valley of curvature h is stable only if the step is below 2/h. Above that each step overshoots by more than the last and the iterate bounces outward. A step size η therefore cannot sit in a valley sharper than 2/η, so larger steps are pushed to flatter regions.
    η<2h

    η is the step size, h the sharpness at the minimum. On a bowl f(w) = ½·h·w², each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by (1 − η·h). That is below 1 in size only when η·h is between 0 and 2. At η·h = 1 you land on the bottom in one step; past 2 you diverge. To read it: a sharpness of 50 allows steps up to 0.04; a sharpness of 2 allows up to 1.

    Two caveats.

    • "Flat" depends on parameterisation. Dinh et al. (2017) showed a ReLU network can have one layer scaled up and the next down with the function unchanged, making a minimum look arbitrarily sharp or flat. So "flat minima generalise" is not a theorem about raw curvature; useful measures are scale-aware and the evidence is empirical.
    • Large steps sit at the edge, not inside. Cohen et al. (2021) found that in full-batch training, sharpness rises until about 2/η and then hovers there while the loss keeps falling, unevenly. This edge of stability means the step size, not the loss alone, shapes the geometry you end up with. The toy above shows only the stability rule behind it.

    Sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM, Foret et al., 2021) makes the preference explicit: at each step, nudge the weights in the worst nearby direction and take the gradient there, so the optimiser favours neighbourhoods of low loss. It often helps a little on vision benchmarks at about twice the compute.

    Practice you already know steers the same choice: learning-rate schedules and warm-up (F6.2), gradient mechanics (F1.3), dropout and weight decay (F6.3).

    The lottery ticket hypothesis: a small network inside the big one

    Pruning means deleting a trained network's smallest-magnitude weights. It has long been known that most weights can go with little loss. Frankle and Carbin (2019) asked the opposite: if the small network suffices, why not train it directly? From a random start it usually does worse.

    Their answer, the lottery ticket hypothesis: a large random network contains a small sub-network (the "winning ticket") that, trained alone from the same initial values it had in the big network, matches the full network in the same number of steps. Like buying many lottery tickets: the win was in one of them from the start. The recipe:

    1. Save the starting weights, then train the dense network.
    2. Prune the smallest-magnitude weights (say 50%), leaving a mask of survivors.
    3. Rewind the survivors to their saved starting values and train again (repeat 2-3 to prune more).

    The key controls are the same mask with fresh random values, and a random mask of the same size. The claim is that mask and initial values come as a pair. It matters because it suggests training is partly a search for a sub-network present from step one. Simulation 3 runs this for real on a tiny network.

    What later research found:

    • Rewinding to step 0 fails on big networks. For ResNets on ImageNet and for transformers, tickets appear only if you rewind to a point a few percent into training ("late rewinding", Frankle et al., 2020). Early in training the network commits to a basin, after which different runs behave alike up to noise ("linear mode connectivity").
    • The mask carries most of the information. A winning mask with weights reduced to their signs can give good accuracy untrained (Zhou et al., 2019). Sufficiently wide random networks contain sub-networks that work with no training (Ramanujan et al., 2020; Malach et al., 2020, proved for wide enough layers). Choosing connections can substitute for learning values.
    • Doubts. With a proper training budget, a pruned architecture trained from scratch matches the pruned-and-fine-tuned one (Liu et al., 2019), so the structure matters, not the inherited weights. Pruning at initialisation mostly fails to beat simple controls (Frankle et al., 2021). Finding a ticket needs the dense training run first: it saves inference cost, not training cost.
    • Production pruning is more modest. You train, prune, fine-tune and quantise; no rewinding. Unstructured holes rarely speed up hardware unless many are removed, so structured pruning, quantisation and distillation (P3.1, R12.3) dominate.

    Sparse sub-networks, flat valleys and minimum-norm solutions are three views of one fact: a big network's trained result is far simpler than its parameter count. It also explains why mixture-of-experts and low-rank adapters (M2.4) work: the change you need lives in a small subspace.

    In-context learning as implicit gradient descent

    Give a language model three labelled messages in the prompt ("complaint", "enquiry", "complaint") and a fourth to label. It labels it, with no weight change. That is in-context learning (ICL), the behaviour that made prompting useful (M2.1, M1.3).

    How can a fixed network learn from its input? Several 2022-23 papers (Akyürek et al.; von Oswald et al.; Dai et al.) suggest attention can implement a step of gradient descent: the forward pass is a tiny training run on the examples, and the answer is the prediction of the model it just "trained".

    The exact version: each example is an input vector xi with a number yi, and the task is linear regression. Start at zero weights and take one gradient step on the examples (average squared error, step size η). The new weights and prediction are

    w^1=ηk∑i=1kyixi,y^(xq)=ηk∑i=1kyi(xi·xq)

    k is the number of examples, xq the new query. The second expression is what one linear attention head computes (F5.5): compare the query with each example's key by dot product (xi · xq), then mix the examples' values (yi) using those scores as weights. Like a student answering by leaning most on the worked examples that look most like the question. So one attention layer outputs exactly the prediction after one gradient step on the prompt examples (von Oswald et al., 2023); stacked layers take more steps.

    Evidence, strongest first:

    • A construction. A transformer layer's weights can be chosen to do this. That shows it is possible, not that it happens.
    • Small trained transformers learn something like it. Trained from scratch on many random linear-regression tasks, they solve new ones in context (Garg et al., 2022). The trained single-layer model matches the gradient-descent construction (von Oswald et al.); larger ones behave like ridge regression or exact least squares (Akyürek et al.). The trained solution is a preconditioned gradient descent (Ahn et al., 2023), gradient flow provably converges to it (Zhang, Frei and Bartlett, 2024), and one gradient step is the best a single linear-attention layer can do (Mahankali et al., 2023).
    • The link to real LLMs is weaker. Dai et al. (2023) saw similar signatures in GPT-style models, but Shen et al. (2024) found the match inconsistent across models, datasets and example counts. It remains a hypothesis: real tasks are not linear regression, real attention uses softmax, and real models do many things at once.

    What the theory clarifies:

    • Prompting can feel like fine-tuning. Examples steer the model either through attention (prompt) or through weights (training). So few-shot vs fine-tune (M2.3) is a real trade-off.
    • Diminishing returns, and format matters. The curves fall steeply then flatten. Example selection and order change what the "gradient steps" see, matching practical experience.
    • It may not be "learning" at all. The rival story: pretraining already taught many tasks and the examples only say which one this is, a Bayesian "task recognition" view (Xie et al., 2022). Supporting it, models often do well with randomised example labels because format and label space carry the signal (Min et al., 2022). Bigger models do more real learning from examples; small ones mostly recognise (Pan et al., 2023). Both likely operate, in proportions that vary.
    • What it does not explain: where the circuits sit. Mechanistic work on induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022) finds heads that copy earlier prompt patterns and appear to power much ICL; see R5.3-R5.4.
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most harm comes from stretching a theory past where it was shown. Watch for these stretches in papers, vendor pitches and AI-written summaries.

    • "Flat minima generalise" treated as a law

      It is a correlation with exceptions, and "flat" depends on parameterisation.

      Spot itAsk which sharpness measure, computed how, and whether it tracks held-out error across your own runs. One number with no spread across seeds is decoration.
    • Calling a pruned model a lottery ticket

      Prune-and-fine-tune is ordinary compression. A ticket needs the rewind and the control against a random start with the same mask.

      Spot itAsk whether the sparse network was retrained from original (or early) weights, and whether a random re-initialisation of the same mask was tried.
    • Sparsity that saves nothing

      "80% removed" sounds five times cheaper. Unstructured holes need index storage and rarely speed up GPUs.

      Spot itAsk for measured latency and memory on the target hardware. Twenty million weights, 80% pruned, at four bytes of value plus four of index, is 32 MB against 80 MB dense: 60% saved, not 80%, and no faster without suitable hardware.
    • Believing a lottery result from one run

      Small differences between pruned variants are within seed noise, as Simulation 3 shows.

      Spot itAsk for several seeds and the spread. A gain smaller than the spread is no finding.
    • "The prompt fine-tunes the model"

      Nothing is stored. The effect vanishes with the context and a long irrelevant prompt can swamp it.

      Spot itRun the prompt with examples removed, shuffled and with wrong labels. If accuracy barely moves, the model was recognising a task, not learning from examples.
    • Importing the linear-regression result into an LLM claim

      "Transformers do gradient descent" was shown for small models on synthetic linear tasks. Applying it to a production LLM, or to promise how many examples suffice, overreaches.

      Spot itCheck the paper's setting: model size, task type, layers. A synthetic linear task means a hypothesis for LLMs, not a fact.
    • Trusting few-shot results without testing order and selection

      The same five examples in another order can swing accuracy by many points. One hand-picked prompt tested on 50 cases proves little.

      Spot itAsk for results across several shuffles and example sets, with an interval on a validation set big enough to support one (F2.1, F2.7).
    • Overfitting through the optimiser's choices

      If start, step size and stopping time select the solution, tuning them on the test set tunes the model to it.

      Spot itAsk which data picked the learning rate, step count and seed. It should be a validation set, never the final test set (F4.9).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. One example has inputs x = (3, 4) and target y = 10. A linear model starts at zero and is trained to zero loss. What weights does it reach, and why?

    w = x·y ÷ (x·x) = (3, 4)·10 ÷ 25 = (1.2, 1.6). Check: 3×1.2 + 4×1.6 = 10. Every step is a multiple of x, so the weights stay on the line through the origin along (3, 4); the only fitting point there is (1.2, 1.6), also the smallest-norm perfect fit (norm 2).

    2. A minimum has sharpness 50. What is the largest step size that can stay there? What happens at η = 0.1?

    2 ÷ 50 = 0.04. At η = 0.1 each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by |1 − 0.1 × 50| = 4, so the iterate is thrown out to a flatter region or bounces. Large steps carry an implicit preference for flat minima.

    3. Why might "this minimum is flat, so it will generalise" mislead?

    Flatness depends on parameter scaling. In a ReLU network, scaling one layer up and the next down leaves the function unchanged but makes a minimum look sharper or flatter (Dinh et al., 2017). Flatness is a useful empirical correlate only if measured scale-aware, and it is not a cause.

    4. What control separates a real lottery ticket from a good pruning mask?

    Keep the mask but give the survivors fresh random values (and separately try a random mask of the same size). If the original-start version trains better, the pairing matters, which is the lottery claim. If both do equally well, pruning just found a good architecture, as Liu et al. (2019) argue is often the case.

    5. A 20-million-weight model is pruned by 80%; survivors are stored as a four-byte value plus a four-byte index. How big is it against dense four-byte weights, and is it faster?

    4 million × 8 bytes = 32 MB vs 20 million × 4 bytes = 80 MB: 60% saved, not 80%. Speed depends on hardware. General GPUs gain little from irregular holes unless sparsity is very high or structured, so measure.

    6. Two prompt examples: (x₁, y₁) = ((1, 0), 2) and (x₂, y₂) = ((0, 1), −2). With the one-step formula and η = 0.5, what is the prediction for query x = (2, 1)?

    ŷ = (η ÷ k) Σ yᵢ (xᵢ·xq) = (0.5 ÷ 2) × (2×2 + (−2)×1) = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5. Same via weights: w₁ = 0.25 × (2·(1,0) + (−2)·(0,1)) = (0.5, −0.5), then 0.5×2 + (−0.5)×1 = 0.5. Attention weights example values by key-query similarity: that is the whole step.

    7. In Simulation 4 with high noise, why does full least squares do worst near four examples, and how does that relate to early stopping?

    There are four unknowns, so near four examples the fit can pass through every noisy label and is very sensitive to the noise (the double-descent peak). Gradient descent stopped after a few steps has not yet fitted the small, noise-dominated directions, so it acts like a regularised fit. Early stopping is implicit regularisation, the same idea as the minimum-norm bias in Simulation 1.

    8. A model does as well with randomly wrong labels in its few-shot examples as with correct ones. What does that say about how it uses them?

    It mostly uses them to recognise the task and format (input style, label space), not to learn the input-label mapping (Min et al., 2022). Examples still matter for format. But the "gradient descent on the examples" picture does not fully describe this model on this task.
