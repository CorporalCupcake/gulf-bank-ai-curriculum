    Why Gradient Descent Finds Good Solutions, and What a Prompt Might Be Doing Inside the Model

    Lottery tickets, the shape of the loss surface and the quiet biases of the optimiser, then the theory that a prompt's examples act like training steps run inside a single forward pass.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA modern network has far more parameters than it needs, so there are countless ways to fit the training data, and gradient descent does not pick one at random: it has built-in preferences (nearby, small, flat, simple) that explain much of why it generalises, and one influential theory says that when you put examples in a prompt, the network quietly runs a small gradient-descent-like procedure on them, which is a useful picture and an unfinished one.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit-risk team has 20 labelled borrowers and fits a model with 1,000 free numbers. The textbook says this cannot work: with more knobs than examples you can match the 20 labels in a huge number of ways, most of them nonsense on a 21st borrower. Classical statistics would stop you.

    Yet in deep learning this is the normal situation, and the models work. Image and language networks have more parameters than training examples, can fit pure noise if you ask them to (Zhang et al., 2017), and still predict well on data they never saw. Something other than "fits the data" must be choosing among the fits. The question this lesson is about is: what is gradient descent choosing, and why is the choice usually good?

    Picture a landscape. Every setting of the parameters is a point, and the height is the training loss. In a huge model the bottom of the landscape is not a single pit. It is a vast flat valley floor where the loss is zero, full of different solutions. Gradient descent rolls downhill from wherever it starts and stops at some point on that floor. Which point depends on where it started, how big its steps were, which optimiser it used and when it stopped. Those choices act as an unwritten regulariser. That is implicit regularisation: preferences nobody added to the loss, built into the way we search.

    Three ideas to hold, each of which gets its own treatment below.

    • The optimiser has taste. Plain gradient descent from a small start moves only along directions the data points in, so it never invents weight where the data gave no reason to. It lands on the smallest solution that fits. That is a preference for simplicity that nobody wrote down (R6.3, implicit regularisation).
    • Shape matters, not just height. Two solutions with the same training loss can differ in how sharp the valley is around them. Large steps cannot stay in a sharp valley, so they drift towards flat ones, and flat ones tend to survive small changes in the data (loss landscapes).
    • Most of the network is spare. You can often delete 80–90% of the weights after training and keep the accuracy. The lottery ticket hypothesis asks whether a small network was hiding inside the big one from the start (lottery tickets).

    Then the second topic turns the lens on language models. When you put a few worked examples into a prompt, the model's weights do not change, yet its behaviour does. One of the cleanest theories of why says the forward pass is doing something that looks like gradient descent on those examples (R6.4, in-context learning as implicit gradient descent). It is a theory about a mechanism, with real proofs in small settings and real doubts about large ones.

    Hold onto this"Fits the training data" does not pin down a model. The training procedure picks among the fits, and that choice carries most of the generalisation. When someone says a model "just memorises", ask which solution the optimiser would have chosen and why. That is a sharper question than "is it overfitting?".
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Implicit regularisation: the bias nobody wrote down

    Start with the case you just played with, which is exactly solvable. A linear model has more weights than examples. Gradient descent on squared error updates the weights by a multiple of each example's input vector: w ← w − η·(error)·x. Every step adds a combination of the training inputs and nothing else. So after any number of steps the weights equal the starting weights plus something inside the span of the data. Whatever the starting point had in directions the data does not touch stays exactly as it was.

    If the run converges to zero loss, the answer is the unique solution that is the smallest possible change from the start:

    w∞=w0+X⊤(XX⊤)−1(y−Xw0)

    X holds the training inputs, one example per row; y the targets; w0 the starting weights. The correction term lives entirely in the span of the data. From w0 = 0 this is the minimum-norm interpolating solution, the same answer as ridge regression (F2.5) in the limit where the penalty shrinks to zero. In Simulation 1, x = (2, 1), y = 2 gives (0.8, 0.4), the green point. Nobody asked for small weights; the algorithm delivered them anyway.

    That is a fact about linear models. The research programme is to find its analogues for the networks people really use. The main findings, in plain terms:

    • Classification with separable data. If one straight line can separate the classes, gradient descent on logistic loss never stops (the loss keeps shrinking as weights grow), but the direction of the weights converges to the maximum-margin separator, the same one a support vector machine would choose (Soudry et al., 2018). Again a preference nobody coded.
    • Depth changes the preference. In deep linear networks and matrix factorisation, gradient descent from a small start tends to find low-rank solutions, learning the strongest patterns first and the weaker ones later (Arora et al., 2019). Where a one-layer model prefers small weights, a deep one prefers simple structure.
    • The optimiser is part of the model. Adam and SGD, big steps and small steps, small versus large initial weights, momentum, batch size, and when you stop all select different points on the valley floor. Early stopping is implicit regularisation you choose on purpose: stopping before convergence returns something between the start and the minimum-norm answer, much like a ridge penalty (Simulation 4 below shows this working).
    • It is not a complete explanation. The preferences are proved for simple models and observed, not proved, for large ones. Neyshabur et al. (2015) put it as "in search of the real inductive bias": the network's size is not what controls generalisation, something about the search is. Nobody can yet state a theorem that predicts the generalisation of a real language model from first principles.

    This is the same story as double descent and grokking in the neighbouring lesson: a model that can interpolate does not have to generalise badly, because interpolation is not a single thing. It also reframes the bias-variance picture of F4.1 (bias, variance and generalisation): counting parameters is the wrong complexity measure for these models.

    Loss landscapes: valleys, sharpness and step size

    The landscape picture is easy to say and hard to see. A real network has millions to trillions of weights, so the surface lives in that many dimensions. Four facts from the research are worth carrying:

    • Bad local minima are rarely the problem. In high dimensions most points where the slope is zero are saddles, not traps, because to be a true pit the surface must curve upwards in every one of millions of directions. For over-parameterised networks, optimisation usually succeeds: gradient descent reaches near-zero training loss. The mystery is the quality of what it finds, not whether it finds anything.
    • The good regions are connected. Solutions found from different random starts can often be joined by a path along which the loss stays low (Garipov et al., 2018; Draxler et al., 2018). Later work found that networks trained from the same early checkpoint land in the same basin, which is why averaging weights (model "soups", merging; R2.5) can work at all.
    • Sharpness is the other axis. At a minimum, the surface curves upward by different amounts in different directions. The largest curvature, written h here, measures how sharp the valley is. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber argued in 1997 that flat minima should generalise better, since a small shift in the weights (or the data) barely changes the loss. Keskar et al. (2017) reported that large-batch training finds sharper minima and generalises worse.
    • The step size acts on sharpness. This one has a precise, checkable form. Gradient descent on a valley of curvature h is stable only if the step size is below 2/h. Above that, each step overshoots the bottom by more than the previous distance and the iterate bounces outward. So a given step size cannot sit in a valley sharper than 2/η. Larger steps are therefore forced towards flatter regions.
    η<2h

    η is the step size and h the sharpness (largest curvature) at the minimum. On a bowl f(w) = ½·h·w², each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by (1 − η·h). Its absolute value is below 1 only when η·h is between 0 and 2. At η·h = 1 you land on the bottom in one step; past 2 you diverge.

    Two caveats keep this honest.

    • "Flat" is not a parameterisation-free idea. Dinh et al. (2017) showed that in a ReLU network you can rescale one layer up and the next down without changing the function at all, which makes a minimum look arbitrarily sharp or flat. So "flat minima generalise" cannot be a theorem about raw curvature alone; the useful measures are scale-aware, and the evidence is empirical.
    • Large steps do not just avoid sharp regions, they sit at their edge. Cohen et al. (2021) found that when training with full-batch gradient descent, the sharpness grows until it reaches about 2/η and then hovers there, with the loss still falling over longer stretches but not smoothly. This edge of stability contradicts the textbook picture that you should be safely inside the stable zone, and it means the step size, not the loss alone, controls the geometry you end up with. The toy above shows only the stability rule behind it, not the self-correcting dynamics.

    One practical offspring is sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM, Foret et al., 2021): at each step, nudge the weights in the worst direction nearby and take the gradient there, so the optimiser prefers regions where the loss stays low in a neighbourhood. It often helps a little on vision benchmarks at roughly twice the compute, and has been studied heavily in 2024–26. It is a good example of turning an implicit preference into an explicit one.

    Connections to practice you already know: learning-rate schedules and warm-up (F6.2), the gradient mechanics in F1.3, and why dropout and weight decay help (F6.3) are all, partly, ways of steering which point on the valley floor you reach.

    The lottery ticket hypothesis: a small network inside the big one

    Training a large network, then deleting the weights with the smallest magnitude, is called pruning. It has been known since the 1990s that you can remove most of a trained network's weights and lose almost nothing. The puzzle Frankle and Carbin asked in 2019 was different: if the small network is enough, why can't we train the small network directly? When people tried, from a random start, it usually did worse.

    Their answer, the lottery ticket hypothesis: a large randomly initialised network contains a small sub-network (the "winning ticket") that, if trained by itself starting from the same initial values it had in the big network, can match the full network's accuracy in the same number of steps. The recipe is:

    1. Save the starting weights, then train the dense network.
    2. Prune the smallest-magnitude weights, say 50%, leaving a mask of survivors.
    3. Rewind the survivors to their saved starting values and train again (repeat steps 2–3 to prune more).

    The crucial control is the comparison with the same mask but fresh random starting values, and with a random mask of the same size. The claim is that the mask and its initial values come as a pair: the structure plus the lucky starting point is the ticket. The idea was a sensation partly because it suggests that training is largely a search for a good sub-network that was present from the first step. Simulation 3 runs this experiment, for real, on a very small network.

    What the research has found since, which is what to remember:

    • Plain rewinding to step 0 fails for big networks. For ResNets on ImageNet and for transformers, tickets are found only if you rewind not to the very start but to an early point in training (a few percent in), the "late rewinding" of Frankle et al. (2020). The reason: early in training, the network settles into a basin of the landscape, after which different random runs behave the same up to noise ("linear mode connectivity"). The ticket is a statement about that early commitment.
    • The mask carries most of the information. Zhou et al. (2019) found that a winning ticket's mask, even with the weights set to just their sign, can give good accuracy untrained. Ramanujan et al. (2020) and Malach et al. (2020) showed that sufficiently wide, randomly weighted networks contain sub-networks that do the job with no training at all, and proved it for wide enough layers. This says the "search" view is real: choosing connections can substitute for learning values.
    • Doubts about novelty and use. Liu et al. (2019) found that, with a proper training budget, a pruned architecture trained from scratch matches the pruned-and-fine-tuned one, so the structure matters, not the inherited weights. Frankle et al. (2021) showed that methods that prune at initialisation, before any training, mostly fail to beat simple controls. Finding tickets still requires training the dense network first, so they save inference cost but not training cost.
    • Practical pruning is more modest. In production, you train, prune, fine-tune and quantise; you do not rewind. Pruning's value is smaller and cheaper models, but unstructured sparsity (random holes) rarely speeds up real hardware unless a lot is removed, so structured pruning, quantisation and distillation (P3.1, R12.3) dominate in practice.

    Link to the rest of the lesson: sparse sub-networks, flat valleys and minimum-norm solutions are three views of one fact, that a big network's training result is much simpler than its parameter count. It also explains why mixture-of-experts and low-rank adapters (M2.4) work: most of the change you need lives in a small subspace.

    In-context learning as implicit gradient descent

    Now the second topic. You give a language model three labelled examples in the prompt, for instance three customer messages tagged "complaint", "enquiry", "complaint", and then a fourth message. It labels the fourth. No weights changed. Yet the model learned the task from the examples: that is in-context learning (ICL), the behaviour that made prompting useful (M2.1, M1.3).

    How can a fixed network learn something from its input? The suggestion, from several 2022–23 papers (Akyürek et al.; von Oswald et al.; Dai et al.), is that attention can implement a step of gradient descent, so the forward pass over the prompt is a tiny training run on the examples, and the answer is the prediction of the model it just "trained".

    Here is the cleanest version, where the maths is exact. Suppose each example is an input vector xi with a number yi to predict, and the task is linear regression. Start the in-context model at zero weights and take one gradient step on the examples (average squared error, step size η). The new weights are

    w^1=ηk∑i=1kyixi,y^(xq)=ηk∑i=1kyi(xi·xq)

    k is the number of examples and xq the new query. The second expression is what a single linear attention head computes (F5.5, attention): the query is compared with each example's key by a dot product (xi · xq), and the result mixes the examples' values (yi) with those scores as weights. So one attention layer can output exactly "the prediction after one step of gradient descent on the examples in the prompt" (von Oswald et al., 2023). Stack layers and you can take more steps.

    The evidence, in order of strength:

    • A construction. The weights of a transformer layer can be chosen so that it does this. That shows it is possible, not that it happens.
    • Trained small transformers do learn something like it. When a small transformer is trained from scratch on thousands of random linear-regression tasks, with examples in the prompt, it learns to solve new tasks in context (Garg et al., 2022). Von Oswald et al. found that the trained single-layer model matches the gradient-descent construction, and Akyürek et al. found that larger ones behave like ridge regression or even exact least squares, depending on size. Ahn et al. (2023) showed the trained solution is a preconditioned gradient descent, better than the plain kind, and Zhang, Frei and Bartlett (2024) proved that gradient flow on such a transformer converges to it. Mahankali et al. (2023) proved that for one layer of linear attention, one step of gradient descent is the best you can do.
    • The link to real LLMs is weaker. Dai et al. (2023) argued that GPT-style models show similar signatures, but a careful check by Shen et al. (2024) found that the match between pretrained LLMs doing ICL and gradient descent is inconsistent across models, datasets and numbers of examples, so the equivalence remains an unproven hypothesis for them. Real tasks are not linear regression, real attention has softmax, and real models do many things at once.

    Three things the theory clarifies, and one it does not.

    • Why prompting can feel like fine-tuning. Examples in a prompt and examples in a training set both steer the model, one through attention, one through weights. That is why the few-shot vs fine-tune choice (M2.3) is a real trade-off and not magic versus engineering.
    • Why more examples help with diminishing returns, and why the format matters. The curves in the simulation fall steeply and then flatten. It also suggests that example selection and order change what the "gradient steps" see, which matches practical experience.
    • Why it does not always need "learning". The competing story is that pretraining already taught the model many tasks, and the examples merely tell it which task this is, a Bayesian "task recognition" view (Xie et al., 2022). Evidence for it: models often still do well when example labels are randomised, because the format and the label space carry the signal (Min et al., 2022). Pan et al. (2023) separated the two: bigger models do more real task learning from the examples, small ones mostly recognise. Both mechanisms are probably at work, in proportions that depend on the model and task.
    • What it does not explain. Where, in a trained LLM, the circuits sit. For that, see the mechanistic work on induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022), which copy patterns from earlier in the prompt and appear to power much ICL; see R5.3–R5.4.
    Part 3

    How it connects

    This is theory, but it touches almost every practical decision about training and deploying models.

    R6.1 · Double descent and grokkingThe same puzzle, from the data side

    Why test error can fall again after the model can interpolate, and why a network can generalise long after it fits the training set. Implicit regularisation is the common thread.

    F1.3 · Gradients and optimisationWhat gradient descent actually does

    Simulation 1 is the update rule from F1.3 on its simplest possible problem. The "stays in the span of the data" fact comes straight from the gradient formula.

    F6.1 · F6.2 · Learning rates and schedulesWhy step size is not just speed

    The stability rule η < 2/h is why big learning rates need warm-up, and why decay at the end often improves generalisation: you let the iterate settle into the bottom of whichever valley it is in.

    F4.4 · F6.3 · Overfitting and regularisationExplicit versus implicit

    Weight decay, dropout and early stopping are explicit and implicit regularisation working together. Early stopping is the cleanest case of implicit regularisation you can control.

    F4.1 · Bias, variance and generalisationWhy counting parameters misleads

    The classical U-shaped curve assumes complexity grows with parameter count. Here, the same count can give very different effective complexity depending on the optimiser.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsRidge is minimum norm in disguise

    The minimum-norm answer is the limit of ridge regression; early-stopped gradient descent traces a similar path. It is the same idea in a simpler model you can check by hand.

    F5.5 · AttentionQuery, key, value, in the ICL formula

    The ICL formula above is attention with the examples as keys and values. If attention is clear to you, this theory is a short step.

    M2.1 · M2.3 · Prompting, RAG, fine-tuningChoosing how to adapt a model

    If prompt examples are weak, short-lived gradient steps, then fine-tuning (real gradient steps on a lot more examples) should do better on stable tasks, and prompts win on speed and flexibility.

    P3.1 · R12.3 · Pruning, quantisation, distillationThe deployment cousin of lottery tickets

    Where the lottery-ticket finding becomes engineering: shrink a trained model to make it cheaper and faster on bank infrastructure.

    R5.3 · R5.4 · Induction heads and in-context learningThe circuit view of ICL

    The gradient-descent theory says what ICL computes in an idealised model; mechanistic interpretability looks for the actual components in real ones. They are complementary and not yet reconciled.

    R2.1 · Scaling lawsWhy bigger works, empirically

    Scaling laws describe that loss falls smoothly with size; this lesson is about the shape of the solution space that makes it so. Theory lags behind the empirical laws.

    Where it shows up at the bank, indirectly but really:

    • Few labels, large model. AML confirmed cases, rare complaint types and KYC edge cases give hundreds of labels, not millions. The implicit-regularisation view tells you why a heavily over-parameterised model, fine-tuned gently (small learning rate, early stopping, a small adapter), stays close to its pretrained solution and does not wildly overfit, and why aggressive training erases that advantage.
    • Few-shot prompts in production. Classifying Arabic and English complaint letters or tagging transaction narratives with five examples in the prompt is in-context learning. The theory gives you a vocabulary for why the choice and order of examples matters and why the answer is brittle.
    • Model risk and validation. Validators ask "why should we believe this model generalises?". "It has a low test error on a time-ordered split" is the answer that counts; the theory in this lesson is background that explains why the usual advice (simple baselines, early stopping, flat minima, multiple seeds) works, not a substitute for the test.
    • Shrinking models for on-premises use. Data residency rules push some banks to run models in their own data centres, where a pruned or quantised model is cheaper. Whether the smaller model is as good on your task is an empirical question that needs your own evaluation set.
    • Reproducibility. If the starting point and the step size select a solution, then two training runs with different seeds are different models. For credit scoring and other regulated settings, fix seeds, report variation across seeds and check that decisions on individual cases are stable (F7.7, drift and stability).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    These are theory topics, so "now" means which claims have survived. In brief: the implicit-bias results for simple models are solid; the flat-minima and edge-of-stability pictures are well supported empirically but not settled; lottery tickets are real as a finding about pruning and weaker as a training method; and ICL-as-gradient-descent is a rich, contested theory for small transformers that has not been shown to be the mechanism in large language models.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most of the harm here comes from stretching a theory beyond where it was shown. These are the stretches to watch for in papers, vendor pitches and AI-written summaries.

    • "Flat minima generalise" treated as a law

      It is a correlation with exceptions, and "flat" depends on how you parameterise the model. A claim that a model is safe because its minimum is flat needs a scale-aware measure and a test on new data.

      Spot itAsk which sharpness measure, computed how, and whether it correlates with held-out error across your own runs. If the answer is a single number with no variation across seeds, it is decoration.
    • Calling a pruned model a lottery ticket

      Pruning a trained model and fine-tuning it is ordinary compression. A ticket needs the rewind step and the control against a random start with the same mask.

      Spot itAsk whether the sparse network was retrained from the original (or early) weights, and whether a random re-initialisation of the same mask was tried.
    • Sparsity that saves nothing

      "80% of weights removed" sounds like five times cheaper. Unstructured holes need index storage and rarely speed up GPUs.

      Spot itAsk for measured latency and memory on the target hardware, not the sparsity percentage. Twenty million weights, 80% pruned, stored as four-byte value plus four-byte index, is 32 MB against 80 MB dense: 60% saved, not 80%, and no faster unless the hardware is built for it.
    • Believing a lottery ticket result from one run

      Small differences between pruned variants are within seed noise, as Simulation 3 shows.

      Spot itAsk for several seeds and a spread. If the claimed improvement is smaller than the spread, there is no finding.
    • "The prompt fine-tunes the model"

      Said as fact, it suggests prompt examples have lasting, general effects. Nothing is stored: the effect vanishes with the context, and it can be overwhelmed by a long irrelevant prompt.

      Spot itRun the same prompt with examples removed, shuffled and with wrong labels. If accuracy barely moves, the model was recognising a task, not learning from the examples.
    • Importing the linear-regression result into an LLM claim

      "Transformers do gradient descent" was shown for small models on synthetic linear tasks. Claiming it for a bank's production LLM, or using it to promise how many examples are enough, goes beyond the evidence.

      Spot itLook for the setting in the paper: model size, task type, number of layers. If it is a synthetic linear task, treat the claim as a hypothesis for LLMs.
    • Trusting few-shot results without testing order and selection

      The same five examples in a different order can swing accuracy by many points. A single hand-picked prompt tested on 50 cases proves little.

      Spot itAsk for results across several shuffles and several example sets, and an interval on a validation set of a size that supports one (F2.1, F2.7).
    • Overfitting through the optimiser's choices

      If starting point, step size and stopping time select the solution, then tuning them against the test set is tuning the model to it.

      Spot itAsk which data picked the learning rate, number of steps and seed. They should come from a validation set, never the final test set (F4.9).

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our models have far more adjustable settings than we have examples, and they still work, because the way they are trained prefers simple solutions, and we use that. What it means for us is practical: gentle fine-tuning and early stopping keep a model close to what it already knew, a much smaller version of a trained model is often almost as good, and examples in a prompt can stand in for training on small tasks. We confirm each of those on our own data before relying on it; none of it is guaranteed.”
    To a technical interviewer or a validator
    “Over-parameterised models have many zero-loss solutions, so generalisation is decided by the optimiser's implicit bias: with gradient descent from a small start it is minimum norm in linear models, max-margin on separable data, and low rank in deep linear nets. The step size also matters through the stability bound 2/h on sharpness, which pushes training away from sharp valleys, with the caveat that sharpness is not reparameterisation invariant. Lottery tickets are best read as pruning plus a statement about early basin commitment; they save inference, not training. For in-context learning, a linear attention head computes exactly one gradient step on the prompt examples, trained small transformers converge to preconditioned gradient descent, and the evidence for pretrained LLMs is mixed, with task recognition accounting for part of the effect. I would use it as a modelling heuristic, not a mechanism claim.”
    “If the model has more parameters than data, why doesn't it just memorise?”

    It can memorise, and networks do fit random labels. But on real data gradient descent finds the memorising fit last or not at all, because it moves along directions the data supports and from a small start reaches a small, simple solution first. Then early stopping, weight decay and a modest step size keep it there. It is a preference of the training procedure, not a guarantee, which is why we still hold out time-ordered test data.

    “Can we just prune our fraud model by 90% and save the cost?”

    Maybe, but check what the saving is on our hardware. Unstructured sparsity rarely speeds up inference unless the hardware supports it, and the real gains tend to come from quantisation, structured pruning or distillation. Then rerun the full evaluation, including the rare-positive slices and calibration, because compression tends to hurt the tail first. For a small tabular model the saving is probably not worth the validation work.

    “What is a lottery ticket, in one sentence?”

    A small sub-network of a large randomly initialised network which, trained alone from the same starting weights (or from an early checkpoint for big models), matches the full network; the evidence is that the pair of mask and starting values matters, but finding one needs a full training run first, so it is mainly a scientific result about training.

    “Does in-context learning mean the model is training itself on my examples?”

    It is one useful picture and for small models on simple tasks it can be made exact: attention computes a gradient-like step on the examples. For large language models the evidence is mixed. Part of the effect is the model recognising a task it already knows from the format, and part is genuine learning from the examples, with larger models doing more of the latter. Nothing is stored, so there is no lasting change and no learning across sessions.

    “Should we use five examples or fifty in the prompt?”

    Test it. The theory says returns diminish (more examples reduce variance, then flatten), that order and choice matter, and that long contexts can dilute attention. On our validation set, compare 0, 5, 20 and 50 examples with several shuffles, report intervals, and weigh the accuracy gain against cost and latency. If a few hundred labelled cases exist and the task is stable, fine-tuning may beat both.

    “Is this useful, or academic?”

    Mostly a source of better habits and sharper questions, not tools. It explains why simple baselines, early stopping, multiple seeds and honest held-out data work, it frames the prompt-versus-fine-tune decision, and it protects you from claims that overreach. I would not build a bank system on the theory itself; I would use it to read papers and vendor claims critically.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. One training example has inputs x = (3, 4) and target y = 10. A linear model starts at zero weights and is trained by gradient descent to zero loss. What weights does it reach, and why those?

    w = x·y ÷ (x·x) = (3, 4)·10 ÷ 25 = (1.2, 1.6). Check: 3×1.2 + 4×1.6 = 3.6 + 6.4 = 10. Every gradient step is a multiple of x, so the weights stay on the line through the origin along (3, 4); the only point on that line that fits is (1.2, 1.6). It is also the smallest-norm perfect fit (norm 2).

    2. A minimum has sharpness (largest curvature) 50. What is the largest step size gradient descent can use and stay there? What happens at η = 0.1?

    The limit is 2 ÷ 50 = 0.04. At η = 0.1, each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by |1 − 0.1 × 50| = 4, so the iterate is thrown out and ends in a flatter region or bounces. This is why large step sizes carry an implicit preference for flat minima.

    3. Why might "this minimum is flat, so it will generalise" be misleading?

    Flatness depends on how the parameters are scaled. In a ReLU network you can scale one layer up and the next down by the same factor without changing the function, making a minimum look sharper or flatter at will (Dinh et al., 2017). Flatness is a useful empirical signal only if measured in a scale-aware way, and it is a correlate, not a cause.

    4. What control separates a real "lottery ticket" from a good pruning mask?

    Keep the same mask but replace the surviving weights with fresh random starting values (and, separately, use a random mask of the same size). If the original-start version trains better, the pairing of mask and initial values matters, which is the lottery claim. If both do equally well, it is just a good architecture found by pruning, as Liu et al. (2019) argue is often the case.

    5. A 20-million-weight model is pruned by 80%, and the remaining weights are stored as a four-byte value plus a four-byte index. How big is it, against a dense four-byte version, and does it run faster?

    Remaining 4 million × 8 bytes = 32 MB, against 20 million × 4 bytes = 80 MB: a 60% saving, not 80%. Speed depends on hardware. General GPUs get little benefit from irregular holes unless sparsity is very high or the pattern is structured, so measure.

    6. Two examples in a prompt: (x₁, y₁) = ((1, 0), 2) and (x₂, y₂) = ((0, 1), −2). Using the one-step gradient formula with η = 0.5, what does the model predict for the query x = (2, 1)?

    ŷ = (η ÷ k) Σ yᵢ (xᵢ·xq) = (0.5 ÷ 2) × (2×2 + (−2)×1) = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5. Same as forming the weights w₁ = 0.25 × (2·(1,0) + (−2)·(0,1)) = (0.5, −0.5), then (0.5)(2) + (−0.5)(1) = 0.5. Attention weights the example values by key-query similarity, and that is the whole step.

    7. In Simulation 4 with high noise, why does the full least-squares fit do worst around four examples, and what does this have to do with early stopping?

    There are four unknowns, so with about four examples the fit can pass through every noisy label, and the solution is highly sensitive to that noise (the double-descent peak). Gradient descent stopped after a few steps has not yet fitted the small, noise-dominated directions, so it behaves like a regularised fit. Stopping early is implicit regularisation, the same idea as the minimum-norm bias in Simulation 1.

    8. A study finds that a model does as well with randomly wrong labels in its few-shot examples as with correct ones. What does that say about how it uses the examples?

    It is mostly using them to recognise the task and its format (input style, label space), not to learn the input-label mapping from them (Min et al., 2022). That does not mean examples are useless: they matter a lot for format. It means the "gradient descent on the examples" picture does not describe this model on this task, at least not fully.
