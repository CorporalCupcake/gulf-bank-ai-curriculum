    R4.2 Edge About 9 minutes

    Why Gradient Descent Finds Good Solutions

    Part 1

    The puzzle

    In one sentenceMany different settings of a network fit the training data perfectly, and gradient descent quietly picks one of them, usually a simple one that predicts well on new data.

    Running example: 20 borrowers with known outcomes and a model with 1,000 free numbers (weights). Countless weight settings match the 20 labels, and most fail on borrower 21. Deep networks live in this situation and still predict well, so something other than "fits the data" chooses among the fits.

    How it differs from ordinary regularisation

    • Explicit regularisation: you add a penalty to the loss, such as ridge, to prefer small weights.
    • Implicit regularisation: nobody wrote a penalty. The training procedure itself (start, step size, stopping time, optimiser) leans towards certain solutions.

    This ties to R4.1: it is why huge models can fit their training data and still generalise.

    Part 2

    How it works

    Idea 1: gradient descent prefers small weights

    1. Start small. Weights begin at or near zero.
    2. Each step moves along the data. In a linear model, every step adds a multiple of an example's inputs and nothing else.
    3. Unused directions stay put. In directions the data never touches, the weights stay where they started.
    4. Land on the closest perfect fit. Start at zero and reach zero loss, and you hold the perfect fit with the smallest weights.

    Real networks show cousins of this. If a line can split two classes, the direction of the weights settles on the widest-margin split, the one a support vector machine picks. Deep networks started small tend to learn strong patterns first and weak ones later. Early stopping is implicit regularisation on purpose, much like a ridge penalty. The optimiser (Adam or SGD, step size, batch size) is part of the model.

    Idea 2: valleys, flat or sharp

    A real network has millions of weights, so its loss landscape has millions of dimensions. Four facts to carry.

    • Bad pits are rare. A true trap needs upward curvature in every direction, so most flat spots are saddles, like a mountain pass.
    • Good regions connect. Solutions from different starts can often be joined by a low-loss path. That is why averaging weights ("model soups") can work.
    • Sharpness matters. A narrow ravine jumps in loss when weights shift slightly. A wide bowl barely moves. Flat minima tend to generalise better.
    • Step size filters valleys. A step bigger than 2 divided by the valley's curvature overshoots further each time and is thrown out. So large steps end in flatter valleys, even when a sharp valley has equal training loss.

    Caveat: "flat" depends on how weights are scaled, so flat-minimum claims are empirical. Sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM) makes the preference explicit by testing the worst nearby weights, often helping a little for roughly twice the compute.

    Idea 3: lottery tickets

    Pruning deletes a trained network's smallest weights, and most can go with little loss. Yet training that small network from scratch usually does worse. The lottery ticket hypothesis says a big random network contains a small sub-network that, trained from the original starting values, matches the full one.

    1. Save the starting weights and train the full network.
    2. Prune the smallest weights (say half), keeping a mask of survivors.
    3. Rewind the survivors to their saved starting values and train again.

    The claim is that mask and starting values come as a pair. The test is two controls, the same mask with fresh random values and a random mask of the same size. A real ticket beats both.

    Part 3

    How to read it

    • Parameter count is the wrong size measure. A model with 1,000 weights can behave far more simply, because training picks a simple solution.
    • "It just memorises" is not a full answer. Ask which of the perfect fits the optimiser would choose.
    • Equal training loss does not mean equal models. A sharp and a wide valley can both have zero training loss, and no training metric tells them apart. Only held-out data does.
    • Treat tickets with care. Rewinding to step 0 fails on big networks. For large ResNets and transformers, tickets appear only if you rewind a few percent into training. A pruned structure trained from scratch often matches the ticket, so structure may matter more than inherited weights. Finding a ticket needs the dense run first, so it saves inference cost, not training cost.
    Show the step-size rule

    In a valley whose curvature is h, plain gradient descent is stable only if the step size is below 2 divided by h. A step size of 0.1 therefore cannot stay in any valley sharper than h = 20. In words: bigger steps cannot rest in narrow valleys, so they end up in wide ones.

    Part 4

    Is it usable today?

    IdeaPlain meaningUsable in production
    Implicit regularisationThe optimiser prefers small or simple solutionsYes: early stopping, weight decay, step size are everyday knobs
    Flat minima / SAMWide valleys generalise betterSAM is available and often helps a little, at about twice the compute
    Lottery ticketsA small sub-network trains as well as the full oneNot as stated. Production prunes, fine-tunes and quantises, with no rewinding
    • Strong at: explaining why big models generalise, and which knobs matter.
    • Weak at: guarantees. Proofs cover simple models; for large networks it is observation. Scattered holes from pruning rarely speed up hardware, so quantisation, structured pruning and distillation usually win.

    In banking, this is the reason to tune and document step size, stopping point and weight decay, since they change which model you deploy even when training loss is identical.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging models by parameter count

      Training shapes the solution, so a huge model can act simple.

    • Trusting training loss to compare runs

      Two runs at zero loss can differ in sharpness and in test error. Compare on held-out data.

    • Assuming a pruned network can be trained from scratch

      Small networks often do worse from random starts, which is the puzzle tickets address.

    • Expecting the lottery recipe to help production

      It needs the dense run first and gains little hardware speed.

    1. What is implicit regularisation?

    A preference for certain solutions that comes from the training procedure, not from any penalty you added.

    2. Starting at zero, which perfect fit does gradient descent reach in a linear model?

    The one with the smallest weights.

    3. Why do large steps tend to end in flat valleys?

    In a sharp valley a large step overshoots more each time and is thrown out, so only wide valleys keep it.

    4. What are the three steps of the lottery ticket recipe?

    Train the full network, prune the smallest weights, then rewind the survivors to their original starting values and retrain.

    5. How does early stopping relate to ridge?

    It returns a point between the start and the minimum-weight answer, much as a ridge penalty does.
