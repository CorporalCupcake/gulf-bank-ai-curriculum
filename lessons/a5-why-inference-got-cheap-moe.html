    Why Inference Got Cheap: MoE, Speculative Decoding, Caching, Routing, and Where a Model Gets Its Knowledge

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a bank assistant answering 200,000 questions a day. Every call carries the same 6,000-word instruction block. Paying full price each time is like refuelling from empty before every taxi trip.

    The key fact: generating text is memory-bound. For each token the chip reads every active weight from memory and does very little arithmetic with it, like a chef who spends the shift walking to the pantry. Anything that reads fewer weights, gets more tokens per read, or avoids repeat work cuts the cost. Prices fell because these levers stacked.

    TrickWhat it avoidsDoes it change the answer?
    Mixture of experts (MoE)Using most of the weights per tokenDifferent model
    Speculative decodingOne big-model pass per tokenNo
    Prompt cachingRe-processing a repeated prefixNo
    Routing and cascadesUsing a big model on an easy questionYes. Measure it
    Retrieval or memory instead of stuffingReading the whole corpus every callYes

    Sort the tricks by the last column. Speculative decoding and caching are free lunches. Routing, smaller experts and retrieval trade quality for money, so they need evaluation.

    Hold onto thisWhen a vendor cuts a price, or your own bill is high, ask which of the five levers moved.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Mixture of experts: a big model that only uses a slice per token

    Think of a hospital. A dense model sends every patient past every specialist. A mixture of experts (MoE) has a triage nurse, the router, who sends each token to the best few of many parallel "expert" blocks. Two numbers matter: total parameters, which is what you must store in memory, and active parameters, which is what each token actually uses and so sets the compute. 600 billion total and 35 billion active stores like a huge model and computes like a mid-sized one.

    Common misreading: "only 35 billion active, so it is small to host". Hosting memory follows the total.

    Cheaper, but not free:

    • Memory does not shrink. All experts sit in fast memory across several accelerators. The saving is real under heavy traffic, less so for a single user.
    • Quality sits between active and total. An MoE performs like a dense model larger than its active count but smaller than its total. See Mixture-of-experts at scale.
    • Experts are not subject specialists. They split on token patterns, not "banking" or "French".

    Speculative decoding: verify many tokens for the price of one

    Checking is cheaper than writing. A teacher marks a whole page in one pass, though the pupil wrote it one word at a time. A small draft model guesses the next few tokens; the big target model checks them all in one pass, keeps those it agrees with, and corrects the first it does not. Every pass yields at least one token, often several.

    The check makes the output follow exactly the big model's distribution: a speed trick, not a quality trade. A useless draft wastes time, never accuracy. Templated letters speed up a lot; creative text much less. A draft right about 80% of the time, at a tenth of the big model's cost, gives roughly a 2.4x speed-up.

    The benefit is biggest for single-user latency. On a saturated server there is no spare capacity for verification, so it shrinks.

    Caching, and the KV cache behind it

    The KV cache is the model's notes on what it has already read (see context windows and the KV cache). Within a request, the notes avoid recomputing the past for every new token. Across requests they enable prefix caching: if a new prompt starts with exactly the same tokens as a recent one, the server reuses the notes and processes only the new tail. It is a bookmark: you resume rather than re-read.

    • Order matters. The match is on an exact prefix. Put stable material first (system prompt, policies, examples) and the varying part last (the customer's question). One changed token at the front, such as a timestamp, breaks the whole cache.
    • Pricing reflects the saving. Providers charge a fraction of the input price for cache reads, and caches expire after minutes to hours.
    • Governance. Ask your provider how tenant isolation works before putting customer data in cached prompts.

    Model routing and cascades

    A router is a hospital front desk: colds go to the nurse, chest pain to the consultant. Prices differ by an order of magnitude and most questions are easy, so a router picks the model. Three designs:

    • Cascade. Always try the cheap model; escalate if its answer looks unreliable.
    • Pre-router. A small classifier reads only the query and picks a model first. Cheaper, but blind to answer quality.
    • Rules. Balance enquiries go to the small model; credit memos and complaint-flagged cases go to the big one. Often the best start.

    Everything depends on how well you can tell easy from hard. A cascade needs a confidence signal, and self-reported confidence is often overconfident, so calibrate it against labelled examples (F7.4).

    The cost saving is certain; the accuracy loss you must measure, per category, on your own labelled data. For regulated decisions, route by risk as well as difficulty: a cheap model should not be the sole author of an adverse decision reaching a customer.

    Long context, retrieval or memory: choosing how a system knows things

    A model knows what training baked into its weights (stale, unauditable), what is in its context now, and what the system fetches or stores for it. Think of reading the whole library, using the catalogue to fetch a few books, or keeping a notebook.

    DesignHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
    Long contextWhole document or corpus in the prompt.Simple. Reasons across documents.Pays to read it all every call. Slower. Middle evidence under-used. Capped by the window.
    Retrieval (RAG)Search an index, put only the best few chunks in the prompt. See chunking.Cheap. Scales. Cites sources. Per-user permissions.A missed passage is never seen. Weak at "summarise everything".
    MemorySystem stores preferences, case notes and summaries and reloads them. See memory.Continuity across sessions. Compact.Summaries lose detail and can fix in errors. Privacy duties apply.

    Rule of thumb: long context for one-off analysis of a bounded document, retrieval for a large or changing corpus with permissions and citations, memory for continuity about a person or case. Real systems mix them (see agentic RAG).

    Cost is one axis of three. Stuffing can also lose on accuracy (the model gets distracted), latency and access control (a customer-facing assistant cannot hold documents the customer must not see). Retrieval loses when the answer needs the whole document. Build an evaluation set (statistics for evals) and compare all three.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging an MoE by one parameter count

      "600 billion parameters, so as good as a 600 billion dense model", or "35 billion active, so cheap to host."

      Spot itAsk for both numbers. Quality sits between active and total; hosting memory follows total.
    • Breaking the cache by accident

      A timestamp, user name or random ID near the top makes every call a miss, and the bill stays high.

      Spot itCheck the cache-hit field in the usage output. Move variable text to the end and confirm tenant isolation.
    • A router that sends hard cases to the cheap model

      Confidence is miscalibrated, so the model is sure and wrong. Complaints and rare products look unfamiliar.

      Spot itMeasure accuracy of accepted answers by category, oversampling rare ones. Pair every cost metric with a quality metric.
    • Memory that stores mistakes

      A wrongly summarised fact persists and is stated as truth.

      Spot itLet staff and customers view and correct memory; set expiry.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. A model is described as "600 billion parameters, 35 billion active". Which number drives the compute per token, which drives the hosting memory, and why is it not simply "small"?

    Active (35 billion) drives compute per token; total (600 billion) drives memory, because every expert must be loaded even if a given token uses only a few. It computes like a mid-sized model but needs the hardware of a huge one.

    2. Speculative decoding is run with a draft model that is often wrong. What happens to the speed, and what happens to the answers?

    Speed suffers, since most drafts are rejected and the draft effort is wasted; with a poor enough draft it can be slower than plain decoding. The answers do not change at all, because the big model checks and corrects every token.

    3. Why does speculative decoding not change outputs, while a cascade does?

    In speculative decoding every token is checked or corrected by the big model; the small one only proposes. In a cascade an accepted cheap answer is final, so the output comes from a different model.

    4. Your prompt is a 5,000-token policy followed by the customer's message, but the header begins "Today is 6 November 2026, 14:03:22". Cache hit rate is zero. Why, and what is the fix?

    Prefix caching needs an identical beginning, and the timestamp changes every second. Move the timestamp after the stable policy block.

    5. A cascade accepts 60% of questions at the cheap model (AED 2 per 1,000); the big model costs AED 30. Why is the cost not simply 40% of the big model's, and what is still unknown?

    The cheap model runs on every question, then the 40% escalated also pay the big price: about AED 14 per 1,000 against AED 30 for the big model alone. What is still unknown is the quality of the accepted 60%, which you must measure.

    6. A 300,000-token policy library is queried 50,000 times a day. Why does stuffing it into every call cost far more than retrieval, and does caching close the gap?

    Each stuffed call pays to read all 300,000 tokens, against about 3,500 for retrieval: nearly 90 times more input, which at the simulation's prices is roughly AED 150,000 a day against AED 1,750. A 90% cache hit rate cuts stuffing to about a fifth, but it is still over 16 times retrieval.

    7. Name two situations where long context beats retrieval and two where retrieval wins.

    Long context wins for one bounded document read as a whole, or questions needing every part. Retrieval wins for a large, changing corpus, or where users see different documents or answers must cite a source.
