    Why Inference Got Cheap: MoE, Speculative Decoding, Caching, Routing, and Where a Model Gets Its Knowledge

    Why the price per token fell so far, which of the tricks behind it change the answers and which do not, how to send easy questions to cheap models, and how to choose between long context, retrieval and memory for what a system knows.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceServing a language model is limited by moving memory, not by arithmetic, so the cheap-inference tricks all avoid moving or recomputing things (use only some of the weights, verify several tokens per pass, reuse the prefix, give easy questions to a small model), and the same cost logic decides whether a system should read everything, retrieve what it needs, or remember a summary.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank's customer-service assistant answers 200,000 questions a day. Each carries the same 6,000-word instruction block: tone, policy, escalation rules. A naive bill treats every call as brand new. A smart one notices that 95% of the input is identical every time, that most questions are "what is my card limit", and that the answer is only needed as fast as a person reads it.

    Each of those observations is a cost lever, and together they explain why the price of a given level of capability has fallen sharply, by large factors over a few years, without any one miracle. The key fact underneath is that generating text is memory-bound. To produce one token, the accelerator must read every active weight from memory, and then it does a tiny amount of arithmetic with them. The chip is mostly waiting. Anything that reads fewer weights per token, produces more tokens per read, or avoids doing the work twice, makes serving cheaper.

    TrickWhat it avoidsDoes it change the answer?
    Mixture of experts (MoE)Reading and multiplying most of the weights for each tokenIt is a different model: quality is its own, not a free copy of a dense one
    Speculative decodingRunning the big model once per tokenNo. The output distribution is provably the same
    Prompt cachingRe-reading and re-processing a repeated prefixNo (apart from numerical noise)
    Routing and cascadesUsing a big model on an easy questionYes. Some answers come from the small model, so you must measure
    Retrieval or memory instead of stuffingPaying to read the whole corpus on every callYes. It changes what the model can see

    That last column is the useful sorting. Some tricks are free lunches (speculative decoding, caching); some trade quality for money and need evaluation (routing, smaller experts, retrieval). A good engineer knows which is which, and a good manager asks.

    Hold onto thisPrice per token is a story about memory traffic and utilisation, not about clever mathematics. When a vendor cuts a price, ask which of the five levers moved. When your own bill is high, check the same five.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Mixture of experts: a big model that only uses a slice per token

    In a standard (dense) transformer, every token passes through every weight. Most of those weights sit in the feed-forward blocks, one per layer (see attention for the other half). An MoE layer replaces that one block with E parallel blocks, called experts, plus a small router: a linear layer that scores the experts for each token and sends the token to the top k (often 1, 2 or a handful), mixing their outputs by the router's weights. Everything else, attention and embeddings, is shared and runs for every token.

    Two numbers then separate what the model is from what it costs:

    Ntotal=Nshared+E·NexpertNactive=Nshared+k·Nexpert

    Total parameters set the memory you must hold and, loosely, how much the model can store. Active parameters set the arithmetic and, at small batch sizes, the weights read per token. A model with 600 billion total and 35 billion active parameters stores like a very large model and computes per token like a mid-sized one.

    Why this is cheaper in practice but not simply "free":

    • Training the router is delicate. Left alone it sends most tokens to a few favourite experts and the rest never learn. Training adds a load-balancing loss (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2021), or in some recent designs a bias term adjusted during training, so experts share the work. Production systems also cap how many tokens one expert accepts per batch.
    • Memory does not shrink. All experts must sit in fast memory, spread over several accelerators, with tokens shipped between them (expert parallelism). The cost is network traffic and GPU count, not arithmetic. Under heavy traffic with large batches the saving is real, because every expert gets work. A single user at small batch may touch only a few experts per token and wastes the idle ones.
    • Experts are not subject specialists. Analysis of trained models suggests they specialise on syntax or token patterns more than on topics like "banking" or "French". Do not expect to hand-pick an "Arabic expert".
    • Quality rule of thumb. An MoE tends to perform like a dense model with more parameters than its active count but fewer than its total. It is a trade, not a loophole. The R1 lesson (Mixture-of-experts at scale) goes into routing, fine-grained experts and the research frontier.

    Speculative decoding: verify many tokens for the price of one

    Decoding is sequential: token 17 needs token 16. But the expensive part is reading the weights, and reading them once can check many tokens at once, the same way a model scores a whole prompt in parallel. Speculative decoding exploits that asymmetry (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023).

    1. A small draft model quickly proposes γ tokens, say 4.
    2. The big target model scores all of them in a single forward pass.
    3. Walk from the left. Each draft token is accepted with a probability that depends on how closely the draft and target agree; at the first rejection, discard the rest and sample a corrected token from the target.
    4. You always gain at least one token per big-model pass, and often several.

    The accept-or-correct rule is built so the final text has exactly the target model's distribution. This is a speed trick, not a quality trade. If the draft is useless, you lose a little time on wasted drafts, but never accuracy. With an average acceptance probability α per token (assumed independent, which is only roughly true), the expected number of tokens per big-model pass is

    E[tokens]=1−αγ+11−αspeed-up=E[tokens]γc+1

    c is the cost of one draft token relative to one big-model pass (say 0.1 if the draft is a tenth as costly). One pass of the target is the unit of time. With α = 0.8, γ = 4 and c = 0.1 you get (1 − 0.85) ÷ 0.2 = 3.36 tokens per pass for a cost of 1.4 passes: a 2.4× speed-up.

    Variants you will see named: draft models trained alongside the target; heads attached to the target itself (Medusa, EAGLE) that predict several future tokens; and self-speculation that skips layers. Another family, multi-token prediction, trains the model to predict ahead and then reuses that as a free draft. The mechanics differ but the verify-in-one-pass logic is the same. The benefit is biggest for single-user latency, and shrinks when the server is already saturated with large batches, since the hardware then has no spare capacity to spend on verification.

    Caching, and the KV cache behind it

    While reading a prompt, the model computes, for every token and layer, a key and a value vector that later tokens attend to. Stored, these form the KV cache (see context windows and the KV cache). It has two jobs. Within one request it avoids recomputing the whole past for every new token. Across requests it makes prefix caching possible: if a new prompt starts with exactly the same tokens as one the server processed recently, it can reuse those stored vectors and skip the work, then only process the new tail.

    Three consequences matter for a bank.

    • Order matters. The match is on an exact prefix. Put the stable material first (system prompt, policy documents, tool definitions, few-shot examples) and the varying part last (the customer's question). One changed token at the front, such as a timestamp in the header, breaks the whole cache.
    • Pricing reflects the saving. Providers typically charge a fraction of the normal input price for cache reads, sometimes with a small extra charge for the first write, and caches expire after minutes to hours unless refreshed. Terms differ by provider and change often, so read the current pricing page. I could not confirm current figures from reliable sources for this lesson, so the simulation uses clearly labelled illustrative numbers.
    • Memory is the limit. KV cache per token is large and grows with context. Serving engines manage it like virtual memory, paging it in blocks (vLLM's PagedAttention, Kwon et al., 2023), which lets the server fit many more concurrent requests. That utilisation gain is as big a part of falling prices as any model trick.

    Caching also has a security and data-governance side: cached prefixes are tied to an account or tenant, and a shared cache across customers could in principle leak timing information. Ask your provider how isolation works before putting customer data in cached prompts.

    Model routing and cascades

    Models come in sizes with prices that differ by an order of magnitude or more, and most questions are easy. A router decides which model sees each request. Three common designs:

    • Cascade. Always try the cheap model; if its answer looks unreliable, escalate to the expensive one (Chen et al., 2023, FrugalGPT). Costs the cheap call on every request, plus the expensive call on escalations.
    • Pre-router. A small classifier looks only at the query and picks a model before any answer is generated (Ong et al., 2024, RouteLLM). Cheaper than a cascade, but it cannot see the answer's quality.
    • Rules. Product-level logic: balance enquiries go to the small model; credit-memo drafting and anything with a complaint flag goes to the big one. Boring and often the best start.

    Everything depends on one question: how well can you tell easy from hard? A cascade needs a confidence signal: token probabilities, a self-reported score, agreement among several samples, or a small verifier. Self-reported confidence from language models is often overconfident, so this needs calibration (F7.4) against labelled examples. The simulation uses the classic picture of two overlapping score distributions: the better they separate, the cheaper the cascade at a given quality.

    Two cautions. The cost saving is certain; the accuracy loss is what you must measure, per category, on labelled data from your own traffic. And for regulated decisions, route by risk as well as difficulty: a cheap model should not be the sole author of anything that reaches a customer as an adverse decision.

    Long context, retrieval or memory: choosing how a system knows things

    A model knows three kinds of things: what training baked into its weights (stale, unauditable), what is in its context window right now, and what the system fetches or stores for it. For the last two there are three designs.

    DesignHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
    Long context ("stuff it all in")Put the whole document or corpus in the prompt.Simplest. Cross-document reasoning. No retrieval errors.Pay to read it all every call (unless cached). Slower. Accuracy can fall as context grows, with evidence often under-used in the middle (Liu et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024). Capped by the window. Access control is all-or-nothing.
    Retrieval (RAG)Search an index, put only the best few chunks in the prompt. See chunking and hybrid search.Cheap per call. Scales to any corpus. Citations point to a source. Per-user permissions can be applied at retrieval.If retrieval misses the passage, the model never sees it. Needs chunking, an index, evaluation. Weak at "summarise everything" or questions needing many scattered facts.
    MemoryThe system writes down what it learns (preferences, case notes, summaries) and reloads it later. See memory.Personalisation and continuity across sessions. Compact.Summaries lose detail and can fix in errors. Stale or wrongly stored facts persist. Privacy and retention duties apply to what is stored.

    A useful rule: long context for one-off analysis of a bounded document, retrieval for a large or changing corpus with permissions and citations, memory for continuity about a person or case. Real systems mix them, and the lines are blurring: caching makes repeated long contexts far cheaper, and agentic retrieval (agentic RAG) lets the model decide what to look up. Prompting-versus-RAG-versus-fine-tuning is covered in M2; this section is the cost-and-fit view among the in-context options.

    Cost is only one axis. A stuffed prompt that fits and is cached can still lose to retrieval on accuracy (the model gets distracted), on latency (reading 200,000 tokens takes seconds even when cached) and on access control (a customer-facing assistant cannot hold documents the customer must not see). Conversely, retrieval loses when the answer needs the whole document, such as "find every clause that contradicts this one". The honest procedure is to build an evaluation set (see statistics for evals) and compare all three on it.

    How it connects

    Cost engineering links models, infrastructure, evaluation and governance. Places it comes back:

    Routing, fine-grained and shared experts, and the recurrent challengers. This lesson gives you the economics; that one gives the design space.

    Where the KV cache comes from and why long prompts are expensive. Caching and paging build directly on it.

    P3 · Quantization, pruning, distillationThe other half of the cost story

    Smaller numbers, smaller models trained from bigger ones. Often the cheap model in a cascade is a distilled one.

    M2 · Model choice, cost and latencyChoosing the tier

    Routing is model choice made per request instead of per project.

    A5.1 – A5.2 · Reasoning modelsThinking tokens are the new cost driver

    A reasoning model can spend many times more output tokens per answer. Routing hard questions to it and easy ones away from it is the obvious combination.

    M3 · RetrievalChunking and search

    The retrieval option rests on chunking, hybrid search and query rewriting. Their quality decides whether RAG beats stuffing.

    M4 · MemoryWhat to remember between sessions

    The third option in the knowledge choice, with its own failure modes and retention duties.

    A15 · Gateways and token budgetsWhere routing and caching live

    In many banks the router, cache and token budgets sit in a central AI gateway, so every team gets the savings and the controls.

    A4 · EvaluationProve the cheap path is good enough

    Every quality-changing trick needs a test set and intervals. The question is always "how much accuracy did I trade, and is the difference real".

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Customer-service assistants. Huge volume, mostly easy intents, a long shared system prompt. Caching and routing give the largest savings here.
    • Document processing. KYC files, trade documents and contracts: bounded documents, where long context often beats building a retrieval pipeline.
    • Policy and regulation search. A large, changing corpus (CBUAE or SAMA circulars, internal policy) with citations: retrieval, with caching of the instructions.
    • Relationship-manager copilots. Client history across sessions: memory plus retrieval, with careful retention rules.
    • Deployment choice. An MoE you host yourself needs several GPUs for its total size even if compute is light, which matters for on-premises and sovereign setups (hybrid and on-premises).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The techniques are mature and mostly bundled inside providers' serving stacks. The live arguments are how far MoE can be pushed, how much of the price fall is hardware and utilisation versus algorithms, and what happens to costs when reasoning models make each answer far longer.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging an MoE by its total parameter count

      "It has 600 billion parameters, so it is as good as a 600 billion dense model", or "it only uses 35 billion, so it is a small model that is cheap to host."

      Spot itAsk for both numbers. Quality sits between active and total; hosting memory follows total.
    • Expecting speculative decoding to help a saturated server

      The saving shows on a single request; at full batch the spare compute is gone.

      Spot itBenchmark at your real concurrency, not with one request.
    • Breaking the cache by accident

      A timestamp, user name or random ID near the top of the prompt makes every call a miss. The bill stays high and nobody knows why.

      Spot itCheck the cache-hit field in the API usage output. Move variable text to the end.
    • Caching customer data across users

      Personal data placed in a shared prefix.

      Spot itReview what sits in cached prefixes; keep personal data after the cache boundary and confirm tenant isolation with the provider.
    • A router that sends hard cases to the cheap model

      Confidence is miscalibrated, so the model is sure and wrong. Complaints, edge cases and rare products are exactly the ones that look unfamiliar.

      Spot itMeasure accuracy of the accepted answers by category, with the rare categories oversampled in the test set. Look at escalation rate over time.
    • Measuring the saving but not the loss

      The dashboard shows cost per question falling and no one checks quality.

      Spot itRequire a quality metric beside every cost metric, with a confidence interval (a 2-point drop on 200 test cases is noise).
    • Trusting the advertised context window

      The window is how much fits, not how much the model uses well.

      Spot itTest on your documents with the evidence placed in the middle, at the length you plan to use.
    • RAG with a retrieval step that silently misses

      The right clause is not in the top five; the model answers confidently from the wrong one.

      Spot itMeasure retrieval recall separately from answer quality, on questions where you know the source.
    • Memory that stores mistakes

      A wrongly summarised preference or an outdated fact persists across sessions and is stated as truth.

      Spot itLet staff and customers view and correct stored memory; log what was written and when; set expiry.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Most of what we pay for is repeated and easy. Our assistant sends the same long instruction block with every call, and most customer questions are simple. If we arrange the prompt so that block is cached and send simple questions to a smaller model, I'd expect a large cut in cost without touching the hard cases. The part to be careful about is the smaller model: I'd test it on our own questions, report the accuracy next to the saving, and keep complaints and credit decisions on the stronger model.”
    To a technical interviewer or architect
    “Decoding is memory-bandwidth bound, so the levers are fewer weights read per token, more tokens per read, and no recomputation. MoE cuts active parameters but not memory footprint. Speculative decoding is lossless but helps most at low batch size. Prefix caching needs stable prefixes and a look at tenant isolation. Routing changes outputs, so it needs calibrated confidence and per-category evaluation. For knowledge, I'd retrieve for a large governed corpus, stuff for a bounded document, and add memory only with retention controls, and I'd compare them on a labelled set rather than argue.”
    “Why did the price per token fall so much?”

    Several things stacked: better hardware and much better utilisation of it (batching, paged memory), sparse MoE models that compute with a fraction of their weights, quantization, distillation into smaller models with the same capability, caching, and competition. Only some of those preserve exact outputs. I'd avoid giving one figure for how much each contributed, since the split is not public.

    “Does speculative decoding make answers worse?”

    No. The accept-and-correct rule guarantees the same distribution as the big model alone, so it is a pure speed-up. It is not free to run, since you need a draft model and spare capacity, and the benefit shrinks when the server is already busy.

    “If MoE only uses 5% of the parameters, can we run it on one GPU?”

    Not usually. All experts have to be in memory, because any token might need any of them. Compute drops, memory does not. You still need enough accelerator memory for the total, unless you quantize hard or offload and accept slower speed.

    “Why not just put all our policies in the prompt? Context windows are huge now.”

    It can work for a bounded set, and caching helps with cost. But we pay to process it on every call, latency rises, accuracy tends to suffer when the relevant passage is buried, and everyone using the assistant sees the same documents, which breaks permissions. For a large, changing corpus with citations I'd retrieve. I'd test both on questions we already know the answers to.

    “How do we know the router is not sending hard questions to the cheap model?”

    By measurement. Build a labelled set that deliberately over-represents the hard and rare categories, run the cascade, and report accuracy of accepted cheap answers per category with intervals. Track the escalation rate in production, as a falling rate may mean the router is getting overconfident, and review a random sample of accepted answers each week.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. An MoE has 16 billion shared parameters and 128 experts of 4 billion each, with top-2 routing. What are its total and active counts, and how many 80 GB GPUs do the weights need at 8 bits?

    Total = 16 + 128 × 4 = 528 billion. Active = 16 + 2 × 4 = 24 billion. At 1 byte each the weights are 528 GB, so at least 7 GPUs (528 ÷ 80 = 6.6), before the KV cache and overhead. Compute per token is about 24 ÷ 528 = 4.5% of a dense model of the same total size.

    2. With acceptance α = 0.7, draft length γ = 3 and draft cost c = 0.1, what is the speed-up from speculative decoding?

    Expected tokens per pass = (1 − 0.74) ÷ 0.3 = (1 − 0.2401) ÷ 0.3 = 2.53. Time per iteration = 3 × 0.1 + 1 = 1.3. Speed-up = 2.53 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 1.9×.

    3. Why does speculative decoding not change the model's outputs, while a cascade does?

    In speculative decoding every token is accepted or corrected by a rule that makes the result follow the target model's distribution exactly; the small model only proposes. In a cascade, the cheap model's answer is the final answer whenever it is accepted, so the output comes from a different model and quality may differ.

    4. Your prompt is a 5,000-token policy followed by the customer's message, but the header begins with "Today is 6 November 2026, 14:03:22". Cache hit rate is zero. Why, and what is the fix?

    Prefix caching needs an identical beginning. The timestamp changes every second, so the prefix differs from the first tokens onward and nothing matches. Move the timestamp (and any user-specific text) after the stable policy block, or drop the seconds.

    5. A cascade accepts 60% of questions at the cheap model, which costs AED 2 per 1,000 and the big model AED 30. What is the cost per 1,000?

    The cheap model runs on all questions: 2. The remaining 40% escalate: 0.4 × 30 = 12. Total AED 14 per 1,000, against AED 30 for the big model alone (a 53% saving). Note that the quality of the 60% accepted is the thing still to be measured.

    6. A 300,000-token policy library is queried 50,000 times a day at AED 10 per million input tokens. What does stuffing cost per day without caching, compared with retrieval at 3,500 tokens per call?

    Stuffing: 300,000 × 10 ÷ 1,000,000 = AED 3 per call, so AED 150,000 per day. Retrieval: 3,500 × 10 ÷ 1,000,000 = AED 0.035 per call, so AED 1,750 per day: about 86 times cheaper. A 90% cache hit rate at a 10% read price cuts stuffing to about 19% of the original, roughly AED 28,500 per day, still over 16 times retrieval.

    7. Name two situations where long context beats retrieval and two where retrieval wins.

    Long context wins for one bounded document that must be understood as a whole (a contract and its amendments) and for questions that need every part, such as "find contradictions". Retrieval wins for a large corpus that changes, and wherever different users may see different documents or answers must cite a source.
