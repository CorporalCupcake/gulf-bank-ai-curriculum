    Cloud Building Blocks and GPUs: What You Are Really Renting

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Renting a cloud is like renting a car instead of buying one. A bank retrains a fraud model once a quarter; buying hardware for that peak leaves it idle the rest of the time. Renting means you pay for use, but you must ask: what am I renting, where is it, who can walk in, and what happens if I forget to hand back the keys?

    Every cloud is the same four things under different names.

    • Compute. Someone else's processors, rented by the second: a virtual machine, a container, a function, or a GPU.
    • Storage. Cheap and huge (object), fast and attached (block), or shared like a network drive (file).
    • Networking. Which things can talk to which, and who pays when data crosses a boundary.
    • Identity. Who or what may do what. Auditors read this layer first.

    AI needs special compute. A GPU is thousands of simple workers all doing the same step at once, which suits neural networks. The surprise: running a large language model, the crowd usually waits for data from memory rather than running short of arithmetic.

    Hold onto thisAsk four things of any design. What shape is the workload (steady, bursty, interruptible)? Where does the data live and what does moving it cost? Which identity does each piece run as? For GPUs, does the model fit in memory, and is it bound by memory or arithmetic?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Compute and how you pay for it

    The compute ladder runs from most control and work to least.

    LevelWhat you rentTypical use in a bank data teamWatch for
    Virtual machineA slice of a server, with an operating system you manage.Long training jobs, legacy tools, custom setups.You patch and secure it; you pay while it is on, used or not.
    Container / KubernetesA packaged program that starts in seconds on a shared pool.Model-serving endpoints, scheduled pipelines (P2).Running a cluster is a skill; most teams use a managed service.
    Serverless functionCode run per request, billed by the millisecond.Light glue: validate a file, call a model.Cold starts, time limits, rarely GPUs.
    Managed ML platformA service that trains or hosts a model for you.Notebooks, training jobs, hosted endpoints and foundation models.Convenient and costly; lock-in; check where the model runs.

    The pricing model can change the bill two or three times over. It works like a taxi, a monthly pass or a lift share.

    • On-demand. List rate for hours used. Flexible, dearest per hour.
    • Committed (reserved, savings plan). Promise steady spend for one or three years for roughly a third to over half off. You pay whether or not you use it.
    • Interruptible (spot, preemptible). Spare capacity at a steep discount that the provider can reclaim at short notice. Only for work that can stop and resume.

    Think of the commitment as a monthly pass: it wins only if you ride often enough. If it costs 62% of a full month on-demand, it wins once you use the machine more than about 62% of the hours. An always-on endpoint clears that easily. A 3-hour nightly job does not and should not be reserved.

    Most surprise bills are something left on, like a GPU notebook over a weekend. Autoscaling and tagging (labelling every resource with team and project) are the two habits that catch it.

    Storage: three kinds, three jobs

    You pay for capacity, read speed and how often you touch the data. Cheap storage is slow to reach; fast storage is dear, like a warehouse versus your desk drawer.

    KindThink of it asGood forNot good for
    Object storage (S3-style buckets)A huge, cheap filing system; each file stored whole, fetched by name.Data lakes, Parquet, model files, document images, backups. Default home for ML data (P1).Editing part of a file, many tiny random reads.
    Block storageA disk attached to one machine.Operating systems, databases, training scratch space.Sharing between machines.
    File storageA shared network drive.Many machines reading the same files; legacy apps.Cheap bulk storage at huge scale.
    • Encryption keys. Data is encrypted at rest by default; the bank question is who holds the key. With customer-managed keys you can revoke access by disabling the key. Regulators care about this.

    For AI, two facts bite. Slow storage starves expensive GPUs: a GPU waiting on a disk is money burning. And model files are big (a 70-billion-parameter model is about 140 GB), so load speed decides how fast a new serving replica starts.

    Networking: regions, zones and who can reach what

    A region is a geographic cluster of data centres, such as a city area. Inside it are several availability zones: separate buildings with independent power and cooling. Banks choose by region because residency rules are about where data sits.

    Zones give resilience, like two generators in two buildings: both must fail for you to go down. But everything in one region shares the region's network and control plane, and your own deployment mistakes. That shared part caps what extra zones can buy.

    The other half of networking is who can reach what.

    • Private network and rules. Your fenced-off address space, with rules like "the scoring service may call the feature store, nothing else may".
    • Private endpoints and links. Reach managed services, and the bank's on-premises systems, without the public internet. Banks often require this (next lesson).
    • Egress. Data in is usually free; data out is billed per gigabyte. Keep compute next to the data. This is data gravity.

    Distance also costs time: 100 ms or more across continents, too slow for a real-time fraud score.

    Identity: who is allowed to do what

    Nearly every cloud action is made by an identity: a person, a group, or a program (service account, role). The platform checks a policy: may this identity do this action on this resource? Like a hotel key card that opens your room and the gym, not every door.

    • Least privilege. A training job reads one bucket and writes one folder, not "all storage".
    • Roles and groups, not individuals. When someone changes team, one change removes access.
    • Short-lived credentials. A token that expires in an hour, not a password in a config file.

    A least-privilege policy for a training job. Syntax varies by provider; the shape does not.

    {
  "identity": "svc-fraud-training",            // a program, not a person
  "allow": [
    { "action": "storage:read",  "resource": "bucket/fraud-features/2026/*" },
    { "action": "storage:write", "resource": "bucket/fraud-models/candidates/*" }
  ],
  "deny": [
    { "action": "*", "resource": "bucket/customer-pii-raw/*" }   // explicit no
  ],
  "expires": "1h"                              // short-lived token
}

    In practice you request access by ticket, get a time-limited, project-scoped role, and cannot copy raw customer data to your laptop. That is by design.

    GPUs: what makes AI hardware different

    A CPU is one chef cooking a complicated dish quickly. A GPU is a thousand people each chopping one onion at the same time. Deep learning is mostly matrix multiplication (F1.2), which splits into countless identical small sums, so a GPU is far faster at it.

    Three things define a GPU.

    • Memory capacity (80 GB, 192 GB). The weights plus working space must fit. If not, the model is split over several GPUs, adding cost.
    • Memory bandwidth (TB/s). How fast weights stream from memory to the arithmetic units.
    • Compute (TFLOPS). Raw arithmetic rate.

    Weights take parameters times bytes per number: a 70-billion-parameter model is 140 GB at 16 bits, 70 GB at 8 bits, 35 GB at 4 bits. Serving also needs the KV cache, the stored attention memory for each active conversation (M1.3), which grows with context and users. Training needs several times more memory than the weights alone, so a model served on two GPUs may need dozens to train (distributed training).

    The surprise: generating one token means reading all the weights once, with little arithmetic per byte. It is a chef with a huge stove waiting on a slow delivery van. The job is memory-bound: speed is set by bandwidth, not TFLOPS. A rough ceiling for one user:

    tokens per second (one user)≈memory bandwidthbytes of weights

    3.35 TB/s on a 140 GB model gives at most about 24 tokens a second. Serving many users at once reads the weights once for everyone, so total throughput rises almost free until arithmetic becomes the limit. That is why engines batch requests.

    • Quantization speeds things up as well as shrinking them. Fewer bits, fewer bytes to stream, faster tokens (P3.1).
    • Training is the opposite regime. Large batches give so much arithmetic per byte that it is compute-bound. Training cares about total TFLOPS and GPU-to-GPU speed.
    • Idle costs the same as busy. GPUs are rented in expensive chunks, usually eight to a server: Simulation 1 with bigger numbers.

    Not everything needs a GPU. Tabular models such as credit-scoring gradient boosting (F4.2), SQL and feature pipelines run far more cheaply on CPUs.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Paying for idle or mis-shaped resources

      A GPU notebook left on all weekend, or a one-year commitment for a job that runs 3 hours a night.

      Spot itCheck utilisation by resource, not total spend. Under 10% average use is a candidate. Compare expected hours to the break-even. Set auto-shutdown and budget alerts.
    • Over-broad identity

      A shared admin key, an "owner" role to save time, credentials in code.

      Spot itAsk which identity a job runs as and what it can touch. Scan repositories for secrets. "My own login" or "the team key" is the answer you are looking for.
    • Public by accident

      A bucket or model endpoint reachable from the internet.

      Spot itAsk for the network path and public-access setting of every store. Private endpoints and policy checks should make this impossible, not just unlikely.
    • Surprise egress and distance

      Compute in one region, data in another: slow training, big bill.

      Spot itCheck that data and compute share a region. Read the data-transfer line on the invoice.
    • Counting TFLOPS when the job is memory-bound

      Buying the biggest headline number for single-user generation.

      Spot itDivide bandwidth by model bytes for the tokens-a-second ceiling. A claim above it means the model is smaller or quantized.
    • Forgetting the KV cache, training overhead and zone limits

      "The model is 140 GB and the GPU is 192 GB, so it fits", then it runs out of memory at long contexts. Or three zones are treated as disaster recovery.

      Spot itAsk for memory at long context and peak users, and budget far more to train than to serve. Ask what happens if the whole region is gone.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A one-year commitment costs 60% of on-demand for the whole month. A nightly job needs a machine 4 hours a day. Which is cheaper, and how do you decide?

    On-demand. The job uses about 16% of the month, far below the 60% break-even, so the commitment pays for idle hours. Commit only to steady, near-always-on work.

    2. Why do two zones help a lot, a third zone help little, and why is the real gain smaller than the simple maths suggests?

    A second zone covers one building failing. After that, what remains are shared failures: the region's network, control plane, your own bad deployment. Those hit every zone together, so they set a ceiling. The next step is a second region.

    3. A vendor claims 500 tokens a second for one user on a 70B model at 16 bits on a single GPU. What should make you doubt it?

    Single-user speed is capped by how fast the GPU can read the weights, and 140 GB of weights cannot be read 500 times a second on current hardware. The model must be smaller, quantized or batched. Ask which.

    4. Why does a smaller number format (16 to 8 to 4 bits) make a model both fit on fewer GPUs and run faster?

    Fewer bits means fewer bytes to store and fewer bytes to read per token. Memory use falls and, because serving is memory-bound, speed rises.

    5. Why can serving 32 users together be nearly as fast per user as serving one?

    Each step reads the weights once whatever the user count, and reading is the bottleneck. Extra users add arithmetic, which the chip has spare. Past the crossover point, arithmetic becomes the limit and per-user speed falls.

    6. A training job must read a feature bucket and write a model file. What identity should it use, and what should it not use?

    A dedicated workload identity with read on that bucket path, write on one output path, and short-lived tokens. Not a person's login, shared admin key, owner role or credentials in code.

    7. A team can serve a model on one GPU and assumes it can train it on the same one. Why is that likely wrong?

    Training also stores gradients and optimiser state, several times the size of the weights alone. A model that is served on one or two GPUs may need many more to train, or memory-saving methods.
