    Cloud Building Blocks and GPUs: What You Are Really Renting

    Compute, storage, networking and identity as four decisions you can discuss with a platform team, and then the one piece of hardware that makes AI different: why a GPU is limited by memory bandwidth more often than by arithmetic.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA cloud is rented computers, disks, networks and permissions, each billed differently and each a place where banks make control decisions; a GPU is a machine for doing the same arithmetic on many numbers at once, and for large language models the scarce resource is usually the memory that holds the weights and the speed of reading it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine the bank needs to score 40 million customers every night for a campaign, and once a quarter retrain a fraud model that needs eight expensive accelerators for two days. Buying hardware for the peak means it sits idle the rest of the time. Renting it by the hour means you pay for what you use, but you now have to think like a landlord's customer: what am I renting, where is it, who can walk in, and what does it cost if I forget to hand back the keys?

    Every cloud, whichever vendor, is the same four things dressed in different names.

    • Compute. Someone else's processors, rented by the second. A virtual machine, a container, a function that runs for 200 milliseconds, or a GPU.
    • Storage. Places to keep bytes. Cheap and huge (object storage), fast and attached (block disks), or shared like a network drive (file storage).
    • Networking. Which things can talk to which, over what path, and who pays when data crosses a boundary.
    • Identity. Who or what is allowed to do what. In a bank this is the layer that auditors read first.

    You do not need to run any of it. You need enough fluency to tell a platform engineer "this job is bursty and fault tolerant, so I want it on interruptible capacity, reading from object storage through a private endpoint under its own service identity", and to be understood.

    The second half of the lesson is about why AI work needs a special kind of compute. A CPU is a few very clever workers. A GPU is thousands of simple workers who all do the same step at the same moment. Neural networks are mostly one operation, multiplying big grids of numbers, repeated billions of times, which is exactly the shape of job that army is good at. The surprise is that for running a large language model, the army usually waits for data to arrive from memory rather than being short of arithmetic.

    Hold onto thisFor every cloud design, ask four things. What is the shape of the workload (steady, bursty, interruptible)? Where does the data live and what does moving it cost? Which identity does each piece run as? And for a GPU workload, does the model fit in memory and is it bound by memory or by arithmetic? Most architecture arguments are one of those four in disguise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Compute and how you pay for it

    The compute ladder runs from "most control, most work" to "least control, least work".

    LevelWhat you rentTypical use in a bank data teamWatch for
    Virtual machineA slice of a physical server, with an operating system you manage.Long training jobs, legacy tools, anything needing a custom setup.You patch it, you secure it, you pay while it is on, used or not.
    Container / KubernetesA packaged program that starts in seconds on a shared pool of machines.Model-serving endpoints, scheduled pipelines. The standard way to deploy a model (P2).Cluster operation is a skill of its own; most teams use a managed service.
    Serverless functionCode that runs per request and is billed by the millisecond.Light glue: validate a file, call a model, post a result.Cold starts, time limits, no GPUs on most offerings.
    Managed ML platformA service that runs training or hosts a model for you.Notebooks, training jobs, hosted endpoints, hosted foundation models.Convenient and costly; lock-in; check where the model actually runs.

    The second decision is the pricing model, and it is worth understanding because it changes the bill by two or three times. All three big providers offer the same three patterns under different names:

    • On-demand. Pay the list rate for the hours you use. Flexible, most expensive per hour.
    • Committed (reserved, savings plan, committed use). Promise to pay for a steady amount for one or three years in return for a discount, often in the region of a third to over a half off. You pay whether or not you use it.
    • Interruptible (spot, preemptible). Spare capacity at a steep discount that the provider can take back at short notice. Only for work that can stop and resume.

    The choice reduces to one number, the break-even utilisation. If a commitment costs a fraction c of the on-demand price for the whole month, it wins once you would have used the machine for more than that fraction of the hours.

    ubreak-even=c

    If a one-year commitment costs 62% of the on-demand price (illustrative), it beats on-demand whenever the machine would be on more than 62% of the time, about 15 hours a day. A serving endpoint that runs 24 hours is far above that line. A nightly scoring job that runs 3 hours is far below it, and should not be reserved.

    Two more compute habits. First, autoscaling: let the platform add and remove instances with load, so you pay for the afternoon peak only in the afternoon. Second, tagging: label every resource with a team and project, because the first question finance asks is "whose is this?". Most surprise bills come from something left on, a notebook with a GPU attached over a weekend, or a test endpoint nobody deleted.

    Storage: three kinds, three jobs

    Storage choices follow one rule: you pay for capacity, for how fast you can read it, and for how often you touch it. Cheap storage is slow to reach; fast storage is dear.

    KindThink of it asGood forNot good for
    Object storage (S3-style buckets)A very large, cheap filing system where each file is stored whole and fetched by name.Data lakes, Parquet files, model files, document images, backups. The default home for ML data (see P1).Editing part of a file, many tiny random reads.
    Block storageA disk attached to one machine.Operating systems, databases, a training job's scratch space.Sharing between many machines.
    File storageA shared network drive.Many machines reading the same files, legacy applications.Cheap bulk storage at huge scale.

    Three things matter beyond that. Tiers: data you rarely touch can sit in a cold tier at a fraction of the price, with a delay or a fee to read it. A retention rule that moves last year's logs to cold storage is routine. Durability versus availability: object stores are designed so that stored data is almost never lost (copies in several places), which is different from the service being reachable at every instant. Encryption and keys: data is encrypted at rest by default, and the question that matters for a bank is who holds the key. With provider-managed keys the provider can in principle decrypt; with customer-managed keys in a key service (or a hardware module the bank controls) you can revoke access by disabling the key. Regulators and internal security teams tend to care about this distinction.

    For AI work specifically, two storage facts bite. Training reads the same data many times, so slow storage starves expensive GPUs: a GPU waiting on a disk is money burning. And model files are large: a 70-billion-parameter model in 16-bit numbers is about 140 GB, so how fast you can load it decides how fast a new serving replica comes up.

    Networking: regions, zones and who can reach what

    A region is a geographic cluster of data centres (for example, a city area). Inside it are several availability zones, which are separate buildings or halls with independent power and cooling, close enough for low-latency links. The unit of choice for banks is usually the region, because that is where data physically sits, and data residency rules are written about location.

    Zones give you resilience. If one machine is up 99.5% of the time, two copies in two zones behind a load balancer fail together only when both are down, so the combined availability is 1 minus the product of the failure chances. But the chain is only as strong as its shared parts, and everything in one region shares the region.

    A=Aregion·(1−(1−a)n)

    a is one zone's availability, n the number of zones the service runs in, and Aregion the chance the region itself (shared network, control plane, your own deployment mistakes) is working. The second factor climbs fast with n; the first caps the whole thing. Past two or three zones, more copies buy almost nothing.

    The other half of networking is who can reach what. The vocabulary you need:

    • Private network (VPC / virtual network). Your own fenced-off address space inside the cloud. Resources in it have no route from the internet unless you build one.
    • Subnets and security groups. Rules saying "the scoring service may call the feature store on this port, and nothing else may".
    • Private endpoints. A way to reach a managed service (object storage, a hosted model API) over the private network instead of the public internet. For a bank this is often a requirement rather than an option.
    • Private links to the bank. A dedicated or VPN connection from the data centre to the cloud, so that on-premises systems and cloud workloads talk without crossing the public internet. This is the hinge of hybrid designs (next lesson).
    • Egress. Data going into the cloud is usually free; data leaving it (to the internet, to another region, sometimes to another zone) is billed per gigabyte. Moving 50 TB out is a line on the invoice, and a reason to keep compute next to the data. Engineers call this data gravity.

    Latency is physics. A request across a region takes a millisecond or two; across continents, 100 ms or more. A real-time fraud score with a 100 ms budget cannot call a model in a distant region. See the latency-budget thinking in P3.

    Identity: who is allowed to do what

    In the cloud, nearly every action is an API call, and every API call is made by an identity: a person, a group, or a program (a service account, a role, a managed identity). The platform then checks a policy: does this identity have permission to perform this action on this resource? Everything else in cloud security leans on this check.

    Four ideas carry most of the weight.

    • Least privilege. Give each identity the minimum it needs and no more. A training job needs to read one bucket and write one folder, not "all storage".
    • Roles and groups, not individuals. Permissions attach to roles ("fraud-analyst-readonly"); people get roles through groups. When someone moves team, one change removes their access.
    • Short-lived credentials. A workload should receive a token that expires in an hour, issued because of what it is, rather than a password in a config file that lives forever. Keys committed to a repository are one of the most common causes of cloud breaches.
    • Separation of duties and audit. The person who builds a model should not be the one who approves its production release; every access is logged to a store the actor cannot edit.

    A least-privilege policy for a training job looks like this. The syntax differs by provider; the shape is always the same.

    {
  "identity": "svc-fraud-training",            // a program, not a person
  "allow": [
    { "action": "storage:read",  "resource": "bucket/fraud-features/2026/*" },
    { "action": "storage:write", "resource": "bucket/fraud-models/candidates/*" }
  ],
  "deny": [
    { "action": "*", "resource": "bucket/customer-pii-raw/*" }   // explicit no
  ],
  "expires": "1h"                              // short-lived token
}

    For human access there is one more layer: single sign-on with multi-factor authentication against the bank's own identity provider, and often just-in-time access where an engineer requests elevated rights for an hour with an approval and a reason. For data scientists, the practical effect is that you will ask for access through a ticket, you will get a role scoped to a project, and you will not be able to copy raw customer data to your laptop. That is by design.

    GPUs: what makes AI hardware different

    A CPU core is built to run one stream of unpredictable instructions quickly: branches, lookups, operating system tasks. A GPU gives up that cleverness to fit thousands of small arithmetic units that run the same instruction on different data at once. Deep learning is almost entirely matrix multiplication (F1.2), a task made of millions of independent multiply-and-add steps, so a GPU can be tens to hundreds of times faster than a CPU at it. Modern chips add dedicated matrix units (Nvidia calls them tensor cores) that do small matrix blocks in one step, and support for low-precision numbers (16-bit, 8-bit and now 4-bit), which move less data and do more operations per second.

    Three quantities define a GPU for your purposes.

    • Memory capacity (for example 80 GB or 192 GB). The model's weights must fit, along with working space. If they do not, you split the model over several GPUs, which adds cost and communication.
    • Memory bandwidth (terabytes per second). How fast weights can be streamed from the GPU's memory to its arithmetic units.
    • Compute (trillions of operations per second, TFLOPS). The raw arithmetic rate, which depends on the number format.

    Memory first. The weights take (parameters × bytes per number). A 70-billion-parameter model is 140 GB at 16 bits (2 bytes), 70 GB at 8 bits, 35 GB at 4 bits. Serving also needs room for the KV cache, the stored attention keys and values for each active conversation (M1.3), which grows with context length and the number of users. Training needs much more: with the common mixed-precision Adam recipe, each parameter costs roughly 16 bytes (weights, gradients, and optimiser state) before counting activations. That is why a model that can be served on two GPUs may need dozens to be trained (distributed training).

    Now the idea that surprises people. Generating one token requires reading all the weights once from memory and doing about 2 operations per parameter. The ratio of arithmetic to bytes read is tiny, around 1 operation per byte at 16 bits when you serve one user, while the chip can do hundreds of operations for every byte it can fetch. The arithmetic units mostly wait. Such a job is memory-bound, and its speed is simply bandwidth divided by model size.

    tokens per second (one user)≈memory bandwidthbytes of weights

    A GPU with 3.35 TB/s serving a 140 GB model cannot generate faster than 3,350 ÷ 140 ≈ 24 tokens a second for a single user, however many TFLOPS it advertises. Serving a batch of users reads the weights once for all of them, so more users raise total throughput almost for free, until arithmetic becomes the limit. That is why serving engines batch requests, and why smaller or lower-precision models are faster: fewer bytes to read.

    Three consequences to remember.

    • Quantization is a speed trick as much as a memory trick. Fewer bits means fewer bytes to stream, so tokens come faster (P3.1, and at the frontier R12).
    • Training is the opposite regime. Large batches and long sequences give the chip so much arithmetic per byte that it becomes compute-bound. Training cares about total TFLOPS and about how fast GPUs talk to each other; serving of single conversations cares about bandwidth and memory.
    • GPUs are rented in expensive chunks. A single high-end accelerator costs a few dollars to several tens of dollars an hour on-demand, depending on provider and generation; an eight-GPU server is the typical unit. Utilisation matters: an idle GPU costs the same as a busy one, which is the pricing lesson of Simulation 1 with bigger numbers.

    Not everything needs a GPU. Classical machine learning on tabular data (gradient boosting for credit scoring, F4.2), SQL, feature pipelines and small models run well, and far more cheaply, on CPUs. Reaching for a GPU for a logistic regression is a common and expensive habit.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Residency and regulation. Where customer data may sit, who may process it, and what the bank must tell its regulator before using a cloud provider. In the UAE and Saudi Arabia these are real constraints (the central banks, SAMA's cloud and cybersecurity frameworks, personal-data laws, and national cloud rules), and they change. Ask compliance for the current requirement rather than relying on a summary, including this one.
    • Fraud scoring. Always-on, low latency, multi-zone, private network paths, tight identity scoping.
    • Overnight scoring and campaigns. Bursty, restartable, a natural fit for cheap interruptible capacity.
    • Staff copilots and document AI. A hosted model API, or a self-hosted open model on GPUs inside the bank's boundary. The choice is a data-control, cost and capability decision.
    • Training an in-house model. A burst of GPU rental, ideally with data already in the same region to avoid egress and slow reads.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Cloud problems rarely look like technology failures. They look like a bill, a headline or an audit finding.

    • Paying for idle resources

      A GPU notebook left on over a weekend, a test endpoint never deleted. The cost is the same as when it is busy.

      Spot itLook at utilisation by resource, not total spend. Anything with average utilisation under 10% and a tag nobody recognises is a candidate. Set auto-shutdown and budgets with alerts.
    • Committing to the wrong shape

      A one-year commitment bought for a job that runs 3 hours a night.

      Spot itCompare expected monthly hours to the break-even fraction. Below it, the commitment loses money.
    • Over-broad identity

      A shared admin key, an "owner" role to save time, credentials in code or a notebook.

      Spot itAsk which identity a job runs as and what it can touch. Scan repositories for secrets. A reply of "my own login" or "the team key" is the answer you are checking for.
    • Public by accident

      A storage bucket or model endpoint reachable from the internet.

      Spot itAsk for the network path and the public-access setting of every store. Private endpoints and policy checks should make this impossible, not merely unlikely.
    • Surprise egress and distance

      Compute in one region, data in another; training slow, bill large.

      Spot itCheck that data and compute share a region, and read the data-transfer line on the invoice.
    • Counting TFLOPS when the job is memory-bound

      Buying the chip with the biggest headline number for single-user generation.

      Spot itDivide bandwidth by model bytes to get the ceiling on tokens a second. If the claimed speed exceeds it, something is off (or the model is smaller or quantized).
    • Forgetting the KV cache and training overhead

      "The model is 140 GB and the GPU is 192 GB, so it fits," then it runs out of memory at long contexts or with many users. Or planning to train on the hardware you serve on.

      Spot itAsk for memory with a long context and the peak number of concurrent users. For training, budget about 16 bytes per parameter before activations.
    • Mistaking zones for disaster recovery

      Three zones in one region protect against a building failing, not against a regional outage, a bad deployment or a deleted account.

      Spot itAsk what happens if the whole region is gone, and when the recovery path was last tested.
    • Using a GPU when a CPU would do

      Tabular gradient boosting on a GPU instance because "it is AI".

      Spot itAsk whether the model is a neural network at scale. If not, a CPU job is probably cheaper and just as fast.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Cloud lets us rent capacity for the weeks we need it instead of buying it for the peak, but the bill depends on how we match the job to the pricing: steady services on a commitment, nightly jobs on pay-as-you-go. The real controls are where the data sits, who is allowed to touch it, and whether the path stays private. For AI, the scarce item is GPU memory, so the first question on any model is how much it needs, not how fast the chip is.”
    To a platform or architecture team
    “The scoring service is latency sensitive and always on, so I'd run it across two zones in-region with committed capacity behind a private endpoint, under a workload identity that can read the online feature store and nothing else. The nightly retraining is restartable, so interruptible GPUs with checkpoints to object storage. Data and compute in the same region to avoid egress. For the LLM service I'll need weights plus peak KV cache in memory, so let's size that on concurrent users and context length before choosing the instance.”
    “Why not just put everything on GPUs?”

    Because most of our models, scorecards, gradient boosting, rules, feature pipelines, run on CPUs at lower cost with no loss in speed. GPUs help neural networks at scale. They are billed the same idle or busy, so I'd use them only for work that needs them and keep them busy.

    “Why does this 70B model need so many GPUs?”

    Weights alone are 140 GB at 16 bits, which is more than one 80 GB card. Add 20% or more for the KV cache and overhead and it spreads over several. At 8 or 4 bits it needs about half or a quarter of that, with a quality cost to be tested. Long contexts and many users increase the cache further.

    “The vendor says this chip has twice the TFLOPS. Will our chatbot be twice as fast?”

    Probably not. Generating tokens for a few users is limited by memory bandwidth, because every token reads all the weights. What would help is bandwidth, fewer bytes per weight, and batching. Compute only matters once we batch many users or for training.

    “Is it safe to put customer data in the cloud?”

    It can be, with controls: an approved in-country region, encryption with keys the bank controls, private network paths, least-privilege identities and audit logs, plus whatever the regulator requires for outsourcing and data location. The risk usually sits in configuration mistakes rather than the provider's buildings. Compliance and security should confirm the current rules for each workload.

    “Should we reserve capacity or stay on-demand?”

    Compare expected monthly hours with the commitment's break-even fraction, around 60% in the example. Always-on endpoints clear it; batch and experiments do not. Start on-demand to learn the shape, then commit only for the steady base load and leave bursts flexible.

    “What is the difference between a region and a zone?”

    A region is a geographic area, which is where data location rules apply. A zone is an independent data-centre building within it. Zones protect against a building failing, regions against a wider outage, and neither protects against our own bad deployment.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A one-year commitment costs 60% of on-demand for the whole month. A nightly job needs a machine for 4 hours a day. Which is cheaper?

    The job uses about 120 of 730 hours, which is 16%. The commitment costs 60% of a full month of on-demand, while on-demand costs only 16%. On-demand is about 3.7 times cheaper. The commitment pays only above 60% utilisation, about 438 hours.

    2. One zone's copy is up 99% of the time. What is the availability with two independent zones, and why is the real number lower?

    1 − 0.01² = 99.99%, about 53 minutes of downtime a year. Real systems are lower because zones share things: the region's control plane, networking, your own release process, a common dependency. Failures are not independent, so the region ceiling in the formula limits you.

    3. A GPU has 8 TB/s bandwidth. A model is 40 GB. What is the best-case speed for one user?

    8,000 GB/s ÷ 40 GB = 200 tokens a second, at most. Real speed is lower because of attention, communication and software overheads. If a claim exceeds 200 for this setup, the model must be smaller or quantized.

    4. How much memory do the weights of a 13-billion-parameter model need at 16, 8 and 4 bits?

    13B × 2 bytes = 26 GB; × 1 byte = 13 GB; × 0.5 byte = 6.5 GB. Add the KV cache and overhead on top. At 16 bits it fits one 80 GB card comfortably; at 4 bits it can run on a modest GPU.

    5. Why can serving 32 users together be nearly as fast per user as serving one?

    Each step reads the full set of weights once, whatever the number of users, and reading is the bottleneck. Extra users add arithmetic, which the chip has spare. Throughput rises nearly 32 times until the arithmetic time equals the memory time, after which per-user speed falls.

    6. A training job has to read a feature bucket and write a model file. What identity should it use, and what should it not use?

    A dedicated service identity (workload identity) granted read on that bucket path and write to one output path, with short-lived tokens. Not a person's login, not a shared admin key, not an owner role, not credentials stored in code.

    7. Training a 7-billion-parameter model with Adam in mixed precision: roughly how much memory for weights, gradients and optimiser state alone?

    About 16 bytes per parameter: 7B × 16 ≈ 112 GB, before activations. That does not fit on one 80 GB card, so memory-saving or multi-GPU methods are needed, whereas serving the same model at 16 bits needs only about 14 GB.
