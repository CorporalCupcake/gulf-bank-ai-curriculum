    From DataFrame to Honest Chart, and From Notebook to Code You Can Rerun

    How matplotlib and seaborn actually fit together, what makes a chart tell the truth, why a notebook can show results that no clean run would produce, and the small habits that make your work reproducible by someone else, including future you.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA chart is a claim, so choose axes, bins and encodings that make the true claim easiest to see; a notebook is a scratchpad with hidden state, so restart, run all, pin seeds and environments, and move anything reused into plain modules before anyone relies on it.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    On Monday you show the head of fraud a bar chart. Monthly card-fraud losses: AED 41.2m, 42.0m, 43.1m and 44.0m. The y-axis starts at 40. The last bar is more than three times the height of the first. Everyone in the room reads "losses have more than tripled". The real increase is 6.8%.

    You did nothing dishonest on purpose. The plotting library chose a sensible-looking axis for you. That is the first lesson: a default is a decision somebody else made, and you own the chart that comes out.

    On Thursday the same head of fraud asks you to rerun that analysis with last week's data. You open the notebook. It runs, and gives a different alert count from the one in your slide. You have no idea why. Somewhere you edited a threshold in a cell, ran a later cell, deleted the earlier one, and the notebook remembers none of that. The number on your slide came from a state that no longer exists.

    Both stories have the same root. In analysis work, the output is only as trustworthy as the process that produced it, and the reader cannot see the process. Plotting is how you show results to people. Reproducible code is how you show them to your own future self, your reviewer and the model validator.

    Hold onto thisAsk two questions of every chart you make and every chart you are shown. What is the one thing the reader should conclude? And could someone reach a different, wrong conclusion from the same picture? For notebooks, ask a third: if I restart the kernel and run everything top to bottom, do I get the same numbers?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Plotting with matplotlib and seaborn

    Matplotlib is the engine; seaborn is a driver for the common routes. Matplotlib draws everything: lines, rectangles, text, ticks. Seaborn sits on top of it and takes a DataFrame plus column names, does the grouping and statistics, and calls matplotlib to draw. Because seaborn returns matplotlib objects, you can always reach in and adjust them. You need both, and the sooner you stop treating them as rivals the better.

    Matplotlib has two ways to talk to it. The pyplot state machine (plt.plot(...), plt.title(...)) keeps a hidden "current figure" and "current axes". It is quick in a throwaway cell and confusing once you have more than one panel. The object-oriented style creates the objects explicitly and calls methods on them. Use this one for anything you will keep.

    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4), layout="constrained")
sns.histplot(data=tx, x="amount_aed", hue="segment",
             log_scale=True, bins=40, element="step", ax=ax)
ax.set_xlabel("Transaction amount (AED, log scale)")
ax.set_title("Card spend by segment, March 2026")
fig.savefig("amount_by_segment.png", dpi=200)

    Three objects to know. A Figure is the whole canvas. An Axes is one plot area inside it, with its own x and y scales. An Artist is anything drawn: a line, a bar, a label. ax is where you set labels, limits and scales. Most "how do I change X" questions are answered by "find the Axes and call a method on it".

    Seaborn has two layers. Axes-level functions (histplot, scatterplot, boxplot, lineplot) draw into an Axes you pass with ax=. Figure-level functions (displot, relplot, catplot) create their own figure and can split the data into a grid of panels with col= and row=. Figure-level ones are excellent for exploration ("one panel per segment") but control the layout themselves, so they fit awkwardly into a figure you assembled by hand. Newer still is the seaborn.objects interface, a layered "grammar of graphics" style; it is useful to recognise, and the older functions remain the everyday route.

    The chart-making loop that works: tidy the data first (one row per observation, one column per variable, the "long" form seaborn expects), then pick the chart by the question, then label and annotate. The question picks the chart:

    You want to showReach forBank example
    One distributionHistogram, or ECDFTransaction amounts, days past due
    Compare distributions across groupsOverlaid ECDFs, boxplots, violins; small multiplesTicket size by segment or branch
    Change over timeLine chart; one line per group, few groupsWeekly alert volume, PSI by month
    Relationship of two numbersScatter; hexbin or transparency when denseUtilisation against limit
    Compare a few quantitiesBars from zero, or dots sorted by sizeFraud loss by channel
    Rate and its uncertaintyPoint with interval bars (F2.1)Recall by model with 95% interval

    Why these choices? People judge position along a shared scale far more accurately than length without a shared baseline, and far better than angle, area or colour (Cleveland and McGill, 1984). That is why bars, dots and lines beat pie charts and bubble charts for accuracy. It also explains the zero rule: a bar encodes value as length from a baseline, so if the baseline is not zero the length lies. A dot or line encodes value as position, so a non-zero axis is fine, provided it is labelled.

    Two more mechanics decide whether a chart is honest. The first is binning. A histogram is a bar chart of counts per bin, and bin width is a choice that can create or hide structure. The second is overplotting: with 200,000 points a scatter plot is a solid blob, and you read density wrongly. Simulation 2 shows the first.

    Habits that make charts honest without making them dull:

    • Title states the finding, not the variable: "Business cards drive the tail of spend", not "Amount by segment".
    • Label units and currency. "AED", "AED millions", "per 10,000 transactions". A bare number invites misreading.
    • Direct-label lines instead of a distant legend where you can; the eye should not travel.
    • Use colour to carry meaning, not decoration. Keep to few hues, and choose a palette that survives greyscale and common colour-vision deficiency (seaborn's default and the "colorblind" palette are reasonable).
    • Show the denominator and the uncertainty. A 100% jump in AML alerts from 3 to 6 is a count of three. Say so on the chart.
    • Arabic text needs care: matplotlib does not shape or reorder right-to-left text on its own, so Arabic labels appear as disconnected, reversed letters until you pass them through a reshaper (the arabic-reshaper and python-bidi packages are the usual route) and use a font that has the glyphs. Test it before it goes in a board pack.
    • Save as vector (SVG or PDF) for documents, PNG at 200 dpi or more for slides, and set size in inches so text stays legible when pasted.

    Notebooks to reproducible code

    A Jupyter notebook is a document that talks to a running Python process, the kernel. Each cell you run changes the kernel's memory. The page you see is a record of cell text and old outputs; the kernel holds the real state. They can disagree, because you can run cells in any order, edit a cell without rerunning it, or delete a cell whose variable still lives in memory. This is why the number on your slide may not be what the notebook would produce today. Large studies of public notebooks found that a large share cannot be rerun to the same result, with out-of-order execution and missing dependencies the leading causes (Pimentel et al., 2019).

    Notebooks are still the right place to explore, because feedback is instant. The discipline is knowing when you have left exploration and are producing something others will rely on.

    The fix is a small set of habits, each closing a specific hole.

    • Restart and run all, before you share or believe a number. It deletes hidden state and proves the notebook runs top to bottom. Do it every time you finish a piece of work, and ideally in a loop while you work.
    • Make cells top-to-bottom linear. Imports and settings first, then loading, cleaning, analysis. Avoid cells that overwrite a variable that earlier cells depend on; give new names (df_clean) rather than reusing df.
    • Set seeds, and understand what they do. Anything random (train-test splits, model initialisation, sampling, bootstrap) must take a seed so the run repeats. Sklearn takes random_state=; NumPy prefers rng = np.random.default_rng(42) passed explicitly to code that needs it. A seed makes a run repeatable. It does not make the result true: Simulation 4 shows why you should also look at how much the answer moves across seeds.
    • Record the environment. A result depends on library versions. Use one environment per project, and a lock file that records exact versions (uv or pip-tools or conda lock files all do this). Note the Python version. A requirements.txt with no pins reproduces nothing six months on.
    • Pin the data, not just the code. Say which extract, which date, which query. For a bank this often means a snapshot table name and as-of date in a config cell at the top, not "the latest data". Point-in-time correctness (F3.1) is part of reproducibility.
    • Put paths and parameters in one place at the top: input table, threshold, date range, seed. Nothing magic in the middle.
    • Keep outputs out of version control. A notebook file stores outputs, including tables with customer data. Strip outputs before committing (nbstripout is a common tool), both for clean diffs and because outputs can leak personal data into a repository. Treat that as a data-handling rule, not a style tip.
    • Move code out of the notebook when it earns it. A function used in two notebooks, anything with tests, anything that feeds production: put it in a .py module (F10.3) and import it. The notebook becomes the thin story on top: load, call functions, plot, comment.
    # src/fraud/features.py  (plain module, importable, testable)
import pandas as pd

def add_velocity(tx: pd.DataFrame, window: str = "1h") -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Count of the customer's transactions in the trailing window."""
    tx = tx.sort_values("ts")
    tx["n_recent"] = (tx.set_index("ts").groupby("customer_id")["amount_aed"]
                        .rolling(window).count().to_numpy())
    return tx

# notebook cell: thin, readable, reruns cleanly
# from fraud.features import add_velocity
# tx = add_velocity(load_snapshot("2026-03-31"))

    Where does the boundary sit? A useful rule: notebook for looking, module for doing. If someone else will run it, or it will run on a schedule, or a regulator may ask how a number was produced, it should be a script or pipeline with a seed, a lock file, tests and a recorded data version. If you only needed to find out, a notebook is fine, and you should say so when you share it.

    A caution on seeds: they make runs repeatable only on the same library versions, hardware settings and, for some GPU operations, with deterministic modes switched on. "Same seed, different answer" after an upgrade is normal and is another reason for lock files.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Model validation. An independent validator will try to rerun your development notebook. If it does not, the delay costs weeks. Clean reruns with pinned environments are a courtesy and, increasingly, an expectation.
    • Regulatory and board packs. A figure in a pack has to be defensible and regenerable months later. Charts that start the axis at a convenient number are the first thing a sceptical reader challenges.
    • Monitoring. Monthly PSI, alert volumes and calibration plots are the same notebook run on new data. Modules plus a scheduled job beat a notebook someone remembers to open.
    • Handover. Colleagues leave and models outlive them. The bar is that a stranger can reproduce your headline number from the repository without asking you.
    • Data protection. Notebook outputs, plots with small cell counts and saved images can all contain personal data. Handle them under the same rules as the data itself.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Matplotlib and seaborn are mature and stable; the interesting movement is around them. Notebook tooling is shifting from "a file of cells with hidden state" towards environments that enforce consistency, and AI coding assistants now write much of the plotting boilerplate, which raises the cost of not being able to check what they drew.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These appear in human and AI-generated analyses alike. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Truncated bar axis

      A 7% rise drawn as a threefold one. The most common honest mistake.

      Spot itLook at the first tick label on the y-axis of any bar chart. If it is not zero, the lengths mislead.
    • Dual axes that manufacture correlation

      Two lines on separate y-axes can be scaled to cross or track each other at will.

      Spot itAsk what happens if one axis is rescaled. Prefer two stacked panels sharing the x-axis.
    • Averages drawn as if they were the data

      Bars of mean ticket size per branch hide whether branches differ by shape, outliers or sample size.

      Spot itAsk for the distribution (box, violin, ECDF) and the count per group.
    • A binning choice that makes the story

      One hump or two depending on the number of bins.

      Spot itRedraw with half and double the bins. If the conclusion changes, say so and show an ECDF instead.
    • Overplotting and hidden density

      Thousands of points in one blob; the dense region looks the same as a sparse one.

      Spot itReduce marker opacity, switch to hexbin or 2D histogram, or sample and say you did.
    • Unlabelled units, currencies and denominators

      "Losses up 12%" with no base, period or currency.

      Spot itEvery axis and every headline number should answer: of what, in what unit, over what period.
    • The notebook that only works in one order

      A cell uses a variable defined in a cell further down, or one that has since been deleted.

      Spot itRestart the kernel and run all. Any error or changed number is the finding. Look at the execution counters: if they are not 1, 2, 3 in order, the state is suspect.
    • Seeds as a cure-all, or a cherry-pick

      Fixing random_state=42 and calling the result stable, or trying seeds until one looks good.

      Spot itRerun across several seeds and report the spread. A single repeatable number from a small test set is a point from a wide distribution.
    • Unpinned environments and "latest" data

      The notebook ran in March; in September a library update or a refreshed table changes the answer.

      Spot itLook for a lock file, the Python version and a named data snapshot with a date. If any is missing, the result cannot be recreated.
    • Personal data in committed outputs

      A table preview with customer IDs saved in the notebook and pushed to a shared repository.

      Spot itOpen the raw notebook file and search for output cells. Strip them automatically before commit.
    • Trusting an AI-generated chart because it looks professional

      The code plotted the wrong column, filtered out a segment, or used the default axis.

      Spot itCheck three numbers on the chart against a direct computation from the DataFrame. Read the axis limits and the filter lines.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “I've drawn losses from zero so the bars show the true size: they're up about 7% since January, not a threefold jump. Every number on this page can be regenerated from one command against the 31 March snapshot, so if you ask me in six months I'll get the same figures.”
    To a validator or a technical interviewer
    “Development is in notebooks, but the feature and training code is a versioned package with tests. The environment is locked, the seed and the data snapshot are in a config file, and I check that restart-and-run-all gives identical outputs. For headline metrics I report the spread across repeated splits or a bootstrap interval, not one seed's result.”
    “Why did you start the y-axis at zero here but not on that line chart?”

    Bars encode value as length from a baseline, so a non-zero start changes the visual ratio. A line encodes value as position and trend, where the interesting variation can be small relative to the level. A non-zero line axis is fine if it is labelled, and I'd mention it. If the audience is likely to misread, I add a reference line or show the change as a percentage.

    “Do we need to stop using notebooks?”

    No. They're the fastest way to explore and communicate. The rule is that nothing leaves exploration without a clean run, pinned versions and the reusable parts moved into tested modules. Notebook for looking, module for doing.

    “Why not just fix the random seed and be done?”

    A seed makes a run repeatable, not representative. On a small test set a different split can move recall by many points. I fix the seed so others can reproduce, then show the spread over seeds or folds, or a bootstrap interval, so nobody mistakes one draw for the truth.

    “Matplotlib or seaborn?”

    Both. Seaborn for fast statistical charts straight from a DataFrame, matplotlib underneath for anything custom: annotations, layouts, saving. Seaborn returns matplotlib objects, so I mix them in the same figure.

    “The AI assistant produced this chart. Can we use it?”

    After a check. I verify a few plotted values against a direct calculation, read the filters and axis limits, and confirm the data source is the approved one. Then it's as good as any other chart. The risk is the polish, not the plotting.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A bar chart's y-axis runs from 90 to 100. One bar is 98 and another is 96. How much taller does the first look, and how much larger is it really?

    Heights from the axis start: 8 and 6, so it looks 8 ÷ 6 = 1.33 times as tall, a 33% difference. The values differ by 98 ÷ 96 = 1.02, about 2%. Lie factor is about 16 (33 ÷ 2).

    2. What is the difference between a Figure and an Axes in matplotlib, and why prefer fig, ax = plt.subplots() to bare plt.plot() for kept work?

    The Figure is the whole canvas; an Axes is one plot area with its own scales and labels. Explicit objects make it clear which plot each call affects, work cleanly with several panels and with seaborn's ax= argument, and do not depend on hidden "current" state.

    3. You see a histogram of loan amounts with one smooth hump. How could the picture be hiding two customer types?

    Too few bins can smooth a two-peaked distribution into one. Redraw with more bins, use a log scale for money, or compare an ECDF. If peaks appear and persist across several bin widths, they are likely real; if they appear at only one width, they are noise.

    4. A notebook's cells show execution numbers 3, 1, 5, 2. What does that tell you, and what do you do?

    The cells were run out of order, so the displayed outputs may come from states that top-to-bottom execution would not produce. Restart the kernel and run all. If anything errors or changes, fix that before sharing.

    5. Your recall estimate changes from 76% to 85% when you change the seed of the split. Is the model unstable?

    Probably not. The model may be fine and the test set small. With 60 frauds in the test split, the standard error of an 80% recall is about √(0.8 × 0.2 ÷ 60) ≈ 5 points, so a range like this is expected. Report an interval or the spread over many splits, and gather more positives if the decision needs a tighter answer.

    6. List four things that must be recorded so a colleague can reproduce a result in six months.

    The code version, the library versions and Python version (a lock file), the random seeds, and the data snapshot with its as-of date. Also useful: the parameters and the command that runs everything.

    7. When should code leave the notebook?

    When it is reused across notebooks, needs tests, feeds production or a scheduled job, or has become hard to read in cells. Move it into a module, import it, and keep the notebook as the narrative.

    8. Why might Arabic axis labels look broken in a matplotlib chart, and what is the fix?

    Matplotlib does not do right-to-left shaping or reordering by default, so letters appear disconnected and reversed. Reshape and reorder the text with a library such as arabic-reshaper with python-bidi, and use a font that includes Arabic glyphs. Check the chart visually before it is circulated.
