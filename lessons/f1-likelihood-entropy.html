    Likelihood, Entropy and the Language of Training

    Why nearly every loss function is the same idea in disguise, and how the same few lines of maths train a language model, shrink it into a smaller one, and tell a credit team its applicants have changed.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining a model means choosing the settings that make the data you actually saw as unsurprising as possible. Likelihood measures that, cross-entropy is the same measure averaged per example, and KL divergence is the extra surprise you pay for believing the wrong distribution.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A product manager hands you a small book of personal loans from a new salary-transfer segment: 200 loans, 9 of which defaulted within a year. The bank's long-run default rate on personal loans is about 3%. Which default rate should you use for this segment?

    Turn the question around. Pick any candidate rate, say 3%, and ask: if the true rate were 3%, how probable is the book I actually saw? Then try 4%, 5%, 10%. Each candidate gives the same data a different probability. Some candidates make 9 defaults out of 200 look routine. Others make it look like a freak event. The candidate that makes your data least surprising is the maximum likelihood estimate.

    That's the whole of F1.6. Everything else in this lesson, from the log loss on a fraud model to the training of a large language model to the drift report your credit validator sends every month, is this one move dressed up for a different job.

    Three things to take from it.

    • The peak is always at the observed rate. With 9 defaults in 200, no rate explains the data better than 9 ÷ 200 = 4.5%. The 3% long-run rate makes this book about half as likely. That's not proof the segment is riskier, but it's a measurable amount of evidence.
    • More data makes the curve narrower, not taller in meaning. At 2,000 loans the same 4.5% is pinned down to roughly 3.6–5.5%. At 200 loans, anything from about 2.2% to 8% is plausible. The width of the curve is your uncertainty.
    • Maximum likelihood overfits small samples. Zero defaults in 15 loans gives a best estimate of 0%: "these customers never default." Nobody believes that. Adding a prior, the bank's experience written as imaginary past loans, pulls the estimate to a sensible 2.6%. That is exactly what regularisation does to a machine learning model.
    Hold onto thisLikelihood scores a model by how probable it makes the data you actually saw. Taking the negative log turns that into a loss you minimise. Almost every loss you'll meet in a bank or an AI lab, squared error, log loss, cross-entropy, is a negative log-likelihood under some assumption about the noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Likelihood is probability read backwards

    A probability model takes parameters and tells you how likely each possible outcome is. A likelihood uses the same formula the other way round: the data is fixed, because you've already seen it, and you vary the parameters. For the loan book, with k defaults among n independent loans and a default rate p:

    L(p)=pk(1−p)n−k

    The probability of seeing this particular set of defaults and non-defaults if the rate were p. (The binomial count of arrangements is left out because it doesn't depend on p, so it can't change which p wins.)

    Two warnings about the word. First, a likelihood is not a probability of the parameter. The curve in Simulation 1 doesn't integrate to 1, and "the likelihood of 3% is 0.51" means "3% makes the data 0.51 times as probable as 4.5% does", not "there's a 51% chance the rate is 3%". To get a probability of the parameter, you need a prior and Bayes' rule, which is the MAP idea below. Second, only ratios of likelihoods mean anything. That's why the chart scales the peak to 1.

    Why everyone takes the log

    The raw likelihood is a product of one probability per data point, so it gets very small very fast. The 9-in-200 book has a probability of about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ under the best rate. With 2,000 loans it's around 10⁻¹⁶⁰, and a million transactions would underflow any computer. Taking logs turns the product into a sum:

    ℓ(p)=klnp+(n−k)ln(1−p)⇒p^=kn

    The log-likelihood. Set its slope to zero and you get the observed default rate. Logs don't move the peak, because the log is increasing, but they make the maths and the computing stable.

    Logs give you three more things for free. Sums have simple gradients, so training by gradient descent works on them (F1.3). A sum over data points divided by n is an average, so you can train on mini-batches. And the log-likelihood curve near its peak is close to a parabola whose curvature tells you the standard error, which is where the "within 1.92 of the peak" plausible range comes from: 1.92 is half of 3.84, the 95% point of a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom.

    Finally, flip the sign. Optimisers minimise, so we minimise the negative log-likelihood (NLL). Divide it by the number of examples and you have the average loss that every training log prints.

    Every familiar loss is a negative log-likelihood

    Choosing a loss function is choosing what you believe about the noise. Write down a probability model for one outcome given the model's prediction, take −log, and a familiar loss falls out.

    −lnN(y∣y^,σ)=(y−y^)22σ2+ln(σ2π)

    Gaussian noise gives squared error. If you assume the actual value scatters around the prediction in a bell curve of fixed width σ, the NLL is the squared error scaled by a constant, plus another constant. Minimising one minimises the other. Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.

    −lnP(y∣p) =−[ylnp+(1−y)ln(1−p)]

    A coin flip gives log loss. For a yes/no outcome (fraud or not, default or not) with predicted probability p, the NLL is binary cross-entropy, also called log loss. Logistic regression is maximum likelihood with this loss, which is why it produces calibrated probabilities when the model is right.

    −lnqy=−∑ipilnqi

    A many-sided die gives cross-entropy. Here p is the one-hot label (1 for the right class, 0 elsewhere), so the sum keeps one term. For one of many classes, such as routing a complaint, or the next token in a sentence, the NLL is minus the log of the probability the model gave the right class. That's categorical cross-entropy, the loss that trains every LLM.

    The fraud mode shows the property that makes log loss special. Predict 1% fraud on a transaction that turns out to be fraud and the loss is −ln 0.01 ≈ 4.6. Predict 0.2% and it's 6.2. A single confident miss costs as much as hundreds of confident correct calls, and a prediction of exactly 0 costs infinity. That's harsh, but it's what makes log loss a proper scoring rule: the only way to minimise it on average is to report your true belief. It's why log loss trains calibrated probabilities (F7.4). Squared error on probabilities, the Brier score, is also proper but much gentler on confident mistakes.

    The same logic tells you when to change the loss. Fraud loss amounts in AED are heavy-tailed: a few huge cases dominate. Squared error assumes Gaussian noise, so it lets those cases drag every prediction around. Assume Laplace noise instead and the NLL becomes absolute error. Model the log of the amount and you're assuming multiplicative noise. Pick a quantile loss and you're fitting a percentile rather than a mean. Each is a likelihood choice in disguise.

    Overfitting, priors and regularisation

    Maximum likelihood has one big weakness: it believes the data completely. With 0 defaults in 15 loans it says 0%. With a million-parameter model and ten thousand examples, it will happily find settings that make the training data almost certain, including the noise. That's overfitting, seen from the likelihood side.

    The fix is to add what you knew before the data arrived. Bayes' rule (F1.4) says the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Take the log, and the most probable parameter value, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, maximises a sum:

    θMAP=argmaxθ[lnL(θ)+lnπ(θ)]

    Fit to the data, plus a penalty for parameter values your prior thinks are implausible. In Simulation 1 the prior acts like s imaginary past loans at 3%, so the MAP is (k + 0.03s) ÷ (n + s). With 100 imaginary loans and 9 of 200 real ones, that's 12 ÷ 300 = 4.0%.

    Now the punchline. Put a Gaussian prior centred on zero on every weight of a model, with spread τ. Its negative log is the squared size of the weights over 2τ², plus a constant. So:

    NLL+λ‖w‖2⟺MAP withw∼N(0,τ2) λ=12τ2

    L2 regularisation (ridge, weight decay) is a Gaussian prior. A bigger λ means a tighter prior: you believe more strongly that weights should be small. L1 (lasso) is the same with a Laplace prior, whose sharp peak at zero is why it sets some weights exactly to zero.

    Credit risk has its own version of this problem: low-default portfolios. Sovereign, bank and large-corporate books in the Gulf may see no defaults for years, so the MLE says 0%. Regulators don't accept that. Banks use conservative estimators, such as Pluto and Tasche's "most prudent estimation", pool with external data, or put explicit priors on the rate. The common thread is the same as MAP: don't let a small sample speak alone.

    Label smoothing is a related trick for classifiers. Instead of training against a one-hot label (100% Fraud), you train against a slightly softened one (say 90% Fraud, the rest spread across the other classes). That stops the model pushing its logits towards infinity to chase a zero loss, which tends to improve calibration and generalisation. One catch, found by Müller, Kornblith and Hinton in 2019: label-smoothed teachers make worse teachers for distillation, because smoothing erases the information about which wrong classes are nearly right. Hold that thought for the distillation section.

    Entropy: average surprise

    Now switch from fitting models to measuring uncertainty. The surprise of an outcome with probability p is −log p. A certain event has surprise 0. A coin landing heads has surprise 1 bit (using log base 2). A 1-in-1,000 event has about 10 bits. Notice this is exactly the per-example loss from the last section: the NLL of an outcome is how surprised the model was by it.

    Entropy is the average surprise when outcomes really do follow distribution p:

    H(p)=−∑ipilog2pi

    Measured in bits with log base 2, or in nats with natural logs (1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits). A fair coin has 1 bit of entropy. Four equally likely outcomes have 2 bits. A distribution with all its mass on one outcome has 0.

    Shannon's 1948 result gives entropy a hard, practical meaning: it's the minimum average number of bits per symbol that any code can achieve. That's why compression and prediction are the same problem. A model that predicts the next token well can compress text well, and vice versa.

    Entropy appears in a bank more often than people notice. Decision trees choose splits by information gain, which is the drop in entropy of the default label after the split. The entropy of an LLM's next-token distribution is a cheap uncertainty signal: a high-entropy answer on an account balance question is a reason to route to a human. And "entropy collapse" is a named failure in reinforcement learning on LLMs, where the model becomes too certain too early and stops exploring (Part 4).

    Cross-entropy and KL: the price of the wrong distribution

    Suppose outcomes follow p, but you act as if they follow q: you've built a code, or a model, around the wrong distribution. Your average surprise is now the cross-entropy, and the extra you pay over the unavoidable minimum is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:

    H(p,q)=−∑ipilogqi =H(p)⏟unavoidable+KL(p‖q)⏟your model's fault KL(p‖q)=∑ipilogpiqi≥0

    KL is zero only when q = p. It's large when q gives low probability to things that p says happen often, and infinite if q says "impossible" about something that happens.

    Here's the link back to likelihood. Average the NLL over a large training set and you're estimating the cross-entropy between the data distribution and the model. The data's own entropy is fixed, so maximising likelihood is the same as minimising KL(data ‖ model). Every model trained with log loss or cross-entropy is quietly minimising a KL divergence in one particular direction.

    Direction matters, because KL is not symmetric. KL(p ‖ q) is an average over what p says happens, so it punishes q for missing things. It's called forward or mode-covering KL: a model trained this way spreads probability over everything the data does, even at the cost of putting some mass on nonsense in between. KL(q ‖ p) is an average over what q does, so it punishes q for putting mass where p has little. It's reverse or mode-seeking KL: a model trained this way picks one good answer and commits, even if it ignores other valid ones. Neither is right in general. Simulation 3 lets you feel the difference.

    Perplexity, the number LLM papers quote, is just cross-entropy exponentiated:

    Perplexity=eH(p,q)

    With cross-entropy measured in nats per token. Read it as "the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among this many tokens". A validation loss of 2.0 nats per token is a perplexity of about 7.4. Lower is better, but only comparable between models that use the same tokeniser and the same test text.

    How LLMs are trained, distilled and kept on a leash

    Pretraining is cross-entropy. An LLM sees a sequence of tokens and outputs a probability distribution over its vocabulary for the next one. The loss is −log of the probability it gave the token that actually came next, averaged over trillions of tokens. That's categorical maximum likelihood: the model is being pushed towards the forward KL minimum against the distribution of human text, which is why it learns to cover everything people write, including the things you'd rather it didn't. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) uses the same loss on curated examples.

    Distillation is matching a teacher's distribution. A big teacher model doesn't just know the right next token. It knows that "card" is likely, "account" is plausible and "salary" is rare. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean's 2015 insight was to train a small student on the teacher's full probability distribution, softened with a temperature, rather than on one-hot labels. The loss is cross-entropy against the teacher's soft targets, which is forward KL(teacher ‖ student) plus a constant. The "dark knowledge" in the wrong-but-close answers is what makes it work, and it's why label-smoothed teachers distil worse.

    Classic distillation runs on fixed text. The student never sees its own mistakes, so at deployment it drifts into states it was never trained on. On-policy distillation fixes that: the student generates text, and the teacher scores every token the student produced. The usual loss is reverse KL(student ‖ teacher) per token. Reverse KL suits a small student, because it can't cover everything a large teacher can do, so it's better off committing to the teacher's main mode than smearing itself thinly. This is how Qwen3's small models were trained from larger ones, and in late 2026 it is one of the most active techniques in post-training (Part 4).

    RLHF and its relatives use KL as a leash. When you fine-tune a model to maximise a reward, whether that's human preference or "the answer passed the checker", it will find ways to game the reward that degrade everything else. The standard defence is a KL penalty that keeps the new policy close to a reference model, usually the SFT model:

    maxθE[r(x,y)]−βKL(πθ‖πref)

    Get as much reward as possible, but pay β for every bit of divergence from the reference model. Small β lets the model change more and risks reward hacking; large β keeps it safe but teaches little. Note the direction: the model being trained comes first, so this is reverse KL.

    DPO (direct preference optimisation, 2023) starts from exactly this objective, solves it in closed form, and turns preference learning into a simple classification loss on pairs of answers. There's no separate reward model and no explicit KL term, but the leash is still there, implicitly, through the log-ratio between the policy and the reference. When someone says DPO "has no KL", the accurate reply is that it has one baked in, controlled by β.

    For a bank, the leash is the practical point. If you fine-tune an open-weight model on your own complaint data or policy documents, the KL to the base model is a measurable proxy for how much general behaviour you've disturbed. Recent work (Part 4) finds that forgetting tracks that KL quite closely.

    PSI: KL divergence in a credit-risk suit

    Every scorecard in a Gulf bank gets a monthly or quarterly monitoring report, and one of the first numbers on it is the population stability index. It asks: are this month's applicants distributed across score bands the way the development sample was? Split the development scores into bands, usually deciles, so each holds a share ei (often 10%). Count the share ai of this month's applicants in each band. Then:

    PSI=∑i(ai−ei)lnaiei =KL(a‖e)+KL(e‖a)

    PSI is the sum of the KL divergences in both directions, sometimes called the Jeffreys divergence. That's why it's symmetric when KL isn't: swapping "development" and "this month" gives the same number. A band that moves from 10% to 15% contributes 0.05 × ln 1.5 ≈ 0.020.

    Practitioners read it with a traffic light: below 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 is a moderate shift worth investigating, and above 0.25 is a major shift that often triggers a redevelopment discussion. Those cut-offs are industry convention, not a regulatory rule, and they have no statistical derivation. Yurdakul and Naranjo showed that, with no real change at all, sampling noise alone gives an expected PSI of roughly (B − 1)(1/n + 1/N), where B is the number of bands and n and N are the two sample sizes. With 10 bands and 150 applicants in a niche segment, that's already about 0.06 before anything has moved.

    You'll meet the same formula under another name. A scorecard's information value (IV), used to rank characteristics during development, is also a Jeffreys divergence, between the distribution of goods and the distribution of bads across a characteristic's bins (B4.2). PSI asks "has the population moved?"; IV asks "does this variable separate good from bad?". Same maths.

    Notice what PSI can't see. It compares who applies, not whether the model still works. A recession can raise default rates at every score while the score distribution barely moves: PSI stays green and the model's calibration falls apart. And because PSI only counts bands, any shift that happens within bands is invisible. Treat PSI as a smoke alarm for the input population, and pair it with outcome-based checks (Gini, calibration by band, F7.7) once labels arrive.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Likelihood and KL are the grammar underneath much of the curriculum. Once you see them, a lot of separate-looking topics turn out to be one idea.

    F1.4 · Bayes' rule and base ratesMAP is Bayes with a log

    Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Regularisation is the prior. A strong prior is how you survive a 15-loan segment.

    F1.5 · DistributionsThe distribution picks the loss

    Gaussian, Laplace, Bernoulli, Poisson: each assumption about noise gives its own negative log-likelihood.

    F1.3 and F1.8 · Gradients and optimisationLogs make training possible

    Sums of log-probabilities have clean gradients and work on mini-batches. Cross-entropy with softmax has the famously simple gradient: prediction minus label.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsEvery GLM is maximum likelihood

    Logistic, Poisson and Gamma regression are all fitted by maximising a likelihood. Deviance is twice a log-likelihood gap.

    F4.4 · RegularisationL2 is a Gaussian prior, L1 a Laplace one

    The penalty strength is how confident you are that weights should be small.

    F7.4 · CalibrationLog loss rewards honest probabilities

    It's a proper scoring rule. A model trained on it, and not overfitted, tends to be calibrated. Temperature scaling minimises it on held-out data.

    F7.7 · Drift monitoringPSI is a symmetrised KL

    Read it with sample size in mind, and pair it with outcome monitoring, because a stable population can still hide a broken model.

    B4.2 · Scorecards, WOE and IVInformation value is the same formula

    WOE is a log-ratio of good and bad distributions. IV sums it up exactly as PSI does, between goods and bads instead of then and now.

    M1.3 · Pretraining, then post-trainingCross-entropy, then a KL leash

    Pretraining minimises forward KL to human text. Post-training maximises reward while a KL term keeps the model near where it started.

    M1.4 · Sampling: temperature and top-pTemperature changes entropy

    Dividing logits by a temperature above 1 raises the entropy of the next-token distribution; below 1 sharpens it.

    P3.1 · Quantisation, pruning and distillationDistillation is KL matching

    A smaller on-premises model for Arabic complaint triage is usually trained to match a large model's token distributions.

    R3.1 · RLHF, DPO, GRPOWhere the KL term lives, or doesn't

    PPO-style RLHF penalises KL to a reference. DPO hides it in the loss. Many reasoning recipes now drop it.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Maximum likelihood and KL divergence themselves are settled science. What's moving, fast, is how KL is used in post-training: which direction, on whose samples, how it's estimated, and whether it's needed at all. In credit risk, the live question is how to read PSI properly.

    How we got here

    1. 1922
      Maximum likelihood gets its name

      Fisher sets out the method of maximum likelihood and the idea of a likelihood as distinct from a probability.

      Fisher, “On the Mathematical Foundations of Theoretical Statistics”, Phil. Trans. Royal Society A, 1922
    2. 1948–51
      Entropy, then KL divergence

      Shannon defines entropy as the limit of lossless compression. Kullback and Leibler define the divergence between two distributions as information for discrimination.

      Shannon, Bell System Technical Journal, 1948; Kullback & Leibler, Annals of Mathematical Statistics, 1951
    3. 2005
      Prudent estimates for portfolios with no defaults

      A principled upper-bound approach for low-default portfolios, now standard in Basel-style PD estimation.

      Pluto & Tasche, “Estimating Probabilities of Default for Low Default Portfolios”
    4. 2015
      Distillation with soft targets

      Train a small model on a large model's temperature-softened output distribution.

      Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, “Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network”
    5. 2019
      Label smoothing helps calibration, hurts teachers

      Smoothing improves calibration but erases the similarity information a student would learn from.

      Müller, Kornblith & Hinton, “When Does Label Smoothing Help?”, NeurIPS 2019
    6. 2020
      PSI has a sampling distribution

      A rescaled PSI is approximately chi-squared, with a mean that depends on bands and both sample sizes. The authors argue for critical values in place of the fixed 0.1 and 0.25 cut-offs.

      Yurdakul & Naranjo, “Statistical Properties of the Population Stability Index”, Journal of Risk Model Validation
    7. 2022–23
      KL becomes the leash in RLHF, then DPO

      InstructGPT trains against a reward model with a per-token KL penalty to the SFT model. DPO solves the same KL-constrained objective in closed form.

      Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023
    8. 2024
      On-policy distillation for language models

      GKD and MiniLLM train students on their own generations. MiniLLM argues for reverse KL so a small student doesn't over-spread; GKD tries several divergences and finds the best one depends on the task.

      Agarwal et al., ICLR 2024; Gu et al., ICLR 2024
    9. 2025
      Reasoning RL drops or rethinks the KL term

      DAPO removes the KL penalty for long chain-of-thought training, arguing the model must be allowed to move far from its start. Tang and Munos show that common ways of putting a KL estimate into the loss, including GRPO's, don't give the true KL gradient. Separately, “entropy collapse” is documented: reasoning RL sharply reduces the policy's entropy early and performance plateaus.

      Yu et al., “DAPO”, 2025; Tang & Munos, 2025; Cui et al., “The Entropy Mechanism of RL for Reasoning Language Models”, 2025
    10. 2025
      Forgetting tracks KL to the base model

      On-policy RL forgets less than SFT, and the amount forgotten is predicted by the KL between the fine-tuned and base model on the new task. Accepted at ICLR 2026.

      Shenfeld, Pari & Agrawal, “RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less”
    11. May 2025
      On-policy distillation goes into a frontier model family

      Qwen3's smaller models are trained from Qwen3-32B or 235B by aligning the student's logits with the teacher's on the student's own outputs. The report says this beat RL in both performance and training cost.

      Qwen Team, “Qwen3 Technical Report”
    12. Oct 2025
      On-policy distillation as a cheap alternative to RL

      Using per-token reverse KL against a teacher, an 8B model reached 74.4% on AIME'24 with about 1,800 GPU hours, versus 67.6% for an RL baseline that used about 17,900. The same method restored instruction-following lost after fine-tuning on internal documents, without losing the new knowledge.

      K. Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, “On-Policy Distillation”
    13. 2026
      Fixing reverse KL's weak spots

      A wave of papers treats reverse-KL on-policy distillation as the default and patches its failures. Entropy-aware OPD switches to forward KL on tokens where the teacher is uncertain, to keep diversity. Others document instability and propose truncated reverse KL and token masking, or study when the teacher actually helps.

      “Entropy-Aware On-Policy Distillation”, arXiv 2603.07079, Mar 2026; arXiv 2603.25562, Mar 2026; “Rethinking On-Policy Distillation”, arXiv 2604.13016, Apr 2026
    The current consensus, in one linePretraining and SFT minimise forward KL to data. Post-training increasingly minimises reverse KL to a teacher, or uses KL to a reference only as a leash, and practitioners now argue about the direction and the estimator rather than taking either for granted. In credit risk, read PSI against sample size, not only against 0.1 and 0.25.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in model reviews, validation findings and AI-generated analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Trusting the MLE on a small sample

      “The new segment has a 0% default rate.” Or a PD of 1.2% from 83 loans with 1 default, used straight in pricing.

      Spot itAsk for the counts behind every rate, and an interval. If n is small, ask what prior, pooling or prudent estimate was used.
    • A loss that doesn't match the noise

      Squared error on heavy-tailed AED loss amounts lets a few large frauds dominate the fit. The model looks fine on average and wrong for typical cases.

      Spot itPlot the residuals. If the tails are long or the spread grows with the size of the prediction, ask why the loss assumes Gaussian noise.
    • One confident miss dominating the log loss

      A model outputs 0.0001 on a few transactions that turn out to be fraud. Average log loss jumps, and someone “fixes” it by clipping probabilities at 1% without telling anyone.

      Spot itLook at the largest per-example losses, not just the mean. Ask whether probabilities are clipped, and where.
    • Comparing perplexity across tokenisers or test sets

      An Arabic model with a larger vocabulary has fewer, more informative tokens, so its per-token perplexity isn't comparable with an English-centric model's.

      Spot itAsk for bits per byte or per character on the same text, or better, a task-level evaluation.
    • Treating KL as a distance

      “The KL between the two months is 0.3, so they're 0.3 apart either way.” KL isn't symmetric, and it's infinite if one distribution has zero where the other doesn't.

      Spot itAsk which direction was computed and how empty bins were handled. If the answer is “we added a tiny epsilon”, ask how much the result changes with a different epsilon.
    • PSI read without sample size

      A 150-customer segment shows PSI 0.08, and a redevelopment is scoped. Most of that is noise. Meanwhile a portfolio of 50,000 shows PSI 0.06 from a real, material shift that nobody investigates.

      Spot itCompute the noise floor, about (bands − 1) × (1/n + 1/N), or use a chi-squared critical value. Then ask what moved, band by band.
    • Green PSI, broken model

      The applicant mix is stable but default rates have risen at every score, because the economy changed. PSI can't see this.

      Spot itAsk for outcome monitoring alongside PSI: actual versus predicted default rate by band, and Gini over time.
    • A post-training run with no KL tracking

      A fine-tuned complaint assistant scores higher on the internal reward but has started answering in English to Arabic messages, or forgot how to refuse.

      Spot itAsk for the KL to the base model over training, and a fixed set of general and safety prompts evaluated before and after.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't add up

      Typical signs: “cross-entropy of 0.05, so the model is 95% accurate”; PSI computed with percentages (10, 15) instead of fractions (0.10, 0.15), which multiplies it by 100; log base 10 in one place and natural logs in another; or text saying “KL is symmetric”.

      Spot itRecompute one band's PSI contribution by hand. It takes 30 seconds and catches most of these.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a credit committee · 30 seconds
    “PSI tells us whether this month's applicants look like the ones the scorecard was built on. It's 0.14, which is amber by convention. Most of it comes from two low-score bands growing after the thin-file campaign, so it's a real shift, not noise: with 6,000 applications, noise alone would give about 0.002. It doesn't tell us the scorecard is wrong. For that we'll check actual against predicted default rates by band once the first-payment data is in, in about eight weeks.”
    To a technical interviewer
    “Most losses are negative log-likelihoods: squared error assumes Gaussian noise, log loss is Bernoulli, cross-entropy is categorical. Minimising cross-entropy on data is minimising forward KL from the data to the model, which is mode-covering. Regularisation is a prior, so L2 is MAP with a Gaussian. In post-training the direction flips. On-policy distillation and the RLHF penalty use reverse KL, which is mode-seeking, and the active questions are which direction to use per token and how to estimate the KL gradient correctly. In monitoring, PSI is a symmetrised KL, so I read it against its sampling noise, not just the 0.1 and 0.25 conventions.”
    “Why not just train on accuracy, since that's what we report?”

    Accuracy is flat almost everywhere: nudging a prediction from 0.6 to 0.61 doesn't change it, so there's no gradient to follow. It also ignores confidence. Log loss is smooth, rewards honest probabilities, and is the likelihood of the data, so it gives the model a reason to be calibrated. We report accuracy-type metrics at a threshold afterwards, but we train on a likelihood.

    “PSI is 0.12. Do we need to rebuild the scorecard?”

    Not on that number alone. First, check it's above the noise floor for this sample size. Then look at which bands moved and whether there's a business reason, such as a campaign or a policy change. Then look at performance: are actual default rates by band still close to predicted, and is the Gini holding up? A population shift with stable performance usually means monitoring more closely or recalibrating, not rebuilding.

    “Isn't KL just a distance between distributions?”

    It measures a gap, but it isn't a distance. It's not symmetric, it doesn't satisfy the triangle inequality, and it can be infinite. The direction encodes a choice: KL(p ‖ q) punishes q for missing what p does; KL(q ‖ p) punishes q for doing what p doesn't. If you need something symmetric and bounded, Jensen–Shannon divergence is the usual choice; PSI gets symmetry by adding both directions.

    “What's the difference between distilling a model and fine-tuning it on the big model's answers?”

    Fine-tuning on the teacher's sampled answers only gives the student one token per position, the one the teacher happened to pick. Distillation gives it the teacher's whole probability distribution at each position, including which alternatives were nearly right, which is far more information per example. On-policy distillation goes further: the student writes, and the teacher grades every token of the student's own attempt, so the student learns to recover from its own mistakes.

    “Why does RLHF have a KL penalty, and why do some reasoning recipes drop it?”

    With a learned reward model, the policy will find outputs that score well but aren't actually good, and the KL penalty stops it wandering far from a sensible reference. With a verifiable reward, such as a maths answer that's checked exactly, there's less to hack, and long reasoning needs to move far from the starting model, so recipes like DAPO drop the term. For a bank assistant trained on a learned or human-judged reward, I'd keep it and monitor it.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones only need the formulas from Part 2.

    1. A segment has 6 defaults in 120 loans. What's the MLE of the default rate? And the MAP, with a prior worth 80 imaginary loans at 3%?

    MLE = 6 ÷ 120 = 5.0%. MAP = (6 + 0.03 × 80) ÷ (120 + 80) = 8.4 ÷ 200 = 4.2%. The prior pulls the estimate towards 3%, and pulls less as real loans accumulate.

    2. A fraud model gives 20% to a transaction that turns out to be fraud. What's its log loss? What if it had said 0.2%?

    −ln 0.2 ≈ 1.61 nats. −ln 0.002 ≈ 6.21 nats. For comparison, correctly giving a genuine transaction a 1% fraud probability costs −ln 0.99 ≈ 0.01. One confident miss at 0.2% outweighs about 600 confident correct calls.

    3. The truth is 50/50 between two tokens. The model says 90/10. Compute H(p), H(p, q), KL(p ‖ q) and KL(q ‖ p) in bits.

    H(p) = 1 bit. H(p, q) = −0.5 log₂ 0.9 − 0.5 log₂ 0.1 ≈ 0.076 + 1.661 = 1.74 bits. So KL(p ‖ q) ≈ 0.74 bits. KL(q ‖ p) = 0.9 log₂(0.9/0.5) + 0.1 log₂(0.1/0.5) ≈ 0.763 − 0.232 = 0.53 bits. Different, because KL isn't symmetric.

    4. A language model's validation loss is 1.8 nats per token. What's its perplexity, and what does that mean?

    e1.8 ≈ 6.0. On average, the model is as uncertain about the next token as if it were picking uniformly from about 6 options. It's only comparable with models using the same tokeniser on the same text.

    5. You train with NLL + λ‖w‖². What prior have you assumed?

    Each weight is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance τ² = 1/(2λ). Doubling λ halves the prior variance: a stronger belief that weights are small.

    6. A 150-applicant segment shows PSI 0.07 across 10 bands, against a development sample of 20,000. Is that evidence of drift?

    Hardly. The noise floor is about 9 × (1/150 + 1/20,000) ≈ 0.060. A PSI of 0.07 is close to what you'd expect with no change at all, and roughly one month in nine would cross 0.1 by chance alone. Look again next month, or pool several months, before acting.

    7. You're distilling a large Arabic-English model into a small one. Why might you prefer reverse KL to forward KL, and what's the risk?

    The small student can't represent everything the teacher can. Forward KL makes it cover every mode, so it spreads probability thinly, including onto incoherent mixtures. Reverse KL lets it commit to the teacher's main mode and do that well. The risk is lost diversity: it may drop valid alternatives, such as answering only in one dialect or one phrasing, and become overconfident. That's what 2026 work like entropy-aware on-policy distillation tries to fix.
