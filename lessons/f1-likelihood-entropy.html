    Likelihood, Entropy and the Language of Training

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A factory tests 200 light bulbs and 9 are faulty. Over the years, about 3% of its bulbs have been faulty. What faulty rate should you believe for this batch?

    Try each candidate and ask: if this were the true rate, how probable is the batch I saw? The rate that makes the data least surprising is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). Log loss, LLM training and a drift report are this one move in different clothes.

    • The peak sits at the observed rate, and more data narrows the curve.
    • The MLE overfits small samples. A prior pulls 0 faulty in 15 to a sensible 2.6%, because we already know bulbs sometimes fail.
    Hold onto thisLikelihood scores a model by how probable it makes the data you saw. The negative log of it is a loss you minimise. Squared error, log loss and cross-entropy are all negative log-likelihoods under some assumption about noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Likelihood is probability read backwards

    A probability fixes the recipe and asks what you will taste. A likelihood fixes the dish and asks which recipe fits. It is not the probability that a setting is true, and only ratios between settings mean anything.

    Why everyone takes the log

    Multiplying probabilities shrinks absurdly fast, so we take the log and flip the sign. The negative log-likelihood is the loss every training log prints.

    Every familiar loss is a negative log-likelihood

    Choosing a loss means choosing a belief about the noise. Take the negative log of that noise model and a familiar loss falls out: bell-curve noise gives squared error, yes/no gives log loss, and many classes give cross-entropy.

    Log loss is harsh on confident misses: predicting a 1% chance of rain on a day it rains costs about 4.6, and 0 costs infinity. That is why it only rewards honest, calibrated probabilities.

    What you assume about the outcomeThe loss it givesWhere you meet it
    Gaussian noiseSquared errorForecasting any number: steps, sales, house prices
    Bernoulli (yes/no)Log lossRain, spam and fraud flags; loan-default models
    Categorical (one of many)Cross-entropyCat-or-dog photo labels, every LLM

    Overfitting, priors and regularisation

    The MLE believes the data completely, so 0 faulty bulbs in 15 gives 0%. A prior adds a penalty for implausible values, giving the MAP estimate. L2 regularisation is a prior that weights are small, and a bigger penalty is a stronger belief. Banks meet the same problem with loan portfolios that almost never default, and pool outside data to compensate.

    Entropy: average surprise

    Surprise is minus the log of an outcome's probability, which is the per-example loss. Entropy is the average surprise, so high entropy means hard to predict. A fair coin has 1 bit, a certain outcome 0, and a high-entropy next-token guess is a cheap signal to route to a human.

    Cross-entropy and KL: the price of the wrong distribution

    Acting on the wrong distribution q costs cross-entropy: the data's own entropy plus the KL divergence, which is the model's fault. Maximising likelihood is minimising KL. KL is not symmetric: forward KL punishes missing anything that happens, so it covers every mode. Reverse KL punishes putting mass where the truth has little, so it commits to one mode.

    Perplexity is exponentiated cross-entropy, read as "choosing among this many tokens". Compare only with the same tokeniser and text.

    How LLMs are trained, distilled and kept on a leash

    Pretraining is cross-entropy on the next token, so the model learns to cover everything people write, including what you would rather it didn't (rudeness, say). Distillation trains a small student on a teacher's full distribution. RLHF adds a KL penalty to a reference model so reward-chasing cannot drift too far. For a bank, that KL also measures how much general behaviour fine-tuning has disturbed.

    PSI: KL divergence in a credit-risk suit

    Banks use KL divergence to ask: are today's applicants still like the ones the model was built on? The population stability index (PSI) is KL divergence in both directions, comparing this month's score bands (ten equal slices of the model's score) with the development sample (the data the model was built on). Read it as a traffic light: below 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 investigate, above 0.25 a major shift. Noise alone gives about 0.06 with 10 bands and 150 applicants, and these cut-offs are convention, not regulation.

    PSI watches who applies, not whether the model still works, so pair it with outcome checks.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Trusting the MLE on a small sample

      A new batch shows a 0% faulty rate (or a new loan segment shows 0% defaults).

      Spot itAsk for the counts and the prior used.
    • One confident miss dominating log loss

      Someone quietly clips probabilities to hide it.

      Spot itLook at the largest per-example losses.
    • PSI over-trusted

      Noise triggers a redevelopment, or a stable mix hides rising defaults.

      Spot itAsk for sample size and defaults by band.
    • Post-training with no KL tracking

      A fine-tuned assistant gains reward but forgets Arabic or refusals.

      Spot itAsk for KL to the base model over training.
    • Numbers that don't add up

      Perplexity compared across tokenisers, or KL treated as symmetric.

      Spot itAsk which direction, log base and test text.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. How does the MAP estimate differ from the MLE?

    MAP blends in a prior, so it sits closer to the prior until real data outweighs it.

    2. Why does predicting 0.2% rain on a day it rains cost far more than predicting 20% under log loss?

    Log loss has no ceiling, so one confident miss outweighs hundreds of correct calls.

    3. Which of entropy, cross-entropy and KL belongs to the model?

    Entropy is the truth's own noise; the KL gap on top of it is the model's fault, and its direction matters.

    4. A 150-applicant segment shows PSI 0.07. Is that drift?

    Hardly, as noise alone gives about 0.06 at that size.

    5. Why prefer reverse KL when distilling into a small model?

    It lets the student commit to the teacher's main mode, at the risk of lost diversity.
