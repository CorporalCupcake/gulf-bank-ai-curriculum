    Likelihood, Entropy and the Language of Training

    Why nearly every loss function is the same idea in disguise, and how the same few lines of maths train a language model, shrink it into a smaller one, and tell a credit team its applicants have changed.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceTraining a model means choosing the settings that make the data you actually saw as unsurprising as possible. Likelihood measures that, cross-entropy is the same measure averaged per example, and KL divergence is the extra surprise you pay for believing the wrong distribution.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a taster guessing a recipe's salt level: the right guess makes the dish they actually tasted least surprising. Data works the same way. A product manager hands you 200 salary-transfer loans, 9 of which defaulted within a year. The bank's long-run default rate is about 3%. Which rate should you use?

    Try each candidate (3%, 4%, 5%, 10%) and ask: if this were the true rate, how probable is the book I saw? The rate that makes the data least surprising is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). That is all of F1.6. Log loss on a fraud model, LLM training and a monthly drift report are this one move in different clothes.

    • The peak sits at the observed rate. 9 ÷ 200 = 4.5%. The 3% long-run rate makes this book about half as likely as 4.5% does. That is evidence the segment is riskier, not proof.
    • More data narrows the curve; its width is your uncertainty. At 200 loans, roughly 2.2% to 8% is plausible. At 2,000 loans the same 4.5% is pinned to about 3.6% to 5.5%.
    • The MLE overfits small samples. 0 defaults in 15 loans gives 0%, like predicting a perfect exam average from one perfect quiz. Adding a prior (the bank's experience, written as imaginary past loans) pulls it to a sensible 2.6%. That is what regularisation does to a model.
    Hold onto thisLikelihood scores a model by how probable it makes the data you saw. The negative log of it is a loss you minimise. Squared error, log loss and cross-entropy are all negative log-likelihoods under some assumption about the noise.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Likelihood is probability read backwards

    A probability fixes the recipe and asks what you will taste. A likelihood fixes the dish you already tasted and asks which recipe fits. The data is fixed; you vary the parameters. For k defaults among n independent loans at default rate p:

    L(p)=pk(1−p)n−k

    How probable this exact set of defaults and non-defaults is if the rate were p. (The count of arrangements is dropped: it doesn't depend on p, so it can't change which p wins.)

    A likelihood is not a probability of the parameter. "The likelihood of 3% is 0.51" means 3% makes the data 0.51 times as probable as 4.5% does, not that there is a 51% chance the rate is 3%. That needs a prior and Bayes' rule (MAP, below). Only ratios mean anything, which is why the chart scales the peak to 1.

    Why everyone takes the log

    A likelihood multiplies one probability per data point, so it shrinks fast, like multiplying 200 small fractions on a calculator. The 9-in-200 book has about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ at the best rate, 2,000 loans give around 10⁻¹⁶⁰, and a million transactions would underflow any computer. Logs turn the product into a sum:

    ℓ(p)=klnp+(n−k)ln(1−p)⇒p^=kn

    The log-likelihood. Set its slope to zero and you get the observed rate. The log is increasing, so the peak doesn't move, but the computing becomes stable.

    Logs also give you:

    • Simple gradients, so gradient descent works (F1.3).
    • Averages: a sum divided by n can be trained on mini-batches.
    • A near-parabolic peak whose curvature gives the standard error. That is the "within 1.92 of the peak" range: 1.92 is half of 3.84, the 95% point of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom.

    Then flip the sign. Optimisers minimise, so we minimise the negative log-likelihood (NLL). Divided by the number of examples, it is the average loss every training log prints. Lower is better; zero would mean every observed outcome got probability 1.

    Every familiar loss is a negative log-likelihood

    Choosing a loss is choosing what you believe about the noise. Write a probability model for one outcome, take −log, and a familiar loss falls out.

    −lnN(y∣y^,σ)=(y−y^)22σ2+ln(σ2π)

    Gaussian noise gives squared error. If actual values scatter around the prediction in a bell curve of fixed width σ, the NLL is squared error times a constant, plus a constant. Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.

    −lnP(y∣p) =−[ylnp+(1−y)ln(1−p)]

    A coin flip gives log loss. For a yes/no outcome (fraud, default) with predicted probability p, the NLL is binary cross-entropy, or log loss. Logistic regression is maximum likelihood with this loss.

    −lnqy=−∑ipilnqi

    A many-sided die gives cross-entropy. With a one-hot label p (1 on the right class), the sum keeps one term: minus the log of the probability the model gave the right class. For routing a complaint, or picking the next token, that is categorical cross-entropy, the loss that trains every LLM.

    Fraud mode shows what makes log loss special. Predict 1% on a transaction that turns out to be fraud and the loss is −ln 0.01 ≈ 4.6. Predict 0.2% and it is 6.2. Predict exactly 0 and it is infinite. One confident miss costs as much as hundreds of confident correct calls, like a quiz that docks far more for a confident wrong answer than a hesitant one. That harshness makes log loss a proper scoring rule: on average you minimise it only by reporting your true belief, so it trains calibrated probabilities (F7.4). Squared error on probabilities (the Brier score) is also proper but gentler on confident mistakes.

    Change the loss when the noise isn't Gaussian. AED fraud losses are heavy-tailed, and squared error lets a few huge cases drag every prediction. Assume Laplace noise and you get absolute error. Model the log of the amount and you assume multiplicative noise. Use a quantile loss and you fit a percentile. Each is a likelihood choice.

    What you assume about the outcomeThe loss it givesWhere you meet it in a bank
    Gaussian noise, fixed spreadSquared error (MSE)Balance and cash-demand forecasts, linear regression
    Laplace noise (heavier tails)Absolute error (MAE), predicts the medianLoss amounts, call-centre handling times
    Bernoulli (yes/no)Log loss, binary cross-entropyPD models, fraud scores, churn, logistic regression
    Categorical (one of many)Cross-entropyComplaint routing, document classification, every LLM's next-token loss
    Poisson countsPoisson devianceNumber of claims, transactions or disputes per month

    Overfitting, priors and regularisation

    The MLE believes the data completely: 0 defaults in 15 loans gives 0%. A million-parameter model on ten thousand examples will find settings that make even the noise near-certain. That is overfitting, seen from the likelihood side.

    The fix is to add what you knew before the data arrived. Bayes' rule (F1.4) says posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Take logs and the most probable value, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, maximises a sum:

    θMAP=argmaxθ[lnL(θ)+lnπ(θ)]

    Fit to the data, plus a penalty for values the prior finds implausible. In Simulation 1 the prior is s imaginary loans at 3%, so MAP = (k + 0.03s) ÷ (n + s). With 100 imaginary loans and 9 of 200 real ones: 12 ÷ 300 = 4.0%.

    Put a Gaussian prior centred on zero, with spread τ, on every weight. Its negative log is the squared weight size over 2τ², plus a constant. So:

    NLL+λ‖w‖2⟺MAP withw∼N(0,τ2) λ=12τ2

    L2 regularisation (ridge, weight decay) is a Gaussian prior. Bigger λ means a tighter prior: you believe more strongly that weights are small. L1 (lasso) is a Laplace prior; its sharp peak at zero is why it sets some weights exactly to zero.

    Low-default portfolios are the credit version. Sovereign, bank and large-corporate books in the Gulf may see no defaults for years, so the MLE says 0%, which regulators reject. Banks use conservative estimators such as Pluto and Tasche's "most prudent estimation", pool external data, or set explicit priors. Same principle as MAP: don't let a small sample speak alone.

    Label smoothing trains against a slightly softened label (90% Fraud, the rest spread over other classes) instead of a one-hot one. That stops the model pushing its logits towards infinity to chase zero loss, and improves calibration. The catch (Müller, Kornblith and Hinton, 2019): smoothed teachers distil worse, because smoothing erases which wrong classes are nearly right.

    Entropy: average surprise

    The surprise of an outcome with probability p is −log p. A certain event has surprise 0, heads on a coin has 1 bit (log base 2), a 1-in-1,000 event about 10 bits. This is the per-example NLL from above: how surprised the model was.

    Entropy is the average surprise when outcomes really follow p:

    H(p)=−∑ipilog2pi

    Bits with log base 2, nats with natural logs (1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits). A fair coin has 1 bit, four equally likely outcomes 2 bits, all mass on one outcome 0. High entropy means hard to predict.

    Shannon (1948) showed entropy is the minimum average bits per symbol any code can achieve. So compression and prediction are the same problem: a model that predicts the next token well compresses text well.

    Where you meet it: decision trees split by information gain, the drop in entropy of the default label. High entropy in an LLM's next-token distribution on a balance question is a cheap signal to route to a human. "Entropy collapse" in LLM reinforcement learning is the model becoming too certain too early and ceasing to explore.

    Cross-entropy and KL: the price of the wrong distribution

    Say outcomes follow p but you act as if they follow q, like packing for Dubai using London's forecast. Your average surprise is the cross-entropy. The extra over the unavoidable minimum is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:

    H(p,q)=−∑ipilogqi =H(p)⏟unavoidable+KL(p‖q)⏟your model's fault KL(p‖q)=∑ipilogpiqi≥0

    KL is the extra bits wasted per outcome by believing q. It is zero only when q = p, large when q gives low probability to things p says are common, and infinite if q calls impossible something that happens.

    Averaged NLL over a large training set estimates the cross-entropy between data and model. The data's own entropy is fixed, so maximising likelihood is minimising KL(data ‖ model). Every model trained with log loss quietly minimises a KL in this one direction.

    KL is not symmetric, so direction matters. Think of a shop deciding what to stock:

    • Forward KL(p ‖ q) averages over what p says happens, so it punishes q for missing things. It is mode-covering: stock a bit of everything, even odd mixtures in between.
    • Reverse KL(q ‖ p) averages over what q does, so it punishes q for putting mass where p has little. It is mode-seeking: pick one good answer and commit, ignoring other valid ones.

    Neither is right in general. Simulation 3 lets you feel the difference.

    Perplexity, the number LLM papers quote, is cross-entropy exponentiated:

    Perplexity=eH(p,q)

    With cross-entropy in nats per token. Read it as "as uncertain as choosing uniformly among this many tokens". A validation loss of 2.0 nats is a perplexity of about 7.4. Lower is better, but only comparable between models with the same tokeniser and test text.

    How LLMs are trained, distilled and kept on a leash

    Pretraining is cross-entropy. The model outputs a distribution over its vocabulary for the next token. The loss is −log of the probability it gave the token that actually came next, averaged over trillions of tokens. That is forward KL against human text, so it learns to cover everything people write, including what you'd rather it didn't. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) uses the same loss on curated examples.

    Distillation matches a teacher's distribution. A big teacher knows "card" is likely, "account" plausible and "salary" rare, like a tutor who shows which wrong answers are nearly right. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) trained a small student on the teacher's full distribution, softened with a temperature, instead of one-hot labels. The loss is forward KL(teacher ‖ student) plus a constant. That "dark knowledge" is why label-smoothed teachers distil worse.

    Classic distillation runs on fixed text, so the student never sees its own mistakes. On-policy distillation fixes that: the student writes, and the teacher scores every token. The usual loss is reverse KL(student ‖ teacher) per token, which suits a small student that can't cover everything and does better committing to the teacher's main mode. Qwen3's small models were trained this way, and in late 2026 it is among the most active post-training techniques.

    RLHF and its relatives use KL as a leash. A model tuned to maximise a reward (human preference, or "passed the checker") will game it and degrade everything else. The standard defence is a KL penalty to a reference model, usually the SFT model:

    maxθE[r(x,y)]−βKL(πθ‖πref)

    Get reward, but pay β per bit of divergence from the reference. Small β lets the model change more and risks reward hacking. Large β is safe but teaches little. The trained model comes first, so this is reverse KL.

    DPO (direct preference optimisation, 2023) solves this same objective in closed form and becomes a simple classification loss on answer pairs. There is no reward model and no explicit KL term, but the leash is implicit in the log-ratio to the reference, controlled by β.

    For a bank, the leash is the practical point. If you fine-tune an open-weight model on complaint data or policy documents, the KL to the base model is a measurable proxy for how much general behaviour you have disturbed. Recent work (Shenfeld, Pari and Agrawal, 2025) finds forgetting tracks that KL closely.

    PSI: KL divergence in a credit-risk suit

    Every Gulf bank scorecard gets a monitoring report, and one of its first numbers is the population stability index (PSI). It asks: are this month's applicants spread across score bands like the development sample? Split development scores into bands (usually deciles), each with share ei (often 10%). Take this month's share ai per band. Then:

    PSI=∑i(ai−ei)lnaiei =KL(a‖e)+KL(e‖a)

    PSI is KL in both directions added together (the Jeffreys divergence), so it is symmetric where KL isn't. A band moving from 10% to 15% contributes 0.05 × ln 1.5 ≈ 0.020.

    Read it like a traffic light: below 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 a moderate shift worth investigating, above 0.25 a major shift that often triggers a redevelopment discussion. These cut-offs are convention, not regulation, with no statistical derivation. Yurdakul and Naranjo showed that with no real change, noise alone gives an expected PSI of about (B − 1)(1/n + 1/N), where B is the number of bands and n, N the two sample sizes. With 10 bands and 150 applicants, that is already about 0.06.

    A scorecard's information value (IV) is the same formula, between the distributions of goods and bads across a variable's bins (B4.2). PSI asks "has the population moved?". IV asks "does this variable separate good from bad?".

    PSI compares who applies, not whether the model still works. A recession can raise defaults at every score while the score distribution barely moves: PSI stays green and calibration falls apart. Shifts within a band are invisible too. Treat PSI as a smoke alarm for the input population, and pair it with outcome checks (Gini, calibration by band, F7.7) once labels arrive.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Trusting the MLE on a small sample

      "The new segment has a 0% default rate", or a 1.2% PD from 83 loans with 1 default, used straight in pricing.

      Spot itAsk for the counts and an interval. If n is small, ask what prior, pooling or prudent estimate was used.
    • A loss that doesn't match the noise

      Squared error on heavy-tailed AED loss amounts lets a few large frauds dominate. The model looks fine on average and wrong for typical cases.

      Spot itPlot the residuals. Long tails, or spread growing with the prediction, mean the Gaussian assumption is wrong.
    • One confident miss dominating the log loss

      A model outputs 0.0001 on a few transactions that turn out to be fraud. Average log loss jumps, and someone quietly clips probabilities at 1%.

      Spot itLook at the largest per-example losses, not just the mean. Ask whether probabilities are clipped, and where.
    • Comparing perplexity across tokenisers or test sets

      An Arabic model with a larger vocabulary has fewer, more informative tokens, so its per-token perplexity isn't comparable with an English-centric model's.

      Spot itAsk for bits per byte or per character on the same text, or a task-level evaluation.
    • Treating KL as a distance

      "The KL between the two months is 0.3, so they're 0.3 apart either way." KL isn't symmetric, and is infinite if one distribution has zero where the other doesn't.

      Spot itAsk which direction was computed and how empty bins were handled. If the answer is "a tiny epsilon", ask how the result changes with a different one.
    • PSI read without sample size

      A 150-customer segment shows PSI 0.08 and a redevelopment is scoped, mostly on noise. A 50,000-customer portfolio shows 0.06 from a real, material shift that nobody investigates.

      Spot itCompute the noise floor, about (bands − 1) × (1/n + 1/N), or use a chi-squared critical value. Then ask what moved, band by band.
    • Green PSI, broken model

      The applicant mix is stable but default rates have risen at every score because the economy changed. PSI can't see this.

      Spot itAsk for actual versus predicted default rate by band, and Gini over time, alongside PSI.
    • A post-training run with no KL tracking

      A fine-tuned complaint assistant scores higher on the internal reward but answers Arabic messages in English, or forgot how to refuse.

      Spot itAsk for the KL to the base model over training, and general and safety prompts evaluated before and after.
    • AI-generated analysis that doesn't add up

      Signs: "cross-entropy of 0.05, so the model is 95% accurate"; PSI computed with percentages (10, 15) instead of fractions (0.10, 0.15), which multiplies it by 100; log base 10 in one place and natural logs in another; "KL is symmetric".

      Spot itRecompute one band's PSI contribution by hand. It takes 30 seconds and catches most of these.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A segment has 6 defaults in 120 loans. What's the MLE of the default rate? And the MAP, with a prior worth 80 imaginary loans at 3%?

    MLE = 6 ÷ 120 = 5.0%. MAP = (6 + 0.03 × 80) ÷ (120 + 80) = 8.4 ÷ 200 = 4.2%. The prior pulls towards 3%, and less as real loans accumulate.

    2. A fraud model gives 20% to a transaction that turns out to be fraud. What's its log loss? What if it had said 0.2%?

    −ln 0.2 ≈ 1.61 nats. −ln 0.002 ≈ 6.21 nats. Correctly giving a genuine transaction 1% fraud probability costs only −ln 0.99 ≈ 0.01, so one confident miss at 0.2% outweighs about 600 confident correct calls.

    3. The truth is 50/50 between two tokens. The model says 90/10. Compute H(p), H(p, q), KL(p ‖ q) and KL(q ‖ p) in bits.

    H(p) = 1 bit. H(p, q) = −0.5 log₂ 0.9 − 0.5 log₂ 0.1 ≈ 0.076 + 1.661 = 1.74 bits. So KL(p ‖ q) ≈ 0.74 bits. KL(q ‖ p) = 0.9 log₂(0.9/0.5) + 0.1 log₂(0.1/0.5) ≈ 0.763 − 0.232 = 0.53 bits. They differ because KL isn't symmetric.

    4. A language model's validation loss is 1.8 nats per token. What's its perplexity, and what does that mean?

    e1.8 ≈ 6.0. The model is as uncertain about the next token as if picking uniformly from about 6 options. Comparable only with models using the same tokeniser on the same text.

    5. You train with NLL + λ‖w‖². What prior have you assumed?

    Each weight is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance τ² = 1/(2λ). Doubling λ halves the prior variance: a stronger belief that weights are small.

    6. A 150-applicant segment shows PSI 0.07 across 10 bands, against a development sample of 20,000. Is that evidence of drift?

    Hardly. The noise floor is about 9 × (1/150 + 1/20,000) ≈ 0.060, so 0.07 is close to what no change at all would give, and roughly one month in nine would cross 0.1 by chance. Wait a month, or pool several, before acting.

    7. You're distilling a large Arabic-English model into a small one. Why might you prefer reverse KL to forward KL, and what's the risk?

    The small student can't represent everything the teacher can. Forward KL forces it to cover every mode, spreading probability thinly onto incoherent mixtures. Reverse KL lets it commit to the teacher's main mode and do that well. The risk is lost diversity: it may drop valid alternatives, such as one dialect or phrasing, and become overconfident. Entropy-aware on-policy distillation (2026) targets this.
