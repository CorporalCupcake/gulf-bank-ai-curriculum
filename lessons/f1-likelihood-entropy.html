    Likelihood and Entropy: The Maths Behind Training

    Part 1

    What likelihood is

    In one sentenceLikelihood is how probable your data would be, if a particular setting were true. Training a model means hunting for the setting that makes the data you saw least surprising.

    Toss a coin 10 times and get 8 heads. Is the coin fair? Guess a heads rate, then ask: "if this were the rate, how likely is my result?"

    The tallest point is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE): here 0.8, the rate that makes 8 heads in 10 least surprising. It is simply the observed share. It explains the data about seven times better than a fair coin.

    How it differs from probability

    • Probability fixes the setting and asks what data you will see. "A fair coin: how likely are 8 heads?"
    • Likelihood fixes the data and asks which setting fits. "I saw 8 heads: how well does each rate explain it?"
    • It is not the chance a setting is true. The curve does not add up to 1. Only comparisons count: 0.30 against 0.04.
    Part 2

    Why training uses the log

    Thousands of examples mean multiplying thousands of probabilities, which gets absurdly tiny. So we take the log, turning the product into a sum, and flip the sign so "good" means "low".

    In one sentenceThe negative log-likelihood is a loss: the surprise your model felt at each example, added up. Training is making it small.

    The surprise at one example is minus the log of the probability the model gave to what actually happened.

    Every familiar loss is this in disguise

    Pick a belief about how the outcome is noisy, take the negative log, and a loss you already know falls out.

    If the outcome is…The loss isWhere you meet it
    A number with bell-curve noiseSquared errorPredicting house prices, sales
    Yes or noLog lossSpam, fraud and default models
    One of many categoriesCross-entropyImage labels, every language model

    So choosing a loss is choosing a belief about the noise.

    Part 3

    Entropy, cross-entropy and KL

    In one sentenceEntropy is the average surprise of an outcome, so it measures how hard something is to predict. A certain outcome has 0. A fair coin has 1 bit, the most a coin can have.
    • Entropy: the noise in the truth itself. A 90/10 coin has 0.47 bits, and no model can beat that.
    • Cross-entropy: the average surprise when you use the model's probabilities on real data. Always at least the entropy. This is the training loss.
    • KL divergence: cross-entropy minus entropy. It is the model's own fault, the price of believing the wrong probabilities.

    Minimising cross-entropy and minimising KL are the same job, because the entropy part cannot change. KL is not symmetric: swap the two distributions and you get a different number.

    How to read the numbers

    • Loss near 0 means the model gave high probability to what happened. 0.69 is a coin flip on a yes/no question.
    • One confident miss dominates: calling 1% rain on a day it pours costs 4.6, about 44 times a 90% hit. Check the worst losses, not just the average.
    • Bits or nats: bits use log base 2, nats the natural log, which training code usually reports. Bits × 0.693 = nats.
    Show the formulas

    Surprise of an outcome with probability p = −log p. Entropy = the average surprise = −Σ p log p.

    Cross-entropy = −Σ p log q, where p is the truth and q the model. KL = cross-entropy − entropy.

    Part 4

    Which measure to use

    MeasureAnswersUse it for
    Likelihood / MLEWhich setting fits the data best?Fitting any model's parameters
    Log loss / cross-entropyHow surprised was the model, on average?Training and scoring classifiers and language models
    KL divergenceHow far is one distribution from another?Drift checks, keeping a fine-tuned model near its base
    • Strong at: one idea explains most training losses and rewards honest probabilities.
    • Weak at: trusting small samples. With 0 faulty bulbs in 15, the MLE says a 0% fault rate. A prior belief ("bulbs usually fail about 3% of the time") pulls it back to something sensible.

    In banking, a drift measure called the population stability index is KL divergence between this month's applicants and the ones the model was built on, and loan-default models are trained on log loss.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Reading likelihood as the chance a setting is true

      It is how well a setting explains the data. Only ratios between settings carry meaning.

    • Trusting the MLE on tiny samples

      Zero events in a small sample gives a rate of exactly 0. Ask how many cases there were, and whether prior knowledge was used.

    • Hiding confident misses

      Clipping probabilities to tidy log loss hides the mistakes that matter.

    • Mixing up units and directions

      Bits and nats differ by a factor of 0.693, and KL is not symmetric. State which you used.

    1. What is the difference between probability and likelihood?

    Probability fixes the setting and asks about the data. Likelihood fixes the data and asks how well each setting explains it.

    2. Why is a 1% rain forecast on a rainy day so costly under log loss?

    Surprise is minus the log of the chance given to what happened. At 1% that is 4.6, and it grows without limit as the chance nears 0.

    3. Which of entropy, cross-entropy and KL is the model's own fault?

    KL. Entropy is the noise in the truth, and cross-entropy is entropy plus KL.

    4. A model gives 50/50 on a coin that is really 90/10. Is its loss above or below 0.47 bits?

    Above. 0.47 is the floor set by the truth's entropy, and the 50/50 model scores 1.0 bit.
