    Does the Model Remember? Memorisation, Membership Inference, Private Training and Data Provenance

    How models store pieces of their training data, how attackers test for it, what differential privacy guarantees, and why tracking data provenance is the cheapest defence.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModels store some training data, mostly the rare and repeated; attackers can detect or extract it; differential privacy guards against every attacker but costs accuracy; most practical protection comes from deduplication and provenance.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank fine-tunes a model on customer chats. One customer once typed their IBAN into a chat, and later the model completes a similar opening with that IBAN. Nobody attacked; the model remembered.

    Fitting an example leaves a trace of it, like a path worn across a lawn. Rare or repeated records leave the sharpest traces.

    Three questions follow. Memorisation: does the model reproduce training text? Membership inference: can an attacker tell a record was in training? Private training: can we limit what any attacker learns about one record? Upstream sits provenance: do we know what went in?

    Hold onto thisRemembering is what fitting does, and it concentrates on rare, repeated records. Protect by showing fewer copies, allowing fewer passes and, where stakes justify the cost, adding a formal guarantee.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Memorisation: verbatim, repeated and rare

    Generalising is learning a pattern ("UAE IBANs start with AE"). Memorising is storing one specific example ("this IBAN"). The two blur. A rare kind of customer appears too seldom for the model to learn a pattern, so it keeps the single example it saw. Some memorisation is therefore the price of accuracy on rare cases.

    For language models the working definition is operational. A string is extractable if some prompt makes the model emit it. The standard test feeds the first k tokens (the prefix) of a training sequence and checks whether greedy decoding, always taking the likeliest next token, reproduces the true continuation exactly. Three things recur: more copies mean more extraction, steeply; bigger models memorise more at the same copy count; longer prefixes make extraction easier.

    It clusters in boilerplate and repeated personal details, which a bank has in bulk: statement templates, email footers, a branch address on thousands of tickets.

    Many epochs over a small private set raise memorisation, and good validation loss says nothing about stored strings. Extraction works on deployed models too: in 2023 researchers made a production chat model emit personal training text for a few hundred dollars.

    The practical test is a canary, like a dye marker in a water supply. Plant fake but realistic secrets (a made-up account number) in the fine-tuning data at known repetition counts, say 1, 4 and 16. Then measure how much likelier the model finds those exact strings than random alternatives.

    Membership inference: was this record in training?

    A model is more at ease with data it has seen, so its loss on it is lower. The simplest attack calls "member" below a loss cut (Simulation 1).

    AUC 0.5 is guessing and 0.56 looks harmless, but an attack right for 1 in 100 members and almost never wrong is a real breach. Judge by the true positive rate at 0.1% and 1% false positives.

    Some records have low loss simply because they are easy, such as a short common sentence. Stronger attacks therefore calibrate per record, comparing each loss with what it would be had the record never been seen. LiRA does this with "shadow" models trained with and without the target; cheaper versions use reference models. They are much stronger but cost many trained models.

    For language models pretrained once on web data, most membership attacks barely beat guessing. The data is seen about once, and a near-copy of a member scores almost the same. Many published wins just detected older text, because members and non-members came from different periods. Extraction of repeated strings is the better-evidenced risk there. For small fine-tunes run for several epochs, and for classical models such as credit scorers, membership inference is real.

    Bank cases to watch: a scorer queried by partners (confidence values act as loss signals, so return a decision or rounded score), a fine-tuned assistant (confirming a named customer's messages were in training reveals they contacted the bank), and weights shared with a vendor, which leak far more than an API.

    Private training: what differential privacy promises

    Differential privacy (DP) bounds all attacks at once, instead of testing them one by one. Picture two worlds, with and without your record in training; DP says the trained model is almost equally likely in both. The number ε is the multiplier on how much any outcome can shift: small means your record barely matters, and each +1 multiplies the allowed advantage by about 2.7. The number δ is a tiny chance the guarantee fails; keep it far below one over the number of records.

    For an attacker the useful form is: true positive rate ≤ eε × false positive rate (plus δ). At ε = 2 and 1% false alarms, no attack catches more than about 7% of members. By ε = 8 the factor is nearly 3,000, the cap is 100% and it says almost nothing.

    DP-SGD is how this is done in deep learning. It changes ordinary gradient descent in two ways. First, clip: cap each example's gradient length so no record can push the weights by more than a fixed amount. Second, add noise: sum the clipped gradients and add random noise sized to that cap, which hides whether any one record was there, like a crowd muffling one voice. An accountant then adds up the privacy cost over every step, turning noise level, sampling rate, step count and δ into a final ε. More steps or less noise means a bigger ε, so privacy is a budget spent over training.

    Accuracy suffers for two reasons: clipping biases the gradients, and the noise must be large enough to hide one record. Larger batches dilute it, so DP training wants very large batches and heavy compute. It hits rare cases hardest, which are exactly the ones that need memory, so sub-group fairness can worsen.

    ε is a cap on what is possible, not a measurement of what leaks. Real attacks usually fall well below it. No ε is universally safe: 1 to 2 is strong, 8 to 10 is common in deployments. A quoted ε means little until you know the privacy unit (ideally one customer, but in LLM training often one token sequence, which protects far less), δ and the data covered.

    VaultGemma (Google, 2025), a 1-billion-parameter model pretrained at ε ≤ 2, showed no detectable memorisation on prefixes of random training sequences, unlike similar non-private models. The cost was quality like non-private models from several years earlier. Its guarantee is per sequence, not per person.

    Cheaper partial defences, with no formal guarantee: fewer epochs, early stopping and weight decay, deduplication, scrubbing identifiers, rounding or withholding scores, and output filters for known identifiers. They reduce observed leakage and are worth doing first.

    Data provenance and deduplication

    Provenance is a food label for data: where each piece came from and what was done to it. It answers questions that otherwise stall a project. May we use this? Was the test set in training? Which models held this customer's data if they ask for deletion? Removing a record's influence from trained weights is hard ("machine unlearning"), so the realistic answer is excluding it from the next version or retraining, which needs the record-level trail.

    It also settles licences (audits of public text datasets found licences often missing or misattributed) and contamination (nobody can prove the test set was absent from training without it).

    Per record, store a stable content hash, the source system, collection date and purpose, legal basis, jurisdiction, licence for third-party text, the ordered list of transformations, a near-duplicate cluster id, the fixed split, and the model versions that trained on it. Assign these at first pull and carry them through every step. A test: pick a random customer and ask which models saw their data. If nobody can answer in minutes, the trail is decorative.

    Deduplication has three levels, each catching what the last misses. Exact: hash the normalised text and keep one; this catches pasted rows, forwarded emails and repeated footers. Near-duplicate: catches documents differing by a date, name or signature. Break each into overlapping word groups (shingles), compress them into a MinHash signature of a few hundred numbers that estimates how much two documents overlap, then use locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to find candidate pairs without comparing all pairs. Published work found this cut memorised output roughly tenfold. Semantic: catches paraphrases using embeddings, but can remove useful variety.

    The signature is split into b bands of r numbers each. Two documents become a candidate pair if they match on every number in any one band. More rows per band is stricter, like needing every question in a quiz section right; more bands is more forgiving, like more chances to pass. Candidates are verified exactly, so false ones cost compute, not correctness. Simulation 4 shows the trade-off.

    Deduplicate across splits, not just within, or test scores reflect memory. Keep cluster size as a column, so collapsing identical requests does not hide how common they are.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at:

    • Deduplication and fewer epochs cut extraction cheaply. Risk rises steeply with copies, so removing repeats attacks the cause directly and often improves quality too.
    • Canaries and low-FPR membership tests test your actual pipeline and give a number for the model-risk file.
    • Differential privacy is the only approach whose guarantee holds against every attacker, including ones not yet invented, because it limits what any single record can change.
    • Provenance turns deletion, licence, contamination and incident questions into queries. Model engineering cannot answer them afterwards.

    Weak at, or don't use when:

    • DP-SGD is the wrong tool for small data, small models you need accurate on rare cases, or tight compute. Noise swamps signal and the rare segments suffer most.
    • Random-sample memorisation checks measure the average, not the worst case. They miss the one rare string a targeted attacker goes after.
    • Scrubbing and dedup give no guarantee. Quasi-identifiers (address, rare job title, amount plus date) combine to identify.

    In practice. Start with provenance, exact and near-duplicate dedup (across splits), and the fewest epochs that reach your accuracy target. Then plant canaries, run extraction prompts built from real prefixes, and report attacks as TPR at 0.1% FPR. Add DP-SGD only where the stakes justify the accuracy and compute bill; expect big batches and a noticeable quality drop at ε around 2, much less around 8.

    In banking: a customer-service assistant fine-tuned on chats needs dedup, scrubbing and canaries; scorers exposed to partners need limits on what scores leave the building; data-protection requests and model-risk audits need provenance.

    Private training is production-ready for narrow models and for research-scale pretraining, as VaultGemma shows. For routine LLM fine-tuning it is usually too costly, so the first layer plus access control is the norm. Cheaper DP training that holds accuracy on rare cases would change that.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • "We removed the names, so it can't leak them"

      Identifiers hide in free text, and models memorise repeats.

      Spot itRun extraction prompts with real prefixes and plant canaries.
    • Judging membership inference by AUC

      A low AUC can hide a few members caught with near certainty.

      Spot itAsk for TPR at 0.1% and 1% FPR.
    • An ε quoted with no context

      Of what unit, with what δ, covering which data?

      Spot itAsk for the privacy unit and δ.
    • "Zero memorisation" from random sampling

      Random sequences miss the rare record an attacker targets.

      Spot itAsk whether targeted prefixes for known sensitive strings were used.
    • Members and non-members from different times

      The attack detects the date, not membership, and inflates results.

      Spot itCheck non-members were held out at random from the same period and source.
    • Fine-tuning a DP-pretrained model non-privately

      The base guarantee covers only its own data, not yours.

      Spot itAsk which stages used DP.
    • Deduplicating within training only, or too hard

      Test near-copies in training make memory look like skill; aggressive dedup removes rare cases the model needs.

      Spot itRun near-duplicate checks between splits and compare rare-segment accuracy.
    • Provenance as a one-off spreadsheet

      Columns exist, but nothing links them to training files or model versions.

      Spot itPick a random customer and ask which model versions trained on their data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. An attack has AUC 0.56 and the team calls it harmless. What do you ask for next?

    The true positive rate at 0.1% false positives. AUC averages over thresholds and can hide a few members caught with near certainty.

    2. Does a small ε measure how much a model leaked?

    No. It is a cap on what any attack could achieve; real attacks fall below it.

    3. Random sampling finds no leakage, yet a security team extracts an IBAN with a targeted prompt. Contradiction?

    No. Random samples mostly test common content, while one rare string can still be recoverable.

    4. VaultGemma reports no detectable memorisation at ε ≤ 2. What do you check before saying it cannot leak customer data?

    The privacy unit (sequences, not people), the data covered (not your fine-tuning) and whether targeted prompts were tested.

    5. Why is a deletion request hard to answer even before "unlearning" comes up?

    Without record-level provenance you cannot say which datasets and model versions included the person's data.

    6. A fine-tuned support assistant leaks signature blocks. Dedup, DP-SGD at ε = 2, or output filtering: which first, and why?

    Dedup first. Signature blocks repeat, and repetition drives extraction, so removing copies is cheap and attacks the cause. DP-SGD costs accuracy and compute, so keep it for data where a guarantee is needed.

    7. A partner wants your credit scorer's full probabilities, and offers a cheaper integration if you send the weights. Which do you choose and why?

    Neither by default. Offer a decision or rounded score through an API with rate limits. Confidence values and especially weights make membership inference much easier, so they carry more risk than a bare decision.
