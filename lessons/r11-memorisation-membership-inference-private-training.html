    Does the Model Remember? Memorisation, Membership Inference, Private Training and Data Provenance

    How a model ends up storing pieces of its training data, how an attacker tests whether a record was in it, what differential privacy really guarantees and what VaultGemma showed, and why knowing where every training record came from is the cheapest defence.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModels store some of their training data, mostly the rare and the repeated; attackers can detect or extract it; the only guarantee that holds against every attacker is differential privacy, which costs accuracy; and most practical protection comes earlier, from deduplicating the data and keeping a record of where each piece came from.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two scenarios. A bank fine-tunes a model on customer-service chats. One customer once typed their IBAN into a chat. Later a staff member types a similar opening and the model completes it with that IBAN. Nobody attacked anything; the model remembered.

    Second: a credit model is trained on a collections list. Someone with only the model's scores asks: was this person on the list? Unusually confident scores on their record answer yes. No raw data left the bank, yet membership itself leaked.

    Both have one cause. Fitting an example leaves a trace of it. Like a path worn across a lawn: a route walked by thousands is faint and shared, but a rare, repeated or odd route can be sharp enough to follow back.

    Three different questions follow:

    • Memorisation. Does the model reproduce training text when prompted? A property of the model.
    • Membership inference. Can an attacker tell whether a record was in training? A property of the model plus the attacker's access.
    • Private training. Can we train so that no attacker learns much about any single record? A property of the training algorithm.

    Upstream of all three: do we know what went in? Provenance and deduplication answer that. Without them you cannot answer a regulator, a deletion request, or a validator asking whether the test set was seen in training.

    Hold onto thisRemembering is what fitting does, and it concentrates on rare, repeated or oddly specific records. Protections: show fewer copies, allow fewer chances to fit each record, and where stakes justify the accuracy cost, cap any one record's pull on training with a formal guarantee.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Memorisation: verbatim, repeated and rare

    Two ideas get mixed up. Generalising is learning a pattern that helps on new data ("UAE IBANs start with AE and have 23 characters"). Memorising is storing something specific to one example ("this particular IBAN"). Think of a student who learns the method versus one who memorises the answer key.

    They are not cleanly separable. Feldman (2020) argued that when data has a long tail of rare sub-populations, some memorisation is needed for accuracy: a rare kind of customer appears too seldom to learn a pattern, so the model keeps the one example it saw. Memorisation is partly the price of accuracy on rare cases.

    For language models the working definition is operational. A string is extractable if some prompt makes the model emit it. The usual test (Carlini et al., 2021, 2022) feeds the first k tokens of a training sequence and checks whether the greedy continuation matches the true next tokens exactly. Three findings recur:

    • Duplicates. More copies, more extraction, steeply. One copy is rarely memorised; hundreds often are.
    • Model size. Larger models memorise more at the same copy count.
    • Context. A longer prefix makes extraction easier; a short one may reveal nothing.

    Memorisation clusters in boilerplate, licence text, code and repeated personal details such as signature blocks and addresses. A bank is full of these: statement templates, standard letters, email footers, a branch address on thousands of tickets.

    Two common errors. Repeated epochs raise memorisation, so many passes over a small private set is the riskiest common setup. And memorisation is not what validation loss measures: a model can have good validation loss and still store a few hundred sensitive strings.

    Extraction is real in production. Nasr et al. (2023) made a production chat model diverge and emit training text verbatim, including personal information, for a few hundred dollars of queries. It was patched, but "aligned and polite" does not mean "stores nothing".

    The practical test is a canary (Carlini et al., 2019): like a dye marker in a water supply, you plant fake but realistic secrets (a made-up account number) in the fine-tuning data at known repetition counts, then measure how much likelier the model finds those exact strings than random alternatives. If canaries planted four times are recoverable, real rare strings are at risk.

    Membership inference: was this record in training?

    A model is more at ease with data it has seen, like a student with last year's exam. Shokri et al. (2017): loss on a training record is usually lower than on a comparable unseen one. The simplest attack (Simulation 1) is a threshold: loss below a cut, say "member".

    How to read the attack: AUC of 0.5 is guessing; 0.56 looks harmless. But Carlini et al. (2022) argued the right measure is the true positive rate at very low false positive rates (0.1%, 1%). An attack right for 1 in 100 members and almost never wrong is a real breach for those people, however dull its average. Judge by the left edge of the ROC curve, not the area.

    Two refinements sharpen attacks. Some records have low loss simply because they are easy (a short, common sentence), so per-record calibration compares each loss to what it would be had the record never been seen. LiRA does this with "shadow" models trained with and without the target; reference models are a cheaper version.

    For large language models the picture differs. Duan et al. (2024) tested many attacks on models of 160 million to 12 billion parameters trained on the Pile; most barely beat guessing. Reasons: data seen roughly once, and no clean member/non-member line (a near-copy of a member is not a member yet is almost as likely). Many published successes were a distribution shift: members and non-members came from different periods, so the attack detected "older text". So for LLMs pretrained once on web data, extraction of repeated strings is the better-evidenced risk. For models fine-tuned for several epochs on a small private set, or classical models such as credit scorers, membership inference is real.

    Bank cases:

    • Scoring model queried by the public or partners. Confidence values act as loss signals. Return a decision or rounded score where the business allows.
    • Fine-tuned assistant. Confirming a named customer's messages were in training reveals they contacted the bank.
    • Models shared with a group company or vendor. Weights give far more than an API does.

    Private training: what differential privacy promises

    Differential privacy (DP) bounds all attacks at once, instead of testing them one by one. Picture two parallel worlds, one with your record in the training set and one without. An algorithm is (ε, δ)-differentially private if the trained weights are almost equally likely in both worlds:

    P[M(D)∈S]≤eε·P[M(D′)∈S]+δ

    M is the training procedure, D and D′ differ in one record, S is any set of outcomes. Small ε means output barely depends on any one record. δ is a small chance the guarantee fails; keep it far below one over the number of records. For an attacker: TPR ≤ eε · FPR + δ, for every membership test. At ε = 2 and 1% false alarms, no attack catches more than about 7.4% of members (e² ≈ 7.39). At ε = 8 the factor is e8 ≈ 2,981, so the cap is already 100% and says almost nothing.

    How to read ε: it is the multiplier on how much more likely any outcome becomes when your record is in. ε near 0 means "no difference"; each +1 multiplies the allowed advantage by about 2.7.

    DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) gets this in deep learning by changing ordinary gradient descent in two ways:

    1. Clip. Cap each example's gradient length at C, so no record can push the weights by more than C.
    2. Add noise. Sum the clipped gradients, add Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ·C, then average over the batch. The noise hides whether any one record was there, like a crowd muffling one voice.

    An accountant adds up the cost over all steps, turning σ, sampling rate, step count and δ into a final ε. More steps or less noise gives a larger ε, so privacy is a budget spent over training. Also define the protected unit: ideally one customer, but in LLM training it may be one token sequence, which protects far less if a person's data spans many sequences.

    Why accuracy suffers: clipping biases gradients, and the noise must be big enough to hide one record. Noise per averaged coordinate is about σ·C / B for batch size B, so larger batches dilute it, hence very large batches and heavy compute. The loss is roughly like training on far less data. It hits rare cases hardest, which are exactly what Feldman says need memory, so sub-group fairness can worsen.

    Two readings. First, ε is a cap on what is possible, not a measurement of what leaks. Real attacks usually fall well below it. Auditing work (Nasr et al., 2021, 2023) runs attacks to find the largest ε they can prove, a lower bound, to catch accounting mistakes. Second, no ε is universally "safe": 1 to 2 is strong, 8 to 10 is common in deployments, and beyond that low-false-alarm attacks are barely constrained. An ε on a vendor slide means nothing until you know the privacy unit, δ and the data covered.

    Worked example: VaultGemma (Google, 2025) is a 1B-parameter model pretrained with DP-SGD at ε ≤ 2.0, δ ≤ 1.1×10−10. Given prefixes of 1 million random training sequences, it showed no detectable memorisation, unlike similar non-private Gemma models. The cost: quality roughly that of non-private models from about five years earlier. The guarantee is per sequence of 1,024 tokens, not per person or document, and a 2026 targeted-extraction study found non-zero memorisation (exact figures unconfirmed). A random-sample rate is not a worst-case guarantee.

    Cheaper partial defences, without a guarantee: fewer epochs, weight decay and early stopping (F6.3), deduplication, scrubbing identifiers, rounding or withholding scores, and output filters for known identifiers. They reduce observed leakage and are worth doing.

    Data provenance and deduplication

    Provenance is a food label for data: where each piece came from and what was done to it. It answers questions that otherwise stop a project:

    • May we use this? Licence, consent, purpose, jurisdiction. Longpre et al. (2023, Data Provenance Initiative) audited over 1,800 text datasets and found licences often misattributed or missing, so "commercial use allowed" datasets frequently traced to sources that did not allow it.
    • Was the test set in training? Contamination is leakage (F3.1) in new clothes. Without provenance, nobody can prove the evaluation was clean.
    • A customer asks for deletion. If you do not know which datasets and model versions held them, you cannot say whether the request applies. Removing influence from trained weights is hard ("machine unlearning"); the realistic answer is excluding the record from the next version or retraining, which needs the record-level trail.
    • A model leaks something. Trace it to the source table and fix the pipeline instead of searching blindly.

    A workable record-level schema is short:

    record_id        stable hash of the content, not a row number
source_system    core banking | call-centre transcripts | web crawl | vendor file
collected_at     date, and the purpose it was collected for
legal_basis      consent | contract | legitimate interest | public | unknown
jurisdiction     where the data subject and the storage are
licence          for third-party text and data
transforms       ordered list: redaction v3, translation, dedup v2, filter v1
cluster_id       id of its near-duplicate group
split            train | validation | test, fixed at creation
used_in          model versions and dates that trained on it

    The habit matters more than the schema: assign these when data is first pulled, carry them through every transformation, and store them in the lineage catalogue (P1.3) so one query answers "which models saw records from this source".

    Deduplication has three levels, each catching what the last misses:

    • Exact: hash the normalised text, keep one. Catches pasted rows, forwarded emails, repeated footers.
    • Near-duplicate: documents differing by a date, name or signature. Break each into overlapping word groups ("shingles"), summarise with a MinHash signature that estimates their overlap (Jaccard similarity), and use locality-sensitive hashing to find candidate pairs without comparing all pairs. Lee et al. (2022) found this cut memorised emissions about tenfold and improved results on less data.
    • Semantic: paraphrases, found with embeddings (F1.1). A judgement call: removing "similar" can remove useful variety.

    MinHash compresses each document into a few hundred numbers, split into b bands of r numbers. Two documents become a candidate pair if they agree on every number in any one band. For true similarity s:

    P(candidate)=1−(1−sr)b

    An S-shaped curve in s, rising sharply near s ≈ (1/b)1/r. More rows per band (r) is stricter, like needing more questions right in a quiz section; more bands (b) is more forgiving, like more chances to pass. Place the S so real near-duplicates pass and unrelated pairs rarely do. Candidates are verified exactly, so false candidates cost compute, not correctness.

    Two cautions. Deduplicate across splits, not just within: if one loan letter template sits in both training and test, the score reflects memory. And collapsing a thousand "please send me my statement" messages into one helps privacy and compute but hides how common the request is. Keep the cluster size as a column.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each mistake has a quick check.

    • "We removed the names, so the model can't leak them"

      Identifiers hide in free text, and quasi-identifiers (address, rare job title, amount plus date) combine to identify. Models memorise whatever repeats.

      Spot itRun extraction prompts with prefixes from real records and plant canaries. A field-level check is not enough.
    • Judging membership inference by AUC or average accuracy

      An attack with AUC 0.55 can still identify a few percent of members with near certainty.

      Spot itAsk for TPR at 0.1% and 1% FPR, on a log-axis ROC curve.
    • Member and non-member sets from different times

      The attack detects the date, not membership. This inflated many early LLM results.

      Spot itCheck non-members were held out at random from the same period and source.
    • An ε quoted with no context

      "Differentially private with ε = 8": of what, at what unit, with what δ, and was the accountant right?

      Spot itAsk: privacy unit (record, customer, sequence), δ, steps, any non-private data or pretrained weights, and whether an audit attack was run.
    • Fine-tuning a DP-pretrained model non-privately

      The base model's guarantee covers only its own data. Your private fine-tuning data gets no protection.

      Spot itAsk which stages used DP and which data each touched.
    • "Zero memorisation" from random sampling

      Random sequences miss the rare record an attacker targets.

      Spot itAsk whether targeted prefixes for known sensitive strings were used, and at what prompt lengths.
    • Deduplicating within training only

      Test records have near-copies in training, so memory looks like skill.

      Spot itRun near-duplicate detection between splits and explain any hits above threshold.
    • Deduplicating so hard that signal disappears

      Aggressive semantic dedup removes variety, including rare cases the model needs.

      Spot itCompare accuracy on rare segments before and after; keep cluster sizes as a feature.
    • Provenance as a one-off spreadsheet

      Columns exist, but nothing links them to actual training files or model versions.

      Spot itPick a random customer and ask which model versions trained on their data. If nobody can answer in minutes, the trail is decorative.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. An attack on a model has AUC 0.56 and the team says it is harmless. What further number do you ask for?

    The TPR at a very low FPR, such as 0.1%. AUC averages over all thresholds and can hide a small group of members identified with near certainty. If that TPR is well above 0.1%, the exposure is real.

    2. Under (ε = 3, δ ≈ 0) differential privacy, what is the most an attack can catch at 1% false positives?

    TPR ≤ e³ × 0.01 ≈ 20.1 × 0.01, about 20%. At ε = 1 it would be about 2.7%.

    3. Why does a larger batch help DP-SGD?

    Noise of size σ·C is added to the sum of clipped gradients, then divided by the batch size. The same noise is spread over more signal, so the averaged gradient is cleaner. The cost is compute.

    4. A fine-tuned model shows no leakage when sampling random training sequences, but a security team extracts an IBAN with a targeted prompt. Is that a contradiction?

    No. Random samples mostly test common, low-risk content, so the rate can be near zero while one rare string is recoverable. Average-case and worst-case are different questions, hence canaries and targeted prefixes.

    5. Two letters differ only in the customer's name and amount, so their Jaccard similarity is 0.85. With 5 rows and 20 bands, what is the chance LSH flags them?

    0.85⁵ ≈ 0.444. (1 − 0.444)²⁰ = 0.556²⁰ ≈ 8×10⁻⁶. So the chance of flagging is about 99.999%.

    6. VaultGemma reports no detectable memorisation and ε ≤ 2. What should you check before saying "this model cannot leak customer data"?

    The privacy unit (sequences, not people), the data covered (pretraining only, not your fine-tuning), whether memorisation was tested with targeted prompts, and the quality cost.

    7. Why can a deletion request be hard to answer even before any "unlearning" question arises?

    Without record-level provenance you cannot say which datasets, pipelines and model versions included the person's data. Provenance turns a vague worry into a query.
