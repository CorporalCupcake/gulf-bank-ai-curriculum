    Memorisation and Private Training

    Part 1

    What memorisation is

    In one sentenceA model memorises when it stores specific training examples instead of general patterns, so a prompt or an attacker can get them back out; private training limits how much any one record can leave a trace.

    Say a bank fine-tunes a chat assistant on 50,000 support chats. One customer once typed their IBAN (international bank account number) into a chat. Later the assistant completes a similar opening with that exact IBAN. Nobody attacked it: it remembered, like a path worn across a lawn.

    Three questions follow, plus one upstream:

    • Memorisation: does the model reproduce training text?
    • Membership inference: can an attacker tell that a record was in training?
    • Private training: can we cap what any attacker learns about one record?
    • Provenance: do we even know what went in?

    How it differs from similar things

    • Generalising is learning a pattern ("UAE IBANs start with AE"). Memorising is storing one example ("this IBAN"). The line blurs: a rare kind of customer appears too seldom to form a pattern, so the model keeps the single example.
    • A data breach steals files. Memorisation leaks through the model itself, and shared weights leak far more than an API.
    Part 2

    How it works

    Testing for memorisation

    1. Extraction test. Give the model the first k tokens of a training sequence and see whether it reproduces the true continuation exactly, always taking the likeliest next word.
    2. Canaries. Plant fake but realistic secrets (a made-up account number) in the data at known repeat counts, such as 1, 4 and 16. Then measure how much likelier the model finds them than random alternatives.
    3. Membership inference. A model is more comfortable with data it has seen, so its loss is lower. The simplest attack says "member" when loss falls below a cut.
    4. Calibrated attacks. Stronger attacks (LiRA) compare each loss with what it would be if the record were unseen, using shadow models.

    Private training: differential privacy

    Differential privacy (DP) bounds every attack at once. Picture two worlds, one with your record in training and one without. DP says the trained model is almost equally likely in both.

    1. Clip. Cap how much any single record's gradient (its push on the weights) can count.
    2. Add noise. Sum the clipped pushes and add random noise sized to that cap, like a crowd muffling one voice.
    3. Keep accounts. An accountant adds up the privacy cost of every step into a final number, ε (epsilon). This method is called DP-SGD.
    Part 3

    How to read the numbers

    Attack AUC 0.5
    The attack is guessing. 0.56 looks harmless, but AUC averages over everything.
    True positive rate at 0.1% false alarms
    The number that matters. An attack that is right for 1 in 100 members and almost never wrong is a real breach, even with a modest AUC.
    ε = 2
    Strong. No attack can catch more than about 7% of members at 1% false alarms (the cap is about eε times the false-alarm rate). Each +1 on ε multiplies the allowed advantage by about 2.7.
    ε = 8 to 10
    Common in real deployments, but the cap is nearly useless: at ε = 8 the factor is about 3,000, so the cap is effectively 100%. ε is a ceiling on what is possible, not a measurement of what leaks.
    A quoted ε with no context
    Ask for the privacy unit (one customer, or one text sequence, which protects far less) and δ, a tiny chance the guarantee fails.

    What raises the risk

    • More copies. Extraction rises steeply. Bank boilerplate is full of them, such as statement templates and email footers.
    • Bigger models memorise more at the same copy count.
    • More passes over a small private set.

    In 2023 researchers made a production chat model emit personal training text for a few hundred dollars. VaultGemma (Google, 2025) is a 1-billion-parameter model pretrained at ε ≤ 2 that showed no detectable memorisation on prefixes of random training sequences. Its guarantee is per sequence, not per person, and quality matched non-private models from years earlier.

    Part 4

    Which defence to use

    DefenceUse it whenLimit
    Dedup, fewer passes, scrubbingAlways, as the first layer. Cheap and often improves quality.No guarantee. Address plus job title plus date can still identify someone.
    Rounded or withheld scoresA scorer is queried by partners.Reduces leakage, does not prove safety.
    Canaries and low false-alarm testsProducing evidence for the model-risk file.Tests your pipeline; misses the one rare string a targeted attacker wants.
    DP-SGDStakes justify the cost, data is large.Hurts accuracy, needs big batches and compute, hits rare cases hardest.

    Strong at:

    • Deduplication. Risk climbs steeply with copies, so removing repeats attacks the cause. Published work found that removing near-duplicates cut memorised output roughly tenfold.
    • DP. The only guarantee that holds against attackers not yet invented.
    • Provenance. Record each row's source, legal basis, split and which model versions used it, so deletion and test-set contamination questions become queries.

    Weak at:

    • Small data or tight compute. Noise swamps the signal.
    • Pretrained web-scale models. Most membership attacks barely beat guessing there, because data is seen about once. Extraction of repeated strings is the better-evidenced risk.
    Show the formula

    True positive rate ≤ eε × false positive rate + δ. In words: whatever false-alarm rate an attacker accepts, the share of members they catch can be at most eε times larger, plus a tiny δ.

    In banking, small fine-tunes run for several epochs and classical credit scorers are where membership inference is real; confirming a named customer's chats were in training reveals they contacted the bank.

    Usable today? Dedup, fewer passes, scrubbing, canaries and provenance, yes. DP-SGD, where stakes justify the accuracy cost.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging attacks by AUC alone

      A low AUC can hide an attack that identifies a few members with near certainty. Check the true positive rate at 0.1% and 1% false alarms.

    • Trusting good validation loss

      It says nothing about stored strings. Run extraction and canary tests.

    • Treating ε as a leak measurement

      It is a ceiling. Real attacks usually fall well below it.

    • Deduplicating only within splits

      Duplicates across train and test make scores reflect memory. Deduplicate across splits too.

    1. What makes a record most likely to be memorised?

    Being rare, or being repeated many times, especially in a larger model.

    2. Why does a lower loss reveal membership?

    Models fit seen data more closely, so training records tend to score lower loss than unseen ones.

    3. What do the two steps of DP-SGD do?

    Clipping caps any one record's influence on a step. Noise hides whether any one record was there.

    4. At ε = 2, what is the most an attack can catch at 1% false alarms?

    About 7% of members (e2 is about 7.4, times 1%).

    5. What is the cheapest first step against extraction?

    Remove duplicates, since risk rises steeply with the number of copies.
