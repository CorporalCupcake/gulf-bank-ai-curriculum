    Does the Model Remember? Memorisation, Membership Inference, Private Training and Data Provenance

    How a model ends up storing pieces of its training data, how an attacker tests whether a record was in it, what differential privacy really guarantees and what VaultGemma showed, and why knowing where every training record came from is the cheapest defence.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModels store some of their training data, mostly the rare and the repeated; attackers can detect or extract it; the only guarantee that holds against every attacker is differential privacy, which costs accuracy; and most practical protection comes earlier, from deduplicating the data and keeping a record of where each piece came from.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank fine-tunes a language model on three years of customer-service chats so that it can draft replies. Somewhere in those chats, one customer typed their IBAN and Emirates ID to prove who they were. Months later, a staff member types the start of a similar message and the model completes it with that IBAN. Nobody attacked anything. The model simply remembered.

    Now the second scenario. A credit model is trained on a list of customers in collections. A journalist or a fraudster, with access only to the model's scores, wants to know: was this particular person on that list? If the model scores that person's record with unusual confidence, that is a leak, even though no raw data was ever released. Just learning that someone was in the training set can be sensitive.

    Both come from one fact. Training makes a model fit its examples, and fitting an example well means that example leaves a trace. For most records the trace is faint and shared with thousands of similar ones. For a record that is rare, repeated or unusual, the trace can be sharp enough to read back.

    Three questions follow, and they are different:

    • Memorisation. Does the model reproduce training text or records when prompted? This is a property of the model.
    • Membership inference. Can an attacker tell whether a given record was in the training set? This is a property of the model and the attacker's access.
    • Private training. Can we train so that no attacker, however clever, learns much about any single record? This is a property of the training algorithm.

    And one question sits upstream of all three: do we even know what went in? Provenance and deduplication answer that. A team that cannot list what its data contains cannot answer a regulator, a customer who asks for deletion, or a validator who asks whether the test set was seen in training.

    Hold onto thisRemembering is not a bug someone introduced. It is what fitting does, and it concentrates on records that are rare, repeated or oddly specific. So the protections are: show the model fewer copies, give it fewer chances to fit each record, and, where the stakes justify the accuracy cost, limit how much any one record can move the training with a formal guarantee.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Memorisation: verbatim, repeated and rare

    The term covers two ideas, and mixing them causes arguments.

    • Generalising means learning a pattern that applies to new data: "IBANs in the UAE start with AE and have 23 characters".
    • Memorising means storing something specific to one training example that does not help on new data: this particular IBAN.

    They are not cleanly separable. Feldman (2020) argued that for data with a long tail of rare sub-populations, some memorisation of individual examples is needed to get good accuracy: a rare kind of customer or document appears too seldom for a pattern to be learned from many examples, so the model learns it from the one it saw. Memorisation is a price of accuracy on rare cases, not only a leak.

    For language models the working definition is operational. A string is extractable if some prompt makes the model emit it. The common test, from Carlini and colleagues (2021, 2022), gives the model the first k tokens of a training sequence and checks whether the greedy continuation matches the true next tokens exactly. Three findings recur across studies:

    • Duplicates. The more times a sequence appears in the training data, the more likely it is extracted. The relationship is steep: one copy is rarely memorised, hundreds of copies often are.
    • Model size. Larger models memorise more, at the same number of copies.
    • Context. A longer prompt prefix makes extraction easier. A short, innocent-looking prefix may reveal nothing while a long one unlocks the rest.

    The same studies find that memorisation is not evenly spread over data: boilerplate, licence text, code, and personal details that repeat across documents (a signature block, an address) are over-represented. For a bank, repeated structure is everywhere: statement templates, standard letters, email footers, a branch's address on thousands of tickets.

    Two more points that people get wrong. First, repeated epochs matter: training for many passes over the same data raises memorisation, which is why fine-tuning on a small private set for many epochs is the riskiest common setup. Second, memorisation is not the same as overfitting measured by validation loss. A model can have good validation loss and still store a few hundred sensitive strings.

    Extraction is not only a research curiosity. Nasr and colleagues (2023) showed that a production chat model could be made to diverge from its normal behaviour and emit training text verbatim, at a cost of a few hundred dollars of queries, including personal information. Providers patched that specific attack; the lesson is that "the model is aligned and polite" does not mean "the model stores nothing".

    A practical bank test is a canary (Carlini et al., 2019, "The Secret Sharer"): insert fake but realistic secrets, such as a made-up account number, into the fine-tuning data at known repetition counts, then measure how much more likely the model finds those exact strings than random alternatives. If canaries inserted four times are recoverable, real rare strings are at risk.

    Membership inference: was this record in training?

    The idea, from Shokri and colleagues (2017): a model behaves differently on data it has seen. Its loss on a training record is usually lower than on a comparable unseen record. The simplest attack, from Simulation 1, is a threshold on the loss. If the loss is below a cut, say "member".

    The cut is where the trap is. Averaging over all records, the attack's accuracy can look unimpressive. But it matters how confidently it is right on the few it gets right. Carlini and colleagues (2022) argued that membership inference should be judged by the true positive rate at very low false positive rates, not by average accuracy or AUC. An attack that is right for 1 in 100 members and almost never wrong is a real privacy breach for those people. In the simulation, notice that at a 0.1% false alarm rate even a modest gap lets a sizeable share of members through.

    Two more ideas make attacks sharper. Per-record calibration: some records have low loss simply because they are easy (a short, common sentence). The LiRA attack trains "shadow" models with and without the target record and compares the target's loss to what it would be if the record had never been seen. Reference models do a cheaper version of the same.

    An important qualification for large language models. Duan and colleagues (2024) tested many membership inference attacks on models from 160 million to 12 billion parameters trained on the Pile and found that most barely beat random guessing. Their explanation: huge datasets seen for roughly one pass, and no clean boundary between "member" and "non-member" text, since a near-copy of a member is not a member yet is almost as likely under the model. They also showed that many published successes came from a distribution shift: members and non-members drawn from different time periods, so the attack detected "older text" rather than membership. So for LLMs pretrained once on web-scale data, extraction of repeated strings is the better-evidenced risk. For models fine-tuned for several epochs on a small private set, or for classical models such as credit scorers, membership inference is real.

    For a bank, the standard cases are:

    • A scoring model queried by the public or partners. If the API returns confidence values, the attacker has loss-like signals. Return a decision, or a rounded score, where the business allows.
    • A fine-tuned assistant. Checking whether a named customer's messages were in the training set tells the attacker the customer contacted the bank about something.
    • Models shared with a group company or vendor. Weights give the attacker far more than an API does.

    Private training: what differential privacy promises

    Instead of testing attacks one at a time, differential privacy (DP) bounds all of them. The definition is about the training algorithm. Take any two training sets that differ in one record. An algorithm is (ε, δ)-differentially private if its outputs, the trained weights, are almost equally likely whichever of the two sets was used:

    P[M(D)∈S]≤eε·P[M(D′)∈S]+δ

    M is the training procedure, D and D′ differ in one record, S is any set of outcomes. Small ε means the output hardly depends on any one record. δ is a small probability of the guarantee failing; it should be much smaller than one over the number of records. What this means for an attacker: TPR ≤ eε · FPR + δ, for every membership test. At ε = 2 and 1% false alarms, no attack can catch more than about 7.4% of members (e² ≈ 7.39). At ε = 8 the factor is e8 ≈ 2,981, so at 1% false alarms the cap is already 100% and says almost nothing.

    How do you achieve it in deep learning? DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) changes ordinary stochastic gradient descent in two ways:

    1. Clip. Compute the gradient for each example separately and cap its length at a bound C. Now no single record can push the weights by more than C.
    2. Add noise. Sum the clipped gradients and add Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ·C, then average over the batch. The noise is sized to hide whether any one record was there.

    The total privacy cost is tracked across all training steps by an accountant, which turns σ, the sampling rate, the number of steps and δ into a final ε. More steps, or less noise, means a larger ε. Two consequences: privacy is a budget spent over training, and the unit protected has to be defined. In an ordinary deployment it is one customer's record, but in language model training it may be a single sequence of tokens, which protects far less than one person if a person's data spans many sequences. Always ask what the privacy unit is.

    Why does DP cost accuracy? Clipping biases gradients, and the noise has to be large enough to hide one record among the batch. Noise per averaged coordinate is about σ·C / B for batch size B, so larger batches dilute the noise, which is why private training of large models uses very large batches and heavy compute. The utility loss is roughly equivalent to training on far less data or with a smaller model. It also hits the rare cases hardest, which are exactly what Feldman's argument says a model needs memory for: fairness across sub-groups can worsen under DP.

    Two practical readings of the picture. First, ε is a cap on what is possible, not a measurement of what leaks. Real attacks on DP models usually fall far below the cap, and auditing work (Nasr et al., 2021, 2023) runs attacks to find the largest ε they can prove, a lower bound, to check that the claimed ε was not an accounting mistake. Second, there is no universal "safe" ε. Values of 1 to 2 are strong, 8 to 10 are common in deployments, and above that the guarantee says little about low-false-alarm attacks. Treat an ε in a vendor's slide as meaningless until you know the privacy unit, δ and what data it covers.

    Cheaper, partial defences that are not DP: fewer epochs, weight decay and early stopping (F6.3), deduplication, scrubbing identifiers, rounding or withholding scores, and output filters that block strings matching known identifiers. They reduce observed leakage and are worth doing. They come without a guarantee.

    Data provenance and deduplication

    Provenance is a record of where each piece of data came from and what happened to it. It sounds like paperwork. In practice it answers questions that otherwise stop a project:

    • May we use this? Licence, customer consent, purpose of collection, jurisdiction. Longpre and colleagues (2023, Data Provenance Initiative) audited more than 1,800 text datasets and found that licences were frequently misattributed or missing, so a dataset labelled "commercial use allowed" often traced back to sources that did not allow it.
    • Was the test set in the training set? Contamination is a leakage problem (F3.1) in new clothes. Without provenance, nobody can prove the evaluation was clean.
    • What do we do about a deletion request? A customer asks to be removed. If you do not know which training sets and model versions contained them, you cannot even say whether the request applies. Removing a record's influence from trained weights is hard (the topic of "machine unlearning"); the realistic answer is usually retraining or excluding the record from the next version, which needs the record-level trail.
    • What did we see in an incident? If a model leaks something, you want to trace it to the source table and fix the pipeline, not search blindly.

    A workable record-level schema is short:

    record_id        stable hash of the content, not a row number
source_system    core banking | call-centre transcripts | web crawl | vendor file
collected_at     date, and the purpose it was collected for
legal_basis      consent | contract | legitimate interest | public | unknown
jurisdiction     where the data subject and the storage are
licence          for third-party text and data
transforms       ordered list: redaction v3, translation, dedup v2, filter v1
cluster_id       id of its near-duplicate group
split            train | validation | test, fixed at creation
used_in          model versions and dates that trained on it

    The point is not the schema but the habit: assign these when the data is first pulled, carry them through every transformation, and store them in the lineage and metadata catalogue (P1.3) so a query can answer "which models saw records from this source".

    Deduplication has three levels, each catching what the last misses:

    • Exact: hash the normalised text and keep one. Cheap and catches copy-pasted rows, forwarded emails, repeated footers.
    • Near-duplicate: two documents that differ by a date, a name or a signature. Break each into overlapping word groups ("shingles"), summarise with a MinHash signature that estimates their overlap (Jaccard similarity), and use locality-sensitive hashing to find candidate pairs without comparing all pairs. Lee and colleagues (2022) applied this to language-model corpora and found that removing duplicates cut memorised emissions about tenfold and improved results while training on less data.
    • Semantic: paraphrases with the same content, found with embeddings (F1.1). Useful, but a judgement call: removing "similar" can remove useful variety.

    The mechanics of the near-duplicate step are a neat piece of engineering. MinHash compresses each document into a few hundred numbers, split into b bands of r numbers. Two documents become a candidate pair if they agree on every number in any one band. For true similarity s, that probability is:

    P(candidate)=1−(1−sr)b

    An S-shaped curve in s, which rises sharply near s ≈ (1/b)1/r. More rows per band (r) makes the test stricter; more bands (b) makes it more forgiving. You choose where the S sits so that real near-duplicates almost always pass and unrelated pairs almost never do. Candidates are then checked exactly, so false candidates cost compute, not correctness.

    Two practical cautions. Deduplicate across splits, not just within them: if the same loan letter template sits in both training and test, the model's score reflects memory. And decide deliberately what to keep: for a customer-service model, collapsing a thousand "please send me my statement" messages into one is right for privacy and compute, and wrong if you also needed to learn how common the request is. Keep the cluster size as a column.

    Memorisation is another way identity survives: a model can regurgitate what was supposedly de-identified. Minimisation and consent sit upstream.

    Membership inference and extraction are the privacy attacks in that family. Poisoning is the integrity attack that provenance also helps with.

    G1.1 · Threat modellingWho can query what

    Whether the attacker has an API, scores, logits or weights decides which attack in this lesson applies.

    A11.2 · Privacy-enhancing technologiesDifferential privacy, clean rooms, homomorphic encryption

    The wider toolbox for using data you cannot freely share. DP in training is one tool; the others protect data in use or in transit.

    F3.1 · LeakageTest contamination is leakage

    Duplicates across train and test inflate scores exactly as time leakage does. Dedup and split assignment are the cure.

    P1.3 · Lineage and metadataWhere provenance lives

    Record-level provenance is lineage pushed down from tables to rows, and is what answers a deletion or audit request.

    R2.2 · Synthetic dataSynthetic data is not automatically private

    A generator trained on real customers can memorise them too. "Synthetic" needs the same membership tests.

    M1.3 · PretrainingWhy one pass over web data is safer than ten over your own

    Epoch count and dataset size explain much of the difference between pretraining and small fine-tunes.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Fine-tuned assistants on chats, emails and call transcripts. Identifiers and templated text repeat; epochs are many. Dedup, redact, cap epochs, and test with canaries before release.
    • Models trained on customer lists (collections, watchlists, SAR-adjacent data). Membership itself is sensitive. Withhold raw scores from external callers.
    • Vendor and group sharing. Sharing weights is sharing a lossy copy of the data. Treat it as a data-transfer decision, and ask legal which personal-data rules apply; both the UAE and Saudi Arabia have personal-data protection laws, but how they treat model weights is a question for counsel, and I have not tried to state it here.
    • Evaluation integrity. Provenance proves that the validation set was not in training, which model validators (G3) increasingly ask for.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mistakes in claims about privacy, and in privacy work itself. Each has a quick check.

    • "We removed the names, so the model can't leak them"

      Identifiers hide in free text, and quasi-identifiers (an address, a rare job title, an amount and date) combine to identify. Models memorise whichever strings repeat.

      Spot itRun extraction prompts with prefixes from real records, and plant canaries. Do not rely on a field-level check.
    • Judging membership inference by AUC or average accuracy

      An attack with AUC 0.55 can still identify a few percent of members with near certainty.

      Spot itAsk for the true positive rate at 0.1% and 1% false positive rate, on a plotted ROC curve with logarithmic axes.
    • Member and non-member sets drawn from different times

      The attack detects the date, not membership, and appears to work. This inflated many early LLM results.

      Spot itCheck that the non-member set was held out at random from the same period and source, not collected later.
    • An ε quoted with no context

      "The model is differentially private with ε = 8." Of what, at what unit, with what δ, and was the accountant correct?

      Spot itAsk: privacy unit (record, customer, sequence), δ, number of steps, whether any non-private data or pretrained weights were involved, and whether an audit attack was run.
    • Fine-tuning a DP-pretrained model non-privately

      The base model's guarantee covers its own data only. Fine-tuning on private customer data without DP gives no protection for that data.

      Spot itAsk which training stages used DP and which data each stage touched.
    • "Zero memorisation" from random sampling

      A test on random sequences misses the rare record an attacker targets.

      Spot itAsk whether the test used targeted prefixes for known sensitive strings, and with what prompt lengths.
    • Deduplicating within training only

      Test records have near-copies in training; scores are inflated and memorisation looks like skill.

      Spot itRun near-duplicate detection between splits. Any hits above the threshold need explaining.
    • Tuning dedup so hard that signal disappears

      Aggressive semantic dedup removes real variety, including rare cases the model needs.

      Spot itCompare accuracy on rare segments before and after, and keep cluster sizes as a feature.
    • Provenance as a one-off spreadsheet

      The columns exist, but nothing links them to the actual training files or model versions.

      Spot itPick a random customer and ask which model versions trained on their data. If nobody can answer in minutes, the trail is decorative.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Any model trained on our data keeps some trace of it, and the traces are strongest for details that appear many times or are unusual. That is a privacy exposure, not a hypothetical. We reduce it in order of cost: clean and de-duplicate the data, keep a record of where each piece came from, limit how many times the model sees it, and withhold raw scores from outside callers. For the most sensitive cases there is a formal method, differential privacy, with a real accuracy cost. Before release I would have us test the model by trying to extract planted fake secrets.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd measure leakage with canaries and extraction tests using real prefixes, and with membership inference reported as true positive rate at low false positive rate against reference or shadow models, with member and non-member sets from the same period. Deduplication covers exact and MinHash-LSH near-duplicates, within and across splits. If we use DP-SGD I'd state the privacy unit, ε, δ and the accountant, and run an audit attack to check the claim. Provenance is recorded per record and linked to model versions.”
    “Can a model really give out customer data it was trained on?”

    Yes, it has been shown on production and research models. The risk is highest for strings that are repeated, rare or distinctive, for larger models, and for fine-tuning with many passes over a small private set. It is much lower for facts that appear once among billions of tokens. We test for it before release rather than assume.

    “Is differential privacy the answer then?”

    It is the only approach with a guarantee against every attacker, but it costs accuracy, compute and tuning effort, and the guarantee is only as meaningful as its unit and ε. VaultGemma shows it is feasible at 1B parameters, at roughly the quality of older non-private models. I'd use it where the data is highly sensitive or shared across institutions, and use deduplication, scrubbing and access limits everywhere else.

    “Membership inference barely worked in that big paper. So is it a non-issue?”

    For web-scale pretraining seen about once, largely yes. Our case differs: small private data, several epochs, or a tabular scoring model with exposed confidence values. There membership inference is real, and the right measure is true positives at very low false positives, not average accuracy.

    “A customer asked us to delete their data. What about the model?”

    First: can we say which datasets and model versions included them? That needs record-level provenance. Removing influence from trained weights is unreliable, so the practical approach is to exclude the record from the next training run and, for high-risk cases, retrain. Whether the law requires this for model weights is a question for legal; I wouldn't guess.

    “Does deduplication matter if we're not worried about privacy?”

    Yes. It also cuts compute, removes test contamination, and improves results in published work, because repeated text over-weights a few patterns. The one thing to keep is how often something repeated, as a cluster-size column.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. An attack on a model has AUC 0.56 and the team says it is harmless. What further number do you ask for?

    The true positive rate at a very low false positive rate, such as 0.1%. A low AUC averages over all thresholds and can hide a small group of members identified with near certainty. If that number is well above 0.1%, there is a genuine exposure.

    2. Under (ε = 3, δ ≈ 0) differential privacy, what is the most an attack can catch at 1% false positives?

    TPR ≤ e³ × 0.01 ≈ 20.1 × 0.01 = about 20%. At ε = 1 it would be about 2.7%.

    3. Why does a larger batch help DP-SGD?

    Each step adds noise of size σ·C to the sum of clipped gradients, then divides by the batch size. A larger batch leaves the same noise spread over more signal, so the averaged gradient is cleaner. The cost is compute, which is why private training of large models uses very large batches.

    4. A fine-tuned model shows no leakage when sampling random training sequences, but a security team extracts an IBAN with a targeted prompt. Is that a contradiction?

    No. A random sample mostly tests common, low-risk content, so the rate can be near zero while a specific rare string is recoverable. Worst-case and average-case are different questions. That is why canaries and targeted prefixes are used.

    5. Two letters differ only in the customer's name and amount, so their Jaccard similarity is 0.85. With 5 rows and 20 bands, what is the chance LSH flags them?

    0.85⁵ ≈ 0.444. (1 − 0.444)²⁰ = 0.556²⁰ ≈ 8×10⁻⁶. So the chance of flagging is about 99.999%.

    6. VaultGemma reports no detectable memorisation and ε ≤ 2. What should you check before saying "this model cannot leak customer data"?

    The privacy unit (sequences, not people), what data it covers (its pretraining only, not any fine-tuning you add), whether memorisation was tested with targeted prompts, and the quality cost. A guarantee on pretraining data says nothing about data used later.

    7. Why can a deletion request be hard to answer even before any "unlearning" question arises?

    Because without record-level provenance you cannot say which datasets, pipelines and model versions included the person's data. Provenance turns a vague worry into a query.
