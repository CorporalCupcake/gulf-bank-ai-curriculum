    From Idea to Production: Scoping, Running and Landing an AI Project in a Gulf Bank

    How to shape a project so it can finish, what a business case must admit, which gates and kill criteria stop endless pilots, and how hierarchy, relationships and multinational teams change the way you get a decision.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost AI projects stall not on modelling but on things that were never scoped (who owns the outcome, who approves the data, who changes the workflow), so write those down in week one, set the stopping rules before you start, and learn how decisions really get made in the organisation you are in.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank's contact centre handles 40,000 calls a month. Someone suggests a model that drafts the call summary, so agents stop typing for two minutes after every call. The data science team builds a demo in three weeks. It looks good. Senior people clap. The pilot, with 20 hand-picked agents, saves "about 80% of wrap-up time".

    Then nothing happens for nine months. The summaries need to sit inside the CRM, which belongs to another team with a release freeze. Information security has questions about customer data leaving the bank's network. Model risk asks whether this is a "model" under policy and what monitoring it will have. The contact-centre head owns the agents, but the budget belongs to digital. The 20 enthusiasts loved it; nobody asked the other 400 agents, who do not trust a summary they did not write. The pilot is neither killed nor shipped. It is "ongoing".

    This is the typical shape of failure, and the model was not the problem. Industry surveys put the share of AI pilots that never reach production anywhere from a third to the large majority, depending on who counts and how (Part 4). The causes they cite repeat: data access, integration, unclear success criteria, no owner, no monitoring. All of those are scoping and running problems. They are cheap to fix in week one and very expensive in month nine.

    The second half of this lesson is about the room you will work in. A Gulf bank is usually hierarchical, relationship-driven and multinational at the same time: a board and executive committee with real authority, staff from twenty or more countries, and meetings where English is the shared language and not everyone's first. None of that is a problem. It is just information about how to get a decision made and how to hear the bad news that nobody wants to say out loud.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Adoption and realisation are the multipliers nobody measures in a pilot. A pilot with enthusiasts measures the technology. A business case needs the whole workforce, and an honest answer to "freed time becomes what?". Two minutes saved per call is only money if the bank handles more calls with the same people, avoids overtime or avoids hiring. Otherwise it is slack, which is still worth something, but less than a salary.
    • Running cost is part of the design. A prototype that costs AED 90,000 a month to serve may be a perfectly good idea that a different architecture, or a smaller model, would make viable (P3).
    • A scope is a set of decisions, not a feature list. Who will use this, what do they do today, what changes, who owns the result, and what would make us stop? The next section turns these into a checklist.
    Hold onto thisA pilot proves that something can work. A project proves that it will work here, for everyone, at a cost the bank accepts. Scope for the second thing from the first day, and write down what evidence would make you stop.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C3.3 · Scoping: the one page you write before the model

    C1 covered turning a vague request into a problem and finding the baseline. This is the next step: turning that problem into a bounded piece of work with an owner, a timeline and a way to end. A useful scope fits on one page and answers ten questions. If you cannot answer one, that gap is your first task.

    QuestionWhat a good answer looks likeWhat goes wrong if it is missing
    1. Decision or action"Which calls get an automatic summary", "which payments go to an analyst". A verb, not a model.A model that scores things nobody acts on.
    2. Owner of the outcomeOne named business executive whose targets move if this works. Not the data team.No one fights for integration, training or budget.
    3. Users and the workflowWho touches it daily, where in their screen, what they stop doing.The tool sits in a separate window and is ignored.
    4. BaselineWhat happens today, with a number (C1.3): handling time, loss rate, review hours.No way to say whether it helped.
    5. Success and kill criteria"Proceed if recall at 300 alerts a day is above X on a time-ordered test; stop if not by week 10."The pilot cannot fail, so it never ends.
    6. Data and accessTables, owners, legal basis, where data may sit (A11), who has approved it.Three months lost waiting for a data-sharing approval.
    7. Control requirementsIs it a model under policy (G3.1)? Who validates? Which risk tier? Fairness, privacy, explainability asks.A finished model that cannot be approved.
    8. Integration and operationsWhich systems it calls and which call it. Latency, uptime, who is on call, who retrains.A notebook with no home.
    9. Cost and appetiteBuild and run cost, and how much time you are willing to spend before deciding.Open-ended work that grows to fill the time.
    10. Out of scopeA written list of things you will not do now, including the attractive extras.Scope creep that arrives as "while you're in there".

    Two ideas make this practical. The first is appetite, borrowed from the Shape Up method: decide up front how much time the problem is worth (six weeks, say), and then shape the solution to fit that, rather than estimating the solution and being surprised by the time. Fixed time, variable scope. In a bank this works well because it forces the "what is the thin slice that proves value?" conversation.

    The second is reference-class estimating. People estimate their own projects from the inside, picturing the plan going well, and are reliably too optimistic. Flyvbjerg's work on large projects argues for starting from how long similar projects actually took and adjusting from there. For you: ask the platform, security and model-risk teams how long a typical new use case took last year. Those queue times are often longer than the modelling.

    A line that helps in the first meeting: "Before I build anything, can I confirm who owns the outcome, what we will measure, and what would make us stop?" It sounds like process, but it is the cheapest risk reduction you have.

    Stages, gates and the arithmetic of stalling

    Break the work into stages. Each stage ends in a gate: a short decision meeting with a pre-agreed answer to "continue, change or stop?". Gates make it cheap to stop early and expensive to drift. Typical stages in a bank are below; durations are rough and vary a lot between institutions.

    StageQuestion it answersWhat you hand over at the gateTypical length
    0. FrameIs this worth doing at all?The one-page scope and an order-of-magnitude case1–2 weeks
    1. Baseline and data checkDoes the data exist, and what does the do-nothing option score?Baseline numbers, data-access approvals, a leakage and label audit2–4 weeks
    2. Thin slice, offlineCan a simple model beat the baseline on honest, time-ordered data?Result with an interval (F2.1), error analysis, a go or stop call4–8 weeks
    3. Pilot in the real workflowDo real users, on real systems, get the value?Adoption, outcome and cost numbers from a controlled trial (F3.6)6–12 weeks
    4. Production readinessCan it be run, monitored and defended?Validation pack (C2.3), security sign-off, monitoring, runbook, model-inventory entry4–12 weeks, often in parallel with 3
    5. Run and reviewIs it still working and still worth it?Periodic review (G3.5), retraining plan, benefits trackingContinuous

    Why do projects stall? Because getting to production is a chain of things that all must go right, and probabilities of a chain multiply. If five conditions (data, a measurable result, risk approval, integration, adoption) each hold with probability pi, then

    P(reaches production)=p1×p2×…×p5

    Five items that each look "pretty likely" (60–75%) combine to roughly a one-in-seven chance. Raising any single one helps, but the biggest gains come from the weakest link, and from checking the cheap-to-test links first. This assumes the links are independent, which they are not entirely; treat it as an illustration of why chains are fragile, not a forecast.

    The habits in the simulation are all front-loaded: they cost days in the first weeks and save months later. That is the real content of "scoping". It is also why a kill criterion is a gift. If a project is going to fail at gate 3, you want it to fail in week 6 at a cost of AED 150,000, not in month 14 at AED 2 million. A team that has never killed a project has no real gates.

    Running it: cadence, change control and stakeholders

    Once started, a project is mostly about keeping many people aligned with little friction. A light structure goes a long way.

    • A short weekly update to the sponsor, in writing. Three lines: progress against the plan, one risk and what you need from them. Use the answer-first format (C2.1). Executives who are never surprised keep trusting you.
    • A steering group that decides, not just listens. Four to six people: sponsor, business owner, technology, risk or compliance, you. It meets at gates, with a decision paper circulated before. If a meeting has no decision on its agenda, cancel it.
    • A risk and dependency log. Each line has an owner and a date. Typical lines: data approval pending with a named team, release freeze in the integration system, validator capacity, key person leave, Ramadan and Eid timing (see the next section).
    • Change control for scope. New requests are welcome, written down and traded: "We can add that; it moves the pilot by three weeks, or we drop X. Which do you prefer?" The aim is to make the cost visible so the sponsor chooses, rather than saying no.
    • A named hand-over. Who will run this in production, retrain it and answer the regulator's questions in two years? If the answer is "the project team", you have a prototype, not a system (P2.1).
    • A benefits tracker. The business case is revisited after launch with real adoption and cost numbers. This protects the programme's credibility, and yours, when the numbers differ from the plan (A16).

    Finally, involve the control functions early, as collaborators. Invite model risk, information security and compliance to the framing meeting, not the final review. They will find the issues anyway; finding them in week 2 is a favour to everyone. Chapter-and-verse on what they expect is in G3.3 and C3.5.

    C3.4 · Working across cultures in Gulf organisations

    A note on how to read this section. Everything here is a tendency, drawn from research on organisational culture and from what practitioners report, not a rule about any person. Banks differ: a new digital bank in Dubai International Financial Centre or Abu Dhabi Global Market may feel very different from a long-established family-and-state-linked institution. Individuals differ more. Treat these as hypotheses to test in your first months, ask colleagues you trust, and be wary of anyone who gives you a confident one-line account of "how Arabs do business".

    With that caution, five patterns are useful to know.

    PatternWhat you may seeWhat to do
    Hierarchy and decision rightsDecisions are made by a few senior people. Grades and titles matter. Challenge in a large meeting may feel like a loss of face for a senior person. Junior staff may defer in public.Find out who decides and who advises. Pre-wire: brief key people one-to-one before a meeting so nothing important is a surprise. Put facts in front of the decider early, in a form they can use.
    Relationships come before transactionsTrust is built through repeated contact, time spent in conversation and introductions through people who vouch for you. A cold email to an unknown team may sit unanswered.Meet in person where you can. Ask your sponsor to introduce you. Spend time on people, not only tasks, and keep promises small and exact.
    Indirect communication, especially for bad news"We will see", "let us revert", or a polite silence can mean "not yet", "not me" or "no". Disagreement can come as a question, a delay or a comment after the meeting.Ask concrete follow-up questions with names and dates. Make it easy to say "no" safely by offering options. Check understanding by asking people to restate the next steps.
    A multinational workplaceEspecially in the UAE, most of the workforce comes from abroad: South Asia, the Levant, Egypt, Europe, Africa, the Philippines and more, working alongside Emirati and Saudi colleagues. Each person brings different habits about directness, hierarchy and speaking up, and English is often a second or third language.Keep language plain. Put decisions and actions in writing straight after meetings. Do not assume one style is "the" culture. Where residency or visa status depends on a job, staff may be cautious about contradicting a manager; give people routes that feel safe.
    Calendar and rhythmRamadan brings shorter working days and a different energy; Eid holidays cluster; the week differs by country (a Saturday–Sunday weekend in the UAE since 2022, Friday–Saturday in Saudi Arabia). Prayer times and local public holidays shape meeting times. Hijri-based dates can shift by a day.Plan gates and releases around Ramadan and Eid with the calendar in front of you. Check public-holiday dates for the year, because some are announced late. Do not schedule a big deadline for the week before Eid.

    Notice what these have in common: they change the route to a good decision, not the standard of truth. You are never asked to hide a defect. You are asked to deliver it in a way the organisation can hear. A direct-style colleague from a flat organisation may see pre-wiring as slow or political; to the person receiving the news, it is respect and it gets the problem fixed sooner.

    Three cautions that matter for a data scientist in particular.

    • Hierarchy can mute bad news. If juniors will not volunteer problems, your project's early warnings disappear. Counter it with structure: ask specific questions ("what in the data looks odd to you?"), invite concerns one-to-one, thank people visibly when they bring bad news, and make "I found a problem" a normal sentence.
    • Language needs real effort. Customer-facing text, complaints and documents are often Arabic, English or both, with dialects. A reviewer who is better in Arabic will give better feedback on Arabic output (A14). Offer slides and one-page summaries in both languages when the audience warrants it.
    • Nationalisation targets are part of the landscape. Emiratisation in the UAE and Saudisation in Saudi Arabia set hiring expectations in the financial sector. As a newcomer, mentoring, handing over knowledge and building capability in national colleagues is both valued and part of a sustainable project. Check the current targets with HR rather than relying on what you read; they change.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Fraud detection. High value, clear baseline and clear owner, but real-time integration and the cost of false alerts make stages 3–4 the long ones (P5.1).
    • Credit and IFRS 9 models. Validation, governance and documentation dominate timelines; budget for them from day one.
    • AML and KYC. Regulatory scrutiny is high, labels are scarce, and compliance is the owner. Early involvement of compliance decides whether the project ships.
    • Contact centre and staff copilots. Adoption is the main risk, as in Simulation 1. Plan training and change management as work packages, not afterthoughts.
    • Cross-border or multinational group projects. Data residency and group-versus-local decision rights add stages. Name who decides, in each country.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    Project delivery is less a research field than a body of evidence and practice. The live questions are why so many AI pilots stall, how generative AI changes the picture, and how supervisors in the region expect banks to govern AI.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with a few searches. The survey figures below come from vendor and consultancy reports of varying quality; use them as a sense of scale and go to the original before quoting a number.

    1. 2015
      Hidden technical debt in machine learning

      Google engineers argued that the model is a small part of a real ML system; data pipelines, configuration, serving and monitoring make up most of the code and the maintenance cost. A foundation for thinking about run cost and hand-over.

      Sculley et al., NeurIPS, 2015
    2. 2023
      Reference-class forecasting for big projects

      Flyvbjerg and Gardner's book draws on a large database of projects to argue that the usual failure is not bad luck but optimistic planning, and that you improve by "thinking slow, acting fast": plan carefully, then deliver in small, quick steps.

      Flyvbjerg & Gardner, How Big Things Get Done, 2023
    3. 2024
      The root causes of AI project failure

      RAND interviewed 65 data scientists and engineers and reported that more than 80% of AI projects fail, twice the rate of ordinary IT projects. Their five root causes are mostly about people and scoping: misunderstood problems, missing data, chasing technology instead of the problem, weak infrastructure, and problems too hard for AI.

      Ryseff, De Bruhl & Newberry, RAND, 2024
    4. 2025
      Many generative AI pilots deliver little, and agent projects are expected to be cancelled

      An MIT NANDA report widely cited the figure that about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots show no measurable profit-and-loss impact, though its methodology has been criticised. Gartner predicted that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 because of cost, unclear value or inadequate risk controls. Both are forecasts or contested estimates, not measurements.

      MIT NANDA, 2025 (from memory; check the original); Gartner press release, June 2025
    5. 2026
      Pilots still stall, mostly on plumbing

      A Zapier and Centiment survey fielded in May 2026 reported that about 84% of respondents had at least one AI pilot that never reached deployment, with IT infrastructure, data quality and integration the commonest blockers. Another 2026 survey of enterprise technology leaders found most had agent pilots but only a small minority had scaled one across the organisation, with missing production monitoring a frequent reason. Treat both as indicative, not as definitive rates.

      Zapier, 2026; Digital Applied, March 2026 (secondary summaries)
    6. 2026
      CBUAE guidance on responsible AI for licensed institutions

      The Central Bank of the UAE issued guidance on consumer protection and the responsible use of AI and machine learning by the institutions it supervises in early 2026. Law-firm commentary describes it as non-binding, but expected to shape supervision: documented governance in proportion to risk, periodic testing for bias, and accountability that stays with the bank when a third party supplies the system. Commentators disagree on the exact publication date, and I did not find equivalent SAMA material; read the primary text on the CBUAE rulebook, and ask your compliance team what applies to your bank.

      Commentary by Pinsent Masons, Hadef & Partners and others (secondary); primary text not reviewed here

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These patterns are common in bank AI work, and every one can be spotted early. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Pilot purgatory

      The pilot succeeds on its own terms and is never decided. It runs for quarters, with a small team keeping it alive.

      Spot itAsk: what exact result would make us stop, and by when? If no one can answer, there are no kill criteria.
    • Solution looking for a problem

      "We should do something with LLMs." The use case is chosen to fit the technology.

      Spot itAsk who does the work today, what it costs and who will lose time or gain money. No named person means no owner.
    • No business owner

      The data team sponsors its own project. The business is "supportive".

      Spot itAsk whose annual targets change if this works. If nobody's do, nobody will fund integration or training.
    • Enthusiast pilot, sceptic rollout

      The pilot users are volunteers. Wider adoption is much lower, and savings shrink.

      Spot itCheck how pilot users were chosen. Ask for adoption among a random or whole-team group, as in Simulation 1.
    • Counting freed time as cash

      "Saves 800 hours a month" becomes AED 96,000 of benefit with no plan for those hours.

      Spot itAsk what the freed time is used for: fewer overtime hours, no hiring, more volume or other work. Without an answer, discount the benefit.
    • Control functions met at the end

      Validation, security and privacy review appear in the last month and find structural problems.

      Spot itLook at the plan: when does model risk first appear? If after the build, expect delay.
    • Scope creep through goodwill

      Every small request is accepted to keep people happy. The date slips without anyone deciding it should.

      Spot itCompare the current backlog with the original scope page. If it has doubled and the date has not moved, someone is absorbing the cost.
    • Heroic prototype, no hand-over

      The model works on one analyst's laptop. Production needs a team that does not exist.

      Spot itAsk who is on call at 2 a.m. and who retrains it in a year. A person's name is the right answer.
    • Reading silence as agreement

      A quiet meeting or a soft "yes, we will see" is taken as approval. Problems surface late.

      Spot itLook for a date and a name attached to each yes. Check understanding by asking participants to restate next steps.
    • Stereotyping instead of observing

      Applying cultural generalisations to individuals, or using them as an excuse for avoiding a hard conversation.

      Spot itAsk yourself what you have actually observed from this person. Use patterns to form questions, then check with them and with colleagues you trust.
    • Calendar blind spots

      A release or gate scheduled in the week before Eid or in the middle of Ramadan, with reduced availability on the other side.

      Spot itOverlay the plan on the public-holiday and Ramadan calendar for the year, with the bank's own freeze dates.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a sponsor at kick-off · 30 seconds
    “To make this land, I'd like to agree three things in the first fortnight: who in the business owns the outcome and will own the result, what we will measure against today's baseline, and what result would make us stop. I'll also bring model risk and information security in now, so nothing surprises us in month six. In return I'll give you a one-page update every week and a clear go or stop decision at each gate.”
    When something is not working · to a senior stakeholder, privately first
    “I'd like to show you something before the steering meeting, so you're not hearing it for the first time in front of the group. The pilot result holds for the early users, but on newer customers recall is lower than the deck says. Here is the corrected number, here is what I think explains it, and here are two options. I'd suggest we present it as a refinement with a plan. Which option do you prefer?”
    “The demo works. Why can't we launch next month?”

    The demo shows the model can do the task. Launch needs four more things: the data and security approvals, integration into the workflow, validation and monitoring, and a team that will run it. I'd suggest we list those with owners and dates this week, so we can see which are on the critical path. Two of them can start now in parallel.

    “How much will it save?”

    I'll give a range, not a point. The inputs are volume, time saved per case, how many staff will use it and how much of the freed time turns into real cost or capacity. The pilot gives the first two. We'll know adoption and realisation only after a wider rollout, so I'd present a base case and a cautious case, and I'd track both after launch.

    “Can we add this other feature? It's a small thing.”

    Yes, and I'd like to be clear about the trade. Adding it moves the pilot by about three weeks, or we can drop the second dashboard to keep the date. Which is more valuable to you? I'll note the decision in the scope page so we all remember it.

    “A colleague says this approach is how things are done here. You disagree on the method. What do you do?”

    Ask to understand the reasoning first, in private. There is often a reason: a regulatory expectation, a past incident, a sponsor preference. If I still think it is a risk, I'd bring a small piece of evidence, not an opinion, such as a test on last quarter's data. And I'd offer my colleague the chance to present the result jointly. The goal is a shared fix, not winning.

    “How do you work with a team from many different backgrounds?”

    Keep language plain, put decisions and actions in writing straight after each meeting, and make it safe to raise problems: specific questions, one-to-one check-ins, visible thanks when someone flags a risk. I avoid assuming how any individual will behave from where they are from; I watch, ask and adjust.

    “Why didn't you tell us the problem in the meeting?”

    I raised it with you beforehand so we could prepare a plan, not because I wanted to hide it. The facts presented in the meeting are the same facts you saw. I'd rather bring a problem with options than a problem alone. If I should have raised it earlier, tell me what timing works best for you.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the arithmetic from Part 2.

    1. Build cost AED 600,000, running cost AED 15,000 a month. 30,000 calls a month, 2 minutes saved, 70% adoption, 50% of freed time realised, AED 120 an hour. What is the payback?

    Hours saved = 30,000 × 0.70 × 2 ÷ 60 = 700. At AED 120 and 50% realisation: 700 × 120 × 0.5 = AED 42,000 benefit. Net = 42,000 − 15,000 = 27,000 a month. Payback = 600,000 ÷ 27,000 ≈ 22 months.

    2. Five gates pass with probabilities 0.8, 0.8, 0.7, 0.7 and 0.6. What share reaches production, and what happens if you raise the 0.6 to 0.85?

    0.8 × 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.188, about 19%. With 0.85: 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.85 = 0.267, about 27%. A single fix to the weakest gate raises output by more than 40% in relative terms.

    3. Why is a "kill criterion" more useful written before the pilot than after?

    Before, no one has a stake in the result, so it can be decided on the merits. After, people have invested time and reputation, and every result looks like it needs "one more round". Writing it first also forces the team to define what success means.

    4. A pilot with 20 volunteer agents shows 80% less wrap-up time. What do you ask before extrapolating?

    How the 20 were chosen; what adoption looked like among a representative group; whether the time saved is measured or self-reported; and what the freed time becomes. Volunteers are enthusiastic and usually good users, so the pilot overstates adoption and effect.

    5. Why bring model risk and information security in at framing rather than at the end?

    Their requirements shape the design (what data may be used, where it sits, how it is validated and monitored). Learning them in week 2 changes a plan; learning them in month 6 changes the product. They also have capacity limits, so booking validators early protects the timeline.

    6. A senior stakeholder says "inshallah, we'll look at it" to your request for branch data. What is a sensible response?

    Do not treat it as approval or as a refusal. Find who actually owns the data, make a small, specific request with a date, and ask your sponsor for an introduction. Follow up in person or by call. Remember this is one individual; the phrase can mean many things, and the answer is in whether a person and a date get attached to it.

    7. You find an error that makes the headline number too good, the night before a steering meeting. Does a culture of pre-wiring mean you can keep quiet until later?

    No. Pre-wiring changes the order and the delivery, not the facts or the deadline. Speak to the sponsor privately beforehand with the corrected number, the cause and options, then present the same facts in the room. Staying silent is a breach of professional honesty and usually costs far more trust later.
