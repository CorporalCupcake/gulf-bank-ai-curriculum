    From Idea to Production: Scoping, Running and Landing an AI Project in a Gulf Bank

    How to shape a project so it can finish, what a business case must admit, which gates and kill criteria stop endless pilots, and how hierarchy, relationships and multinational teams change the way you get a decision.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost AI projects stall not on modelling but on things that were never scoped (who owns the outcome, who approves the data, who changes the workflow), so write those down in week one, set the stopping rules before you start, and learn how decisions really get made in the organisation you are in.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A pilot is a taste test. A project is running the restaurant. Take a contact centre with 40,000 calls a month, where a model drafts call summaries so agents stop typing for two minutes after each call. The demo takes three weeks, 20 hand-picked agents love it, and the pilot reports "about 80% less wrap-up time". Then nothing happens for nine months:

    • The summaries must sit inside the CRM, owned by another team with a release freeze.
    • Information security and model risk ask about customer data and monitoring, and the agents' head and the budget sit in different teams.

    The pilot is neither killed nor shipped. It is "ongoing". The model was not the problem. The usual causes are data access, integration, unclear success criteria, no owner and no monitoring. All are scoping and running problems, cheap to fix in week one and expensive in month nine.

    The second half covers the room you will work in. A Gulf bank is often hierarchical, relationship-driven and multinational at once. That is information about how to get a decision made and how to hear bad news.

    Three takeaways.

    • Adoption and realisation are the multipliers pilots never measure. Adoption is the share of calls where agents use the tool. Realisation is the share of freed time that becomes money, for example more calls with the same people, less overtime or avoided hires. Otherwise it is slack, worth less than a salary.
    • Running cost is part of the design. A prototype costing AED 90,000 a month to serve may need a smaller model (P3).
    • A scope is a set of decisions, not a feature list. Who uses it, what changes for them, who owns the result, and what would make us stop?
    Hold onto thisA pilot proves something can work. A project proves it will work here, for everyone, at a cost the bank accepts. Scope for the second from day one, and write down what evidence would make you stop.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C3.3 · Scoping: the one page you write before the model

    A scope is a trip itinerary: destination, driver, cost, and when you turn back. One page answers ten questions. Any you cannot answer is your first task.

    QuestionGood answer
    1. Decision or actionA verb, not a model: "which calls get a summary".
    2. Owner of the outcomeOne business executive whose targets move if this works. Not the data team.
    3. Users and workflowWho uses it daily, where on their screen, what they stop doing.
    4. BaselineToday's number (C1.3).
    5. Success and kill criteria"Proceed if recall at 300 alerts a day beats X; stop if not by week 10."
    6. Data and accessTables, owners, legal basis, where data may sit (A11).
    7. Control requirementsIs it a model under policy (G3.1)? Who validates?
    8. Integration and operationsSystems it touches; uptime, on-call, retraining.
    9. Cost and appetiteBuild and run cost, and how long before deciding.
    10. Out of scopeA written list of what you will not do now.

    Appetite (from Shape Up): decide how much time the problem is worth, say six weeks, then shape the solution to fit. Fixed time, variable scope.

    Estimate from the outside. Ask the security and model-risk teams how long a typical use case took last year. Queue times often exceed modelling time.

    A useful opener: "Before I build anything, can I confirm who owns the outcome, what we will measure, and what would make us stop?" It is your cheapest risk reduction.

    Stages, gates and why projects stall

    A gate is a checkpoint on a hiking route: continue, change route or turn back. Each stage ends in a short decision meeting with a pre-agreed answer. Gates make stopping early cheap and drifting expensive.

    StageQuestionHanded over at the gate
    0. FrameWorth doing at all?One-page scope, rough case
    1. Baseline and data checkDoes the data exist? What does do-nothing score?Baseline, data approvals, leakage audit
    2. Thin slice, offlineCan a simple model beat the baseline on honest, time-ordered data?Result with an interval (F2.1), go or stop
    3. Pilot in the real workflowDo real users on real systems get the value?Adoption, outcome and cost (F3.6)
    4. Production readinessCan it be run, monitored and defended?Validation pack (C2.3), security sign-off, runbook
    5. Run and reviewStill working and still worth it?Periodic review (G3.5), benefits tracking

    Projects stall because production is a chain, and odds multiply along a chain. Five "pretty likely" links (60–75% each: data, measurable result, risk approval, integration, adoption) leave roughly a one-in-seven chance. Fix the weakest link first.

    The habits are all front-loaded: they cost days early and save months later. That is why a kill criterion is a gift. Failing at gate 3 in week 6 costs AED 150,000. Failing in month 14 costs AED 2 million. A team that has never killed a project has no real gates.

    Running it: cadence, change control and stakeholders

    Running a project means keeping many people aligned with little friction. Three habits do most of it.

    • A weekly written update to the sponsor. Three lines: progress, one risk, what you need (C2.1).
    • A steering group that decides. Four to six people, meeting at gates with a decision paper circulated first.
    • Change control for scope. Trade requests openly: "We can add that; it moves the pilot three weeks, or we drop X. Which do you prefer?"

    Also name who runs, retrains and defends it in two years (P2.1), and revisit the business case after launch (A16).

    Involve the control functions early. Invite model risk, security and compliance to the framing meeting, not the final review. See G3.3 and C3.5.

    C3.4 · Working across cultures in Gulf organisations

    Everything here is a tendency, not a rule about any person, like a weather forecast: useful for packing, wrong for any given minute. Treat these as hypotheses to test in your first months. Five patterns are worth knowing.

    PatternWhat you may seeWhat to do
    Hierarchy and decision rightsA few senior people decide. Public challenge may cost a senior person face; juniors may defer in meetings.Find who decides. Pre-wire: brief key people one-to-one so nothing important is a surprise.
    Relationships before transactionsTrust comes from repeated contact and introductions. A cold email to an unknown team may sit unanswered.Meet in person. Ask your sponsor to introduce you. Keep promises small and exact.
    Indirect communication, especially bad news"We will see" or polite silence can mean "not yet", "not me" or "no".Ask follow-ups with names and dates. Offer options so "no" is safe to say.
    A multinational workplaceMost staff, especially in the UAE, come from abroad. Habits on directness vary, and English is often a second or third language.Keep language plain. Put decisions and actions in writing straight after meetings.
    Calendar and rhythmRamadan brings shorter days; Eid holidays cluster; weekends differ by country.Plan gates and releases around Ramadan and Eid, and check dates yearly.

    These change the route to a good decision, not the standard of truth. You never hide a defect. Pre-wiring can look political, but to the person receiving the news it is respect.

    Three cautions for a data scientist.

    • Hierarchy can mute bad news. If juniors will not volunteer problems, your early warnings vanish. Ask specific questions ("what in the data looks odd?"), invite concerns one-to-one and thank people visibly.
    • Language needs real effort. A reviewer stronger in Arabic gives better feedback on Arabic output (A14).
    • Nationalisation targets are part of the landscape. Emiratisation and Saudisation set hiring expectations, and mentoring national colleagues is valued.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each is common and can be spotted early.

    • Pilot purgatory

      The pilot succeeds on its own terms and is never decided. It runs for quarters.

      Spot itAsk what exact result would make us stop, and by when. No answer means no kill criteria.
    • No business owner

      The data team sponsors its own project, or the use case was picked to fit "something with LLMs". The business is "supportive".

      Spot itAsk whose annual targets change if this works. If nobody's, nobody funds integration or training.
    • Pilot numbers that do not survive rollout

      Pilot users are volunteers, so wider adoption is lower. "Saves 800 hours a month" becomes AED 96,000 of benefit with no plan for those hours.

      Spot itCheck how pilot users were chosen and what the freed time is used for. No answer, discount the benefit.
    • Control functions met at the end

      Validation, security and privacy review arrive in the last month and find structural problems.

      Spot itFind when model risk first appears in the plan. After the build means expect delay.
    • Drift and no hand-over

      Every small request is accepted to keep people happy, the date slips, and the model that works on one analyst's laptop needs a team that does not exist.

      Spot itCompare the backlog with the original scope page. Ask who is on call at 2 a.m. The right answer is a name.
    • Misreading the culture

      A soft "yes, we will see" is taken as approval, or generalisations are applied to individuals.

      Spot itLook for a date and a name on each yes. Use patterns to form questions, then check with the person and trusted colleagues.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer.

    1. In Simulation 1, why can low adoption or low realisation sink a project that looked fine in the pilot, while a somewhat higher build cost rarely does?

    Adoption and realisation multiply the benefit, which must first cover running cost. Low values can push net benefit to zero, so payback becomes "never". A higher build cost only stretches the payback time.

    2. Five gates each look likely (60–80%). Why is the overall chance of reaching production so low, and where do you act first?

    Odds multiply along the chain, so five decent gates leave about 19%. Act on the weakest gate: lifting a 60% gate to 85% takes the overall chance to about 27%, over 40% more output.

    3. Why write a kill criterion before the pilot rather than after?

    Before, nobody has a stake in the result, so it is judged on merit. After, people have invested time and reputation, and every result needs "one more round".

    4. A pilot with 20 volunteer agents shows 80% less wrap-up time. What do you ask before extrapolating?

    How the 20 were chosen; adoption in a representative group; whether time saved is measured or self-reported; what the freed time becomes. Volunteers overstate adoption and effect.

    5. Why bring model risk and information security in at framing, not at the end?

    Their requirements shape the design: what data may be used, where it sits, how it is validated. Learning them in week 2 changes a plan; in month 6 it changes the product.

    6. A senior stakeholder says "inshallah, we'll look at it" to your request for branch data. What do you do?

    Treat it as neither approval nor refusal. Find who owns the data, make a small, specific request with a date, and ask your sponsor for an introduction.

    7. You find an error that makes the headline number too good, the night before a steering meeting. Does pre-wiring let you wait?

    No. Pre-wiring changes order and delivery, not facts or deadlines. Tell the sponsor privately beforehand with the corrected number, the cause and options, then present the same facts in the room.
