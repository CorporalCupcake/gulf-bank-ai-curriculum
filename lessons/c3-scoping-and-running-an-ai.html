    From Idea to Production: Scoping, Running and Landing an AI Project in a Gulf Bank

    How to shape a project so it can finish, what a business case must admit, which gates and kill criteria stop endless pilots, and how hierarchy, relationships and multinational teams change the way you get a decision.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMost AI projects stall not on modelling but on things that were never scoped (who owns the outcome, who approves the data, who changes the workflow), so write those down in week one, set the stopping rules before you start, and learn how decisions really get made in the organisation you are in.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A pilot is a taste test. A project is running the restaurant. Think of a contact centre handling 40,000 calls a month. A model drafts call summaries, so agents stop typing for two minutes after each call. The demo takes three weeks, 20 hand-picked agents love it, and the pilot reports "about 80% less wrap-up time".

    Then nothing happens for nine months:

    • The summaries must sit inside the CRM, owned by another team with a release freeze.
    • Information security asks about customer data leaving the network.
    • Model risk asks whether this counts as a "model" and how it will be monitored.
    • The contact-centre head owns the agents, but digital owns the budget.
    • The other 400 agents distrust summaries they did not write.

    The pilot is neither killed nor shipped. It is "ongoing". The model was not the problem. Surveys put the share of AI pilots that never reach production anywhere from a third to a large majority, and cite the same causes: data access, integration, unclear success criteria, no owner, no monitoring. All are scoping and running problems. They cost days to fix in week one and months in month nine.

    The second half of this lesson covers the room you will work in. A Gulf bank is usually hierarchical, relationship-driven and multinational at once, and English is the shared language but not everyone's first. That is not a problem. It is information about how to get a decision made and how to hear bad news.

    Three takeaways.

    • Adoption and realisation are the multipliers pilots never measure. Adoption is the share of calls where agents actually use the tool. Realisation is the share of freed time that becomes money. Saving two minutes a call is only cash if the bank handles more calls with the same people, cuts overtime or avoids hiring. Otherwise it is slack, which is worth something, but less than a salary. Enthusiasts measure the technology. A business case needs the whole workforce.
    • Running cost is part of the design. A prototype costing AED 90,000 a month to serve may be a good idea that a smaller model or different architecture makes viable (P3).
    • A scope is a set of decisions, not a feature list. Who uses it, what changes for them, who owns the result, and what would make us stop?
    Hold onto thisA pilot proves something can work. A project proves it will work here, for everyone, at a cost the bank accepts. Scope for the second from day one, and write down what evidence would make you stop.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    C3.3 · Scoping: the one page you write before the model

    A scope is a trip itinerary: destination, who is driving, what it costs, and when you turn back. C1 gave you a problem and a baseline. Scoping turns them into bounded work with an owner, a timeline and an ending. One page answers ten questions. Any you cannot answer is your first task.

    QuestionGood answerIf missing
    1. Decision or action"Which calls get a summary", "which payments go to an analyst". A verb, not a model.A model that scores things nobody acts on.
    2. Owner of the outcomeOne business executive whose targets move if this works. Not the data team.No one fights for integration, training or budget.
    3. Users and workflowWho touches it daily, where on their screen, what they stop doing.The tool sits in a separate window and is ignored.
    4. BaselineToday's number (C1.3): handling time, loss rate, review hours.No way to say it helped.
    5. Success and kill criteria"Proceed if recall at 300 alerts a day beats X on a time-ordered test; stop if not by week 10."The pilot cannot fail, so it never ends.
    6. Data and accessTables, owners, legal basis, where data may sit (A11), approvals.Months lost waiting for data-sharing approval.
    7. Control requirementsIs it a model under policy (G3.1)? Who validates? Risk tier? Fairness, privacy, explainability asks.A finished model that cannot be approved.
    8. Integration and operationsSystems it calls and that call it; latency, uptime, on-call, retraining.A notebook with no home.
    9. Cost and appetiteBuild and run cost, and how long you will spend before deciding.Open-ended work that fills the time.
    10. Out of scopeA written list of what you will not do now, including attractive extras.Creep that arrives as "while you're in there".

    Appetite (from Shape Up): decide how much time the problem is worth, say six weeks, then shape the solution to fit. It is like fixing a holiday budget first and picking the trip after. Fixed time, variable scope. It forces the "what is the thin slice that proves value?" conversation.

    Reference-class estimating: people plan from the inside, picturing everything going well, and run late. Flyvbjerg's work says start from how long similar projects actually took. Ask the platform, security and model-risk teams how long a typical new use case took last year. Queue times often exceed modelling time.

    A useful opener: "Before I build anything, can I confirm who owns the outcome, what we will measure, and what would make us stop?" It sounds like process and is your cheapest risk reduction.

    Stages, gates and the arithmetic of stalling

    A gate is a checkpoint on a hiking route: you decide there whether to continue, change route or turn back. Each stage ends in a short decision meeting with a pre-agreed answer to "continue, change or stop?". Gates make stopping early cheap and drifting expensive. Lengths are rough and vary between banks.

    StageQuestionHanded over at the gateLength
    0. FrameWorth doing at all?One-page scope, order-of-magnitude case1–2 weeks
    1. Baseline and data checkDoes the data exist? What does do-nothing score?Baseline, data-access approvals, leakage and label audit2–4 weeks
    2. Thin slice, offlineCan a simple model beat the baseline on honest, time-ordered data?Result with an interval (F2.1), error analysis, go or stop4–8 weeks
    3. Pilot in the real workflowDo real users on real systems get the value?Adoption, outcome and cost from a controlled trial (F3.6)6–12 weeks
    4. Production readinessCan it be run, monitored and defended?Validation pack (C2.3), security sign-off, monitoring, runbook, inventory entry4–12 weeks, often parallel with 3
    5. Run and reviewStill working and still worth it?Periodic review (G3.5), retraining plan, benefits trackingContinuous

    Projects stall because production is a chain, and chain probabilities multiply. A relay team is only as fast as its worst handover. With five conditions (data, measurable result, risk approval, integration, adoption), each holding with probability pi:

    P(reaches production)=p1×p2×…×p5

    Five "pretty likely" links (60–75%) give roughly a one-in-seven chance. Fix the weakest link first, and test the cheap links early. Links are not fully independent, so read this as an illustration of fragility, not a forecast.

    The habits are all front-loaded: they cost days early and save months later. That is what "scoping" means. It is also why a kill criterion is a gift. Failing at gate 3 in week 6 costs AED 150,000. Failing in month 14 costs AED 2 million. A team that has never killed a project has no real gates.

    Running it: cadence, change control and stakeholders

    Running a project means keeping many people aligned with little friction. Six light habits do most of it.

    • A weekly written update to the sponsor. Three lines: progress, one risk, what you need. Use the answer-first format (C2.1). Executives who are never surprised keep trusting you.
    • A steering group that decides. Four to six people: sponsor, business owner, technology, risk or compliance, you. It meets at gates with a decision paper circulated first. No decision on the agenda means cancel the meeting.
    • A risk and dependency log. Each line has an owner and a date: pending data approval, release freeze, validator capacity, key-person leave, Ramadan and Eid timing.
    • Change control for scope. Write requests down and trade them: "We can add that; it moves the pilot three weeks, or we drop X. Which do you prefer?" Make the cost visible so the sponsor chooses.
    • A named hand-over. Who runs it, retrains it and answers the regulator in two years? "The project team" means a prototype, not a system (P2.1).
    • A benefits tracker. Revisit the case after launch with real adoption and cost. It protects your credibility when numbers differ from plan (A16).

    Involve the control functions early, as collaborators. Invite model risk, information security and compliance to the framing meeting, not the final review. They will find the issues anyway, and week 2 is cheaper than month 6. See G3.3 and C3.5.

    C3.4 · Working across cultures in Gulf organisations

    Everything here is a tendency, not a rule about any person. Think of a weather forecast: useful for packing, wrong for any given minute. A new digital bank in DIFC or ADGM may feel very different from an established family-and-state-linked institution, and individuals differ more. Treat these as hypotheses to test in your first months, and be wary of anyone offering a one-line account of "how Arabs do business". Five patterns are worth knowing.

    PatternWhat you may seeWhat to do
    Hierarchy and decision rightsA few senior people decide. Grades and titles matter. Public challenge may cost a senior person face; juniors may defer in meetings.Find who decides and who advises. Pre-wire: brief key people one-to-one so nothing important is a surprise. Put facts in front of the decider early, in a usable form.
    Relationships before transactionsTrust comes from repeated contact and introductions by people who vouch for you. A cold email to an unknown team may sit unanswered.Meet in person. Ask your sponsor to introduce you. Spend time on people, and keep promises small and exact.
    Indirect communication, especially bad news"We will see", "let us revert" or polite silence can mean "not yet", "not me" or "no". Disagreement can arrive as a question, a delay or a comment after the meeting.Ask follow-ups with names and dates. Offer options so "no" is safe to say. Ask people to restate next steps.
    A multinational workplaceEspecially in the UAE, most staff come from abroad (South Asia, Levant, Egypt, Europe, Africa, Philippines), alongside Emirati and Saudi colleagues. Habits on directness, hierarchy and speaking up vary, and English is often a second or third language.Keep language plain. Put decisions and actions in writing straight after meetings. Where visas depend on a job, staff may avoid contradicting a manager, so give safe routes.
    Calendar and rhythmRamadan brings shorter days; Eid holidays cluster; weekends differ (Saturday–Sunday in the UAE since 2022, Friday–Saturday in Saudi Arabia). Hijri dates can shift by a day.Plan gates and releases around Ramadan and Eid. Check holiday dates yearly, as some are announced late. No big deadline the week before Eid.

    These change the route to a good decision, not the standard of truth. You never hide a defect; you deliver it in a way the organisation can hear. A colleague from a flat organisation may see pre-wiring as political. To the person receiving the news it is respect, and it gets the problem fixed sooner.

    Three cautions for a data scientist.

    • Hierarchy can mute bad news. If juniors will not volunteer problems, your early warnings vanish. Ask specific questions ("what in the data looks odd?"), invite concerns one-to-one, thank people visibly, and make "I found a problem" a normal sentence.
    • Language needs real effort. Customer text and documents are often Arabic, English or both, with dialects. A reviewer stronger in Arabic gives better feedback on Arabic output (A14). Offer slides and summaries in both languages when the audience warrants it.
    • Nationalisation targets are part of the landscape. Emiratisation (UAE) and Saudisation (Saudi Arabia) set hiring expectations. Mentoring and knowledge hand-over to national colleagues is valued and makes projects sustainable. Check current targets with HR; they change.

    Try three decisions below. Stronger and weaker moves exist, and the feedback says why.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each pattern is common in bank AI work and can be spotted early.

    • Pilot purgatory

      The pilot succeeds on its own terms and is never decided. It runs for quarters.

      Spot itAsk what exact result would make us stop, and by when. No answer means no kill criteria.
    • Solution looking for a problem

      "We should do something with LLMs." The use case is picked to fit the technology.

      Spot itAsk who does the work today, what it costs and who gains. No named person means no owner.
    • No business owner

      The data team sponsors its own project. The business is "supportive".

      Spot itAsk whose annual targets change if this works. If nobody's, nobody funds integration or training.
    • Enthusiast pilot, sceptic rollout

      Pilot users are volunteers. Wider adoption is much lower and savings shrink.

      Spot itCheck how pilot users were chosen. Ask for adoption across a random or whole team, as in Simulation 1.
    • Counting freed time as cash

      "Saves 800 hours a month" becomes AED 96,000 of benefit with no plan for those hours.

      Spot itAsk what the time is used for: less overtime, no hiring, more volume. No answer, discount the benefit.
    • Control functions met at the end

      Validation, security and privacy review arrive in the last month and find structural problems.

      Spot itFind when model risk first appears in the plan. After the build means expect delay.
    • Scope creep through goodwill

      Every small request is accepted to keep people happy. The date slips without anyone deciding it should.

      Spot itCompare the backlog with the original scope page. Doubled backlog with the same date means someone is absorbing the cost.
    • Heroic prototype, no hand-over

      The model works on one analyst's laptop. Production needs a team that does not exist.

      Spot itAsk who is on call at 2 a.m. and who retrains it in a year. The right answer is a name.
    • Reading silence as agreement

      A quiet meeting or a soft "yes, we will see" is taken as approval. Problems surface late.

      Spot itLook for a date and a name on each yes. Ask participants to restate next steps.
    • Stereotyping instead of observing

      Applying generalisations to individuals, or using them to avoid a hard conversation.

      Spot itAsk what you have actually observed from this person. Use patterns to form questions, then check with them and trusted colleagues.
    • Calendar blind spots

      A release or gate lands the week before Eid or mid-Ramadan, with reduced availability around it.

      Spot itOverlay the plan on the holiday and Ramadan calendar and the bank's freeze dates.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only Part 2 arithmetic.

    1. Build cost AED 600,000, running cost AED 15,000 a month. 30,000 calls a month, 2 minutes saved, 70% adoption, 50% of freed time realised, AED 120 an hour. What is the payback?

    Hours saved = 30,000 × 0.70 × 2 ÷ 60 = 700. Benefit = 700 × 120 × 0.5 = AED 42,000. Net = 42,000 − 15,000 = 27,000 a month. Payback = 600,000 ÷ 27,000 ≈ 22 months.

    2. Five gates pass with probabilities 0.8, 0.8, 0.7, 0.7 and 0.6. What share reaches production, and what if you raise the 0.6 to 0.85?

    0.8 × 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.188, about 19%. With 0.85 it is 0.267, about 27%. Fixing the weakest gate alone raises output by over 40% in relative terms.

    3. Why write a kill criterion before the pilot rather than after?

    Before, nobody has a stake in the result, so it is judged on merit. After, people have invested time and reputation, and every result needs "one more round". Writing it first also forces a definition of success.

    4. A pilot with 20 volunteer agents shows 80% less wrap-up time. What do you ask before extrapolating?

    How the 20 were chosen; adoption in a representative group; whether time saved is measured or self-reported; what the freed time becomes. Volunteers are enthusiastic, so the pilot overstates adoption and effect.

    5. Why bring model risk and information security in at framing, not at the end?

    Their requirements shape the design: what data may be used, where it sits, how it is validated and monitored. Learning them in week 2 changes a plan; in month 6 it changes the product. Validators also have limited capacity, so booking early protects the timeline.

    6. A senior stakeholder says "inshallah, we'll look at it" to your request for branch data. What do you do?

    Treat it as neither approval nor refusal. Find who owns the data, make a small, specific request with a date, and ask your sponsor for an introduction. Follow up in person or by call. The phrase can mean many things; the answer is whether a person and a date get attached.

    7. You find an error that makes the headline number too good, the night before a steering meeting. Does pre-wiring let you wait?

    No. Pre-wiring changes order and delivery, not facts or deadlines. Tell the sponsor privately beforehand with the corrected number, the cause and options, then present the same facts in the room. Staying silent breaches professional honesty and costs far more trust later.
