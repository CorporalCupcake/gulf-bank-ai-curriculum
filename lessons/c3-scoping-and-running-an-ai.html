    Scoping and Running an AI Project

    Part 1

    Pilot versus project

    In one sentenceScoping is writing down, before you build, who owns the result, what you will measure and what would make you stop. Running is keeping everyone aligned until it is live.

    A pilot is a taste test. A project is running the restaurant. A pilot proves something can work. A project proves it will work here, for everyone, at a cost the bank accepts.

    Take a contact centre with 40,000 calls a month. A model drafts call summaries so agents stop typing for two minutes after each call. Twenty hand-picked agents love the pilot. Then nothing happens for nine months, because the summaries must sit inside a system owned by another team, security and model risk have questions, and nobody owns the budget. The model was never the problem.

    How it differs from a data-science task

    • A task ends when the model scores well. A project ends when real users use it and the benefit shows up in the numbers.
    • A scope is a set of decisions, not a feature list: who uses it, what changes for them, who owns the result and when you stop.
    Part 2

    How it works

    The one-page scope

    Write this page before the model. Any question you cannot answer is your first task.

    • Decision: a verb, not a model, such as "which calls get a summary".
    • Owner: one business executive whose targets move if this works. Not the data team.
    • Users and workflow: who uses it daily, where, and what they stop doing.
    • Baseline: today's number, to beat.
    • Success and kill criteria: "proceed if X; stop if not by week 10".
    • Data and controls: tables, owners, legal basis, and who validates the model.
    • Operations and cost: systems touched, who is on call, build and running cost.
    • Out of scope: a written list of what you will not do now.

    A useful opener: "Before I build anything, can I confirm who owns the outcome, what we will measure, and what would make us stop?"

    Stages and gates

    A gate is a checkpoint on a hiking route: continue, change route or turn back. Each stage ends in a short decision with an agreed answer.

    1. Frame: is it worth doing? Output: the one-page scope.
    2. Baseline and data check: does the data exist, and what does doing nothing score?
    3. Thin slice, offline: can a simple model beat the baseline on honest, time-ordered data?
    4. Pilot in the real workflow: do real users on real systems get the value?
    5. Production readiness: can it be run, monitored and defended? Validation pack, security sign-off, runbook.
    6. Run and review: is it still working and still worth it?

    Five "pretty likely" links leave a small chance. Illustrative numbers, not bank data. Fix the weakest link first.

    Running it

    • Weekly written update to the sponsor. Three lines: progress, one risk, what you need.
    • A steering group that decides. Four to six people, meeting at gates, with a decision paper sent first.
    • Change control for scope. Trade openly: "We can add that; it moves the pilot three weeks, or we drop X. Which do you prefer?"
    • Involve control functions early. Invite model risk, security and compliance to the framing meeting, not the final review.
    Part 3

    How to read a business case

    Pilots overstate benefit because they skip two multipliers. Adoption is the share of calls where agents really use the tool. Realisation is the share of freed time that becomes money (more calls with the same people, less overtime, avoided hires). Otherwise it is slack.

    Pilot claim
    40,000 calls × 2 minutes ÷ 60 × AED 120 an hour = about AED 160,000 a month.
    Roll-out reality
    Adoption 30%, realisation 20%: AED 160,000 × 0.3 × 0.2 = about AED 9,600 a month. A tool costing AED 5,000 a month to run nets AED 4,600.
    Payback
    A build cost of AED 600,000 at AED 4,600 net a month takes over ten years. The pilot suggested months.
    What it tells you
    Low adoption or realisation can push net benefit to zero, so payback becomes "never". A somewhat higher build cost only stretches the payback.

    Working in a Gulf organisation

    These are tendencies, like a weather forecast: useful for packing, wrong for any given minute. Test them against what your sponsor and trusted colleagues tell you.

    • Hierarchy: a few senior people decide. Find them, and brief key people one-to-one so nothing important is a surprise in a meeting.
    • Relationships first: trust comes from repeated contact. Meet in person and ask your sponsor for introductions.
    • Indirect bad news: "we will see" can mean "not yet" or "no". Ask for a name and a date on every yes, and offer options so no is safe to say.
    • Mixed workforce: keep language plain and put decisions in writing after meetings.
    • Calendar: plan gates and releases around Ramadan and Eid.

    These change the route to a decision, never the truth. You never hide a defect.

    Part 4

    Which approach to use

    ApproachFixesCosts
    Open-ended pilotQuick demoOften stalls with no decision
    Gated projectCheap, early stoppingNeeds a scope and kill criteria up front
    Fixed time, variable scopeDrift, endless requestsYou must cut features to fit
    • Strong at: making stopping cheap. Failing at a gate in week 6 costs far less than failing in month 14.
    • Weak at: speed on day one. The checks cost days early and save months later.

    In banking, estimate from the outside: ask the security and model-risk teams how long a typical use case took last year. Queue times often exceed modelling time.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Pilot purgatory

      The pilot succeeds and is never decided. Ask what exact result would make you stop, and by when. No answer means no kill criteria.

    • No business owner

      The data team sponsors its own project. Ask whose annual targets change if it works. If nobody's, nobody funds integration.

    • Trusting pilot numbers

      Pilot users are volunteers, so adoption is lower later. Check how they were chosen and what freed time becomes.

    • Meeting control functions at the end

      Validation and security reviews arrive late and find structural problems.

    1. Why can low adoption sink a project that looked fine in the pilot?

    Adoption multiplies the benefit, which must first cover running cost. Low adoption can push net benefit to zero, so payback becomes "never".

    2. Five gates each look likely. Why is the overall chance low?

    Odds multiply along the chain, so five decent gates leave a small chance. Act on the weakest gate first.

    3. Why write a kill criterion before the pilot?

    Before, nobody has a stake in the result. After, people have invested time and reputation, and every result needs "one more round".

    4. A pilot with 20 volunteer agents shows 80% less wrap-up time. What do you ask?

    How the 20 were chosen, whether adoption in a typical group is lower, whether time saved was measured, and what the freed time becomes.

    5. A senior stakeholder says "inshallah, we'll look at it" to a data request. What now?

    Treat it as neither approval nor refusal. Find who owns the data, make a small, specific request with a date, and ask your sponsor for an introduction.
