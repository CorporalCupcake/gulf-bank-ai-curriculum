    Paying for Thought: Reasoning Models, Test-Time Compute and Models You Run Yourself

    What a "thinking" model buys you and what it costs in tokens, money and seconds, when voting beats thinking, and how distillation, quantization and open weights let a bank run a capable model inside its own walls.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA reasoning model spends extra tokens at answer time to be right more often on hard problems, with sharply diminishing returns and a bill that grows with every token, while a small or quantized open-weight model trades some capability for control, and the skill is knowing which problems deserve which.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Ask a colleague "what is 17 × 24?" and you get an answer in a second. Ask "does this trade finance file breach our sanctions policy?" and a good analyst reads, cross-checks, backtracks and only then answers. Same person, very different effort. The effort is the difference.

    A standard language model gives every question the same effort: one pass, one token at a time, with almost no room to reconsider. A reasoning model is trained to write out a long working-out first (often hidden, sometimes summarised), trying approaches, checking itself and correcting course, and only then to answer. The extra tokens are scratch paper. More scratch paper means more thinking, and more thinking costs more, in both money and waiting time.

    This is called test-time compute (or inference-time compute): computing power spent when the model answers, not when it was trained. For a decade progress came mostly from spending more on training. Since late 2024 a second dial has opened: leave the model alone and let it think longer on the question in front of it.

    The second half of this lesson is the opposite worry. If thinking makes tokens more expensive, and if some data cannot leave the bank, can you run a smaller model yourself? Often yes. You shrink a model by teaching a small one to imitate a big one (distillation), by storing its numbers in fewer bits (quantization), and by starting from open weights you are allowed to host in your own data centre. Each step gives up something, and the trade is worth understanding precisely.

    Hold onto thisThinking is a dial, not a feature. Every use case should get the lowest setting that meets its accuracy target, because cost and latency both grow with the thinking tokens, while accuracy flattens. Model size and hosting are a second dial with the same logic: the smallest, most local model that clears the bar.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A5.1 · What thinking buys you, and what it costs

    How the model learns to think. The base recipe is in M1 (pretraining then post-training). For reasoning, the post-training step uses reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers: maths, code, logic puzzles, structured tasks. The model writes a long chain of thought, a verifier checks only the final answer, and the model is rewarded when it is right. Nobody writes the reasoning for it. Behaviours such as "wait, that doesn't add up, let me retry" emerge because they lead to more correct answers. This is the story told in the DeepSeek-R1 paper (2025), and the major labs' reasoning models follow the same broad idea, though their exact recipes are not public.

    What it buys. On tasks with a verifiable structure (multi-step arithmetic, code, reconciling figures, applying a rule with exceptions) accuracy rises with the amount of thinking, roughly with each doubling of tokens giving a smaller gain than the last. On tasks that are mostly recall or style (summarise this email, translate this letter) there is little to gain, because the answer was never limited by the amount of thought.

    What it costs. Three bills arrive together.

    • Money. Thinking tokens are output tokens, and output tokens are the expensive kind, usually several times the price of input tokens. You pay for them even if the provider shows you only a summary of the thinking.
    • Latency. A model writes at roughly tens to a few hundred tokens a second. A 20,000-token think at 80 tokens a second is a four-minute wait. That rules thinking out of anything a customer waits on in real time, such as a card authorisation or a chat reply.
    • Variance. The number of thinking tokens differs from one question to the next, so cost and delay per request are less predictable. Budgeting needs a distribution, not an average.

    Simulation 1 puts numbers on the trade for three levels of problem difficulty.

    The same curve explains what vendors now sell as an effort or thinking budget setting: low, medium, high, or a token cap. Some systems also decide for themselves how long to think, a feature usually called adaptive or hybrid reasoning. Research suggests models are still imperfect at rationing their own effort: they can overthink easy questions and under-think hard ones, and past a point more thinking can even talk a model out of a correct answer (see Part 4).

    Thinking is not the only way to spend test-time compute. The other main route is sampling several answers and combining them, often called self-consistency or best-of-n. Ask the same question k times at some randomness, then take the most common final answer (majority vote) or the one a verifier scores highest. It works because independent errors tend to scatter while the right answer repeats. Its limit is exactly that independence. If the model is consistently wrong on a question, because it misreads a clause the same way each time, then ten votes are ten copies of the same mistake.

    P(majority right)=∑j>k/2k(kj)pj(1−p)k−j

    p is the chance a single sample is right; k is the number of samples. If p = 0.7 and k = 5, majority voting is right with probability 0.837, up from 0.7. This assumes samples are independent and that wrong answers do not all agree, which are the generous assumptions. With p below 0.5 the vote makes things worse: it amplifies whichever answer is more common.

    Verifiers and process rewards. Voting asks "which answer is most common?". A verifier asks "which answer is correct?", and can be much better when checking is easier than solving (running unit tests on generated code, recomputing a total). A process reward model scores each reasoning step, not only the end. These are the engines behind much of the progress and are covered in R3 on verifiers. For banking, the practical lesson is that checkable work, such as reconciliation or rule application, is where reasoning pays most, because you can verify the result independently.

    Is the visible reasoning true? Not necessarily. Studies find that a model's written chain of thought does not always reflect what actually drove its answer; it can omit a hint it used, or rationalise afterwards. So the thinking text is useful evidence, not a certified audit trail. R4 goes into this, and it matters directly for explainability claims to validators. Some research also explores reasoning that never becomes text, in the model's hidden vectors (R1 on latent reasoning).

    A5.2 · Small and on-premises models

    Three techniques make a model smaller. They are different things and often combined.

    TechniqueWhat it doesWhat you give up
    DistillationA small "student" is trained on the outputs (answers, probabilities or reasoning traces) of a large "teacher". The student learns to imitate the behaviour, not the size.Breadth. A student matches the teacher on the tasks it was taught and falls behind on the rest. Licensing: some providers' terms forbid using outputs to train competing models.
    QuantizationStores each weight in fewer bits: 16-bit to 8-bit or 4-bit. The model is the same shape, just stored coarsely, so it needs a quarter to a half of the memory.A little precision. At 8 bits the loss is usually tiny; at 4 bits it is usually small for large models and larger for small ones; below that it grows quickly (see R12).
    Pruning and sparsityRemoves weights or whole components that contribute little.Needs retraining to recover quality, and real speed gains require hardware that exploits the sparsity.

    The full treatment is P3. Here we need the consequence: what decides how big a machine you need? Two things fill the GPU memory.

    • The weights. Parameters × bytes each. A 70-billion-parameter model at 16 bits is 70 × 2 = 140 GB. At 4 bits it is 70 × 0.5 = 35 GB.
    • The KV cache. For every user in the conversation, the model keeps its working memory of the text so far (M1 on the KV cache). It grows with context length and with the number of simultaneous users. With many users and long documents it can exceed the weights.

    Quantizing the weights is therefore not the whole answer. Simulation 3 shows why a bank sizing an on-premises service must plan for concurrency and document length, not only model size.

    Open weights are not open source. "Open-weight" means the trained parameters are published so you can download and run them. Licences differ: some (Apache 2.0, MIT) are permissive for commercial use; others add user thresholds, revenue clauses, acceptable-use restrictions or limits on using outputs to train other models. The training data and code are often not released. A bank's legal and model-risk teams will need to read the specific licence for the specific checkpoint, and keep a record of provenance and version, because the model is then a component you own and must validate (G3).

    Why a bank runs models itself. Four reasons, in rough order of how often they decide the question. Data that must not leave a jurisdiction or a controlled environment (residency rules, confidentiality of customer files; see P4). Predictable cost at high volume. Latency and availability control. And the ability to fine-tune on internal data. Against that, you take on the engineering: serving, scaling, patching, monitoring and the GPU supply chain. For many workloads a regional cloud region, or a sovereign-cloud offering, is a middle path (A11).

    Small and reasoning together. The two halves of this lesson meet in distilled reasoning models: small models trained on a large model's reasoning traces. They can solve structured problems well for their size, at the price of long outputs, so a small model that thinks for 10,000 tokens is cheap per token and still slow per answer. Check both numbers.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Document-heavy review. Trade finance, credit memos and covenant checks: multi-step, checkable, tolerant of a minute's wait. Good candidates for higher effort, behind a human reviewer.
    • Customer-facing chat and voice. Latency-bound. Low or no thinking, a fast model, retrieval for facts, and escalation to a stronger model only on hard turns.
    • Bulk classification. Complaints, transaction narratives, KYC document types in Arabic and English, millions a day. A small tuned model, perhaps quantized and on-premises, beats a frontier model on cost by a wide margin.
    • Sensitive data. Customer files, internal investigation notes and regulator correspondence often call for a model in a controlled environment; the question is whether a small open model is good enough or whether a vendor's private deployment is required.
    • Agents and code. Developer assistants and back-office agents benefit from reasoning, but need token budgets and step limits so a runaway loop does not become a runaway invoice.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    As of late 2026, a thinking mode is a standard option from the major model providers. Most expose an effort or token-budget control, and several decide automatically when to think. Exact names, price points and limits change every few months, so treat any table you saw last quarter as stale and read the vendor's current documentation.

    1. 2022
      Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency

      Writing out steps in the prompt lifts accuracy on multi-step problems, and voting over several sampled chains lifts it further. The ancestors of today's reasoning models.

      Wei et al., arXiv 2201.11903; Wang et al., arXiv 2203.11171
    2. 2024
      Scaling test-time compute can beat scaling parameters

      On some problems, spending extra compute at inference was more effective than a model many times larger, with the best split depending on question difficulty.

      Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314
    3. 2025
      Reasoning learned by reinforcement learning with open weights

      DeepSeek-R1 showed long, self-checking reasoning emerging from reward on final answers, released with open weights and with smaller distilled variants. It also showed that distilling reasoning traces into small models works well.

      DeepSeek-AI, arXiv 2501.12948
    4. 2025
      Simple budget control

      The "s1" work showed that a small set of reasoning examples plus a simple trick to force more or less thinking at inference gives a controllable budget curve.

      Muennighoff et al., arXiv 2501.19393
    5. 2026
      Overthinking and adaptive effort are the active problems

      Recent papers study models that think too long on easy questions, lose correct answers after long deliberation, and fail to ration effort across questions. Others train models to choose between no, short and long thinking per prompt, reporting large cuts in tokens (about 41% in one small-model study) at similar accuracy. These are 2026 preprints on small models and benchmarks; treat them as direction, not settled practice.

      arXiv 2608.20256; arXiv 2608.07968; arXiv 2604.10739
    6. 2026
      Banks move open models onto their own infrastructure

      Reports from NVIDIA's GTC Berlin in October 2026 describe European banks and payment firms putting open-weight models into production on infrastructure they control, one claiming closed-model quality at about 60% of the cost per answer. These are vendor-sourced claims. I found no comparable public detail for specific UAE or Saudi banks; the regional drivers are data-residency expectations and national AI programmes (A11), and you should ask internally rather than assume.

      NVIDIA newsroom coverage, October 2026 (secondary reports)

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    FailureWhat it looks likeHow to spot it
    OverthinkingLong thinking on a trivial request; a correct first answer abandoned for a wrong one.Token counts per request by task type; accuracy at low versus high effort on your own test set. If high is not better, stop paying.
    Cost surpriseThe bill is several times the forecast because hidden thinking tokens were not counted.Track output tokens including reasoning tokens in the gateway, and look at the 95th percentile per request, not the mean.
    Latency breachA reasoning model placed in a live customer journey times out.Test end-to-end latency at the high percentiles, with the real prompt length.
    Unfaithful explanationsThe visible reasoning is quoted to a regulator as the model's rationale, but it was a plausible story.Test whether changing a factor in the input changes the decision as the explanation claims; do not treat the thinking text as an audit record.
    Correlated errors under votingTen samples agree, confidently, and are wrong.Measure the consistent-wrong share on labelled data. Agreement is not accuracy.
    Quantization regressionsThe 4-bit model matches on an English benchmark but breaks on Arabic, long documents or numbers.Run your own evaluation, per language and per task, original against quantized, with paired intervals (A4).
    Memory planning that ignores KV cacheThe model "fits" and then runs out of memory or crawls at peak hours.Size for peak concurrent users and the longest documents; load-test at that scale.
    Licence and provenance gapsAn open model is deployed without checking use restrictions, or its version drifts without anyone noticing.Keep a register: checkpoint hash, licence, source, date, validation result.
    Distillation hides a holeThe student is excellent on the teacher's examples and poor on rare cases such as unusual clause wording.Test on held-out cases chosen to be unlike the training set, including the rare categories.
    Security surfaceA self-hosted model with no patching, exposed endpoints or unvetted downloaded files.Treat downloaded weights and serving software as supply chain; scan, pin versions and isolate the service (G1).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    Explaining reasoning models in 20 seconds. "A reasoning model writes out a long working before answering, so it is more accurate on multi-step problems that can be checked. The cost is that you pay for those tokens and wait for them. So we set the effort per use case, test whether more thinking helps on our own data, and don't use it where speed matters or the question is simple."

    Proposing a hosting decision. "Before we choose cloud or on-premises, can we agree the data class, the monthly volume and the accuracy bar? If the data is restricted and the bar is met by a quantized 30–70B open model, I'd cost it on peak concurrency and document length, not on model size alone, and compare it with a regional private deployment."

    “Why not just turn thinking on everywhere? It is more accurate.”

    On hard, checkable tasks it is. On easy ones it adds cost and delay for almost no gain, and it makes costs less predictable. I'd measure accuracy at each effort level on our own labelled cases and set the lowest level that meets the bar. That also gives us a defensible budget.

    “Can we show the model's reasoning to the regulator as the explanation?”

    I'd be careful. Research finds the written reasoning does not always reflect what drove the answer. It is useful for reviewers, but for a formal explanation we should use methods we can test, such as changing an input and confirming the decision moves as claimed, and keep the reasoning text as supporting evidence only.

    “Would a smaller model be good enough for this?”

    Often, for narrow, high-volume tasks, particularly if we fine-tune or distil from a larger model. I'd test it on a few hundred of our own cases, including Arabic and the rare categories, and compare with intervals. For open-ended or multi-step analysis, I'd expect the larger one to earn its cost.

    “We have 8 GPUs. Is that enough to host a 70B model?”

    For the weights, easily: about 140 GB at 16-bit, 35 GB at 4-bit. The real question is concurrency and context. Each user's cache is about 2.7 GB for an 8,000-token context at 0.33 MB a token, so 100 users is about 264 GB before replicas or throughput targets. I'd size to peak load and test it.

    “Is an open-weight model free?”

    The weights are free to download under the licence, but the service is not: GPUs, engineers, security, monitoring and validation. Below a certain volume a hosted API is cheaper. Above it, or where data cannot leave, hosting ourselves can win. I'd model both with our actual volume and prices.

    “Does quantizing hurt accuracy?”

    At 8 bits usually negligibly, at 4 bits usually a little for large models, and more for small ones or unusual domains. It is not uniform: numbers, long context and less-represented languages can degrade first. So we compare the original and quantized model on our tasks, paired, before we approve it.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    1. What is test-time compute, and name two ways to spend it.

    Compute used when the model answers rather than when it was trained. Two ways: let the model generate a long chain of thought before the answer (thinking), and sample several answers and combine them by vote or verifier (best-of-n). They can be combined.

    2. A model writes 12,000 thinking tokens per answer at 80 tokens a second, at AED 55 per million output tokens. What are the wait and the cost of 5,000 such answers?

    Wait = 12,000 ÷ 80 = 150 seconds, two and a half minutes (plus overhead). Cost = 5,000 × 12,000 = 60 million tokens × AED 55 per million = AED 3,300, for the thinking alone.

    3. A single sample is right 70% of the time. What does a majority vote of 3 give, assuming independence?

    Right if at least 2 of 3 are right: 3 × 0.7² × 0.3 + 0.7³ = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784. Cost is three times as high, and the gain disappears for questions where the model is consistently wrong.

    4. Why is voting useless if the model's wrong answers are all the same wrong answer?

    Voting helps only when errors are scattered and the right answer repeats. If every sample makes the same mistake, the majority is that mistake. The accuracy ceiling becomes the share of questions the model can get right at all.

    5. A 32B model at 8 bits: how much memory for the weights? And for the KV cache of 50 users at 8,000 tokens, at 0.26 MB a token?

    Weights: 32 × 1 byte = 32 GB. KV: 50 × 8,000 = 400,000 tokens × 0.26 MB = 104,000 MB ≈ 102 GB. The cache is more than three times the weights. Total about 134 GB × 1.1 ≈ 147 GB, which needs 2 GPUs of 80 GB.

    6. What is the difference between distillation and quantization?

    Distillation trains a different, smaller model to imitate a larger one's behaviour. Quantization keeps the same model and stores its numbers in fewer bits. Distillation changes what the model is; quantization changes how it is stored.

    7. List three checks before approving an open-weight model for production.

    Licence terms for commercial use and for outputs; provenance and version record; evaluation on our own tasks and languages, against the current model, with intervals. Also security scanning of the weights and serving stack, and monitoring plans.

    8. Why might a reasoning model make a customer chatbot worse even if it is more accurate?

    Latency and cost. A multi-second or multi-minute wait loses the customer, and thinking tokens multiply cost at chat volume. Better to use a fast model with retrieval and escalate rare hard cases.
