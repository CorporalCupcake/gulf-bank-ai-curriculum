    Reasoning Models and Running Models Yourself

    Part 1

    What a reasoning model is

    In one sentenceA reasoning model writes a long working-out first, then gives its answer. The extra writing is scratch paper that buys better answers on hard problems, at the price of money and waiting time.

    Running example: "What is 17 × 24?" gets an answer in a second. "Does this loan statement add up across 40 lines and 3 exceptions?" needs someone who works step by step, checks and backtracks. A reasoning model does the second kind of work. Spending effort when the model answers, rather than when it was trained, is called test-time compute.

    How it differs from an ordinary model

    • Ordinary model: answers straight away. Fast and cheap, fine for recall, rewriting and summaries.
    • Reasoning model: thinks first. Slower and dearer, better at multi-step arithmetic, code, reconciling figures and rules with exceptions.

    It learns this through reinforcement learning: it is rewarded for right final answers on checkable problems such as maths and code. Habits like "wait, that does not add up, retry" appear because they win rewards.

    Part 2

    How thinking is spent

    1. You send the question and set an effort or thinking budget (a cap on thinking tokens).
    2. The model writes thinking tokens, trying approaches and checking itself. You may only see a summary.
    3. It then writes the answer.
    4. You are billed for all of it. Thinking tokens count as output tokens, the dearer kind, even when hidden.

    There is a second way to spend test-time compute: voting (called self-consistency). Ask the same question several times and take the most common answer, like polling five analysts. A verifier goes further and asks "which answer is correct?", for example by running unit tests or recomputing a total. It wins when checking is easier than solving.

    How to read it

    • On hard, checkable tasks accuracy climbs steeply, then crawls. Each doubling of thinking adds less.
    • On easy tasks (classify an email) there is little to gain, yet you still pay. Models also overthink easy questions and can talk themselves out of a right answer.
    • Delay: 12,000 thinking tokens at 80 tokens a second is a 150-second wait. Fine for an overnight batch, impossible for live chat or card authorisation.
    • Cost: thinking length varies per question, so budget with a spread, not an average.
    • Voting: if one try is right 70% of the time, three votes lift that to about 78%, at three times the cost. It only works when errors are scattered. If the model misreads a clause the same way every time, ten votes are ten copies of one mistake.
    • Visible reasoning is not proof. It can leave out a hint the model used or be a story told afterwards. Treat it as evidence, not an audit trail.

    Rule of thumb: give each use case the lowest thinking setting that meets its accuracy target.

    Part 3

    Running a model yourself

    In one sentenceAn open-weight model is one whose trained numbers (weights) are published, so you can run it on your own servers instead of calling a provider.

    Banks do this first because some data cannot leave a jurisdiction or a controlled environment. Other reasons are predictable cost at high volume and control of delay and availability. The price is engineering: serving, scaling, patching, monitoring and getting GPUs.

    Open weights are not open source. You get the parameters, but the training data and code are often withheld, and licences vary. Some limit user numbers or ban using outputs to train other models. Legal and model-risk teams must read the licence, because you now own a component you must validate.

    How models are made small enough

    TechniqueWhat it doesWhat you give up
    DistillationA small "student" model trains on a large "teacher" model's outputs, like an apprentice learning from a masterBreadth: good on taught tasks, weaker elsewhere. Some providers forbid training on their outputs.
    QuantizationStores each weight in fewer bits (16 down to 8 or 4), like a lower-resolution photo of the same pictureA little precision: tiny loss at 8 bits, small at 4 bits for large models, growing fast below

    Distillation makes a different, smaller model. Quantization keeps the same model and shrinks how its numbers are stored.

    What fills a GPU's memory

    • Weights: parameters times bytes each. A 70-billion-parameter model is 140 GB at 16 bits and 35 GB at 4 bits.
    • KV cache: each user's working memory of the text so far, like a desk that fills with open files. It grows with document length and number of users, and can exceed the weights.

    So quantizing a model to a quarter of its size does not cut the cache. Size an in-house service for peak users and longest documents, not just model size. For example, a 32B model at 8 bits is 32 GB, but 50 users with 8,000 tokens each can need around 100 GB of cache, so it still needs several GPUs.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    OptionUse it whenWatch out for
    Ordinary modelRecall, rewriting, summaries, live chatWeak on multi-step logic
    Reasoning modelHard checkable tasks that can waitCost, delay, hidden tokens
    Voting or verifierErrors are scattered, or checking is easySame mistake every time
    Own small modelData cannot leave, or volume is high and steadyEngineering, GPUs, licence, validation

    In banking, reasoning models suit slow, high-value checks such as covenant tests, while data-residency rules push many teams towards open-weight models or a sovereign cloud.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Paying for thinking on easy tasks

      Compare accuracy at low and high effort on your own examples. If high is no better, stop paying for it.

    • Forgetting hidden tokens

      Bills run several times over forecast and live journeys time out. Track all output tokens and test the slowest calls, not the mean.

    • Quoting the reasoning as the explanation

      It may be a plausible story. Change an input and check that the decision moves as the explanation claims.

    • Sizing only for the weights

      The model "fits", then runs out of memory at peak because of the KV cache. Size for peak users and load-test. Also test a quantized model on your own languages and numbers.

    1. What is test-time compute, and what are two ways to spend it?

    Compute spent when the model answers, not when it was trained. Two ways: thinking at length first, or sampling several answers and voting or verifying.

    2. Why can voting not fix a model that is consistently wrong?

    Voting needs scattered errors and a repeating right answer. If every sample makes the same mistake, the majority is that mistake.

    3. What is the difference between distillation and quantization?

    Distillation trains a different, smaller model to imitate a larger one. Quantization keeps the same model and stores its numbers in fewer bits.

    4. Why might a reasoning model make a customer chatbot worse even if it is more accurate?

    Latency and cost. A long wait loses the customer and thinking multiplies cost at chat volume. Use a fast model and escalate hard cases.

    5. Name three checks before approving an open-weight model for production.

    Licence terms for commercial use, a record of provenance and version, and evaluation on your own tasks and languages against the current model.
