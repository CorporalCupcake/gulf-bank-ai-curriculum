    Paying for Thought: Reasoning Models, Test-Time Compute and Models You Run Yourself

    What a "thinking" model buys you and what it costs in tokens, money and seconds, when voting beats thinking, and how distillation, quantization and open weights let a bank run a capable model inside its own walls.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA reasoning model spends extra tokens at answer time to be right more often on hard problems, with sharply diminishing returns and a bill that grows with every token, while a small or quantized open-weight model trades some capability for control, and the skill is knowing which problems deserve which.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    "What is 17 × 24?" gets an answer in a second. "Does this trade finance file breach our sanctions policy?" needs an analyst who reads, cross-checks and backtracks. Same person, different effort.

    A standard model gives every question one pass, one token at a time. A reasoning model first writes a long working-out (often hidden or summarised): it tries approaches, checks itself, corrects course, then answers. The extra tokens are scratch paper. More paper means more thinking, and costs more money and waiting time.

    Spending compute when the model answers, not when it was trained, is test-time compute. It is a second dial next to training spend: leave the model alone and let it think longer on the question in front of it.

    The second half covers running a smaller model yourself, for when thinking makes tokens dear or data cannot leave the bank. Three tools: distillation (a small model imitates a big one), quantization (store numbers in fewer bits) and open weights (published parameters you may host in your own data centre). Each gives something up.

    Hold onto thisThinking is a dial, not a feature. Give each use case the lowest setting that meets its accuracy target: cost and delay keep growing with thinking tokens, while accuracy flattens. Model size and hosting follow the same rule: the smallest, most local model that clears the bar.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A5.1 · What thinking buys you, and what it costs

    How it learns to think. Reinforcement learning is added in post-training (see M1), on problems with checkable answers: maths, code, logic. The model writes a long chain of thought, a verifier checks only the final answer, and a right answer is rewarded. Nobody writes the reasoning. Habits like "wait, that doesn't add up, retry" emerge because they win more rewards (DeepSeek-R1, 2025; the big labs' recipes are not public).

    What it buys. Think of a student given more exam time. On checkable tasks (multi-step arithmetic, code, reconciling figures, rules with exceptions) accuracy rises with thinking, but each doubling of tokens adds less than the last. On recall or style tasks (summarise an email, translate a letter) there is little to gain: the answer was never limited by thought.

    What it costs. Three bills.

    • Money. Thinking tokens are output tokens, usually several times the price of input tokens, and billed even if you only see a summary.
    • Latency. A 20,000-token think at 80 tokens a second is a four-minute wait. That rules it out for card authorisations and live chat.
    • Variance. Thinking length differs per question, so cost and delay are less predictable. Budget with a distribution, not an average.

    Simulation 1 shows the trade at three difficulty levels.

    This curve is what vendors sell as an effort or thinking budget setting (low, medium, high, or a token cap). Some systems choose for themselves (adaptive reasoning), but models ration effort badly: they overthink easy questions, under-think hard ones, and can talk themselves out of a correct answer.

    The other way to spend test-time compute is voting. Like polling five analysts and taking the majority view, self-consistency or best-of-n asks the same question k times with some randomness, then takes the most common answer (or the one a verifier scores highest). Independent errors scatter while the right answer repeats. The limit is that independence: if the model misreads a clause the same way every time, ten votes are ten copies of one mistake.

    P(majority right)=∑j>k/2k(kj)pj(1−p)k−j

    p is the chance one sample is right; k is the number of samples. With p = 0.7 and k = 5, the vote is right 83.7% of the time, up from 70%. Read it as: voting sharpens whatever the model already leans towards. It assumes independent samples and scattered wrong answers, both generous. Below p = 0.5 the vote makes things worse.

    Verifiers and process rewards. Voting asks "which answer is most common?". A verifier asks "which is correct?", and wins when checking is easier than solving, like running unit tests or recomputing a total. A process reward model scores each reasoning step, not just the end (R3). At a bank, checkable work such as reconciliation or rule application is where reasoning pays most.

    Is the visible reasoning true? Not necessarily. Written reasoning can omit a hint the model used, or rationalise afterwards. Treat it as evidence, not an audit trail (R4); this matters for explainability claims to validators. Some research skips text and reasons in hidden vectors (R1).

    A5.2 · Small and on-premises models

    Three techniques shrink a model, often combined.

    TechniqueWhat it doesWhat you give up
    DistillationA small "student" trains on a large "teacher's" outputs (answers, probabilities, reasoning traces). It copies behaviour, not size. Like an apprentice learning from a master.Breadth: matches the teacher on taught tasks, lags elsewhere. Some providers' terms forbid training on their outputs.
    QuantizationStores each weight in fewer bits (16 to 8 or 4). Like a lower-resolution photo: same picture, a quarter to a half of the memory.A little precision. Tiny loss at 8 bits; small at 4 bits for large models, bigger for small ones; fast growth below (R12).
    Pruning and sparsityRemoves weights or components that add little.Needs retraining, and speed gains need hardware that exploits sparsity.

    Details are in P3. What decides the machine size? Two things fill GPU memory.

    • Weights. Parameters × bytes each. 70 billion at 16 bits is 70 × 2 = 140 GB; at 4 bits, 35 GB.
    • KV cache. Each user's working memory of the text so far (M1), like a desk that fills with open files. It grows with context length and users, and can exceed the weights.

    So quantizing weights is not the whole answer: size on-premises services for concurrency and document length, not just model size (Simulation 3).

    Open weights are not open source. You can download and run the published parameters, but training data and code are often withheld. Licences vary: Apache 2.0 and MIT are permissive; others add user thresholds, revenue clauses, acceptable-use limits or bans on training other models with outputs. Legal and model-risk teams must read the licence for the specific checkpoint and record provenance and version, because you now own a component you must validate (G3).

    Why a bank runs models itself, most decisive first:

    • Data that cannot leave a jurisdiction or controlled environment (P4).
    • Predictable cost at high volume.
    • Control of latency and availability.
    • Fine-tuning on internal data.

    The price is engineering: serving, scaling, patching, monitoring, GPU supply. A regional or sovereign cloud is a middle path (A11).

    Small and reasoning together. Distilled reasoning models are small models trained on a large model's reasoning traces. A small model that thinks for 10,000 tokens is cheap per token but still slow per answer. Check both numbers.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    FailureWhat it looks likeHow to spot it
    OverthinkingLong thinking on a trivial request; a right first answer dropped for a wrong one.Tokens per request by task type; accuracy at low versus high effort on your own set. If high is no better, stop paying.
    Cost surpriseBill is several times forecast: hidden thinking tokens were not counted.Track all output tokens, reasoning included, at the gateway; watch the 95th percentile, not the mean.
    Latency breachA reasoning model in a live customer journey times out.Test end-to-end latency at high percentiles with real prompts.
    Unfaithful explanationsVisible reasoning is quoted to a regulator as the rationale, but it was a plausible story.Change an input factor and check the decision moves as the explanation claims. Thinking text is not an audit record.
    Correlated errors under votingTen samples agree, confidently, and are wrong.Measure the consistently-wrong share on labelled data. Agreement is not accuracy.
    Quantization regressions4-bit matches on English benchmarks but breaks on Arabic, long documents or numbers.Evaluate per language and task, original against quantized, with paired intervals (A4).
    Memory planning that ignores KV cacheThe model "fits", then runs out of memory or crawls at peak.Size for peak users and longest documents; load-test at that scale.
    Licence and provenance gapsOpen model deployed without checking restrictions, or its version drifts unnoticed.Keep a register: checkpoint hash, licence, source, date, validation result.
    Distillation hides a holeStudent is excellent on the teacher's examples, poor on rare cases such as unusual clause wording.Test on held-out cases unlike the training set, rare categories included.
    Security surfaceSelf-hosted model with no patching, exposed endpoints or unvetted downloads.Treat weights and serving software as supply chain: scan, pin versions, isolate (G1).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. What is test-time compute, and name two ways to spend it.

    Compute spent when the model answers, not when it was trained. Two ways: a long chain of thought first (thinking), or several sampled answers combined by vote or verifier (best-of-n). They can be combined.

    2. A model writes 12,000 thinking tokens per answer at 80 tokens a second, at AED 55 per million output tokens. What are the wait and the cost of 5,000 such answers?

    Wait = 12,000 ÷ 80 = 150 seconds (two and a half minutes, plus overhead). Cost = 5,000 × 12,000 = 60 million tokens × AED 55 per million = AED 3,300, thinking alone.

    3. A single sample is right 70% of the time. What does a majority vote of 3 give, assuming independence?

    Right if at least 2 of 3 are right: 3 × 0.7² × 0.3 + 0.7³ = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784, at three times the cost. The gain vanishes where the model is consistently wrong.

    4. Why is voting useless if the model's wrong answers are all the same wrong answer?

    Voting needs scattered errors and a repeating right answer. If every sample makes the same mistake, the majority is that mistake. The ceiling is the share of questions the model can get right at all.

    5. A 32B model at 8 bits: how much memory for the weights? And for the KV cache of 50 users at 8,000 tokens, at 0.26 MB a token?

    Weights: 32 × 1 byte = 32 GB. KV: 50 × 8,000 = 400,000 tokens × 0.26 MB = 104,000 MB ≈ 102 GB, over three times the weights. Total about 134 GB × 1.1 ≈ 147 GB: 2 GPUs of 80 GB.

    6. What is the difference between distillation and quantization?

    Distillation trains a different, smaller model to imitate a larger one. Quantization keeps the same model and stores its numbers in fewer bits. One changes what the model is; the other, how it is stored.

    7. List three checks before approving an open-weight model for production.

    Licence terms for commercial use and outputs; provenance and version record; evaluation on our own tasks and languages against the current model, with intervals. Also security scanning and a monitoring plan.

    8. Why might a reasoning model make a customer chatbot worse even if it is more accurate?

    Latency and cost. A long wait loses the customer, and thinking tokens multiply cost at chat volume. Use a fast model with retrieval and escalate rare hard cases.
