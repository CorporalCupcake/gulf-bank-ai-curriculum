    Vectors, Matrices and the Geometry of Meaning

    Why every embedding search, recommendation and attention score is a dot product, and how SVD and low-rank structure let models compress what they know, from PCA to LoRA.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModern AI turns everything into arrows: similarity is how closely two arrows point the same way, and a model layer is a matrix that moves arrows, usually doing most of its work in a few directions, which is what makes compression and cheap fine-tuning possible.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture the complaints team at a Gulf retail bank. About 4,000 written complaints arrive each day through the app, email and web chat, in English, Arabic and a fair amount of Arabizi. Someone has to route each one to the right team, and investigators want to see similar past cases. Keyword rules break quickly: “charged twice”, “double debit” and “تم خصم المبلغ مرتين” mean the same thing and share no words.

    An embedding model solves this by turning every piece of text into a list of numbers, typically 768 or 1,024 of them. Treat that list as the coordinates of an arrow in a space with that many dimensions. The model has been trained so that texts with the same meaning produce arrows pointing in roughly the same direction, whatever the language or wording.

    Once meaning is direction, “how similar are these two complaints?” becomes a geometry question: how closely do the two arrows point the same way? The tool that answers it is the dot product. It's the single most-used operation in modern AI. Semantic search, recommendations, the attention inside every transformer, and the final layer of most classifiers are all dot products.

    Two dimensions are enough to see everything that matters. Drag the arrows.

    Three things should stand out.

    • Direction and length are different information. Cosine looks only at direction. The dot product multiplies direction by both lengths. Distance cares about where the tips are. Which one you want depends on whether length means anything.
    • Once vectors have unit length, the three agree. After normalising, the dot product equals the cosine, and the distance is a fixed function of it. They rank neighbours identically. Most of the confusion in practice comes from vectors that aren't normalised when someone assumed they were.
    • The dot product is a projection. It's the length of a's shadow on b, multiplied by b's length. Read “a · b” as “how much of a lies along b”.
    Hold onto thisMeaning lives in direction. Similarity is the angle between directions. A model layer is a matrix that moves directions around, and most matrices that matter do most of their work along a handful of directions. The rest of this lesson unpacks those three sentences.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The dot product, two ways

    Algebraically, the dot product multiplies matching coordinates and adds them up. Geometrically, it's the product of the two lengths and the cosine of the angle between them. These are the same number. That identity is why the dot product is so useful: a cheap sum of products tells you about angles in a space you can't picture.

    a·b=∑i=1daibi=‖a‖‖b‖cosθ

    d is the number of dimensions, ‖a‖ is a's length (the square root of a · a), and θ is the angle between the arrows. In 1,024 dimensions the left-hand side is 1,024 multiplications and additions, which a GPU does in nanoseconds.

    The sign tells you the rough relationship. Positive means the arrows point broadly the same way (θ under 90°). Zero means perpendicular: in embedding terms, unrelated. Negative means opposed. Dividing by both lengths gives the cosine similarity, which runs from −1 to 1 and ignores length completely.

    Cosine, dot product or distance: normalisation decides

    Vector databases offer three standard similarity measures. They are tied together by one identity, which is worth knowing by heart:

    ‖a−b‖2=‖a‖2+‖b‖2−2a·b

    Squared distance is the two squared lengths minus twice the dot product. If both vectors have length 1, it becomes 2 − 2 cos θ: distance and cosine carry exactly the same information, in reverse order.

    So the real choice isn't between three formulas. It's whether length carries meaning you want to keep.

    Many embedding APIs return vectors already scaled to unit length, so all three give the same ranking and the dot product is the fastest to compute. Some open models don't normalise, and their documentation says which measure they were trained for. Use that one. If you use a different measure from the one the model was trained with, the ranking quietly changes.

    Embeddings: meaning as direction

    An embedding model is trained, usually by contrastive learning, on millions of pairs of texts that should match: a question and its answer, a sentence and its translation, a title and its body. Training pulls matching pairs' arrows together and pushes non-matching ones apart. The result is a space where direction encodes meaning. Multilingual models are trained on translation pairs too, so “I was charged twice” and its Arabic equivalent end up close.

    Directions also carry attributes. The famous example from word2vec is that king − man + woman lands near queen. That's a direction for something like gender, shared across many word pairs. Treat this as a tendency, not a law: careful re-analysis showed many published analogies only work because the input words were excluded from the answers. The idea that concepts are directions, though, has held up and now underpins interpretability research.

    Search is then simple to state. Embed the query, compare it with every stored vector, and return the closest. Here's a toy version with 20 complaint phrases.

    The lesson from the orange points is real. In models that don't normalise, length can track things like text length or how generic a passage is, and in recommendation systems it often tracks popularity. Under the dot product, those long vectors become hubs that show up in everyone's results. Under cosine they compete on meaning alone.

    Notice one more thing with the dot product selected: dragging the query further from the centre changes every score but never the order. The query's own length multiplies every score equally. Only the stored vectors' lengths can reorder results. That's not true of Euclidean distance, where the query's length matters too.

    Finding neighbours fast: exact, IVF and HNSW

    Exact search compares the query with every stored vector. With 10 million documents of 1,024 dimensions, that's about 10 billion multiply-adds per query. A GPU can do it, but at high query volumes, or on CPUs, you want to skip most of the work. Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes do this, and they all trade a little recall (the share of true nearest neighbours you actually return) for a lot of speed.

    • IVF (inverted file index) groups the vectors into clusters with k-means, typically thousands of them. At query time it finds the few cluster centres nearest the query and searches only inside those. The nprobe setting, how many clusters to search, is the speed-recall dial you just used in the simulation. Its weakness is boundaries: a true neighbour that sits just across a cluster edge is missed.
    • HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) links each vector to a few dozen near neighbours, forming a graph, with sparse “express” layers on top. A query starts at the top, greedily hops to whichever neighbour is closer, drops a layer, and repeats, like finding an address by motorway, then main road, then side street. It's the default in most vector databases because it gives high recall at low latency, but it holds the full graph in memory.
    • Product quantisation (PQ) and binary quantisation compress the vectors themselves, often 8 to 32 times, so more fit in memory. A common pattern is to search the compressed vectors, then rescore the best few hundred candidates with the full-precision ones.

    None of these is lossless. A recall@10 of 0.95 means one true top-10 neighbour in twenty is silently missing. For a complaints router that's fine. For a sanctions or adverse-media screen, it's a control gap you need to document and test.

    Why high dimensions behave strangely

    Intuition built in two or three dimensions misleads in 768. Three effects matter in practice.

    • Random directions are almost perpendicular. Pick two random directions in d dimensions and their cosine is spread around 0 with a standard deviation of about 1/√d. In 768 dimensions, 95% of random pairs have a cosine between about −0.07 and +0.07. The figure below shows this exactly.
    • There's room for far more concepts than dimensions. Because random directions are nearly perpendicular, you can fit vastly more than d almost-independent directions into d dimensions. This is the geometry behind the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, and behind superposition: models storing many more features than they have neurons.
    • Distances concentrate. For many kinds of data, as dimensions grow the nearest and farthest points end up at nearly the same distance from a query, so “nearest” becomes fragile. Some points become hubs, close to a large share of all queries. Real embeddings dodge the worst of this because meaningful data lies near a much lower-dimensional surface, but hubness does show up in retrieval.

    The practical consequence is that a cosine of 0.3 can be meaningful in a high-dimensional space, because chance alone almost never produces it. The flip side: trained embedding spaces are not random. Most models crowd their vectors into a narrow cone, a property called anisotropy, so even unrelated texts can score 0.5 or more. Absolute cosine values are model-specific. Never carry a threshold like “above 0.8 is a match” from one model to another.

    Attention is a grid of dot products

    Inside a transformer, every token produces three vectors from its current representation, each by multiplying by a learned matrix: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what will I pass on?). The attention score between two tokens is the dot product of one's query with the other's key. Same operation as search, just done between every pair of tokens in the context, at every layer.

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V

    QK⊤ is the grid of every query-key dot product. Softmax turns each row into weights that sum to 1. Each token's output is the weighted average of the value vectors.

    A toy example in 4 dimensions. The token “fee” in “why was I charged this fee” has query (1, 0, 2, 1). Three keys: “charged” (1, 1, 1, 0), “why” (0, 1, 0, 1) and “fee” itself (2, 0, 1, 1). The dot products are 3, 1 and 5. Divide by √4 = 2 to get 1.5, 0.5 and 2.5. Softmax gives weights of about 0.24, 0.09 and 0.67, so “fee” draws mostly on itself and partly on “charged”.

    The division by √d comes straight from the high-dimension point above. If query and key entries are roughly independent with unit variance, their dot product has a standard deviation of √d: about 11 for d = 128. Scores that large push softmax to put nearly all weight on one token, and the gradients vanish. Dividing by √d brings the spread back to about 1. Several recent models go further and normalise queries and keys before the dot product (“QK-norm”), turning attention scores into scaled cosines, which keeps training stable.

    A matrix is a function that moves space

    Now F1.2. A matrix is best understood not as a table of numbers but as a function: put a vector in, get a vector out, and straight lines stay straight. To know what a 2×2 matrix does, you only need to know where it sends the two basis arrows (1, 0) and (0, 1). Those destinations are its columns. Everything else follows by adding and scaling.

    This is all a neural network layer is, before the nonlinearity: a matrix that maps the incoming representation to a new one. An embedding table is a matrix whose rows are the vectors. The query, key and value projections above are matrices. Learning means adjusting these maps.

    Two numbers summarise a matrix's effect on space. The determinant is how much it scales area (volume, in higher dimensions); a negative determinant means space is flipped over. The rank is how many dimensions survive. A 2×2 matrix of rank 1 squashes the whole plane onto a line, and information about the squashed direction is gone for good.

    Eigenvectors: the directions that don't turn

    Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. An eigenvector is one that doesn't: the matrix only stretches it, by a factor called its eigenvalue, λ.

    Mv=λv

    Apply M to v and you get v back, scaled by λ. If λ is negative, it's flipped. If λ is 0, that direction is crushed to nothing, so the matrix has lost rank.

    Eigenvectors matter whenever you apply the same matrix again and again, because repeated application amplifies the direction with the largest eigenvalue and lets the others fade. Two bank examples:

    • Credit rating migration. A one-year transition matrix gives the probability of moving from each rating to each other rating. Apply it year after year, and the portfolio's rating mix converges to the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. The second-largest eigenvalue tells you how fast it gets there.
    • Covariance matrices. These are symmetric, and symmetric matrices always have real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. The eigenvectors are the directions of greatest variation in the data, which is exactly what PCA finds. In the simulation, the Symmetric preset shows the two eigenvector lines at right angles.

    Not every matrix has real eigenvectors. A rotation turns every direction, so its eigenvalues are complex numbers, a sign that the matrix spins rather than stretches. And for non-symmetric matrices, eigenvectors can be nearly parallel and badly behaved. That's one reason the next tool is more useful in practice.

    SVD: rotate, stretch, rotate

    The singular value decomposition works for every matrix, any shape, any rank. It says that every linear map is three simple steps: rotate (possibly with a flip), stretch along the axes, and rotate again.

    M=UΣV⊤=∑iσiuivi⊤

    V⊤ rotates the input so that special directions vi line up with the axes. Σ stretches axis i by the singular value σi, largest first. U rotates the result into place, so vi ends up along ui. The right-hand form says the same thing as a sum of rank-1 pieces, each weighted by its σ.

    Geometrically, any matrix turns the unit circle into an ellipse. The singular values are the ellipse's semi-axis lengths, and the ui are its axis directions. Turn on Singular axes in Simulation 3 to see them, and step through the SVD to watch the three moves. The number of non-zero singular values is the rank. The ratio of largest to smallest is the condition number: how much the matrix can amplify small errors, which is why near-zero singular values cause unstable regression coefficients.

    The sum-of-pieces form is the important one for AI. If the first few σi are large and the rest are small, then keeping only the first few pieces gives a matrix that's nearly identical but far cheaper to store. That's compression, and it leads directly to PCA and LoRA.

    PCA is SVD of centred data

    Put your data in a matrix: one row per customer, one column per feature. Principal component analysis finds the directions in feature space along which customers vary most. The recipe is short: subtract each column's mean (centre the data), then take the SVD. The vi are the principal components, and σi² is proportional to the variance along each one. Equivalently, the components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

    Centring matters. Without it, the first component mostly points at the average customer, which tells you nothing about how customers differ. If features are on different scales, such as AED amounts next to ratios, standardise them too, or the biggest-number column dominates.

    The Eckart–Young theorem makes the compression precise: keeping the top k pieces of the SVD gives the best possible rank-k approximation, and its error is exactly the size of the singular values you dropped.

    ‖X−Xk‖F=σk+12+σk+22+⋯

    ‖·‖F is the Frobenius norm: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. No other rank-k matrix gets closer to X.

    Read the component list as well as the error. With centring on, the first component separates families (school fees, groceries) from remitters, and the second separates lifestyle spenders (travel, dining) from remitters. That's PCA's real value in a bank: it describes the main axes along which customers, accounts or yield curves differ. The classic treasury example is interest rates: three components, usually read as level, slope and curvature, explain the large majority of daily yield-curve movements, which is why rate risk is often hedged against just those three.

    The same idea gives a simple anomaly detector. Reconstruct each transaction or account from the top few components; anything with a large leftover doesn't fit the normal patterns. That's the linear cousin of the autoencoders used in fraud and AML.

    Low-rank approximation and LoRA

    Fine-tuning a large language model means changing its weight matrices. A single square matrix in a mid-sized model might be 4,096 × 4,096: about 16.8 million numbers. Updating every one of them, across hundreds of matrices, is expensive to train and to store, especially if you want a separate version for complaints, for trade finance documents and for Arabic call summaries.

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation) bets that the change a fine-tune needs is low-rank, even though the original matrix isn't. It freezes the original matrix W and learns the update as the product of two thin matrices:

    W′=W+αrBA,B∈ℝd×r,A∈ℝr×d

    r is the rank, often 8 to 64. The update BA can have rank at most r, so it changes the layer along at most r directions. B starts at zero, so training begins from the original model exactly. α/r is a fixed scaling factor. Parameters to train: 2dr instead of d².

    Why should the update be low-rank? Research on “intrinsic dimension” found that fine-tuning large pretrained models can reach most of their full-tuning performance while optimising in a surprisingly small subspace. The model already contains the features; adapting it mostly means re-weighting a few directions. Recent large-scale experiments found LoRA matches full fine-tuning on small and medium instruction and reasoning datasets when applied to all layers, especially the MLP layers, but falls behind when the dataset exceeds the adapter's capacity.

    The practical upside for a bank is significant. One approved base model can carry many small adapters, each a few tens of megabytes, for different tasks or business lines. Each adapter can be versioned, validated and rolled back separately, and the base model's validation evidence still largely applies. QLoRA goes further by holding the frozen base model in 4-bit precision, so a sizeable model can be adapted on a single GPU.

    Low rank turns up elsewhere too. DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention caches a compressed low-rank version of the keys and values, and Matryoshka embeddings are trained so the first 256 dimensions of a 1,024-dimension vector work on their own. In each case the bet is the same: what matters lives in fewer directions than the full space offers.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These are the mistakes that show up in real retrieval and modelling projects, and often in AI-generated code. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • The index uses a different measure from the model

      Vectors from a model trained for cosine are stored unnormalised in a dot-product index. Long or generic documents become hubs that appear in every result list.

      Spot itCheck the model card for the training similarity, and check whether stored vector lengths are all 1. Look for documents that appear in a large share of all results.
    • Sorting the wrong way

      Some libraries return cosine distance (1 − cosine), where lower is better; others return similarity. Mixing them reverses the ranking.

      Spot itSearch for a document using its own text. It must come back first with a perfect score.
    • Thresholds carried across models

      “Treat anything above 0.8 as a duplicate complaint” was tuned on one model. A new model has a different cosine distribution and the threshold means something else.

      Spot itAsk how the threshold was chosen, and on which model version. Re-tune it on labelled pairs after every model change.
    • Mixing vectors from different models or versions

      Each model has its own space. Comparing a v2 query with v1 document vectors produces confident-looking nonsense.

      Spot itCheck that every stored vector records the model and version that made it. An upgrade means re-embedding the whole corpus.
    • Expecting embeddings to handle identifiers and negation

      “I did authorise this payment” and “I did not authorise this payment” can be very close. IBANs, card BINs and policy numbers barely register.

      Spot itBuild a test set of near-miss pairs, including negations and exact codes, and check retrieval on them. Add keyword search for identifiers.
    • Unmeasured ANN recall

      Index settings tuned on 1 million vectors quietly lose recall at 20 million, or when metadata filters remove most of the graph's neighbours.

      Spot itAsk for recall@k of the index against exact search on a sample of real queries, rerun after growth and with filters applied.
    • PCA on uncentred or unscaled data

      The first component points at the average, or simply at whichever column has the biggest numbers, such as balances in AED.

      Spot itCheck that columns were centred and, where units differ, standardised. If PC1's loadings are all the same sign, ask why.
    • Reading components as causes

      A component is a direction of variation, not a customer type or a driver. Its sign is arbitrary, and small data changes can swap components with similar variance.

      Spot itAsk whether the components are stable across time periods and bootstrap samples before anyone names them.
    • Treating embeddings as anonymised

      Customer messages stored as vectors can be partly reconstructed. Moving vectors outside the bank or the country can still move personal data.

      Spot itAsk whether the vector store is covered by the same data classification, residency and retention rules as the source text.
    • LoRA tested only on its own task

      An adapter that improves complaint classification can degrade the base model's general behaviour, Arabic fluency or refusals.

      Spot itAsk for regression tests on the base model's core capabilities with the adapter attached, not just the target metric.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “The system turns every complaint into a numerical fingerprint of its meaning, so a customer writing in Arabic about a double charge lands next to one writing in English about the same thing. Routing and finding similar cases then becomes a nearest-match search, which takes milliseconds. Two things keep it honest: we measure how often the fast search misses the true best match, and we combine it with keyword search, because fingerprints are weak at account numbers and at ‘did’ versus ‘didn't’.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd use a multilingual embedding model evaluated on our own labelled complaint pairs, not just MTEB. Vectors normalised, cosine similarity, so the index can use inner product. HNSW, with recall@10 against exact search tracked as a metric, including under metadata filters. Hybrid with BM25 for identifiers, then a cross-encoder reranker on the top 50, since a single vector has hard limits on what it can represent. Every vector is tagged with its model version. For adaptation, LoRA on all linear layers, with regression tests on base capabilities.”
    “Cosine or dot product: which should we use?”

    Whichever the embedding model was trained with. If the vectors are normalised, they give identical rankings and the dot product is simply faster. The danger is unnormalised vectors from a cosine-trained model in a dot-product index: longer vectors win regardless of meaning.

    “Why not just search every vector exactly?”

    At a few hundred thousand vectors, you often can. At tens of millions with many queries per second, exact search costs too much compute. Approximate indexes such as HNSW or IVF cut that by orders of magnitude for a small, measurable loss of recall. The key word is measurable: we should know the recall and decide whether it's acceptable for each use case.

    “What does a cosine of 0.82 mean? Is that 82% similar?”

    No. Cosine isn't a percentage or a probability, and its typical range depends on the model: many models give unrelated texts 0.5 or more. It's only meaningful relative to other scores from the same model. If we need a decision threshold, we tune it on labelled pairs and convert it into precision and recall.

    “Why does LoRA work if it only trains 1% of the parameters?”

    Because the change a fine-tune needs is usually concentrated in a few directions. The pretrained model already has the features; adaptation mostly re-weights them. A rank-r update can move each layer along r directions, which is enough for most narrow tasks. It falls short when the new task needs a lot of new knowledge, and it can affect behaviour outside the target task, so we test for that.

    “What's the difference between PCA and an embedding?”

    Both map data to a smaller set of coordinates. PCA is linear and unsupervised: it keeps the directions of greatest variance, which may or may not be the ones that matter. A learned embedding is nonlinear and trained for a goal, such as matching paraphrases, so its directions capture meaning for that goal. PCA is transparent and stable, which validators like; embeddings are more powerful but harder to explain.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. a = (3, 4) and b = (4, 3). Find the dot product, the cosine and the Euclidean distance.

    a · b = 12 + 12 = 24. Both lengths are 5, so cos θ = 24 ÷ 25 = 0.96. a − b = (−1, 1), so the distance is √2 ≈ 1.41. Check with the identity: 25 + 25 − 48 = 2.

    2. Two unit vectors have cosine 0.9. What's the distance between them?

    Distance² = 2 − 2 × 0.9 = 0.2, so the distance is √0.2 ≈ 0.447. For unit vectors, distance and cosine always move together.

    3. A unit-length query. Document A has length 2 and cosine 0.7 with it; document B has length 1 and cosine 0.95. Which ranks first under the dot product, and under cosine?

    Dot product: A scores 2 × 0.7 = 1.4, B scores 0.95, so A wins. Cosine: B wins, 0.95 against 0.7. A is a long vector beating a better match on length alone.

    4. What does M = [[0, −1], [1, 0]] do, and what are its eigenvectors?

    It rotates the plane by 90°: (1, 0) goes to (0, 1). Every direction turns, so there are no real eigenvectors; the eigenvalues are ±i. The determinant is 1, so area is preserved, and both singular values are 1.

    5. A matrix has singular values 10, 5, 2 and 1. What share of its squared Frobenius norm does the best rank-2 approximation keep, and what's its relative error?

    Total = 100 + 25 + 4 + 1 = 130. Rank 2 keeps 125 ÷ 130 ≈ 96.2%. The error is √(4 + 1) = √5, and √5 ÷ √130 ≈ 19.6% of the norm. Squared and unsquared figures tell different stories, so always say which one you're quoting.

    6. LoRA with rank 8 on a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix. How many trainable numbers, and what share of a full update?

    2 × 4,096 × 8 = 65,536, against 16,777,216 for the full matrix: about 0.39%.

    7. Why does attention divide by √d, and what would go wrong without it?

    With roughly independent, unit-variance entries, a d-dimensional dot product has standard deviation √d. Without scaling, scores in large heads are huge, softmax puts almost all weight on one token, and gradients through the softmax nearly vanish, which slows or destabilises training. Dividing by √d keeps the scores on a unit scale.
