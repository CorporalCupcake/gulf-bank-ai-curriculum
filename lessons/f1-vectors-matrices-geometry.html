    Vectors, Matrices and the Geometry of Meaning

    Why every embedding search, recommendation and attention score is a dot product, and how SVD and low-rank structure let models compress what they know, from PCA to LoRA.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceModern AI turns everything into arrows: similarity is how closely two arrows point the same way, and a model layer is a matrix that moves arrows, usually doing most of its work in a few directions, which is what makes compression and cheap fine-tuning possible.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a compass. Two people pointing at the same landmark agree on direction, wherever they stand. An embedding model does this for text: it turns each text into a list of 768 or 1,024 numbers, the coordinates of an arrow, and texts with the same meaning get arrows pointing the same way.

    Example: a Gulf retail bank gets about 4,000 written complaints a day in English, Arabic and Arabizi. “Charged twice”, “double debit” and “تم خصم المبلغ مرتين” share no words, so keyword rules miss the match. As arrows, they point the same way.

    “How similar are these two complaints?” then becomes “how closely do the arrows align?” The tool is the dot product, the most-used operation in modern AI: semantic search, recommendations, transformer attention and most classifier outputs are all dot products. Two dimensions show everything that matters. Drag the arrows.

    • Direction and length are different information. Cosine looks only at direction. The dot product multiplies direction by both lengths. Distance compares tip positions. Choose by whether length means anything.
    • With unit-length vectors, all three agree. Dot product equals cosine, and distance is a fixed function of it, so they rank neighbours identically. Most real confusion is vectors that were assumed normalised but aren't.
    • The dot product is a projection. Think of a's shadow on b, times b's length. Read “a · b” as “how much of a lies along b”.
    Hold onto thisMeaning lives in direction. Similarity is the angle between directions. A model layer is a matrix that moves directions around, and the matrices that matter do most of their work along a few directions.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    The dot product, two ways

    Multiply matching coordinates and add them up, or multiply the two lengths by the cosine of the angle between them. Same number. That identity is the point: a cheap sum of products reveals angles in a space you can't picture.

    a·b=∑i=1daibi=‖a‖‖b‖cosθ

    d is the number of dimensions, ‖a‖ is a's length (the square root of a · a), and θ is the angle between the arrows. In 1,024 dimensions the left-hand side is 1,024 multiplications and additions, which a GPU does in nanoseconds.

    How to read it: positive means the arrows point broadly the same way (θ under 90°), zero means perpendicular (unrelated), negative means opposed. Dividing by both lengths gives cosine similarity, from −1 to 1, which ignores length. Raw dot products have no fixed scale, so a value of 24 means nothing alone; compare it only with other scores from the same query.

    Cosine, dot product or distance: normalisation decides

    Vector databases offer three measures, tied by one identity worth knowing by heart:

    ‖a−b‖2=‖a‖2+‖b‖2−2a·b

    Squared distance is the two squared lengths minus twice the dot product. If both vectors have length 1, it becomes 2 − 2 cos θ: distance and cosine carry exactly the same information, in reverse order.

    So the real choice is whether length carries meaning you want to keep. Analogy: comparing two essays by topic (cosine) versus by topic and word count (dot product).

    MeasureWhat it rewardsUse it whenBank example
    CosinePointing the same way. Length ignored.Only meaning matters, and the model was trained with cosine.Finding past complaints that say the same thing as a new one.
    Dot productPointing the same way and being long.Length was trained to mean something, such as popularity or confidence.Recommending products, where an item vector's length can encode how widely it's taken up.
    Euclidean distanceTips being close. Mixes direction and length.Coordinates are measured quantities on comparable scales.Finding customers with similar standardised spending profiles; k-means clustering.

    Many APIs return unit-length vectors, so all three rank the same and the dot product is fastest. Unnormalised open models document which measure they were trained for. Use that one, or the ranking quietly changes.

    Embeddings: meaning as direction

    Embedding models are trained by contrastive learning on millions of pairs that should match (question and answer, sentence and translation). Training pulls matching arrows together and pushes others apart, like seating friends together at a wedding. Multilingual models see translation pairs, so “I was charged twice” and its Arabic version end up close.

    Directions also carry attributes: king − man + woman lands near queen. Treat that as a tendency, not a law; many published analogies only work because the input words were excluded from the answers. “Concepts are directions” has held up and underpins interpretability research.

    Search: embed the query, compare with every stored vector, return the closest. Here is a toy version with 20 complaint phrases.

    The orange points show the real effect. Without normalisation, length can track text length or genericness, and in recommenders it often tracks popularity. Under the dot product, long vectors become hubs that appear in everyone's results. Under cosine they compete on meaning alone.

    With the dot product selected, dragging the query outward changes every score but never the order: the query's length multiplies all scores equally. Only stored vectors' lengths can reorder results. Euclidean distance doesn't have this property.

    Finding neighbours fast: exact, IVF and HNSW

    Exact search compares the query with every vector: for 10 million documents of 1,024 dimensions, about 10 billion multiply-adds per query. Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes skip most of that, trading a little recall (the share of true nearest neighbours returned) for speed.

    • IVF (inverted file) clusters vectors with k-means (thousands of clusters), then searches only the few clusters nearest the query. nprobe, how many clusters to search, is the speed-recall dial from the simulation. Weakness: a true neighbour just across a cluster edge is missed.
    • HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) links each vector to a few dozen neighbours, with sparse “express” layers on top. A query hops greedily closer and drops a layer, like motorway, then main road, then side street. It is the usual default: high recall, low latency, but the whole graph sits in memory.
    • Product and binary quantisation compress the vectors 8 to 32 times. Common pattern: search compressed vectors, then rescore the best few hundred at full precision.

    None is lossless. Recall@10 of 0.95 means one true top-10 neighbour in twenty is silently missing. Fine for a complaints router; for sanctions or adverse-media screening it is a control gap to document and test.

    Why high dimensions behave strangely

    Intuition from 2D or 3D misleads in 768 dimensions. Three effects matter.

    • Random directions are almost perpendicular. The cosine of two random directions in d dimensions is centred on 0 with standard deviation about 1/√d. In 768 dimensions, 95% of random pairs fall between about −0.07 and +0.07 (see the figure below).
    • There is room for far more concepts than dimensions. Nearly-perpendicular directions let you pack vastly more than d almost-independent ones into d dimensions. This underlies the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma and superposition: models storing more features than neurons.
    • Distances concentrate. As dimensions grow, nearest and farthest points can end up almost equally far from a query, so “nearest” turns fragile and some points become hubs. Real embeddings lie near a lower-dimensional surface, which softens this, but hubness still shows up in retrieval.

    How to read a cosine: 0.3 can be meaningful in high dimensions because chance almost never produces it. But trained spaces are not random. Most models crowd vectors into a narrow cone (anisotropy), so unrelated texts can score 0.5 or more. Absolute cosines are model-specific. Never carry a threshold like “above 0.8 is a match” between models, and never read 0.82 as “82% similar”.

    Attention is a grid of dot products

    Think of a room where every token asks a question and every other token holds up a label; attention measures how well each question matches each label. Each token makes three vectors via learned matrices: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?). The score between two tokens is query · key: the same operation as search, between every pair of tokens, at every layer.

    Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V

    QK⊤ is the grid of every query-key dot product. Softmax turns each row into weights that sum to 1. Each token's output is the weighted average of the value vectors.

    Toy example in 4 dimensions. “fee” in “why was I charged this fee” has query (1, 0, 2, 1). Keys: “charged” (1, 1, 1, 0), “why” (0, 1, 0, 1), “fee” (2, 0, 1, 1). Dot products: 3, 1, 5. Divide by √4 = 2: 1.5, 0.5, 2.5. Softmax weights: about 0.24, 0.09, 0.67. “fee” draws mostly on itself and partly on “charged”.

    Why √d: with roughly independent unit-variance entries, a dot product has standard deviation √d (about 11 for d = 128). Scores that large push softmax onto one token and the gradients vanish. Dividing restores a spread near 1. Some recent models normalise queries and keys instead (“QK-norm”), making scores scaled cosines, which keeps training stable.

    A matrix is a function that moves space

    Now F1.2. A matrix is a function: vector in, vector out, straight lines stay straight. Think of a sheet of rubber with a grid drawn on it; the matrix stretches, rotates or squashes the sheet. To know what a 2×2 matrix does, see where it sends the basis arrows (1, 0) and (0, 1). Those destinations are its columns; everything else follows by adding and scaling.

    A neural-network layer is this, before the nonlinearity. An embedding table is a matrix whose rows are the vectors. The query, key and value projections are matrices. Learning means adjusting these maps.

    Two numbers summarise the effect. The determinant is how much area is scaled (negative means flipped). The rank is how many dimensions survive. A rank-1 2×2 matrix squashes the plane onto a line, and the squashed direction is lost for good.

    Eigenvectors: the directions that don't turn

    Most vectors turn when a matrix acts on them. An eigenvector doesn't: it is only stretched, by its eigenvalue λ. Think of stretching a rubber sheet sideways: points along the stretch direction slide along it, while everything else tilts.

    Mv=λv

    Apply M to v and you get v back, scaled by λ. If λ is negative, it's flipped. If λ is 0, that direction is crushed to nothing, so the matrix has lost rank.

    Repeated application amplifies the direction with the largest eigenvalue and fades the rest. Two bank examples:

    • Credit rating migration. A one-year transition matrix, applied year after year, drives the portfolio's rating mix to the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. The second-largest eigenvalue sets how fast.
    • Covariance matrices. Symmetric, so eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors perpendicular. They are the directions of greatest variation, which is what PCA finds. In the simulation, the Symmetric preset shows the two eigenvector lines at right angles.

    A rotation has no real eigenvectors (every direction turns; eigenvalues are complex). Non-symmetric matrices can have nearly parallel, badly behaved eigenvectors. That is why the next tool is more useful in practice.

    SVD: rotate, stretch, rotate

    The singular value decomposition works for any matrix. Every linear map is three steps: rotate (maybe flip), stretch along the axes, rotate again.

    M=UΣV⊤=∑iσiuivi⊤

    V⊤ rotates the input so that special directions vi line up with the axes. Σ stretches axis i by the singular value σi, largest first. U rotates the result into place, so vi ends up along ui. The right-hand form says the same thing as a sum of rank-1 pieces, each weighted by its σ.

    Any matrix turns the unit circle into an ellipse. The singular values are its semi-axis lengths and the ui its axis directions. Turn on Singular axes in Simulation 3 and step through the three moves. The number of non-zero singular values is the rank. Largest divided by smallest is the condition number: how much small errors get amplified, which is why near-zero singular values give unstable regression coefficients.

    The sum form is the one that matters: if the first few σi are large and the rest small, keeping only those pieces gives a nearly identical matrix that is far cheaper to store. Like a JPEG keeping the big features and dropping fine detail. That leads to PCA and LoRA.

    PCA is SVD of centred data

    Put one row per customer and one column per feature. Principal component analysis finds the directions along which customers vary most: centre the data (subtract column means), then take the SVD. The vi are the components; σi² is proportional to the variance along each. Equivalently, they are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

    Without centring, the first component just points at the average customer. If features have different scales (AED amounts beside ratios), standardise too, or the biggest numbers dominate.

    The Eckart–Young theorem: keeping the top k pieces gives the best possible rank-k approximation, with error exactly the size of the dropped singular values.

    ‖X−Xk‖F=σk+12+σk+22+⋯

    ‖·‖F is the Frobenius norm: square every entry, add them up, take the square root. No other rank-k matrix gets closer to X.

    Read the component list as well as the error. With centring on, the first component separates families (school fees, groceries) from remitters, the second separates lifestyle spenders (travel, dining) from remitters. PCA's value is describing the main axes along which customers, accounts or yield curves differ. Treasury example: three components, read as level, slope and curvature, explain most daily yield-curve moves, so rate risk is often hedged against just those.

    It also gives a simple anomaly detector: reconstruct each transaction from the top few components; a large leftover means it doesn't fit normal patterns. That is the linear cousin of the autoencoders used in fraud and AML.

    Low-rank approximation and LoRA

    One square matrix in a mid-sized model might be 4,096 × 4,096, about 16.8 million numbers. Fully updating hundreds of them is costly to train and store, especially with separate versions for complaints, trade finance and Arabic call summaries.

    LoRA (low-rank adaptation) bets that the change a fine-tune needs is low-rank, even if the matrix isn't. Like annotating a textbook with margin notes instead of reprinting it. It freezes W and learns the update as two thin matrices:

    W′=W+αrBA,B∈ℝd×r,A∈ℝr×d

    r is the rank, often 8 to 64. The update BA can have rank at most r, so it changes the layer along at most r directions. B starts at zero, so training begins from the original model exactly. α/r is a fixed scaling factor. Parameters to train: 2dr instead of d².

    Why low-rank? Fine-tuning can reach most of full-tuning performance while optimising in a surprisingly small subspace: the features already exist, and adapting mostly re-weights a few directions. LoRA matches full fine-tuning on small and medium datasets when applied to all layers, especially MLP layers, but falls behind when the data exceeds the adapter's capacity.

    For a bank: one approved base model can carry many small adapters (tens of megabytes), each versioned, validated and rolled back separately, with the base model's validation evidence still largely applying. QLoRA holds the frozen base in 4-bit precision so a sizeable model can be adapted on one GPU.

    Low rank recurs: DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention caches a compressed low-rank version of keys and values, and Matryoshka embeddings make the first 256 of 1,024 dimensions work alone. Same bet: what matters lives in fewer directions than the space offers.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Common mistakes in retrieval and modelling projects, and in AI-generated code, each with its quickest check.

    • Index measure differs from the model's

      Cosine-trained vectors stored unnormalised in a dot-product index make long or generic documents hubs in every result list.

      Spot itCheck the model card, check stored lengths are all 1, and look for documents in a large share of results.
    • Sorting the wrong way

      Cosine distance (lower is better) and similarity (higher is better) get mixed, reversing the ranking.

      Spot itSearch with a document's own text. It must come back first with a perfect score.
    • Thresholds carried across models

      “Above 0.8 is a duplicate” was tuned on one model; another model's cosines sit elsewhere.

      Spot itAsk which model version set the threshold. Re-tune on labelled pairs after every model change.
    • Mixing vectors from different models or versions

      Each model has its own space. A v2 query against v1 documents gives confident nonsense.

      Spot itEvery stored vector should record its model and version. An upgrade means re-embedding everything.
    • Expecting embeddings to handle identifiers and negation

      “I did authorise this payment” and “I did not authorise this payment” sit very close. IBANs, card BINs and policy numbers barely register.

      Spot itTest near-miss pairs, including negations and exact codes. Add keyword search for identifiers.
    • Unmeasured ANN recall

      Settings tuned on 1 million vectors lose recall at 20 million, or when metadata filters remove most neighbours.

      Spot itAsk for recall@k against exact search on real queries, rerun after growth and with filters.
    • PCA on uncentred or unscaled data

      PC1 points at the average, or at the column with the biggest numbers, such as AED balances.

      Spot itCheck centring and standardisation. If PC1's loadings all share a sign, ask why.
    • Reading components as causes

      A component is a direction of variation, not a customer type or driver. Its sign is arbitrary, and components with similar variance can swap with small data changes.

      Spot itCheck stability across time periods and bootstrap samples before naming components.
    • Treating embeddings as anonymised

      Text can be partly reconstructed from its embedding, so moving vectors outside the bank or country can move personal data.

      Spot itAsk whether the vector store follows the same classification, residency and retention rules as the source text.
    • LoRA tested only on its own task

      An adapter that improves complaint classification can degrade general behaviour, Arabic fluency or refusals.

      Spot itAsk for regression tests on base capabilities with the adapter attached.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. a = (3, 4) and b = (4, 3). Find the dot product, the cosine and the Euclidean distance.

    a · b = 12 + 12 = 24. Both lengths are 5, so cos θ = 24 ÷ 25 = 0.96. a − b = (−1, 1), so distance = √2 ≈ 1.41. Identity check: 25 + 25 − 48 = 2.

    2. Two unit vectors have cosine 0.9. What's the distance?

    Distance² = 2 − 2 × 0.9 = 0.2, so 0.447. For unit vectors, distance and cosine move together.

    3. A unit-length query. Document A has length 2 and cosine 0.7; document B has length 1 and cosine 0.95. Which ranks first under the dot product, and under cosine?

    Dot product: A scores 1.4, B scores 0.95, so A wins. Cosine: B wins. A beats a better match on length alone.

    4. What does M = [[0, −1], [1, 0]] do, and what are its eigenvectors?

    It rotates the plane by 90°: (1, 0) goes to (0, 1). Every direction turns, so there are no real eigenvectors; eigenvalues are ±i. Determinant 1, so area is preserved, and both singular values are 1.

    5. Singular values 10, 5, 2, 1. What share of the squared Frobenius norm does the best rank-2 approximation keep, and what's its relative error?

    Total = 100 + 25 + 4 + 1 = 130. Rank 2 keeps 125 ÷ 130 ≈ 96.2%. Error = √5, and √5 ÷ √130 ≈ 19.6% of the norm. Squared and unsquared figures tell different stories, so say which you quote.

    6. LoRA rank 8 on a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix. How many trainable numbers, and what share of a full update?

    2 × 4,096 × 8 = 65,536, against 16,777,216: about 0.39%.

    7. Why does attention divide by √d, and what goes wrong without it?

    A d-dimensional dot product of roughly independent unit-variance entries has standard deviation √d. Unscaled, softmax puts almost all weight on one token and its gradients nearly vanish, slowing or destabilising training. Dividing keeps scores on a unit scale.
