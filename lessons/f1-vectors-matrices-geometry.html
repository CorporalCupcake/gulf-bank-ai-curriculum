    Vectors and Matrices: Lists of Numbers as Arrows

    Part 1

    What a vector is

    In one sentenceA vector is a list of numbers, like [3, 4]. Picture it as an arrow from the centre of a graph to that point.

    Why you care: in machine learning, everything gets turned into a list of numbers.

    • A house becomes [120 m², 3 bedrooms, 15 years old].
    • A sentence becomes a list of about 768 numbers, called an embedding. The model chooses them so that sentences with similar meaning get arrows pointing in similar directions.

    You can't draw 768 dimensions, but the 2D picture still works: arrows, lengths and angles. That is all you need.

    Part 2

    Comparing two vectors

    Most of the time you want to know: how similar are these two things? There are three ways to ask.

    • Angle (cosine similarity): do the arrows point the same way? Ignores how long they are.
    • Dot product: do they point the same way, and how long are they? Long arrows score higher.
    • Distance: how far apart are the two arrow tips?

    How to read the numbers

    • Cosine runs from −1 to 1. 1 means same direction, 0 means unrelated, −1 means opposite.
    • It is not a percentage. A cosine of 0.7 does not mean "70% similar". It just means fairly close in direction.
    • With text embeddings, almost every pair lands between about 0.2 and 0.9, and what counts as "close" differs between models. Use it to rank results ("which sentence is closest?"), not as an absolute score.
    • Dot product has no fixed range. Big and positive means "same way and long". Zero means at right angles. Negative means pointing apart.
    Show the formulas

    Dot product: multiply matching numbers and add. [1, 2] · [3, 4] = 1×3 + 2×4 = 11.

    Length: square each number, add, take the square root. Length of [3, 4] = √(9 + 16) = 5.

    Cosine similarity: dot product ÷ (length of A × length of B). Dividing by the lengths is what removes length and leaves only the angle.

    Part 3

    What a matrix is

    In one sentenceA matrix is a grid of numbers. Picture it either as a table of vectors, or as a machine that reshapes arrows.
    • As a table: a spreadsheet of 1,000 houses with 5 columns is a 1,000 × 5 matrix. Each row is one house's vector.
    • As a machine: multiply a vector by a matrix and you get a new vector, stretched, turned or squashed. Every layer of a neural network is this: a matrix that turns one list of numbers into another.

    How it works, in one line: each number in the output is a dot product of one row of the matrix with your vector. So a matrix with 10 rows scores your vector against 10 patterns at once and gives 10 answers.

    That is why GPUs matter: they do millions of these dot products in parallel.

    In banking, searching policy documents or routing complaints works by turning text into vectors and ranking by cosine similarity.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Mixing scales

      Salary in thousands next to age in years: salary dominates every distance and dot product. Scale features first so each counts fairly.

    • Reading cosine as a percentage

      0.7 is not "70% similar". Compare it with other pairs from the same model.

    • Dot product when length is noise

      Longer documents get bigger scores just for being long. Use cosine, or scale vectors to length 1.

    1. Two arrows point exactly the same way, but one is ten times longer. What is their cosine similarity?

    1. Cosine ignores length, so they count as identical in direction.

    2. The dot product of two vectors is 0. What does that tell you?

    They are at right angles: no overlap in direction, so the things they describe are unrelated.

    3. You're building search over documents of very different lengths. Cosine or dot product?

    Cosine, so long documents don't win just for being long.

    4. What are the two ways to picture a matrix?

    As a table where each row is a vector, or as a machine that turns one vector into another.
