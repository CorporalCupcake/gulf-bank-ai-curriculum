    B1.2 Day one About 10 minutes

    Fraud Models in Production: Late Labels and Feedback Loops

    Part 1

    What the problem is

    In one sentenceA fraud model learns from past outcomes, but those outcomes arrive weeks late and are shaped by the model's own decisions, so the training data is both incomplete and biased.

    Think of a goalkeeper who is graded only on goals, with the results logged weeks late, and where every shot they save vanishes from the record. A coach training from that record is training against a fiction.

    Running example: a bank's model scores a card payment and acts. If it blocks the payment, nobody learns whether it was fraud. If it approves a stolen card, the cardholder notices days later and the chargeback (the formal dispute that returns the money) lands weeks after that. A label is the answer "fraud" or "genuine" attached to a past payment.

    Part 2

    Problem 1: late labels

    The four routes in the picture reach your training table at different speeds, and they label different frauds.

    • Analyst confirms: fast, but only for frauds the model already flagged. It is biased towards the model's successes.
    • Customer reports: catches frauds the model missed and the customer noticed.
    • Chargeback: the formal label, but late and incomplete.
    • Never reported: small frauds nobody noticed. They stay labelled "genuine".

    The maturity curve

    The maturity curve is the share of all eventual fraud that already has a label at a given age. With a 40-day average chargeback delay, a one-week-old group of payments has few labels and a two-month-old group has about three quarters.

    This creates a trap. Caught fraud is confirmed within a day, but missed fraud waits for the chargeback. So a young group of payments over-represents the model's wins, and measured recall looks too high. Recall is the share of real fraud the model catches.

    How to read it

    • A worked reading: a model with a true recall of 80% can report about 93% on a two-week-old group. It has not improved. The missed fraud just is not on paper yet.
    • A slide from 93% towards 80% as the group ages is maturity, not decay.
    • "No label yet" is not "genuine". It means "not yet known", which statisticians call censoring. Counting it as genuine makes young-group precision look worse than it is.

    Before trusting any recent number, ask: how mature are these labels, and which frauds are still missing?

    What teams do about it

    • Embargo: train and evaluate only on payments older than a maturity point, such as when 90% of eventual fraud is labelled. The labels are cleaner, but new attacks are learned late.
    • Evaluate by label age: report recall "as of 30 days" and "as of 90 days" side by side. A drop at 30 days that is absent at 90 is maturity.
    • Use fast proxy signals: analyst decisions, failed one-time passcodes and card blocks are noisy but young. Blend a model trained on these with one trained on mature chargebacks.
    Part 3

    Problem 2: feedback loops

    A feedback loop means the model's decisions decide which outcomes you ever see. Picture a smoke alarm that gets the house evacuated every time: you never learn whether there was a fire.

    Three versions of the loop

    • Blocked means unobserved: drop the blocked rows and the pattern looks rare and safe. The retrained model relaxes, fraud returns, and it tightens again.
    • Blocked means fraud: stamp every block as "fraud" and the model copies its own mistakes with 100% apparent precision. It can never learn that it wrongly blocks a group of honest customers.
    • Models feeding each other: a model trained on labels from analysts who only saw rule alerts becomes a mirror of those rules.

    The ways out

    Every fix buys information you would not otherwise have.

    • Random holdout: approve, or step up, a small capped random share of traffic that would have been blocked. It costs some fraud, but it is the only unbiased view of what blocked traffic would do.
    • Step-up as a probe: asking for proof gives a pass or fail outcome without a loss, so it also collects data.
    • Log every decision: the score, the policy version and the chance of each action, so you can correct for the bias later.
    Part 4

    Problem 3: people have a ceiling

    Analysts can open only so many alerts a day, and attention falls when most alerts are false. Past that capacity, catchable fraud sits behind noise, like a car alarm that sounds every night until nobody looks.

    • Rank the queue by expected loss (fraud probability times amount), as a hospital triages by severity rather than arrival time.
    • Measure precision at k: of the top k alerts, how many are real? It tells you how the queue will feel.
    • Group cases: ten alerts on one compromised card are one job.

    So do not ask "did AUC go up?". Ask "what does the new model do to precision at the volume we can actually handle?" The cut-off should sit where people can still work well.

    Part 5

    In banking, a fraud model that looks fine on this month's numbers can be quietly decaying, and only labels compared at equal age, plus a small random sample of unblocked traffic, will tell you.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Judging a young group as if mature

      "Recall this month is 94%, up from 80%." Or "recall collapsed". Compare metrics at equal label age.

    • Leaving the feedback loop unchecked

      Blocked rows are dropped or stamped fraud, and performance swings after each retrain. Ask how blocked rows are labelled and whether any were let through at random.

    • Setting alert volume to look busy

      A cautious threshold yields thousands of alerts and the catch rate falls. Plot fraud stopped per analyst-hour against daily volume.

    • Trusting the fallback

      If the model service is slow and the default is "approve", attackers can exploit it. Decide the fallback deliberately and log every timeout.

    1. A model's true recall is 70%, yet a 10-day-old group shows about 89%. Why?

    Caught fraud is confirmed within days, while missed fraud waits weeks for chargebacks. The young group is missing exactly the frauds the model failed to catch.

    2. A team stamps every blocked payment "fraud" and retrains. What goes wrong?

    The model repeats its own blocks with perfect apparent precision and never discovers its false positives. Dropping the rows does the reverse: the pattern looks safe and the model relaxes.

    3. Why is "no label yet" not the same as "genuine"?

    In a young group it only means the outcome is not known yet. Treating it as genuine hides the fraud that is still on its way.

    4. Alert volume doubles from 300 to 600 with capacity 300 and an unranked queue. Why might fraud stopped fall?

    Analysts review only a random half, so about half the real fraud goes unseen, and the extra false alerts dull attention. Ranking by score or raising the threshold repairs it.

    5. Why does a small random holdout help, given that it lets some fraud through?

    It is the only unbiased evidence of what blocked traffic would have done, which stops the model forgetting why it blocks.
