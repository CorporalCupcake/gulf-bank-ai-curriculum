    Fraud Models in Production: Late Labels, Feedback Loops, Alert Fatigue and the Three-Way Decision

    Why the truth about a transaction arrives weeks late, why a model that blocks fraud starves itself of evidence, why more alerts can mean less fraud stopped, and how to turn a risk score into approve, step up or decline with the cost arithmetic behind each cut.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA fraud model is not a prediction, it is a policy that acts on people and then learns from what it let through, so you must account for labels that mature slowly, outcomes you never observe, analysts who tire, and a decision with three outcomes whose cut-offs come from costs, not from the model.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a goalkeeper who is graded only on the shots that reach the net. Every shot he saves disappears from the record. Every shot that goes in is logged weeks later, when the league office finally files the paperwork. Meanwhile his coach keeps adding shooters to his drills in proportion to how many goals the record shows. After a year, the record says he is brilliant against left-footed shots (none are logged) and hopeless against volleys (the paperwork is slow), and the coach reorganises the team around that fiction.

    A fraud model at a Gulf bank lives exactly there. It scores a card payment in a few milliseconds and acts. If it blocks, the outcome is never seen: we do not know if the customer would have complained. If it approves and the card was stolen, the cardholder notices days later, disputes, and a chargeback is filed weeks after that. Only then does the row get its label. The model that learns from this record is learning from a distorted, late and self-selected sample of its own past.

    Three practical problems follow from that, and a fourth sits at the end of the pipe where a person has to act.

    • Labels arrive late (B1.3). What you know today about last week is incomplete, and the gap is not random. It is largest for the newest fraud, which is the fraud you most want to learn.
    • Labels depend on what the model did (B1.3). Block a transaction and its outcome disappears. Retrain on what remains and the model forgets why it blocked. This is a feedback loop.
    • People are the bottleneck (B1.4). An analyst can review perhaps a few dozen alerts an hour. Flood the queue and the ones who stay sharp are the ones you never needed; the others stop looking closely.
    • The action is three-way (B1.5). Approve, ask for more proof, or decline. Each has a cost, and the right cut-off between them is an arithmetic result you can derive on a napkin.
    Hold onto thisAsk four questions of any fraud model. When does the label for yesterday's transaction become trustworthy? What happens to the data when the model says no? How many alerts can people really work well? Where, in money, does each cut-off sit? The rest of the lesson is how to answer them.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Delayed labels: how truth reaches the training table

    A card transaction gets its fraud label through one of several routes, each with its own clock:

    RouteTypical lag (order of magnitude)Who ends up labelled
    Analyst confirms an alertMinutes to a day or twoFrauds the system already flagged. Fast, and biased towards what the model already catches.
    Customer reports it (app, call centre, statement)Days; many customers notice only on a statementFrauds the model missed and the customer noticed.
    Dispute and chargeback processWeeks to a few months, set by card-scheme rules and bank processThe formal, auditable label. Late and incomplete.
    Never reportedForeverSmall frauds the customer never saw. These look like legitimate rows permanently.

    Exact lags differ by bank, product and scheme, so measure your own: for each fraud eventually confirmed, plot the days from transaction to label. That distribution is the single most useful object in this topic. Call its cumulative form the maturity curve: the share of eventual fraud that already carries a label at age a. If chargebacks take a mean of 40 days, then a one-week-old cohort has only a small share of its fraud labelled, and a two-month-old cohort roughly three quarters.

    The danger is subtle. The labels that arrive first are not a random slice. Frauds the model caught are confirmed by analysts within a day. Frauds it missed have to wait for the customer and the chargeback. So the mix of labelled fraud in a young cohort is skewed towards the model's successes, and a measured recall computed on young data looks too good. Let r be the model's true recall, dc the quick confirmation delay for caught fraud and τ the slow delay for missed fraud. Assuming exponential delays, the recall you would measure at cohort age a is

    rmeas(a)=r(1−e−a/dc)r(1−e−a/dc)+(1−r)(1−e−a/τ)

    Numerator: caught frauds labelled by age a. Denominator: all frauds labelled by age a, caught or missed. As a grows both brackets tend to one and the ratio tends to r. Example: r = 80%, dc = 2 days, τ = 40 days, a = 14 days gives 0.7993 ÷ (0.7993 + 0.0591) = 93%. The model has not improved. The missed fraud simply has not been written down yet.

    In the other direction, if you count "no label yet" as "legitimate" for precision, young cohorts look worse than they are. Which way a metric errs depends on how it is built, so never read a young-cohort number without asking: how mature are these labels, and which frauds are still missing? This is censoring, the same structure as time to default in survival analysis (F2.8), and the same point-in-time discipline as F3.1 applies to labels: a training set built today must record, for each row, only the labels that were knowable at the time you pretend to be.

    What teams do about it:

    • Embargo. Train and evaluate only on cohorts older than a chosen maturity point, say the age at which 90% of eventual fraud is labelled. You give up recency (the model is always a few weeks behind) and gain cleaner labels. The cost is a model that learns a new attack late; see F3.5 for the label-noise side of this trade.
    • Evaluate by label age. Report recall for the cohort of March "as of 30 days" and "as of 90 days" side by side. A fall in the 30-day figure that is absent at 90 days is maturity, not decay.
    • Use fast proxy signals. Analyst decisions, customer contact outcomes, OTP failures, card blocks, device-risk flags. They are noisy but young. A common design trains one component on fresh, noisy feedback and another on old, clean chargebacks, then blends. Dal Pozzolo et al. showed on three years of real card data that the two sources are worth keeping separate rather than pooled.
    • Predict the missing labels. Treat the label of a young row as a partial observation and model its eventual probability from the maturity curve (the survival-analysis view). Recent work goes further and tries to detect model degradation before the labels arrive (Part 4).
    • Watch for covariate drift as the early warning. The input mix (merchant categories, devices, geographies) shifts without waiting for labels. F7.7 covers PSI and friends; they cannot see a change in the relationship between inputs and fraud, but they are quick.

    Feedback loops: the model changes the data it will learn from

    Delay is a timing problem. A feedback loop is a causal one. The model's decisions determine which outcomes you ever observe. There are two common versions and they push in opposite directions.

    • Blocked means unobserved ("selective labels"). If a payment is declined, you never learn whether it was fraud. Drop those rows from training and the model sees fewer and fewer examples of the pattern it blocks, and the fraud rate in that pattern among approved rows looks low. The retrained model relaxes. Fraud returns. It tightens again. The literature calls this the selective labels problem (Lakkaraju et al., 2017).
    • Blocked means fraud (self-confirming labels). If instead every blocked transaction is stamped "fraud" in the training table, the model learns to reproduce its own past blocks, including its mistakes, and its apparent precision on those rows is 100% by construction. It can never discover that it has been wrongly blocking a customer segment, because nobody is allowed to be proved innocent.
    • Rules and models feeding each other. Model B is trained on labels produced by analysts who only saw what Model A or the rules alerted. Its notion of fraud is a shadow of their notion of fraud. The same holds for any queue that is ordered by a score.

    Sculley and colleagues named this class of problem "hidden feedback loops" in their classic paper on technical debt in machine learning: a model that influences the data it will later be trained on, which makes the system hard to reason about from the training metrics alone.

    The ways out are all forms of deliberately getting information you would not otherwise have:

    • Random holdout or exploration. Let a small random share of otherwise-blocked traffic through, or step it up rather than decline, within a risk limit the business has signed off. It costs real money and must be approved as such, usually capped by amount. It is the only unbiased estimate of what the blocked traffic would have done. This is the same exploration idea as in bandits (F9.7).
    • Randomised thresholds. Jitter the cut-off slightly so that nearby scores get both treatments; it gives you counterfactual information near the boundary and allows inverse-propensity weighting (Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015).
    • Step-up as a probe. A challenge the customer passes or fails gives an outcome without a loss, which is why the three-way decision is also a data-collection tool.
    • Causal care in training. Treat the decision as a treatment and the training set as observational data with a known assignment mechanism (F9.3, F2.6). Keep decision logs with the score, the policy version and the propensity, or you cannot correct later.

    Alert fatigue: a human sets the real ceiling

    A fraud model's output is only useful if someone acts on it. In many banks, for the alerts that are not auto-decided, that someone is an analyst with a queue. Two things limit the system: capacity (how many alerts the team can open per day) and attention (how likely an analyst is to recognise a genuine fraud when most alerts in front of them are false). Industry commentary and vendor surveys consistently describe false-positive shares among alerts of 90% or more in transaction monitoring, and burnout among analysts; those are surveys by vendors and trade groups, not controlled studies, so treat the exact figures with care (Part 4). The mechanism is what matters.

    Precision (the share of alerts that are real) is therefore not a vanity metric. It is the fraction of the analyst's day that is useful, and, by the effect we are about to simulate, it also changes how well the useful part is done. Two mechanisms apply when volume rises beyond what people can handle:

    • Unreviewed alerts. If alerts beyond capacity are ignored or auto-released, the frauds among them are lost. If the queue is first-come, first-served, the ones lost are essentially random: fraud you could have caught sits behind noise.
    • Degraded review. Analysts who see mostly noise spend less time per alert, skim, and miss real cases. The evidence for this in banking is mostly anecdotal and vendor-reported; the broader human-factors literature on alarm fatigue (clinical monitoring, security operations) supports the shape. I model it below as an assumption, clearly labelled.

    Precision targeting: spend scarce attention where the money is

    If you can only handle C alerts a day, the best thing you can do is to send the C highest-risk ones, ordered by expected loss (probability times amount), not first-come first-served. That is precision at k: of the top k alerts, how many are real? It is the metric that tells the fraud operations head what the queue will feel like. It is derived from the same ranking as AUC but answers a different question, and it connects to the thresholds of F7.

    Beyond ordering, there are ways to raise precision without losing much recall:

    • Segment thresholds. Different cut-offs by product, channel or customer segment, because base rates differ. A single global threshold is rarely optimal.
    • Auto-clear the obvious. Use a second, cheap model or rule to dismiss alerts that match patterns analysts always release (the customer's regular merchant, a known recurring payment), with sampling to check the dismissals are safe.
    • Case grouping. Ten alerts on one compromised card are one job, not ten. Group by card, device or beneficiary.
    • Better evidence in the alert. Reason codes (F8), recent history and device match, so each review takes seconds, not minutes.
    • Feedback from analysts as labels, with a random audit sample to defend against the selective-labels loop above.

    Notice what the simulation does not assume: any change to the model. The loss comes from where the threshold sits relative to what people can do. That is why alert volume should be set from capacity and cost, jointly, and revisited when staffing changes. It is also why the question to ask on any new model is not "does AUC go up?" but "what does it do to precision at the volume we can handle?"

    Approve, step up, decline: three actions, two thresholds

    Most real-time systems do not choose between "allow" and "block". They have a middle option: step-up authentication, such as a challenge in the banking app, a one-time code, a biometric or a call-back. It costs the customer a little friction, it stops many fraudsters, and it gives the bank an outcome. So the decision is a three-way fork, and each branch has an expected cost given the model's calibrated fraud probability p and the transaction amount L:

    approve: pL step up: (1−p)cs+p(1−s)L decline: (1−p)cd

    s is the share of fraud a step-up stops (fraudsters fail the challenge; social-engineered victims who hand over the code do not, so s is well below 1 for scams). cs is the cost of challenging a genuine customer: friction, drop-off, support calls. cd is the cost of wrongly declining a genuine customer, much bigger: embarrassment, a call to the contact centre, a blocked card, sometimes a lost relationship. Solve each pair for equality and you get two thresholds on probability:

    p1=cssL+cs,p2=cd−cs(1−s)L+cd−cs

    Approve below p1, step up between, decline above p2. Example: L = AED 1,500, s = 80%, cs = AED 8, cd = AED 120. Then p1 = 8 ÷ (1,200 + 8) = 0.66% and p2 = 112 ÷ (300 + 112) = 27%. A transaction only a little more likely than average to be fraud (0.5% is the base rate) already merits a challenge; you decline only above one in four.

    Three consequences worth remembering. First, the thresholds depend on the amount: p1 falls as L rises, so a AED 15,000 transfer is stepped up at a far lower risk than a AED 40 coffee. A single score cut-off for all amounts leaves money on the table. Second, everything assumes calibrated probabilities. If the model was trained on resampled data, correct it first (F3.5). Third, the formula assumes you can estimate s, which is itself a measurement problem: it must come from experiments, not from the vendor's brochure, and it shifts when fraudsters learn the challenge (deepfaked calls, remote-access scams; see A9).

    Milliseconds: what the budget does to the design

    An authorisation decision has a hard clock. Card-network and payment-switch timeouts leave the issuer a small window (commonly quoted in the tens to low hundreds of milliseconds for the model call; check your own SLA and scheme rules, since I am not quoting a specific number as fact). If your score does not return in time, the system applies a fallback: approve, decline, or a simple rule, depending on risk appetite. That fallback is part of the fraud policy, and its failures show up as sudden losses or sudden declines, not as AUC changes. Four design consequences:

    • Features are split by freshness. Slow, rich features (a customer's 90-day profile) are precomputed and fetched from a low-latency store; fast ones (the last three transactions, the device seen a minute ago) are computed on the stream. The two must match what training saw, or you get silent skew (P1).
    • Model size is a business decision. A gradient-boosted ensemble scores in around a millisecond on a CPU; a large neural network may need a GPU and a careful batch strategy. Latency tails (the slowest 1% of calls), not averages, decide the fallback rate. See P2.
    • Layers. A fast rules layer handles hard blocks (sanctioned beneficiary, known compromised card), the model scores the rest, and a slower asynchronous process later reviews cases after the fact. Rules and models share a policy, they do not compete (B2).
    • Everything is logged for later. The score, the features at decision time, the action, the policy version and, if randomised, the propensity. Without them neither delayed labels nor feedback correction nor audit is possible. The full end-to-end design is in P5.

    Step-up also depends on the local authentication landscape. Gulf regulators have been pushing banks towards stronger, app-based authentication and away from SMS codes for some flows; I have not verified the current CBUAE or SAMA requirements and dates, so confirm with your compliance team before writing them into a design.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Card-not-present and e-commerce fraud. Chargeback delays, 3-D Secure style challenges and the three-way fork are daily reality.

    Where the field is now

    The problems are well known; what is newer is detecting trouble before labels arrive, treating decisions as experiments, and using models to cut alert volume. Evidence about alert fatigue itself is thin and largely from vendors.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging a young cohort as if it were mature

      "Recall this month is 94%, up from 80%." Or the opposite: "recall collapsed".

      Spot itAsk for the metric at equal label age across months. Plot labelled fraud counts by week of transaction; the last weeks slope down.
    • Blocked transactions dropped, or stamped as fraud

      Either choice distorts the next model; neither is neutral.

      Spot itAsk how blocked and declined rows are labelled in the training table, and whether any of them were ever allowed through at random.
    • A model that quietly learns the rules

      It scores high where rules alerted because the labels came from those alerts. Overlap with old rules is near 100%, with no new fraud found.

      Spot itMeasure the share of confirmed fraud found only by the model. If it is tiny, the model is a rule mirror.
    • Oscillating performance after each retrain

      Fraud drops, then rebounds, then drops, in step with the retraining cadence.

      Spot itOverlay retrain dates on the fraud-loss chart. Look for the loop in Simulation 2.
    • Alert volume set to look busy

      A cautious threshold produces thousands of alerts and the team's catch rate falls.

      Spot itPlot fraud stopped per analyst-hour against daily volume. If it bends down above some volume, you are past the peak of Simulation 3.
    • One global threshold

      The same 0.8 cut for AED 30 and AED 30,000 payments, for card-present and online, for new and old customers.

      Spot itCompute the break-even threshold per amount band with the Part 2 formula. If they differ by an order of magnitude, one cut-off is wrong for most of them.
    • Uncalibrated scores fed into cost thresholds

      The 0.66% and 27% cut-offs of the example only work if "0.27" really means 27%.

      Spot itReliability plot on a recent, representative, mature sample, especially after any resampling or weights.
    • Believing the step-up effectiveness number

      A vendor says challenges stop 98% of fraud. Social-engineered customers pass them.

      Spot itAsk how s was measured, on which fraud types, and whether it was split by scam versus account takeover.
    • No fallback policy for timeouts

      The model service is slow for ten minutes; the default is "approve".

      Spot itAsk what happens at timeout, how often it occurred last quarter, and who approved that default.
    • Unfair friction

      Declines and challenges fall more on some nationalities, languages, devices or branches than risk justifies.

      Spot itCompare step-up and decline rates, and their later overturned share, across groups (G2).
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a head of fraud · 30 seconds
    “Two things limit us besides the model: how fast we find out what was really fraud, and how many alerts your team can work well. Chargebacks take weeks, so any figure for the last month is provisional; I'd report recall at fixed label ages. And past a certain volume more alerts catch less fraud, because the queue fills with noise. I'd set volume from your capacity and what a missed fraud costs, and use challenges for the middle band, so we only decline when we are quite sure.”
    To a model validator or technical interviewer
    “The system is a policy, not a predictor. Labels are delayed and selectively observed, so I'd keep decision logs with score, policy version and propensity, run a small capped random holdout on blocked traffic, evaluate by label age with an embargo, and calibrate before deriving thresholds. Approve, step-up and decline cut-offs follow from the cost matrix and are amount-dependent. I'd monitor input and score drift as the early warning and compare to mature performance when it arrives.”
    “Recall jumped from 80% to 93% last fortnight. Great news?”

    Probably not. Frauds the model caught are confirmed in days; frauds it missed take weeks to be disputed. A two-week-old cohort therefore over-represents the model's successes. Compare equal label age with earlier cohorts, and revisit after 60 or 90 days.

    “Why not just retrain daily on everything?”

    Daily data is the most immature and the most self-selected. You would teach the model that recent patterns are rarely fraud (labels missing) and that blocked patterns vanish (no outcomes). Retrain often if you must, but with an embargo or label-age handling, fresh feedback as a separate component, and a randomised sample so the blocked region is still visible.

    “Isn't letting known-risky transactions through on purpose reckless?”

    It is a cost, so it should be capped, sized and signed off like any risk limit: small amounts, a tiny share, bounded loss per day. The alternative is blindness. In the toy case, 10% exploration cost about AED 8,000 per round and avoided oscillation worth tens of thousands. Another route is to step up rather than approve, which produces outcomes without the loss.

    “Can we cut false alerts by raising the threshold?”

    Yes, and you lose fraud. The question is the exchange rate. At each volume compute fraud stopped and net money with realistic attention. If the team is over capacity, a higher threshold plus ranking can stop more fraud, not less. If it is under capacity, raising the threshold only loses catches.

    “Why three actions? Decline is simple.”

    Declining a genuine customer costs much more than challenging one, and many suspicious transactions are only moderately likely to be fraud. A challenge converts uncertainty into information at a small friction cost. In my worked example it begins at 0.66% probability and declines begin at 27%, so most of the risky mass is handled by the cheap middle action.

    “How do you know the model works if you can't see what it blocked?”

    Not from blocked rows alone. Use the random holdout for an unbiased estimate, a counterfactual evaluator with logged propensities, challenge outcomes, customer complaints about declines, and long-run fraud losses per million transactions against a control. And be honest that part of the answer is an assumption.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. True recall is 70%, caught fraud is confirmed in 2 days on average, missed fraud in 30. What recall would you measure on a cohort 10 days old?

    Caught labelled: 0.7 × (1 − e−5) = 0.7 × 0.9933 = 0.6953. Missed labelled: 0.3 × (1 − e−1/3) = 0.3 × 0.2835 = 0.0850. Measured = 0.6953 ÷ 0.7803 ≈ 89%, nineteen points above the truth.

    2. A team stamps every blocked transaction "fraud" and retrains. What goes wrong, and how is it different from dropping blocked rows?

    The model learns to repeat its own blocks, with perfect apparent precision on those rows, and can never discover false positives. Dropping them does the reverse: the blocked pattern looks rare or safe among approved rows and the model relaxes. Both come from not knowing the counterfactual; random exploration or step-up outcomes supply it.

    3. Compute the approve and decline thresholds for L = AED 600, s = 90%, cs = AED 5, cd = AED 80.

    p1 = 5 ÷ (0.9 × 600 + 5) = 5 ÷ 545 ≈ 0.92%. p2 = (80 − 5) ÷ (0.1 × 600 + 80 − 5) = 75 ÷ 135 ≈ 56%. Step up between those.

    4. The amount doubles and nothing else changes. Which way does the approve threshold move, and the decline threshold?

    p1 falls (a larger loss makes even small probabilities worth a challenge). p2 also falls, because the residual loss on stepped-up fraud grows, so declines start earlier. That is why a single score cut-off for all amounts is wrong.

    5. Alert volume doubles from 300 to 600 with capacity 300, queue not ranked. Why might fraud stopped go down?

    Analysts still review 300, but now a random 300 of the 600. Roughly half the real fraud in the queue goes unreviewed, and the extra alerts are mostly false, so precision (and attention) falls. Ranking by score, or raising the threshold back, repairs it.

    6. Your fraud rate looks stable but confirmed fraud found only by the model is shrinking. What is a likely cause?

    The labels are increasingly generated by alerts the model itself produces, so the training and evaluation data tilt towards what it already finds. Add a random review sample of unflagged traffic, and measure model-only detections against it.

    7. Why are delayed labels a survival-analysis problem?

    For a young cohort, a missing fraud label means "not yet" rather than "no". That is right-censoring. The maturity curve is the label-arrival distribution, and survival methods use it to turn partial information into estimates of the eventual rate instead of treating missing as negative.

    8. A model's calibration is off because it was trained on undersampled data. How does this affect step-up thresholds?

    Probabilities are inflated, so the model would challenge or decline far too many transactions at the cost-derived thresholds. Correct the probabilities (prior correction or recalibration on an untouched mature sample) before deriving or applying p1 and p2.
