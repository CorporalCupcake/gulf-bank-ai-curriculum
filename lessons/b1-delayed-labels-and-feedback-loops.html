    Fraud Models in Production: Late Labels, Feedback Loops, Alert Fatigue and the Three-Way Decision

    Why the truth about a transaction arrives weeks late, why a model that blocks fraud starves itself of evidence, why more alerts can mean less fraud stopped, and how to turn a risk score into approve, step up or decline with the cost arithmetic behind each cut.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA fraud model is not a prediction, it is a policy that acts on people and then learns from what it let through, so you must account for labels that mature slowly, outcomes you never observe, analysts who tire, and a decision with three outcomes whose cut-offs come from costs, not from the model.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a goalkeeper graded only on shots that reach the net. Saved shots vanish from the record. Goals are logged weeks later, when the paperwork is filed. The coach adds drills based on that record, so he trains against a fiction.

    A fraud model scores a card payment in milliseconds and acts. If it blocks, nobody learns whether it was fraud. If it approves a stolen card, the cardholder notices days later, disputes, and the chargeback lands weeks after that. Only then does the row get a label. The model learns from a late, self-selected sample of its own past. Four problems follow.

    • Labels arrive late (B1.3). Yesterday's truth is incomplete, and most incomplete for the newest fraud, which is what you most want to learn.
    • Labels depend on what the model did (B1.3). Block a transaction and its outcome disappears. Retrain on what is left and the model forgets why it blocked. This is a feedback loop.
    • People are the bottleneck (B1.4). An analyst reviews a few dozen alerts an hour. Flood the queue and attention drops.
    • The action is three-way (B1.5). Approve, ask for proof, or decline. The cut-offs come from cost arithmetic.
    Hold onto thisAsk four questions of any fraud model. When is yesterday's label trustworthy? What happens to the data when the model says no? How many alerts can people work well? Where, in money, does each cut-off sit?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Delayed labels: how truth reaches the training table

    A transaction gets its fraud label by one of four routes, each on its own clock:

    RouteTypical lagWho ends up labelled
    Analyst confirms an alertMinutes to a day or twoFrauds the model already flagged. Fast, biased towards its successes.
    Customer reports itDays; many only notice on a statementFrauds the model missed and the customer noticed.
    Dispute and chargebackWeeks to months, set by scheme rulesThe formal, auditable label. Late and incomplete.
    Never reportedForeverSmall frauds nobody saw. They look legitimate permanently.

    Measure your own lags: for each confirmed fraud, plot days from transaction to label. Its cumulative form is the maturity curve: the share of eventual fraud already labelled at age a. With a 40-day mean chargeback delay, a one-week-old cohort has few labels and a two-month-old cohort about three quarters.

    Think of exam results where the good scripts are marked first. Early averages look great, then fall as the rest arrive. Likewise, caught fraud is confirmed within a day while missed fraud waits for the chargeback, so young cohorts over-represent the model's successes and measured recall looks too high. With true recall r, quick delay dc for caught fraud and slow delay τ for missed fraud (exponential delays), recall measured at age a is

    rmeas(a)=r(1−e−a/dc)r(1−e−a/dc)+(1−r)(1−e−a/τ)

    Numerator: caught frauds labelled by age a. Denominator: all frauds labelled by age a, caught or missed. As a grows both brackets tend to one and the ratio tends to r. Example: r = 80%, dc = 2 days, τ = 40 days, a = 14 days gives 0.7993 ÷ (0.7993 + 0.0591) = 93%. The model has not improved. The missed fraud simply has not been written down yet.

    Reading it: 93% at 14 days against a true 80% is not improvement. It is missing paperwork. Going from 93% towards 80% as the cohort ages is maturity, not decay. The opposite error also exists: counting "no label yet" as legitimate makes young precision look worse. Before reading any young-cohort number, ask how mature are these labels, and which frauds are still missing? This is censoring, as in survival analysis (F2.8), and the same point-in-time discipline as F3.1 applies: a training set built today must hold only the labels knowable at the date you pretend to be at.

    What teams do about it:

    • Embargo. Train and evaluate only on cohorts older than a maturity point, say when 90% of eventual fraud is labelled. Cleaner labels, but the model learns new attacks late (see F3.5).
    • Evaluate by label age. Report March recall "as of 30 days" and "as of 90 days" side by side. A fall at 30 days that is absent at 90 is maturity.
    • Use fast proxy signals. Analyst decisions, OTP failures, card blocks, device-risk flags: noisy but young. Train one component on fresh feedback and another on mature chargebacks, then blend. Dal Pozzolo et al. found on three years of card data that separate sources beat pooling.
    • Predict the missing labels. Treat a young row's label as partial and model its eventual probability from the maturity curve.
    • Watch covariate drift as early warning. Merchant, device and geography mix shifts without waiting for labels. F7.7 covers PSI; it misses changes in the input-to-fraud relationship but is quick.

    Feedback loops: the model changes the data it will learn from

    Delay is timing. A feedback loop is causal: the model's decisions decide which outcomes you ever see. Compare a smoke alarm that, once it sounds, gets the house evacuated: you never learn if there was a fire. Sculley et al. called this a "hidden feedback loop". Three versions:

    • Blocked means unobserved ("selective labels"). Drop blocked rows and the pattern looks rare and safe among approved rows. The retrained model relaxes, fraud returns, it tightens again (Lakkaraju et al., 2017).
    • Blocked means fraud (self-confirming labels). Stamp every block "fraud" and the model reproduces its own mistakes, with 100% apparent precision by construction. It can never learn it wrongly blocks a segment.
    • Models feeding each other. Model B trained on labels from analysts who only saw what Model A or the rules alerted is a shadow of those rules. The same holds for any score-ordered queue.

    The ways out all buy information you would not otherwise have:

    • Random holdout or exploration. Approve (or step up) a small random share of otherwise-blocked traffic, capped by amount and signed off by the business. Costly, but the only unbiased view of what blocked traffic would do. Same idea as bandits (F9.7).
    • Randomised thresholds. Jitter the cut-off so nearby scores get both treatments, enabling inverse-propensity weighting (Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015).
    • Step-up as a probe. A pass or fail gives an outcome without a loss, so the three-way decision also collects data.
    • Causal care. Treat the decision as a treatment (F9.3, F2.6). Log score, policy version and propensity, or you cannot correct later.

    Alert fatigue: a human sets the real ceiling

    A model's output matters only if someone acts. Two things limit the system: capacity (alerts the team can open per day) and attention (chance an analyst spots a real fraud when most alerts are false). Vendor surveys describe false-positive shares of 90% or more in transaction monitoring; these are not controlled studies, so treat the figures with care. The mechanism is what matters.

    Think of a car alarm that sounds every night: nobody looks. Precision (the share of alerts that are real) is the useful fraction of the analyst's day, and it also drives how well that day is spent. Beyond capacity, two things happen:

    • Unreviewed alerts. Alerts past capacity are ignored or auto-released. In a first-come queue the loss is random: catchable fraud sits behind noise.
    • Degraded review. Analysts seeing mostly noise skim and miss real cases. Banking evidence is mostly anecdotal; alarm-fatigue research in clinical and security settings supports the shape. The simulation below treats it as a labelled assumption.

    Precision targeting: spend scarce attention where the money is

    With capacity for C alerts a day, send the C highest-risk, ordered by expected loss (probability times amount), like a hospital triaging by severity rather than arrival time. Precision at k asks: of the top k alerts, how many are real? It tells the head of fraud operations what the queue will feel like. It uses the same ranking as AUC but answers a different question (F7). More ways to raise precision without losing much recall:

    • Segment thresholds by product, channel or customer, since base rates differ.
    • Auto-clear the obvious (regular merchant, known recurring payment) with sampling to check it is safe.
    • Case grouping. Ten alerts on one compromised card are one job.
    • Better evidence in the alert: reason codes (F8), recent history, device match, so a review takes seconds.
    • Analyst feedback as labels, with a random audit sample to defend against the selective-labels loop.

    The simulation assumes no model change. The loss comes from where the threshold sits relative to what people can do. So set alert volume from capacity and cost together, and ask of any new model not "does AUC go up?" but "what does it do to precision at the volume we can handle?"

    Approve, step up, decline: three actions, two thresholds

    Most real-time systems have a middle option, step-up authentication (in-app challenge, one-time code, biometric, call-back). It is like a bouncer asking for ID rather than refusing entry: small friction, stops many fraudsters, and yields an outcome. For calibrated fraud probability p and amount L, the expected costs are:

    approve: pL step up: (1−p)cs+p(1−s)L decline: (1−p)cd

    s is the share of fraud a step-up stops (well below 1 for scams, where victims hand over the code). cs is the cost of challenging a genuine customer: friction, drop-off, calls. cd is the much larger cost of wrongly declining one. Setting neighbouring costs equal gives two thresholds:

    p1=cssL+cs,p2=cd−cs(1−s)L+cd−cs

    Approve below p1, step up between, decline above p2. Example: L = AED 1,500, s = 80%, cs = AED 8, cd = AED 120 gives p1 = 8 ÷ (1,200 + 8) = 0.66% and p2 = 112 ÷ (300 + 112) = 27%. A transaction barely riskier than the 0.5% base rate already merits a challenge; you decline only above one in four.

    • Thresholds depend on amount. p1 falls as L rises: a AED 15,000 transfer is challenged at far lower risk than a AED 40 coffee. One cut-off for all amounts wastes money.
    • Probabilities must be calibrated. If trained on resampled data, correct first (F3.5).
    • You must measure s by experiment, not from a vendor brochure. It shifts when fraudsters adapt (deepfaked calls, remote-access scams; A9).

    Milliseconds: what the budget does to the design

    An authorisation decision has a hard clock: commonly tens to low hundreds of milliseconds for the model call (check your own SLA and scheme rules). If the score is late, a fallback applies: approve, decline, or a simple rule. The fallback is part of the fraud policy, and its failures show up as sudden losses or declines, not AUC changes. Four design consequences:

    • Split features by freshness. Slow features (90-day profile) are precomputed in a low-latency store; fast ones (last three transactions) are computed on the stream. Both must match training, or silent skew follows (P1).
    • Model size is a business decision. Gradient boosting scores in about a millisecond on a CPU; a large network may need a GPU. Tail latency (slowest 1%), not the average, sets the fallback rate (P2).
    • Layers. Rules handle hard blocks (sanctioned beneficiary, compromised card), the model scores the rest, and an asynchronous process reviews later. Rules and models share a policy (B2).
    • Log everything. Score, decision-time features, action, policy version and any propensity. Without them neither delayed labels, feedback correction nor audit is possible (P5).

    Gulf regulators have pushed banks towards app-based authentication over SMS for some flows. I have not verified current CBUAE or SAMA requirements, so confirm with compliance.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging a young cohort as mature

      "Recall this month is 94%, up from 80%." Or "recall collapsed".

      Spot itCompare metrics at equal label age. Plot labelled fraud by transaction week; the last weeks slope down.
    • Blocked rows dropped, or stamped fraud

      Either distorts the next model.

      Spot itAsk how blocked rows are labelled and whether any were ever let through at random.
    • A model that quietly learns the rules

      It scores high where rules alerted, because labels came from those alerts. It finds no new fraud.

      Spot itMeasure the share of confirmed fraud found only by the model. If tiny, it is a rule mirror.
    • Oscillating performance after each retrain

      Fraud drops, rebounds, drops, in step with retraining.

      Spot itOverlay retrain dates on the fraud-loss chart. Look for the loop in Simulation 2.
    • Alert volume set to look busy

      A cautious threshold yields thousands of alerts and the catch rate falls.

      Spot itPlot fraud stopped per analyst-hour against daily volume. A downward bend means you are past the peak of Simulation 3.
    • One global threshold

      The same cut for AED 30 and AED 30,000 payments, card-present and online, new and old customers.

      Spot itCompute break-even thresholds per amount band. If they differ by an order of magnitude, one cut-off is wrong for most.
    • Uncalibrated scores in cost thresholds

      The 0.66% and 27% cut-offs only work if "0.27" really means 27%.

      Spot itReliability plot on a recent, mature sample, especially after resampling or weights.
    • Believing the step-up effectiveness number

      A vendor says challenges stop 98% of fraud. Scam victims pass them.

      Spot itAsk how s was measured, on which fraud types, split by scam versus account takeover.
    • No fallback policy for timeouts

      The model service is slow for ten minutes; the default is "approve".

      Spot itAsk what happens at timeout, how often last quarter, and who approved the default.
    • Unfair friction

      Declines and challenges fall harder on some nationalities, languages, devices or branches than risk justifies.

      Spot itCompare step-up and decline rates, and overturned share, across groups (G2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. True recall is 70%, caught fraud is confirmed in 2 days on average, missed fraud in 30. What recall would you measure on a cohort 10 days old?

    Caught labelled: 0.7 × (1 − e−5) = 0.6953. Missed labelled: 0.3 × (1 − e−1/3) = 0.0850. Measured = 0.6953 ÷ 0.7803 ≈ 89%, nineteen points above the truth.

    2. A team stamps every blocked transaction "fraud" and retrains. What goes wrong, and how does it differ from dropping blocked rows?

    The model repeats its own blocks with perfect apparent precision and never discovers false positives. Dropping them does the reverse: the pattern looks safe and the model relaxes. Both come from not knowing the counterfactual; random exploration or step-up outcomes supply it.

    3. Compute the approve and decline thresholds for L = AED 600, s = 90%, cs = AED 5, cd = AED 80.

    p1 = 5 ÷ (0.9 × 600 + 5) = 5 ÷ 545 ≈ 0.92%. p2 = 75 ÷ (0.1 × 600 + 75) = 75 ÷ 135 ≈ 56%. Step up between.

    4. The amount doubles and nothing else changes. Which way do the approve and decline thresholds move?

    Both fall. A larger loss makes small probabilities worth a challenge (p1), and the residual loss on stepped-up fraud grows, so declines start earlier (p2). Hence one cut-off for all amounts is wrong.

    5. Alert volume doubles from 300 to 600 with capacity 300, queue unranked. Why might fraud stopped fall?

    Analysts review a random 300 of 600, so about half the real fraud goes unreviewed, and extra false alerts cut precision and attention. Ranking by score, or raising the threshold, repairs it.

    6. Fraud rate looks stable but confirmed fraud found only by the model is shrinking. Likely cause?

    Labels increasingly come from alerts the model itself produces, so data tilts towards what it already finds. Add a random review sample of unflagged traffic and measure model-only detections against it.

    7. Why are delayed labels a survival-analysis problem?

    In a young cohort a missing fraud label means "not yet", not "no": right-censoring. The maturity curve is the label-arrival distribution, and survival methods use it to estimate the eventual rate instead of treating missing as negative.

    8. A model trained on undersampled data is miscalibrated. How does that affect step-up thresholds?

    Probabilities are inflated, so too many transactions are challenged or declined at the cost-derived thresholds. Correct them (prior correction or recalibration on an untouched mature sample) before applying p1 and p2.
