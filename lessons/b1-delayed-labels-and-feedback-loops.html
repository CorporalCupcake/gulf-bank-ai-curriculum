    Fraud Models in Production: Late Labels, Feedback Loops, Alert Fatigue and the Three-Way Decision

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture a goalkeeper graded only on goals, logged weeks late, while saved shots vanish from the record. The coach trains against a fiction.

    A fraud model scores a card payment in milliseconds and acts. If it blocks, nobody learns whether it was fraud. If it approves a stolen card, the cardholder notices days later and the chargeback lands weeks after that. Four problems follow.

    • Labels arrive late (B1.3). Yesterday's truth is incomplete, most of all for the newest fraud.
    • Labels depend on what the model did (B1.3). Block a transaction and its outcome disappears. Retrain on what is left and the model forgets why it blocked.
    • People are the bottleneck (B1.4). Flood the analyst queue and attention drops.
    • The action is three-way (B1.5). Approve, ask for proof, or decline. The cut-offs come from cost.
    Hold onto thisAsk four questions of any fraud model. When is yesterday's label trustworthy? What happens to the data when the model says no? How many alerts can people work well? Where, in money, does each cut-off sit?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Delayed labels: how truth reaches the training table

    A transaction gets its fraud label by one of four routes, each on its own clock:

    RouteTypical lagWho ends up labelled
    Analyst confirms an alertMinutes to a day or twoFrauds the model already flagged. Fast, but biased towards its successes.
    Customer reports itDaysFrauds the model missed and the customer noticed.
    Dispute and chargebackWeeks to months, set by scheme rulesThe formal label. Late and incomplete.
    Never reportedForeverSmall frauds nobody saw. They look legitimate for ever.

    The maturity curve is the share of eventual fraud already labelled at a given age. With a 40-day mean chargeback delay, a one-week-old cohort has few labels and a two-month-old cohort about three quarters.

    Caught fraud is confirmed within a day while missed fraud waits for the chargeback, so young cohorts over-represent the model's successes and measured recall looks too high. A model with a true recall of 80% can report about 93% on a two-week-old cohort. The model has not improved; the missed fraud is simply not yet on paper.

    A drift from 93% towards 80% as the cohort ages is maturity, not decay. Counting "no label yet" as legitimate makes young precision look worse. Before reading any young-cohort number, ask how mature are these labels, and which frauds are still missing? This is censoring (F2.8), and the point-in-time discipline of F3.1 applies: train only on labels knowable at the date you pretend to be at.

    What teams do about it:

    • Embargo. Train and evaluate only on cohorts older than a maturity point, say when 90% of eventual fraud is labelled. Cleaner labels, but new attacks are learned late.
    • Evaluate by label age. Report recall "as of 30 days" and "as of 90 days" side by side. A fall at 30 days that is absent at 90 is maturity.
    • Use fast proxy signals. Analyst decisions, OTP failures, card blocks: noisy but young. Blend a component trained on fresh feedback with one trained on mature chargebacks. Input drift (F7.7) is a quick early warning too.

    Feedback loops: the model changes the data it will learn from

    A feedback loop is causal: the model's decisions decide which outcomes you ever see. Think of a smoke alarm that gets the house evacuated: you never learn if there was a fire. Three versions:

    • Blocked means unobserved. Drop blocked rows and the pattern looks rare and safe among approved rows. The retrained model relaxes, fraud returns, it tightens again.
    • Blocked means fraud. Stamp every block "fraud" and the model reproduces its own mistakes, with 100% apparent precision. It can never learn it wrongly blocks a segment.
    • Models feeding each other. A model trained on labels from analysts who only saw rule alerts becomes a shadow of those rules.

    The ways out all buy information you would not otherwise have:

    • Random holdout or exploration. Approve (or step up) a small, capped random share of otherwise-blocked traffic. Costly, but the only unbiased view of what blocked traffic would do (F9.7).
    • Step-up as a probe. A pass or fail gives an outcome without a loss, so the three-way decision also collects data.
    • Log the decision. Record score, policy version and the chance of each action, or you cannot correct later (F9.3, F2.6).

    Alert fatigue: a human sets the real ceiling

    Two things limit the system: capacity (alerts the team can open per day) and attention (the chance an analyst spots a real fraud when most alerts are false). Vendor surveys report false-positive shares of 90% or more; treat the figure loosely.

    Think of a car alarm that sounds every night: nobody looks. Alerts past capacity are ignored, so catchable fraud sits behind noise. And analysts who see mostly noise skim and miss real cases (a labelled assumption in the simulation).

    Precision targeting: spend scarce attention where the money is

    Send analysts the highest-risk alerts, ordered by expected loss (probability times amount), like a hospital triaging by severity rather than arrival time. Precision at k asks: of the top k alerts, how many are real? It tells you what the queue will feel like (F7). Ways to raise it:

    • Segment thresholds by product, channel or customer, since base rates differ.
    • Auto-clear the obvious (known recurring payment), with sampling to check.
    • Group cases. Ten alerts on one compromised card are one job.

    The model did not change here. The loss comes from where the threshold sits relative to what people can do. Ask of any new model not "does AUC go up?" but "what does it do to precision at the volume we can handle?"

    Approve, step up, decline: three actions, two thresholds

    Most real-time systems have a middle option, step-up authentication (in-app challenge, one-time code, biometric, call-back). Like a bouncer asking for ID rather than refusing entry, it is small friction that stops many fraudsters and yields an outcome.

    The logic is a cost comparison: estimate what each action is expected to cost and pick the cheapest.

    • Approve costs the fraud probability times the amount at risk.
    • Step up costs a small friction charge on every genuine customer challenged, plus the fraud that still gets past the challenge.
    • Decline costs the much larger price of wrongly refusing a genuine customer.

    That yields two cut-offs: approve below the first, decline above the second, challenge in between. Example: a AED 1,500 payment, step-up stopping 80% of fraud, AED 8 of friction, AED 120 for a wrongful decline. A transaction barely riskier than the 0.5% base rate (about 0.66%) already merits a challenge, and you decline only above about one in four (27%).

    • Thresholds depend on amount. A AED 15,000 transfer is challenged at far lower risk than a AED 40 coffee.
    • Probabilities must be calibrated, especially after resampling (F3.5).
    • Measure step-up effectiveness by experiment, not from a vendor brochure; scams weaken it (A9).

    Milliseconds: what the budget does to the design

    An authorisation decision has a hard clock: commonly tens to low hundreds of milliseconds for the model call. If the score is late, a fallback applies: approve, decline, or a simple rule. The fallback is part of the fraud policy. Three consequences:

    • Split features by freshness. Slow features (90-day profile) are precomputed; fast ones (last three transactions) are computed live. Both must match training (P1).
    • Model size is a business decision. Tail latency (slowest 1%), not the average, sets the fallback rate (P2).
    • Log everything. Score, features, action and policy version. Without them neither label correction nor audit is possible (P5).
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Judging a young cohort as mature

      "Recall this month is 94%, up from 80%." Or "recall collapsed".

      Spot itCompare metrics at equal label age.
    • Feedback loops left unchecked

      Blocked rows are dropped or stamped fraud, the model learns only what the rules already flagged, and performance oscillates after each retrain.

      Spot itAsk how blocked rows are labelled and whether any were let through at random. If few confirmed frauds are found only by the model, it is a rule mirror.
    • Alert volume set to look busy

      A cautious threshold yields thousands of alerts and the catch rate falls.

      Spot itPlot fraud stopped per analyst-hour against daily volume. A downward bend means you are past the peak.
    • Wrong or uncalibrated thresholds

      One cut-off for AED 30 and AED 30,000 payments, or scores that do not mean what they say.

      Spot itCompare break-even thresholds per amount band, and check a reliability plot on a mature sample.
    • Taking step-up and fallback on trust

      A vendor says challenges stop 98% of fraud, but scam victims pass them. Or the model service slows down and the default is "approve".

      Spot itAsk how step-up effectiveness was measured, split by scam versus account takeover, and what happens at timeout.
    • Unfair friction

      Declines and challenges fall harder on some nationalities, languages, devices or branches than risk justifies.

      Spot itCompare step-up and decline rates, and overturned share, across groups (G2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model's true recall is 70%, yet a 10-day-old cohort shows about 89%. Why, and what do you do before trusting the figure?

    Caught fraud is confirmed within days while missed fraud waits weeks for chargebacks, so the young cohort is missing exactly the frauds the model failed to catch. Compare cohorts at equal label age.

    2. A team stamps every blocked transaction "fraud" and retrains. What goes wrong, and how does it differ from dropping blocked rows?

    The model repeats its own blocks with perfect apparent precision and never discovers false positives. Dropping them does the reverse: the pattern looks safe and the model relaxes. Random exploration or step-up outcomes supply the missing evidence.

    3. Two payments carry the same 2% fraud probability, one for AED 40 and one for AED 4,000. Should they get the same action?

    No. The cost of being wrong scales with the amount, so the AED 4,000 payment should be challenged at a much lower risk than the coffee-sized one.

    4. The amount doubles and nothing else changes. Which way do the approve and decline cut-offs move?

    Both fall. A larger loss makes small probabilities worth a challenge, and the fraud that slips past a challenge costs more, so declines start earlier. Hence one cut-off for all amounts is wrong.

    5. Alert volume doubles from 300 to 600 with capacity 300, queue unranked. Why might fraud stopped fall?

    Analysts review a random 300 of 600, so about half the real fraud goes unreviewed, and extra false alerts cut attention. Ranking by score, or raising the threshold, repairs it.

    6. Fraud rate looks stable but confirmed fraud found only by the model is shrinking. Likely cause?

    Labels increasingly come from alerts the model itself produces. Add a random review sample of unflagged traffic and measure model-only detections against it.

    7. Why are delayed labels a survival-analysis problem?

    In a young cohort a missing label means "not yet", not "no" (censoring). Survival methods use the maturity curve to estimate the eventual rate instead of treating missing as negative.

    8. A model trained on undersampled data is miscalibrated. How does that affect step-up thresholds?

    Its probabilities are inflated, so too many transactions are challenged or declined. Correct them (prior correction or recalibration on an untouched mature sample) before applying the cost-derived cut-offs.
