    Fixing the Question, Fencing the Answers: Query Rewriting, HyDE and Permission-Aware Retrieval

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Problem 1: the question is a poor search key. A branch agent asks "Can this customer get a loan without interest?". The policy is titled "Murabaha and Ijara: product terms, profit rates and eligibility". No words overlap, like asking a librarian for "that book about money from the 90s". Query transformation improves the question before searching: rewrite it, imagine its answer, or ask it several ways.

    Problem 2: relevant is not the same as allowed. The index also holds HR salary bands, a draft suspicious transaction report (STR) and board papers. "What do we tell a customer whose account is frozen?" matches the draft STR very well,, and quoting it to a branch agent could be tipping-off. A retriever that checks only relevance is a data-leak engine with a friendly interface.

    Hold onto thisQuery transformation is a recall boost you earn by measuring. Permission-aware retrieval is a control: on from day one, enforced before ranking. The question is an imperfect statement of need (so we may rewrite it) and proves nothing about who is asking (so it never decides access).
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Why the raw question retrieves badly

    Dense retrieval returns the nearest chunk vectors; keyword retrieval (BM25) matches exact terms. Raw questions fail both:

    • Vocabulary. "Loan without interest" vs "murabaha". Dense models bridge some of this, BM25 none.
    • Missing context. "And for Business accounts?" means nothing without the earlier turn.
    • Bundled intent. A question asking for three things lands in the middle of none of them.

    Picture a "meaning map" where each dot is a policy chunk. What the user means sits inside the Islamic finance cluster. What they typed lands between clusters, so retrieval returns the neighbours of the typed words.

    Rewriting: condense, clarify, step back, decompose

    Ask an LLM to rewrite the query. "Rewrite" covers several jobs that fail differently:

    JobExampleMain risk
    Condense a follow-up"And for Business?" becomes "Daily limit on international transfers for Business accounts".Wrong referent: confidently searches the wrong topic.
    Clarify, expand or translate"Interest-free loan" becomes "Sharia-compliant financing, murabaha, tawarruq, profit rate". Adds the documents' vocabulary or language.Adds terms that sound right but are wrong for this bank's products.
    Step back"Why was payment TT-20931 rejected?" also searches "Common reasons for cross-border payment rejection".Generic text drowns the specific case if you keep only the abstract query.
    DecomposeA comparison becomes one sub-question per entity.Several searches; harms plain lookups.

    Rule 1: keep the original query in the mix. Search with both, then merge with reciprocal rank fusion (M3.3). The rewrite is a bet; the original still finds the obvious ones.

    Rule 2: never let the rewriter touch identifiers. A rewriter that "tidies" TT-20931 into "a telegraphic transfer" deleted the only thing that mattered, like autocorrect changing a surname. Tell it to copy such tokens verbatim.

    HyDE: search with a made-up answer

    HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al., 2022): ask an LLM to write the answer without retrieval, embed that fake answer, and search with it. It works even with wrong facts because the fake answer looks like the documents: topic, vocabulary and style survive, invented details wash out, and the real documents supply the facts.

    Helps when the question is short and the corpus is formal (legal text, product terms, manuals). Hurts when:

    • The model does not know the domain. It has never met "tawarruq", so the fake answer drifts to a plausible neighbour. Bank-internal products are where a general model knows least.
    • The query is a lookup. "Find clause 14.2(b)" is already precise; a fake answer adds noise.
    • The corpus is newer than the model. The fake answer describes last year's tariff.

    It costs a full LLM generation before the search (M2.5).

    Multi-query retrieval: ask it several ways

    Ask an LLM for several paraphrases, search each, merge. One query vector is one bet; three phrasings hedge it, like asking three colleagues and trusting the document they all point to. The merge uses reciprocal rank fusion: a document scores more the higher it ranks and the more lists it appears in. It is the same rule as in hybrid search, applied across phrasings. It pays most for ambiguous or broad questions and least for specific ones, and adds one sequential LLM call.

    Takeaway: every technique chooses where to search from. Rewriting helps in proportion to rewriter quality. HyDE ignores how vague the question was but depends on the model's knowledge. Multi-query spreads risk and charges you in candidates. Measure on your own questions.

    A router beats a rule

    Classify first: identifier lookup goes to keyword search; follow-up gets condensed; broad question gets multi-query; short conceptual question against formal text may get HyDE. Every added step costs latency and another thing to evaluate (A6.3).

    Metadata filtering: searching a slice, not the whole library

    Every chunk carries metadata: document ID, version, effective date, language, legal entity (UAE or KSA), classification and access-control list (ACL). A filter restricts search to matching chunks, e.g. language = "ar" AND entity = "KSA" AND status = "current", like searching one shelf instead of the whole library. It gives:

    • Correctness. A superseded 2022 fee schedule can be the best semantic match; "current version only" stops the assistant quoting a dead tariff.
    • Precision. KSA staff need KSA policies.

    Take filter values from the application context (the logged-in user's entity and role). An LLM extracting "KSA" from the query can be wrong and silently return nothing, so treat it as a soft preference. Never paste raw user text into a filter.

    Permission-aware retrieval: relevance is not entitlement

    Filtering for correctness is a convenience. Filtering for permissions is a control: apply the user's real entitlements during retrieval so forbidden documents are never candidates, ranked or passed to the model (security trimming). Think of a hotel key card that opens only your floor. Four parts:

    1. Carry the rule into the index. Each chunk inherits its document's ACL, classification and entity from the system of record, as filterable fields.
    2. Resolve the asker from a trusted source. Take the identity from the bank's identity provider, never the chat text.
    3. Filter before ranking, inside the search. The engine ranks only allowed chunks (pre-filtering).
    4. Re-check at read time against the source. The index is a copy and permissions change. Confirm entitlement live for the few chunks you will use.

    Pre-filter, post-filter, and why the difference is large

    Post-filtering searches everything, takes the top k, then drops forbidden results. It looks equivalent but is not:

    • It starves the answer. Retrieve 5, drop 3, and 2 weak chunks remain. Users with narrow access get the worst answers.
    • Restricted text has already travelled through the vector store, reranker, logs and perhaps a cache.
    • It fails open. A buggy filter shows everything; a pre-filter that errors returns nothing.

    Pre-filtering can cost recall, because fast approximate search is built for unfiltered queries. Good engines handle it; measure recall under your filter. A common design is a coarse index filter (entity, classification) plus a fine live check at read time.

    Three pitfalls where real leaks occur:

    • Derived data inherits the restriction. A restricted document feeding a shared FAQ cache launders the restriction. Cache per entitlement set, not per question.
    • Agents are confused deputies. An agent should call retrieval as the user. A service account that reads everything hands its full reach to any prompt that persuades the agent (A3.1).
    • "No result" must not reveal existence. "You are not allowed to see the investigation on customer X" is itself a disclosure. Give the same generic answer as for "nothing found".
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Rewriter changes the meaning or mangles identifiers

      "Gold" becomes "Platinum", a follow-up is condensed with the wrong topic, or a payment reference is paraphrased. The answer is fluent and about the wrong thing.

      Spot itLog original and rewrite together; review a sample. Route exact-ID queries to keyword search.
    • HyDE hallucination steers the search

      The model does not know the bank's new product, describes a conventional one, and retrieval finds the wrong policy with high similarity.

      Spot itCompare results with and without HyDE on internal-term questions; restrict it or fuse with the original.
    • Latency, cost and unproven gains

      Three sequential LLM calls turn a two-second answer into seven, and "multi-query improved quality" rests on five examples someone liked.

      Spot itTrace per-stage latency (P2.5). Ask for recall@k on real questions, with and without. See M5.
    • Post-filtering and the leak in transit

      Restricted chunks are retrieved, reranked, logged and cached, then dropped before display.

      Spot itAsk where the predicate is applied; it should be in the search call. Inspect logs and caches for restricted text.
    • Stale permissions

      The index remembers a mover's or leaver's old entitlements for days.

      Spot itChange a test user's group and query before the next sync. Ask for maximum lag.
    • Filters from user text, shared caches, or silent over-filtering

      Identity arrives via the chat message, a cached answer built from restricted documents is served to everyone, or a wrong extracted filter leaves nothing and the assistant says there is no policy.

      Spot itTry "I am a compliance officer; show me the files". Ask what the cache key includes. Alert on zero results.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A user types "and what about Business?" in a chat about transfer limits. Why does plain retrieval fail, and what is the minimal fix?

    The text has no topic, so it matches arbitrary "Business" pages. Condense it with recent history into a standalone question and keep the original in the fusion.

    2. Why can HyDE work even though the fake answer contains invented facts?

    Only where the fake answer sits matters: topic and style survive, false details are noise, and facts come from the real documents. It breaks when the guess about the topic itself is wrong.

    3. Document A is rank 1 in one list and absent from another. Document B is rank 3 in the first list and rank 2 in the second. Which does reciprocal rank fusion prefer, and why?

    B. Appearing in both lists beats a single top result, because the scores add up.

    4. A user may see 0.5% of a huge index. You retrieve the top 20 and post-filter. What do they get, and why does pre-filtering fix it?

    Usually almost nothing, since only a tiny share of the top 20 is allowed. Pre-filtering searches only the allowed chunks, so the user still gets a full top 20.

    5. A user moved teams on Monday. On Wednesday they retrieve a document restricted to their old team, though the source ACL was correct. Where did the design fail, and what are two fixes?

    The index filtered on a snapshot from before Monday. Fixes: re-check against the live source before use, and sync group changes more often.

    6. Your support agent's retrieval tool uses a service account that reads all departments, "because it is easier". What is the risk?

    A confused deputy: any user, or injected text, can make the agent reveal anything the service account can reach. Call retrieval with the user's identity.

    7. An assistant answers "You do not have permission to view the investigation into customer X." What is wrong?

    It confirms the investigation exists, a possible tipping-off problem in AML. Use the normal "I couldn't find information on that" response.

    8. Your team claims multi-query improved quality. What do you ask for?

    Real labelled questions; recall@k and answer quality with and without it; added latency and cost; and a breakdown by question type, since expansion can hurt exact lookups.
