    Lending on Cash Flow, and Keeping It Fair

    What a bank can learn from a customer's actual money movements that a credit bureau cannot show, how to turn a messy statement into an income and affordability number, and how proxy discrimination and adverse-action explanations decide whether such a model can be used at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOpen-finance data lets you underwrite on observed income, spending and buffers, which helps thin-file and irregular-income customers, but every richer signal is also a richer proxy for who the customer is, so fairness testing, a search for less discriminatory alternatives and explainable decline reasons are part of the model.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit bureau shows how someone has behaved as a borrower: cards, loans, missed payments. It cannot see salary, rent, or whether the account hits zero every 24th. Think of it as a school report that lists exam grades but not how the student studies.

    Take two 27-year-olds applying for AED 50,000, both with a clean bureau file. One is a nurse paid AED 14,000 on the 25th each month, with AED 3,000 left over. The other is a freelance designer with the same average income, but it arrives in lumps and the account touches zero twice a quarter. The bureau cannot tell them apart. Their bank statements can, in seconds.

    That is cash-flow underwriting: use the customer's credits and debits, with consent, as evidence. Open finance (regulated sharing of account data through APIs) extends this to accounts at other banks.

    The same richness creates the fairness problem. A model that sees where people shop and how they get paid can learn things that correlate with nationality, gender, religion or age without being told. And a decline must be explained in terms the customer can act on. More signal, more proxy risk, more need to explain.

    Three things to notice.

    • Average income is the wrong number for volatile income. Lend against what the customer can be relied on to have, a lower bound. Salaried applicants lose almost nothing to caution; irregular ones lose a lot. That is the point of looking at the data, not unfairness.
    • Classifying credits is half the job. Own-account transfers, refunds, other lenders' disbursements and one-off sales are not income. Getting this wrong flatters the most stretched customers.
    • Window, caution and what counts are model choices. Validate them against actual repayment; do not set them by feel.
    Hold onto thisTwo separate jobs: estimating capacity (what the customer can afford, from income, spending and buffers) and predicting risk (likelihood of default, from patterns). Banks use both, and fairness and explanation questions differ for each.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A10.1 · Cash-flow underwriting with open-finance data

    Five steps; the model is only the fourth.

    1. Consent and retrieval. The customer approves named accounts for a stated purpose and period. A licensed provider returns 6 to 24 months of transactions and balances via API.
    2. Cleaning and categorisation. Raw text such as POS 4471 CARREFOUR DXB or Arabic free text is mapped to salary, rent, utilities, transfers, loan repayments, cash withdrawals. Rules plus a model, increasingly an LLM. Errors here flow straight into the decision.
    3. Feature engineering. Recurring income and its regularity, fixed commitments, discretionary spend, lowest monthly balance, days at or near zero, obligations-to-income, new-credit seeking, income concentration in one source.
    4. Models. A capacity rule (income minus commitments minus a living-cost allowance) and/or a probability-of-default model (gradient-boosted trees or regularised logistic regression). Sometimes a sequence model reads raw transactions (foundation models for transaction data).
    5. Decision and monitoring. Approve, decline, price or set a limit, with reasons; then watch drift and outcomes.

    Estimating usable income

    Estimating usable income is the most important, least glamorous step. Like budgeting on your worst typical month rather than your best, it has three parts: find recurring credits (same counterparty, similar amount, similar day); strip non-income credits; summarise with a lower bound, not a mean:

    Ysafe=max(0,y¯−k·s)

    ȳ is average monthly income-type inflow, s its sample standard deviation across months, k a caution multiplier. Read k = 1 as "income I'd expect to beat about five months in six" for a normal-looking series. A median or low percentile is an alternative. The right k is whatever best predicts missed payments in your history (twelve months is a small sample; see F2.1). Misreading to avoid: a bigger k is not "safer" in general; it declines good irregular earners.

    Affordability follows. Total monthly obligations are capped at a share of income (bank policy plus regulator rules; check current CBUAE or SAMA rules, do not assume a number). Instalment room is cap × Y_safe − existing obligations, and the finance it supports is the present value of the instalments:

    A=P·1−(1+r)−nr

    P is monthly instalment room, r the monthly rate, n the months. At 12% a year over 36 months the factor is 30.1, so AED 1,000 a month supports about AED 30,100. For murabaha or tawarruq the profit rate replaces the interest rate; the logic is the same.

    Where the extra signal comes from

    Two sources help most when each knows something the other does not. Toy model: each source scores defaulters higher than non-defaulters by d standard deviations (d = 0 is useless; larger is better), and their errors overlap by correlation ρ. Combined separation:

    d2=d12−2ρd1d2+d21−ρ2

    AUC for separation d is Φ(d/√2), with Φ the normal CDF (F7). With no overlap (ρ = 0), separations add in quadrature. With heavy overlap, the second source adds little: two weather apps fed by the same satellite are not twice as informative. Here d2 is cash flow and d1 the bureau.

    Evidence matches the toy: gains are clearest for people the bureau sees poorly. A FinRegLab study (2019, several US lenders) found cash-flow variables predictive alone and additive to bureau scores, especially for thin-file and lower-score applicants. Vendors report bigger gains (a Saudi buy-now-pay-later firm, Tamara, was reported to cite a 32% rise in approvals), but these are self-reported, not controlled studies. Take direction, not size.

    A10.2 · Fair lending with ML: how proxy discrimination happens

    No sensible bank feeds nationality or gender into a credit model. But other columns carry the same information, like a shopper's loyalty-card history revealing their neighbourhood. In cash-flow data: employer type and salary channel, cash-withdrawal share, remittance patterns, spending categories, income timing, card-spend neighbourhood, even the language of descriptions. A flexible model uses whatever predicts repayment, including the part that stands in for a sensitive group.

    That does not make the feature illegitimate: it can predict repayment and correlate with group. That is the hard case. Validators ask whether the model has a disparate effect, whether the feature has a legitimate business need, and whether a less discriminatory alternative would do almost as well.

    Three standard tests (G2 explains why fairness definitions cannot all hold at once):

    • Outcome gaps: approval rate, pricing and limit by group, as a ratio. A ratio of 0.8 means group B is approved at 80% of group A's rate. The US "four-fifths" rule is an employment-law screening convention, not a lending bright line, and I am not aware of it having standing in the UAE or Saudi Arabia. Use it to start the conversation.
    • Error gaps: are creditworthy customers wrongly declined at different rates by group? This needs outcomes, which exist only for approved applicants (reject inference, F2.6).
    • Proxy detection: can a simple classifier predict the sensitive attribute from the features? If yes, they carry it; find which ones do the work.

    Data caveat: Gulf banks often hold nationality, sometimes gender, as KYC fields, but may have no lawful way to hold other attributes an audit wants. Where missing, firms estimate them from names and addresses, which adds error. Plan this early with legal and compliance.

    The trade-off: dropping a predictive-but-skewed feature throws away signal. Reducing its weight recovers much of the gap at far lower cost, because accuracy is flat near the best mix while the gap shrinks steadily. Like turning down one dial on a mixing desk rather than muting the channel. So the search for a less discriminatory alternative is numerical (retrain with constraints, reweight, drop a feature family, adjust cut-offs), and the fix is a priced business choice that someone senior must own.

    Tools: remove or coarsen the worst proxies; train with a fairness penalty or constraints; compare candidates on accuracy and group outcomes and pick from that frontier; set explicit policy on what may vary by group. What does not work: assuming that dropping the sensitive column makes a model fair.

    Adverse-action explanations and reason codes

    A declined applicant should get reasons that mean something. In the US, ECOA and Regulation B require specific reasons, and CFPB Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03 say a lender cannot hide behind a complex model or generic checklist: reasons must reflect the real drivers. I am not aware of a single equivalent rule for UAE or Saudi personal lending; expect it to come from consumer protection, data-protection rights on automated decisions, and supervisory guidance (A12). Check with compliance.

    Reason codes come from attribution: how much each feature moved this applicant's score relative to a reference, like "you scored 12 below the class average, mostly from the essay". For a logit model, feature j contributes βj(xj − referencej); for trees, SHAP plays that role (F8). The top adverse contributors become the stated reasons. Two traps:

    • The reference changes the reasons. "Worse than the average applicant" and "worse than a typical approved applicant" give different lists. Document the choice as policy.
    • Reasons must be actionable and honest. "Pattern of transactions" is useless. Citing a feature the customer cannot change, or hiding the real driver behind a nicer one, is a compliance problem.

    If a skewed proxy drove a decline, the explanation is also evidence for the fairness review. Explanation tooling and fairness testing use the same attributions.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Counting non-income credits as income

      Own-account transfers, refunds and other lenders' disbursements inflate income, mostly for those borrowing to stay afloat.

      Spot itCompare estimated income with payroll or salary-certificate data on a sample. Count "income" credits with a matching debit in another account of the same customer.
    • Misclassified transactions at scale

      An Arabic description or aggregator name sends rent into "shopping". One bad rule moves thousands of customers.

      Spot itAudit a stratified sample by language and bank source. Report accuracy per category, not one overall figure.
    • Leakage through the statement date

      Features built from statements pulled after trouble began (lower balances, new borrowing).

      Spot itCheck the retrieval timestamp precedes the decision and training features use only earlier transactions.
    • Selection bias from consent

      Stronger applicants may share more, so the sharing group looks safer than the population.

      Spot itCompare consenters with non-consenters on observables and later outcomes. Do not score non-consenters with a consenter-only model unchecked.
    • Training on approved loans only

      The model learns repayment among people the old policy accepted and says nothing about those it rejected.

      Spot itAsk what share of applicants has an outcome. Look for reject inference or an approved test-and-learn slice.
    • "We did not use the protected attribute, so it is fair"

      Cash-withdrawal share, remittance behaviour or payroll channel can carry the same information.

      Spot itRun the proxy test and compare approval rates by group at your operating point.
    • Fixing fairness by rescaling the score by group

      Group-specific cut-offs may be unlawful disparate treatment in some jurisdictions and conflict with consistent-treatment rules.

      Spot itAsk legal and compliance first. Prefer changes to features and training, tested for accuracy and outcome gaps.
    • Reason codes that do not match the real decision

      A generic list on every decline, or reasons computed against a different reference than the model used.

      Spot itTake a few declines and check that removing the top reason's effect would change the decision. Check the reference and mapping are documented.
    • Seasonality read as stress

      Ramadan, Eid, school fees, summer travel and bonus months shift inflows and spend.

      Spot itBacktest across at least two full years; examine score changes around Ramadan and year-end.
    • Gaming and adverse selection

      Customers who know cash flow matters can window-dress: moving funds in before applying, or topping up from a friend.

      Spot itLook for balance spikes just before applications and round-number inbound transfers from unusual counterparties.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Monthly income-type credits over four months are 10,000, 12,000, 8,000 and 14,000. Existing obligations are 1,000. Estimate cautious income with k = 1, and instalment room at a 40% cap.

    Mean = 11,000. Deviations: −1,000, +1,000, −3,000, +3,000; squares sum to 20,000,000. Sample variance = 20,000,000 ÷ 3 = 6,666,667, so s ≈ 2,582. Ysafe ≈ 8,418. Room = 0.4 × 8,418 − 1,000 ≈ 2,367 a month.

    2. At 12% a year over 36 months, how much finance does AED 2,000 a month support?

    Monthly rate 1%. Factor = (1 − 1.01−36) ÷ 0.01 ≈ 30.1. So 2,000 × 30.1 ≈ AED 60,200.

    3. A feature predicts default well and is also correlated with group. List three ways to respond, and one way not to.

    Reduce its weight or coarsen it; replace it with a less skewed feature carrying similar information; train with a fairness constraint and compare candidates on accuracy and gap. Not: group-specific cut-offs without legal advice, or declaring the model fair because the column was excluded.

    4. In Simulation 3 the score has equal weight on two features (w ≈ 0.71) and group B's gap on feature one is 0.8. Why does reducing w to 0.5 cost little accuracy but narrow the approval gap?

    Signal kept is (w + √(1−w²)) ÷ √2: 1.00 at w = 0.71; (0.5 + 0.866) ÷ 1.414 = 0.966 at w = 0.5. The group shift in the score is w × gap, which falls about 30%. Accuracy is flat near its peak: 3.4% of signal for a 30% smaller shift.

    5. Why can the reasons for the same decline differ depending on the reference?

    Contributions are measured relative to the reference. Against the average applicant, weaknesses are where the applicant sits below average. Against a typical approved applicant the bar is higher, so more features count against them and the order changes. The reference is therefore a documented policy.

    6. Why might customers who consent to data sharing look safer than the full applicant population?

    Stronger customers have less to hide and more to gain, so they consent more. Training and calibrating on consenters alone understates risk when applied more widely. Compare consenters with others on observables and track outcomes.

    7. A vendor reports "32% more approvals at the same risk". What do you ask?

    Versus what baseline policy, on which population, measured how (controlled experiment or before-and-after), over what time, and with what matured default outcomes. Approval gains without matured outcomes say little about risk.
