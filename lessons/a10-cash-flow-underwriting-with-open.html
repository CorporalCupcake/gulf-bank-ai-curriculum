    Cash-Flow Lending and Fairness

    Part 1

    What cash-flow lending is

    In one sentenceCash-flow lending decides a loan from what flows through the customer's bank account, not just from their credit-bureau record, and it needs extra care to stay fair.

    A credit bureau shows how someone has behaved as a borrower: cards, loans, missed payments. It cannot see salary, rent or whether the account hits zero every month.

    Take two applicants with clean bureau files: a nurse paid the same amount every month, and a freelancer with the same average income that arrives in lumps. The bureau cannot tell them apart. Their bank statements can.

    How it differs from bureau scoring

    • Bureau scoring: looks at past borrowing behaviour. Weak for people with little borrowing history ("thin files").
    • Cash-flow lending: looks at income, spending and balances, with the customer's consent. Open finance extends this to accounts held at other banks.

    The same richness creates the fairness risk. A model that sees where people shop and how they are paid can pick up nationality, gender or religion without being told. More signal means more proxy risk and more need to explain.

    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Consent and retrieval. The customer approves named accounts and a licensed provider returns 6 to 24 months of transactions.
    2. Categorise. Raw text such as "POS 4471 CARREFOUR DXB" becomes "groceries". Salary, rent and transfers get labels too. Mistakes here flow straight into the decision.
    3. Build features. Income and how regular it is, commitments, lowest balance, days near zero.
    4. Model. A capacity rule (what can they afford?), a default-probability model (how likely are they to miss payments?), or both.
    5. Decide and explain. Approve, decline, price or set a limit, with reasons. Then watch for drift.

    Estimating usable income

    This is the most important and least glamorous step.

    • Find recurring credits: same payer, similar amount, similar day.
    • Remove non-income: transfers between the customer's own accounts, refunds and loans from other lenders are not income.
    • Use a cautious figure, not the average: the average minus a multiple of how much it swings. At one standard deviation below, that is roughly the income they would beat about five months in six.

    Affordability follows. Total repayments are capped at a share of income (check the current central-bank rules). What is left is room for an instalment. At 12% over 36 months, AED 1,000 a month supports about AED 30,100 of finance.

    Part 3

    Keeping it fair

    In one sentenceA proxy is an ordinary-looking feature that carries the same information as a protected trait, so a model can discriminate even when that trait is never used.

    Cash-withdrawal share, remittance patterns or the payroll channel can all be proxies for nationality. Deleting the protected column does not make a model fair.

    How to check

    1. Compare approval rates by group for the whole portfolio, even though group is not a model input.
    2. Check each feature for how strongly it tracks group.
    3. Look for a less discriminatory alternative: a model of similar accuracy with a smaller gap.
    4. Document the choice and who signed it off.

    Dropping a useful feature throws away signal. Turning its weight down often closes much of the gap at little cost to accuracy, because accuracy is flat near the best mix while the gap shrinks steadily. Like turning down one dial rather than muting the channel.

    Explaining a decline

    A declined customer should get reasons they can act on. Reasons come from attribution: how much each feature pushed this applicant's score towards decline, compared with a reference. For tree models, SHAP is the usual tool. The top three adverse contributors become the stated reasons.

    • The reference matters. "Worse than the average applicant" and "worse than a typical approved applicant" give different lists. Fix the choice as policy.
    • Reasons must be honest and useful. "Pattern of transactions" helps nobody, and hiding the real driver is a compliance problem.
    Part 4

    How to read the results

    Freelancer: average 11,000, cautious estimate 8,000
    Instalments are due in the bad months too, so lending against 8,000 is sensible. Too much caution declines good irregular earners.
    Approval ratio (group B ÷ group A) of 0.7
    Group B is approved 70 in every 100 times group A is. Many teams use 0.8 as a screening flag. It is a convention, not a UAE or Saudi rule.
    Top decline reason: "days overdrawn"
    This is something the customer can act on. If the top reason were a skewed proxy, the explanation is also evidence for the fairness review.
    Part 5

    Which to use, and when it breaks

    ApproachSeesBest for
    Bureau score onlyBorrowing historyEstablished customers with long files
    Cash-flow ruleIncome and commitmentsAffordability limits
    Cash-flow ML modelPatterns in transactionsThin files, adding signal to the bureau
    Bureau plus cash flowBothMost gain when the two sources know different things
    • Strong at: thin-file applicants and irregular earners. A 2019 FinRegLab study of several US lenders found cash-flow variables predictive alone and adding to bureau scores, especially for thin files. Vendor claims of bigger gains are self-reported.
    • Weak at: messy categorisation, applicants who will not consent, and a proxy risk that grows with every added feature.
    Show the formula

    Cautious income = average monthly credits − k × (how much they swing), where k is chosen by what best predicts missed payments. Larger k is more cautious.

    In banking, estimating what a customer can afford and predicting whether they will default are separate jobs, and the fairness questions differ for each.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Counting non-income credits as income

      Own transfers, refunds and loan disbursements inflate income, mostly for people borrowing to stay afloat. Compare with payroll data on a sample.

    • "We did not use the protected attribute, so it is fair"

      Proxies carry the same information. Compare approval rates by group.

    • Fixing fairness with group-specific cut-offs

      This may be unlawful different treatment in some places. Ask legal first and prefer changes to features and training.

    • Consent bias and seasonality

      Stronger applicants consent more, so consenters look safer. Ramadan, Eid and school-fee months also shift inflows, so test across at least two full years.

    1. A freelancer's credits swing from 8,000 to 14,000, averaging 11,000. Why lend against less?

    The average hides the bad months, and instalments are due in those too.

    2. A feature predicts default well but is linked to group. What can you do?

    Reduce its weight, replace it with a less skewed feature, or train with a fairness constraint. Do not use group-specific cut-offs without legal advice.

    3. Why can reasons for the same decline differ?

    Contributions are measured against a reference, and a different reference reorders them. Document the reference as policy.

    4. Why might customers who consent look safer than all applicants?

    Stronger customers consent more, so a model built on them understates risk when applied more widely.

    5. A vendor claims "32% more approvals at the same risk". What do you ask?

    Against what baseline, on which population, measured how, and with what matured default outcomes.
