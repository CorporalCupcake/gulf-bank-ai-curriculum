    Lending on Cash Flow, and Keeping It Fair

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit bureau shows how someone has behaved as a borrower: cards, loans, missed payments. It cannot see salary, rent, or whether the account hits zero every 24th: a school report with exam grades but no sign of how the student studies.

    Take two applicants with clean bureau files: a nurse paid the same amount on the 25th each month, and a freelancer with the same average income that arrives in lumps. The bureau cannot tell them apart. Their bank statements can. That is cash-flow underwriting, with consent; open finance extends it to accounts at other banks.

    The same richness creates the fairness problem. A model that sees where people shop and how they get paid can learn nationality, gender or religion without being told, and a decline must be explained in terms the customer can act on. More signal, more proxy risk, more need to explain.

    • Average income is the wrong number for volatile income. Lend against what the customer can be relied on to have.
    • Classifying credits is half the job. Own-account transfers, refunds and other lenders' disbursements are not income.
    Hold onto thisTwo separate jobs: estimating capacity (what the customer can afford) and predicting risk (likelihood of default). Fairness and explanation questions differ for each.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A10.1 · Cash-flow underwriting with open-finance data

    Five steps; the model is only the fourth.

    1. Consent and retrieval. The customer approves named accounts; a licensed provider returns 6 to 24 months of transactions via API.
    2. Categorisation. Raw text such as POS 4471 CARREFOUR DXB is mapped to salary, rent, transfers and so on. Errors here flow straight into the decision.
    3. Features. Income and its regularity, commitments, lowest balance, days near zero.
    4. Models. A capacity rule and/or a probability-of-default model; sometimes a sequence model on raw transactions (A8).
    5. Decision and monitoring. Approve, decline, price or set a limit, with reasons; then watch drift.

    Estimating usable income

    The most important, least glamorous step. Like budgeting on your worst typical month rather than your best, it has three parts: find recurring credits (same counterparty, similar amount, similar day); strip non-income credits; summarise with a lower bound, not a mean. In practice: the average minus a multiple of how much it swings. At one standard deviation, that is roughly the income you would beat about five months in six. Bigger is not "safer"; it declines good irregular earners. Set it by what best predicts missed payments.

    Affordability follows. Total obligations are capped at a share of income (check current CBUAE or SAMA rules). What is left after existing obligations is instalment room, and the finance it supports is the present value of those instalments: at 12% over 36 months, AED 1,000 a month supports about AED 30,100.

    Where the extra signal comes from

    Two sources help most when each knows something the other does not. In the simulation, d is how far apart a source scores defaulters and non-defaulters (0 is useless, bigger is better), and ρ is how much the two sources repeat each other. With heavy overlap the second source adds little: two weather apps fed by the same satellite are not twice as informative.

    Evidence matches the toy: gains are clearest for people the bureau sees poorly. A FinRegLab study (2019, several US lenders) found cash-flow variables predictive alone and additive to bureau scores, especially for thin-file applicants. Vendor claims of bigger gains are self-reported.

    A10.2 · Fair lending with ML: how proxy discrimination happens

    No sensible bank feeds nationality or gender into a credit model. But other columns carry the same information, like a loyalty-card history revealing a shopper's neighbourhood: cash-withdrawal share, remittance patterns, spending categories, employer type. A flexible model uses whatever predicts repayment, including the part that stands in for a sensitive group.

    That does not make the feature illegitimate; it can predict repayment and correlate with group. Validators ask whether there is a disparate effect, whether the feature has a legitimate business need, and whether a less discriminatory alternative would do almost as well. Three standard tests (G2):

    • Outcome gaps: approval rate, pricing and limit by group, as a ratio. A ratio of 0.8 means group B is approved at 80% of group A's rate. The US "four-fifths" rule is a screening convention, not a lending bright line, and I am not aware of it having standing in the UAE or Saudi Arabia.
    • Error gaps: are creditworthy customers wrongly declined at different rates by group? This needs outcomes, which exist only for approved applicants (F2.6).
    • Proxy detection: can a simple classifier predict the sensitive attribute from the features? If yes, they carry it.

    Dropping a predictive-but-skewed feature throws away signal. Reducing its weight recovers much of the gap at far lower cost, because accuracy is flat near the best mix while the gap shrinks steadily. Like turning down one dial rather than muting the channel. The search for a less discriminatory alternative is therefore numerical, and the fix is a priced business choice that someone senior must own. Assuming that dropping the sensitive column makes a model fair does not work.

    Adverse-action explanations and reason codes

    A declined applicant should get reasons that mean something. In the US, ECOA and Regulation B require specific reasons, and a complex model is no excuse. I am not aware of a single equivalent rule for UAE or Saudi lending; expect it to come from consumer protection, data-protection rights and supervisory guidance (A12). Check with compliance.

    Reason codes come from attribution: how much each feature moved this applicant's score relative to a reference, like "you scored below the class average, mostly from the essay". For trees, SHAP does this job (F8). The top adverse contributors become the stated reasons. Two traps:

    • The reference changes the reasons. "Worse than the average applicant" and "worse than a typical approved applicant" give different lists. Document the choice as policy.
    • Reasons must be actionable and honest. "Pattern of transactions" is useless, and hiding the real driver is a compliance problem.

    If a skewed proxy drove a decline, the explanation is also evidence for the fairness review. Both use the same attributions.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Counting non-income credits as income

      Own-account transfers, refunds and loan disbursements inflate income, mostly for those borrowing to stay afloat.

      Spot itCompare estimated income with payroll data on a sample.
    • Misclassified transactions at scale

      An Arabic description sends rent into "shopping". One bad rule moves thousands of customers.

      Spot itAudit a sample by language and source.
    • Leakage through the statement date

      Statements pulled after trouble began.

      Spot itCheck the retrieval timestamp precedes the decision.
    • Selection bias from consent

      Stronger applicants share more, so the sharing group looks safer than the population.

      Spot itCompare consenters with non-consenters.
    • Training on approved loans only

      The model says nothing about people the old policy rejected.

      Spot itLook for reject inference.
    • "We did not use the protected attribute, so it is fair"

      Cash-withdrawal share, remittances or payroll channel can carry the same information.

      Spot itRun the proxy test and compare approval rates by group.
    • Fixing fairness by rescaling the score by group

      Group-specific cut-offs may be unlawful disparate treatment in some jurisdictions.

      Spot itAsk legal first; prefer changes to features and training.
    • Reason codes that do not match the real decision

      A generic list on every decline, or the wrong reference.

      Spot itCheck that fixing the top reason would change the outcome.
    • Seasonality read as stress

      Ramadan, Eid, school fees and bonus months shift inflows and spend.

      Spot itBacktest across at least two full years.
    • Gaming and adverse selection

      Customers who know cash flow matters can window-dress before applying.

      Spot itLook for balance spikes just before applications.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A freelancer's monthly credits swing between 8,000 and 14,000, averaging 11,000. Why lend against less than 11,000, and what goes wrong if the caution is too high?

    The average hides the bad months, and the instalment is due in those too. Too much caution under-lends good irregular earners.

    2. Why does the same monthly instalment support more finance over 36 months than over 12, and what should the bank watch when it stretches the term?

    More instalments means more can be repaid (less than proportionally, since later payments count for less). A longer term gives income more time to change.

    3. A feature predicts default well and is also correlated with group. List three ways to respond, and one way not to.

    Reduce its weight; replace it with a less skewed feature; train with a fairness constraint. Not: group-specific cut-offs without legal advice, or declaring the model fair because the column was excluded.

    4. In Simulation 3, why does reducing the weight on the skewed feature cost little accuracy but narrow the approval gap noticeably?

    Near the best mix, accuracy is flat, so a nudge loses little signal. The group gap moves in step with the weight, so it falls steadily. Like turning down one dial rather than muting the channel.

    5. Why can the reasons for the same decline differ depending on the reference?

    Contributions are measured relative to the reference. Against a typical approved applicant the bar is higher, so more features count against the applicant and the order changes. The reference is therefore a documented policy.

    6. Why might customers who consent to data sharing look safer than the full applicant population?

    Stronger customers have more to gain and less to hide, so they consent more. A model built on consenters alone understates risk when applied more widely.

    7. A vendor reports "32% more approvals at the same risk". What do you ask?

    Versus what baseline, on which population, measured how (controlled experiment or before-and-after), and with what matured default outcomes.
