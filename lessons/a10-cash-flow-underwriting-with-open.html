    Lending on Cash Flow, and Keeping It Fair

    What a bank can learn from a customer's actual money movements that a credit bureau cannot show, how to turn a messy statement into an income and affordability number, and how proxy discrimination and adverse-action explanations decide whether such a model can be used at all.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceOpen-finance data lets you underwrite on observed income, spending and buffers instead of only past borrowing, which helps thin-file and irregular-income customers, but every richer signal is also a richer proxy for who the customer is, so fairness testing, a search for less discriminatory alternatives and explainable decline reasons are part of the model, not an afterthought.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A credit bureau answers one question well: how has this person behaved as a borrower? It knows about cards, loans and missed payments. It knows nothing about the customer's salary, their rent, or whether they run out of money on the 24th of every month.

    Two applicants for a AED 50,000 personal finance look identical to a bureau. Both are 27, both have a clean file with one small card. One is a nurse paid AED 14,000 on the 25th, every month, who ends each month with AED 3,000 left over. The other is a freelance designer with the same average income, but it arrives in lumps, and the account touches zero twice a quarter. The bureau cannot tell them apart. Their bank statements can, in seconds.

    That is cash-flow underwriting: use the stream of credits and debits, with the customer's consent, as the evidence. Open finance (the regulated sharing of account data through APIs, covered in Part 3) is what makes this possible with other banks' accounts, not just your own.

    The same richness creates the second half of this lesson. A model that sees where people shop, how they get paid, and how their balances move can learn things that correlate with nationality, gender, religion or age without ever being told. If it declines someone, the bank must say why in terms the customer can act on. More signal, more proxy risk, more need to explain.

    Three things to notice.

    • Average income is the wrong number for volatile income. What a lender needs is the income the customer can be relied on to have, which is a lower bound. Salaried applicants lose almost nothing to caution; irregular ones lose a lot. That is not unfairness, it is the point of looking at the data.
    • Classification of credits is half the job. A credit is not income. It may be a transfer between the customer's own accounts, a refund, a loan disbursement from another lender, or a one-off sale. Getting this wrong flatters exactly the people who are most stretched.
    • Every choice here (window, caution, what counts) is a model choice that needs validating against actual repayment, not a constant to be set by feel.
    Hold onto thisCash-flow underwriting is two separate things: estimating capacity (what the customer can afford, from income, spending and buffers) and predicting risk (how likely they are to default, from patterns). Banks use both, and fairness and explanation questions differ for each.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    A10.1 · Cash-flow underwriting with open-finance data

    The pipeline has five steps, and the model is only the fourth.

    1. Consent and retrieval. The customer approves access to named accounts for a stated purpose and period. A licensed provider returns transactions through an API, typically 6 to 24 months of history, with balances and sometimes account metadata.
    2. Cleaning and categorisation. Raw descriptions such as POS 4471 CARREFOUR DXB or Arabic free text are mapped to categories (salary, rent, utilities, transfers, loan repayments, cash withdrawals). This is a text-classification and entity-matching problem, often done with rules plus a model, and often with an LLM now. Errors here flow straight into the credit decision.
    3. Feature engineering. Turn the ledger into stable quantities: recurring income and its regularity, fixed commitments, discretionary spend, the lowest balance each month, days with negative or near-zero balance, ratio of obligations to income, new-credit seeking, concentration of income in one source.
    4. Models. A capacity rule (income minus commitments minus a living-cost allowance) and/or a probability-of-default model, usually gradient-boosted trees or a regularised logistic regression on the features. Sometimes a sequence model reads the raw transactions (see foundation models for transaction data).
    5. Decision and monitoring. Approve, decline, price or limit-set, with reasons, then watch drift and outcomes.

    Estimating usable income

    Income estimation is the most important, least glamorous step. A defensible approach has three parts. First, identify recurring credits: same counterparty, similar amount, similar day of month. A salary shows up as one clean series. Second, strip non-income credits. Third, summarise with a lower bound rather than a mean:

    Ysafe=max(0,y¯−k·s)

    ȳ is the average monthly income-type inflow over the window, s its sample standard deviation across months, and k a caution multiplier. With k = 1 for a normal-looking series, roughly one month in six would fall below the figure. Using a median or a low percentile is an alternative. The right k is whatever best predicts missed payments in your own history (F2.1 on standard errors applies: twelve months is a small sample).

    Affordability then follows. A common structure caps total monthly obligations at a share of income (the bank's own policy, plus whatever the regulator specifies; check the current CBUAE or SAMA rules for your product rather than assuming a number). The instalment room is cap × Y_safe − existing obligations, and the finance amount that room supports is the present value of the instalments:

    A=P·1−(1+r)−nr

    P is the monthly instalment room, r the monthly rate and n the number of months. At 12% a year (1% a month) over 36 months the factor is 30.1, so AED 1,000 a month supports about AED 30,100 of finance. For Islamic products such as murabaha or tawarruq the profit rate replaces the interest rate, but the affordability logic is the same.

    Where the extra signal comes from

    Bureau data and cash-flow data measure different things, and the value of combining them depends on how much each knows and how much they overlap. A useful toy model: each source gives a score, defaulters score higher on average than non-defaulters by d standard deviations, and the two sources' errors may be correlated by ρ. The combined separation is

    d2=d12−2ρd1d2+d21−ρ2

    and the AUC for a separation d is Φ(d/√2), where Φ is the normal cumulative distribution (F7 on ROC and AUC). With no overlap (ρ = 0) the separations add in quadrature. With heavy overlap, the second source adds little. Here d2 is the cash-flow source and d1 the bureau.

    The empirical picture matches the toy: the clearest gains are for people the bureau cannot see well, and for people whose stability shows up in cash flow before it shows up in a credit file. The best-known US evidence is a FinRegLab study (2019) with several lenders, which found cash-flow variables predictive on their own and valuable in addition to bureau scores, especially for thin-file and lower-score applicants. Vendors report larger approval gains (for example a Saudi buy-now-pay-later firm, Tamara, was reported to cite a 32% rise in approvals after adding cash-flow data), but those are self-reported and not controlled studies. Treat them as direction, not size.

    A10.2 · Fair lending with ML: how proxy discrimination happens

    No sensible bank feeds nationality or gender into a credit model. The trouble is that other columns carry the same information. In cash-flow data, the candidates are many: the employer type and salary-payment channel, share of cash withdrawals, remittance patterns (which can correlate with nationality or income level), spending categories, the timing of income, the neighbourhood where card spending happens, even the language of transaction descriptions. A flexible model finds whatever predicts repayment, including the part of it that is really a stand-in for a protected or sensitive group.

    This does not make the feature illegitimate. A feature can be genuinely predictive of repayment and correlated with group membership. That is the hard case. The question regulators and validators ask is whether the model produces a disparate effect, whether the feature is justified by a legitimate business need, and whether a less discriminatory alternative would do almost as well.

    Three families of tests are routinely used (G2 covers fairness definitions and why they cannot all hold at once):

    • Outcome gaps: approval rate, pricing and limit by group, summarised as a ratio. In US practice the "four-fifths" ratio of 0.8 is a screening convention from employment law, not a legal bright line for lending, and it has no standing in the UAE or Saudi Arabia that I am aware of. Use it as a way to start the conversation.
    • Error gaps: are creditworthy customers wrongly declined at different rates by group? This needs a way to know who was creditworthy, and outcomes only exist for those approved (reject inference, F2.6).
    • Proxy detection: can a model predict the sensitive attribute from the features? If a simple classifier recovers it well, the features carry it, and you should find which ones do the work.

    A caution about data: in the Gulf, banks often hold nationality, and sometimes gender, as a KYC field, but may have no lawful or practical way to hold the other attributes a fairness audit might want. Where the sensitive attribute is missing, firms estimate it from names and addresses, which introduces its own error. Plan this early with legal and compliance.

    Look at the trade-off the simulation exposes. Dropping a predictive-but-skewed feature reduces accuracy, since the lender throws away a signal. Reducing its weight instead recovers some of the gap at much lower cost, because accuracy is flat near the best mix and the fairness gap changes steadily. This is why the search for a less discriminatory alternative is a numerical exercise (retrain with constraints, reweight, drop a family of features, adjust cut-offs) and not a one-off decision. It also shows that the fairness fix is a business choice with a price, which someone senior must own.

    Practical tools: remove or coarsen the worst proxy features; train with a fairness penalty or constraints; compare candidate models on both accuracy and group outcomes and choose from that frontier; set explicit policy on what is allowed to vary by group. What does not work: assuming that leaving out the sensitive column makes a model fair.

    Adverse-action explanations and reason codes

    When a bank declines an applicant or offers worse terms, it should be able to say why, in terms that mean something to the customer. In the US this is written into law (the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require a statement of specific reasons), and the CFPB has said in guidance (Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03) that a lender cannot hide behind a complex model or a generic checklist: the reasons must reflect what really drove the decision. I am not aware of a single equivalent rule text for UAE or Saudi personal lending; expect the expectations to come from consumer protection regulation, data-protection rights around automated decisions, and supervisory guidance (see A12). Check with compliance rather than assuming either way.

    Technically, reason codes come from attribution: how much each feature moved this applicant's score relative to a reference. For a linear (logit) model, the contribution of feature j is simply βj(xj − referencej). For trees, SHAP values play the same role (F8). The top few adverse contributors become the stated reasons. Two traps matter in practice:

    • The reference changes the reasons. "Worse than the average applicant" and "worse than a typical approved applicant" give different lists. The choice must be a documented policy.
    • The reasons must be actionable and honest. A reason such as "pattern of transactions" is useless. A reason that cites a feature the customer cannot change, or hides the real driver behind a nicer one, is a compliance problem.

    One more tension to be aware of: the reason an applicant is declined is sometimes a feature that is itself a fairness concern. If an explanation reveals that a skewed proxy drove the decline, it is evidence for the fairness review as well as for the customer. Good explanation tooling and good fairness testing use the same attributions.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    This topic joins data access, risk modelling and regulation. These are the neighbouring lessons.

    B4.1 – B4.2 · PD, LGD, EAD and IFRS 9Where the probability of default is used

    A cash-flow score is a new input to probability of default. It must be calibrated and stable before it feeds expected loss or provisioning.

    G2 · Fairness and privacyThe definitions behind Simulation 3

    Equal approval rates, equal error rates and calibration by group cannot all hold together. Know which one your regulator or validator expects.

    F8 · ExplanationsLocal attributions are reason codes

    SHAP and its relatives are the standard engine for adverse-action reasons from tree models.

    F2.6 · Selection biasReject inference

    You only see repayment for people you approved. A new cash-flow model trained on that history inherits the old policy's blind spots.

    F3 · Leakage and featuresPoint-in-time statements

    Features must use only the transactions that existed at application date. Pulling a statement later (after a missed payment) leaks the outcome.

    F7 · Calibration and driftCalibration

    A score is only useful for pricing and limits if its probabilities are right. Cash-flow patterns drift with salary cycles, Ramadan, summer travel and labour-market shifts (drift).

    A8 · Transaction foundation modelsLearning from raw transactions

    Sequence models can replace hand-made features, and make explanation and proxy audits harder.

    A10.3 · LLM credit memosFrom score to written case

    An LLM can summarise statements for the relationship manager, but it must not become an undocumented second decision-maker.

    A12 · RegulationWhat supervisors expect

    Model risk, consumer protection and automated-decision expectations in the Gulf.

    A9 · FraudManipulated statements

    Open finance data is harder to forge than a PDF statement, which is part of its appeal. Fabricated or doctored statements are still a fraud route when documents are uploaded.

    A13 · Payment dataRicher remittance information

    Structured payment messages make categorising credits and debits more reliable.

    G3 · ValidationWhat validators will test

    Expect questions on data provenance, categorisation accuracy, stability and fairness before approval.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Personal finance and credit cards for new-to-credit residents. Many UAE and Saudi customers are recent arrivals or young nationals with short bureau histories. Cash-flow is the natural way to lend to them.
    • Salary-transfer customers. The bank already has the data for its own customers. Its first use is often internal: limit increases, pre-approved offers, early-warning on stress.
    • SME and merchant lending. Business account flows and point-of-sale receipts give a fast view of turnover and seasonality.
    • Islamic financing. The same capacity assessment supports murabaha, tawarruq and ijara products; Sharia governance adds product rules, not a different data approach.
    • Collections and early warning. Falling inflows or rising overdraft days trigger a gentler conversation before default.
    • Buy-now-pay-later and fintech partners. Competitors using cash-flow data set the speed and approval expectations that banks are measured against.

    On the data rails in the Gulf. The UAE's open finance framework (the CBUAE Open Finance Regulation, run on the Al Tareq platform) began live operation with several banks in 2025 and early 2026, though reports differ on exact dates and phases; the first phase covers banks and insurers, and the regulator's rulebook is the authoritative source for what data and use cases are in scope today. Saudi Arabia's SAMA Open Banking Framework covers account information, payment initiation and confirmation of funds, and SAMA moved to a licensing regime for providers in 2026, with Lean Technologies reported as the first licensee. Both depend on explicit customer consent; data-protection law (UAE PDPL and the Saudi PDPL, plus sector rules) governs purpose and retention. Do not assume that any given data field or lending use case is permitted; check the current rules.

    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    A short history, then what is real in banking today. Items from web checks in November 2026 are marked with their sources in the list at the end; secondary or vendor sources are flagged.

    1. 1970s
      Adverse-action notices in US law

      The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974) and Regulation B set the pattern of non-discrimination plus a duty to give specific reasons for refusal. Most explanation practice descends from it.

      ECOA; 12 CFR 1002.9
    2. 2016–19
      Cash-flow underwriting moves from fintech pilot to evidence

      FinRegLab and partner lenders tested cash-flow variables against outcomes and found them predictive alone and in addition to bureau scores, with benefits concentrated at the margins of the credit spectrum.

      FinRegLab, The Use of Cash-Flow Data in Underwriting Credit, 2019
    3. 2018
      Machine learning for credit under scrutiny

      Fuster et al. found that ML models for mortgages raised average accuracy but also shifted who benefits across groups, which put "who wins and loses" on the research agenda.

      Fuster, Goldsmith-Pinkham, Ramadorai, Walther, Journal of Finance, 2022 (working paper 2018)
    4. 2022–23
      The CFPB refuses the black-box excuse

      Circulars said that lenders using complex algorithms must still give specific and accurate adverse-action reasons, and may not use sample checklists that do not reflect the real drivers.

      CFPB Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03
    5. 2022–24
      Gulf open banking frameworks

      SAMA published its Open Banking Framework in 2022, with later releases adding payment initiation. The CBUAE issued an Open Finance Regulation in 2024 and the Al Tareq platform followed.

      SAMA; CBUAE Rulebook
    6. 2024–26
      Rights to your data: mixed progress

      The US CFPB finalised a data-rights rule under section 1033 in October 2024. A federal court has halted enforcement and the Bureau is rewriting it, so US cash-flow lending relies on market arrangements between aggregators and banks. By contrast, the UAE and Saudi regimes are moving from framework to live licensed operation.

      Trade press and law-firm summaries; see sources (check CFPB docket)
    7. 2026
      Licensing and first live flows in the Gulf

      SAMA began licensing open banking providers in March 2026. UAE banks went live on Al Tareq in 2025 and early 2026 (reports disagree on order and dates). Cash-flow underwriting for lending is an announced use case, though public evidence of scale is thin.

      Clyde & Co, March 2026; press reports (secondary)
    8. Now
      LLMs enter the pipeline

      LLMs now categorise messy descriptions, in Arabic and English, and summarise statements for underwriters. They help with data prep; they are not accepted as the credit decision engine, partly because explanations must be faithful (see A10.3 and A7).

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Each failure comes with the quickest check.

    • Counting non-income credits as income

      Transfers between a customer's own accounts, refunds, and loan disbursements from other lenders inflate income, exactly for those who are borrowing to stay afloat.

      Spot itCompare estimated income with salary-certificate or payroll data on a sample. Check how many "income" credits have a matching debit in another account of the same customer.
    • Misclassified transactions at scale

      An Arabic description or a payment-aggregator name sends rent into "shopping" or a gambling-like merchant into "dining". One bad rule moves thousands of customers.

      Spot itAudit a stratified sample of categorised lines by language and bank source. Report accuracy per category, not one overall figure.
    • Leakage through the statement date

      Features built from statements pulled after trouble began (lower balances, new borrowing).

      Spot itCheck that the retrieval timestamp is before the application decision, and that training features use only transactions before it.
    • Selection bias from consent

      Customers who agree to share data are not a random sample. Stronger applicants may share more, so the data-sharing group looks safer than the population.

      Spot itCompare consenters with non-consenters on observable traits and, later, on outcomes. Do not score non-consenters with a model trained only on consenters without checking.
    • Training on approved loans only

      The model learns repayment among the people the old policy accepted, and says nothing about people it rejected.

      Spot itAsk what share of applicants has an outcome. Look for reject inference or a controlled test-and-learn slice with explicit approval of the risk.
    • "We did not use the protected attribute, so it is fair"

      Proxies like cash-withdrawal share, remittance behaviour or payroll channel can carry the same information.

      Spot itRun the proxy test: try to predict the sensitive attribute from model inputs, and compare approval rates by group at your operating point.
    • Fixing fairness by rescaling the score by group

      Using group-specific cut-offs to equalise outcomes may itself be unlawful disparate treatment in some jurisdictions, and conflicts with consistent treatment rules.

      Spot itAsk legal and compliance before any group-aware adjustment. Prefer changes to features and training, tested for both accuracy and outcome gaps.
    • Reason codes that do not match the real decision

      A generic list of reasons attached to every decline, or reasons computed against a different reference than the model used.

      Spot itTake a few declines and check that removing the top reason's effect would really change the decision. Check that the reference and mapping are documented.
    • Seasonality read as stress

      Ramadan, Eid, school fees, summer travel and bonus months shift inflows and spend. A model trained on one calendar can misjudge another.

      Spot itBacktest across at least two full years, and examine score changes around Ramadan and year-end.
    • Gaming and adverse selection

      Once customers know cash-flow matters, they can window-dress: moving funds in before applying, or topping up from a friend.

      Spot itLook for balance spikes just before applications and for round-number inbound transfers from unusual counterparties.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Credit bureaus tell us how someone has borrowed. Open-finance data tells us how they actually live: how reliably money comes in, what goes out, and how close they run to zero. That lets us say yes safely to young, new or self-employed customers we turn away today. The price is care: the same data can quietly favour or penalise groups of customers, so we test outcomes by group before launch, keep the option of a simpler, fairer variant, and make sure every decline can be explained in plain language. I'd start with our own customers, then widen to consented data from other banks.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd separate capacity from risk. Capacity is a cautious income estimate from classified recurring inflows, a lower bound rather than a mean, with commitments and policy caps. Risk is a PD model on stable cash-flow features beside the bureau score, evaluated out-of-time with calibration and incremental lift for thin-file customers. Fairness: outcome and error gaps by group where we can lawfully measure them, a proxy test, and a search over feature subsets and constraints for a less discriminatory alternative, with the accuracy cost shown. Explanations: SHAP against a documented reference mapped to a controlled reason list, checked for fidelity.”
    “Is cash-flow data just a better credit score?”

    Partly. It adds predictive information, especially for thin-file customers, and it also gives something a bureau cannot: a direct measure of affordability. The gain depends on overlap with bureau data, which is why we should measure incremental lift on our own population, not quote vendor numbers.

    “We don't use nationality or gender, so isn't the model fair?”

    No. Other features can carry the same information, and cash-flow data has many of them: payroll channel, remittances, cash share, spending locations. Fairness is tested on outcomes, not inputs. We measure approval and error rates by group where we lawfully can, test whether inputs predict the group, and compare alternatives.

    “If a feature predicts default, why not use it?”

    Often you do. The question is whether it is justified by business need and whether a close alternative gives similar accuracy with a smaller gap. Because the accuracy curve is flat near the best mix, a small cost often buys a large reduction in disparity. That is a decision for the risk committee, shown with numbers.

    “How do we explain a decline from a gradient-boosted model?”

    With local attributions such as SHAP against a documented reference population, mapped to a short list of reasons customers can act on. We check fidelity: if fixing the top reason would not move the score, the reason is wrong. We store the attribution with the decision for audit.

    “Can an LLM read the statements and decide?”

    It can help with categorisation and summaries, with sampling checks. I would not let it make the decision: it is hard to validate, its stated reasons may not match its real drivers, and the explanation duty needs a stable, auditable pathway. The decision stays in a validated model plus policy.

    “What is the legal position in the UAE or Saudi?”

    I'd say what I know and what I don't: open finance and open banking rules govern consent and data access, data-protection law governs purpose and retention, and consumer protection and model-risk guidance apply to decisions. I am not aware of a Gulf equivalent of US adverse-action text, so I'd ask compliance for the current CBUAE or SAMA position before design decisions rely on it.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Monthly income-type credits over four months are 10,000, 12,000, 8,000 and 14,000. Existing obligations are 1,000. Estimate cautious income with k = 1, and the instalment room at a 40% cap.

    Mean = 11,000. Deviations: −1,000, +1,000, −3,000, +3,000. Squares sum to 20,000,000. Sample variance = 20,000,000 ÷ 3 = 6,666,667, so s ≈ 2,582. Ysafe ≈ 8,418. Room = 0.4 × 8,418 − 1,000 ≈ 2,367 a month.

    2. At 12% a year over 36 months, how much finance does AED 2,000 a month support?

    Monthly rate 1%. Factor = (1 − 1.01−36) ÷ 0.01 ≈ 30.1. So 2,000 × 30.1 ≈ AED 60,200.

    3. A feature predicts default well and is also correlated with group. List three ways to respond, and one way not to.

    Reduce its weight or coarsen it; replace it with a less skewed feature carrying similar information; train with a fairness constraint and compare candidates on accuracy and gap. Not: use group-specific cut-offs without legal advice, or declare the model fair because the column was excluded.

    4. In Simulation 3 the score has equal weight on two features (w ≈ 0.71) and group B's gap on feature one is 0.8. Why does reducing w to 0.5 cost little accuracy but narrow the approval gap?

    The signal kept is (w + √(1−w²)) ÷ √2: at w = 0.71 it is 1.00; at w = 0.5 it is (0.5 + 0.866) ÷ 1.414 = 0.966. Meanwhile the group shift in the score is w × gap, which falls by about 30%. Accuracy is flat near its peak, so you give up 3.4% of the signal for a 30% smaller shift.

    5. Why can the reasons for the same decline differ depending on the reference?

    Contributions are measured relative to a reference. Against the average applicant, the applicant's weaknesses are those where they sit below average. Against a typical approved applicant, the bar is higher, so more features count against them and the order changes. So the reference is a policy that must be documented.

    6. Why might the group of customers who consent to data sharing look safer than the full applicant population?

    Customers with stronger finances have less to hide and more to gain, so they consent more often. Training and calibrating on consenters alone then understates risk if the model is applied more widely. Compare consenters with others on observables and track outcomes.

    7. A vendor reports "32% more approvals at the same risk". What do you ask?

    Relative to what baseline policy, on which population, measured how (a controlled experiment or a before-and-after), over what time, and with what default outcomes observed (loans need months to mature). Self-reported approval gains without matured outcomes say little about risk.
