    Explaining a Model: Global Versus Local, and What SHAP Does and Does Not Say

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    Imagine a model that predicts which students will fail an exam. Two people ask about it in one week. A student flagged as "at risk" asks: "Why me?" The head of the school asks: "What is this model relying on?"

    The student needs a reason true for her: 62% of her homework left undone and two late assignments pushed her risk up. The head needs a picture of the whole model. Think of a football season: one match report (local) does not describe how the team plays all year (global), and the season table does not explain one result. Most confusion starts when one question is answered with the other.

    • Global and local are two views of one model. Averaging local explanations over many people gives the global one. Going the other way is where mistakes happen.
    • Every reason is relative to a baseline. "Phone time of 85% while studying pushed her risk up" means up compared with a typical student, like "tall" only means something against a reference group.
    Hold onto thisBefore trusting an explanation chart, ask: is it global or local, and explained relative to what baseline? Most bad conclusions skip one of these.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F8.1 · Global versus local, and who needs which

    Interpretable means simple enough to read directly: a points-based checklist, a short tree, a logistic regression. Explainable means a second method tells a story about a complicated model, which is only an approximation, like a tour guide's summary of a city. For high-stakes decisions, an accurate model that needs no explanation is a real option.

    QuestionScopeTypical toolsWho asks
    What does the model rely on overall?GlobalLinear coefficients; permutation importance; mean absolute SHAPModel risk, validators
    How does the output change as one input moves?Global shapePartial dependence and ICE plots (F8.3)Validators, business owners
    Why this answer for this person?LocalSHAP; LIME; scorecard reason codesCase reviewers, the person affected
    What would have changed the answer?LocalCounterfactuals: the smallest input change that flips the decisionThe person affected, their adviser

    SHAP is not a counterfactual. "Which inputs contributed" differs from "which changes would flip the decision". A student told "few revision hours counted against you" may wait for months, when less phone time this week would have flipped it. For advice, use a counterfactual method and check the change is possible.

    Permutation importance is the most trustworthy simple global tool. Shuffle one column on held-out data and see how far the metric falls, like removing one player and seeing how much the team suffers. Weakness: with two near-copies, the other covers for the shuffled one, so both look unimportant.

    Mean absolute SHAP is the average size of local explanations. It says which features move predictions most, not in which direction, nor whether the model is right on unfamiliar customers.

    F8.2 · Where Shapley values come from

    Shapley values come from game theory: how to split a team's winnings fairly by contribution. SHAP treats a prediction as the game. The players are features, and the payout is this student's prediction minus a baseline prediction.

    A feature's credit depends on who else is playing: late assignments may matter a lot when much homework is undone and little otherwise. So the Shapley value averages a feature's added effect over every order in which the features could be revealed. Two properties matter in practice: the credits add up exactly to the prediction minus the baseline, and a feature the model never uses gets zero.

    Doing this exactly is expensive (thirty features means over a billion combinations), so practical tools approximate or exploit structure. First, the arithmetic by hand.

    • You choose the baseline. It is the average over a background set. Change the background (all students, one class only, last year) and every value changes, because "typical" changed.
    • Values are in log-odds, where effects add. Probabilities do not add: the same +0.5 moves a student near 50% far more than one near 1%. Quote probabilities for the prediction, log-odds for the pushes.

    The versions you will meet: Kernel, Tree, and which "missing"

    MethodWhat it doesCatch
    TreeSHAPExact values for XGBoost, LightGBM, random forests.Fast; the bank default for tabular models.
    KernelSHAPModel-agnostic: samples combinations of known features.Slow, noisy, needs a background.
    Linear, Deep variantsExact for linear models; approximate for neural networks.Check approximations against a slow method on a few cases.

    The deeper choice is how to "remove" a feature from a model that expects all of them. The two answers are not interchangeable.

    • Interventional ("true to the model"). Fill missing features from random background rows. This shows what the model does with each input, but may test impossible combinations, such as a student who revises 15 hours a week yet does no practice papers.
    • Conditional ("true to the data"). Fill with realistic values given the known features. Credit then flows to features merely correlated with the ones the model uses (Simulation 3).

    In code, the choices are arguments:

    import shap, xgboost as xgb

model = xgb.XGBClassifier().fit(X_train, y_train)
background = X_train.sample(200, random_state=0)   # defines "typical"
explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(
    model, data=background,
    feature_perturbation="interventional",   # alternative: "tree_path_dependent"
    model_output="raw")                      # log-odds, where pushes add up
sv = explainer(X_test.iloc[:500])

shap.plots.waterfall(sv[0])   # local: why this person
shap.plots.beeswarm(sv)       # global view built from many local ones

    What SHAP does not mean

    What it supports: "Relative to the reference population, this person's output is higher mainly because of A and B, partly offset by C." That is the model's arithmetic on this input, nothing more. It does not support:

    • Causation or advice. The model learned correlations. A feature pushing risk up does not cause default, and "phone time contributed +0.6" is not "cut phone time and the score falls by 0.6". Inputs move together and interact (Simulation 2).
    • A ranking of correlated features. Revision hours and practice papers done move together. The model may use either, and the choice can flip on retraining at identical accuracy (Simulation 3).
    • Fairness. A protected attribute can be absent from the chart yet enter through correlated fields such as postcode (G2).
    • Validation or stable truth. It describes a model; it does not show the model is right or calibrated. It also depends on the background and the missing-feature choice, and it can be gamed by models that look innocuous under SHAP.

    The rule: with strongly correlated features, do not read the split between them. Group them ("study-effort features" as one), drop one before training, or report their combined push.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at:

    • Leaving nothing unexplained. Pushes add up exactly to prediction minus baseline, because each is an average over every reveal order. A reviewer can check the waterfall arithmetic.
    • One method for both questions. Local values averaged (in absolute size) give the global picture, so the reason for one person and the whole-model summary come from the same numbers.
    • Speed on tree models. TreeSHAP walks the trees instead of enumerating combinations, so thousands of rows explain in seconds to minutes.
    • Working on any model. KernelSHAP needs only predictions, so it still works when the model is a black box or a vendor's API.

    Weak at, or don't use when:

    • You need advice or causes. SHAP describes the model's arithmetic, not what would flip the decision. Use counterfactuals.
    • Features are strongly correlated. The split between near-copies is arbitrary. Group them first.
    • The model is slow or the data is wide. KernelSHAP cost grows with rows, features and background size.
    • A simple model would do. If the model is a logistic regression, its coefficients are the explanation. A second method adds noise and an approval step.
    • You need to prove the model is sound or fair. SHAP shows what the model does, not whether it is right, calibrated or unbiased.

    Alternatives and how to choose:

    OptionPick it whenAvoid when
    SHAP (Tree, Kernel)A boosted-tree or other complex model needs per-person reasons and a global summaryThe question is "what should she change?", or features are near-copies
    Permutation importanceValidators ask what the model needs for accuracy, on held-out dataYou need a reason for one person, or have correlated pairs
    LIME (a small linear model fitted around one point)A quick local look at a black box with no tree structureYou need stable, repeatable reasons: random sampling and the neighbourhood width change the answer
    CounterfactualsThe affected person or a reviewer asks what would change the decisionYou need a model-wide picture, or the changes found are not actionable
    Interpretable model (checklist-style scorecard, GAM)Stakes and scrutiny are high and the accuracy gap is smallComplex interactions carry real signal that you cannot afford to lose

    In practice: start with TreeSHAP on the tree model, with model_output="raw" so pushes add. Use a background of a few hundred rows drawn from the population you actually score, and explain a few thousand held-out rows. Check that baseline plus pushes reproduces the prediction. Read the beeswarm first, then waterfalls chosen by a stated rule. For KernelSHAP, summarise the background (for example with k-means) to keep runtime sensible, and compare a few cases against a slower exact run.

    In banking it appears in three places: turning the largest adverse pushes into plain-language decline reasons for credit, giving fraud and AML analysts a reason for an alert, and giving model-risk teams an attribution summary to monitor for drift. A credit example: debt-to-income of 62% and two late payments pushed one customer's risk up.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Causes and recipes read from SHAP bars

      A top driver is called a cause, or a bar size is quoted as advice: "reduce this and your score improves by that much".

      Spot itWords like "drives" or "leads to". Recompute the prediction with the feature actually changed, and check the change is possible.
    • Ranking correlated features

      "Revision hours is number one; practice papers is fifth." After retraining, the order reverses at identical accuracy.

      Spot itCheck the correlation matrix first. Retrain with another seed, or group or drop one of the pair.
    • Unstated baseline or units

      A waterfall shows pushes from "the average", but of which population? Or log-odds bars are read as percentage points.

      Spot itAsk for the background set and its date. Check the axis label.
    • Global conclusions from a few local cases

      Three well-chosen waterfalls are taken to describe the model.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were chosen. Ask for the summary plot over a representative sample.
    • Importance that is really leakage

      One feature carries an implausible share, such as a status field filled in only after the outcome.

      Spot itAsk when each top feature becomes available relative to decision time (F3.1).
    • Absence from the chart read as absence of bias

      "Nationality is not a feature, so it isn't used." Proxies carry it.

      Spot itTest outcomes by group directly. Check whether features predict the protected attribute.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A student's SHAP values are +3 for homework left undone and −1 for revision hours, with a baseline of −3 log-odds. What do they tell you, and what do they sum to?

    Relative to a typical student, undone homework pushed her risk of failing up a lot and revision pulled it down a little. Together they add up to +2, so nothing is left unexplained.

    2. A student's SHAP values sum to +1.2 log-odds from a baseline of about 2% chance of failing. Why is "her risk is 1.2 points higher" wrong?

    The +1.2 is in log-odds, not percentage points. Converted to probability it takes her from about 2% to about 6%, and the same +1.2 from a different starting point would give a different change. Quote probabilities for the prediction and log-odds for the pushes.

    3. A model uses only revision hours, but practice papers done is highly correlated with it. Why might one SHAP method still give practice papers a share of the credit, and is that wrong?

    The conditional method fills in unknown features realistically, so knowing practice papers tells you something about revision hours, and it gets credit for that information. The interventional method gives practice papers zero, since the model never uses it. Neither is wrong: one asks "what did the model use?", the other "what does this information imply?".

    4. A student is told "cut your phone time and your risk drops by its SHAP value". Give two reasons this can be wrong.

    (a) Interactions: phone time's effect may depend on late assignments or undone homework, so the real change differs from the averaged SHAP value. (b) Correlated inputs: cutting phone time also moves revision hours and sleep, and the model responds to the whole movement.

    5. A colleague says nationality is not in the model, so SHAP shows it has no influence. What is wrong?

    A missing column is not a missing effect: postcode, employer or branch may proxy for it. SHAP attributes only among features present. Test outcomes by group instead (G2).

    6. Mean absolute SHAP or permutation importance on held-out data: which better tests whether the model depends on a feature for accuracy?

    Permutation importance: it measures how much accuracy falls when the feature is scrambled. Mean absolute SHAP measures how much the feature moves predictions, which can be large for a feature the model merely overfits. Both mislead with correlated pairs.

    7. A regulator wants per-customer decline reasons from a boosted-tree credit model, and a vendor offers LIME. Which do you choose, and why?

    TreeSHAP. It is exact for trees, repeatable and additive, so the reasons are stable and reconcile with the score. LIME's sampling makes reasons vary between runs. If the accuracy gap allows, a scorecard avoids the question altogether.
