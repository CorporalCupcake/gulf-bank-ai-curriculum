    Explaining a Model: Feature Importance and SHAP

    Part 1

    What explaining a model means

    In one sentenceExplaining a model means showing which inputs drive its predictions, either across all cases (global) or for one case (local).

    Take a model that predicts which students will fail an exam. Two people ask about it in one week. The head of the school asks, "What does this model rely on?" A flagged student asks, "Why me?" These are different questions and need different tools.

    The left chart is a ranking for the whole model. The right chart is a reason for one person, and it can have a different order from the ranking. Averaging the right-hand charts over many students gives the left-hand one.

    How it differs from similar things

    • Interpretable models (a short tree, a points checklist, logistic regression) can be read directly. Nothing extra is needed.
    • Explainable models are complicated ones (boosting, neural networks) with a second method added to tell a story about them. That story is an approximation, like a tour guide's summary of a city.
    • Feature importance answers the global question: what matters overall?
    • SHAP answers the local question: why this answer for this person? It can also be averaged into a global view.
    • Counterfactuals answer a third question, "what would change the answer?" They are covered in the next lesson.
    Part 2

    Global: permutation importance

    Permutation importance asks a simple question: how much worse does the model get if one column becomes useless? It is the most trustworthy simple global tool. It is like removing one player from a team and seeing how much the team suffers.

    1. Score the model on held-out data it did not train on. Say it is 85% accurate.
    2. Shuffle one column, such as homework undone. Each student now gets someone else's value, which breaks the link with the outcome.
    3. Score again. Say accuracy falls to 70%.
    4. Importance = the drop, here 15 points.
    5. Repeat for every column and rank the drops.

    How to read it

    • A big drop means the model leans heavily on that column.
    • A drop near zero means the model barely uses it. A negative drop is noise: the column is useless.
    • Correlated columns fool it. If revision hours and practice papers done move together, shuffling one leaves the other to cover. Both look unimportant, though together they matter.
    • It says "how much", not "which direction". A big drop does not tell you whether more revision raises or lowers risk.

    Another common global score is the mean absolute SHAP value: the average size of the local pushes described next. Like permutation importance, it says how much a feature moves predictions, not in which direction.

    Part 3

    Local: SHAP

    In one sentenceSHAP splits one prediction into a starting point plus a push from each feature, and the pushes add up exactly to the final answer.

    The starting point is the average prediction over a set of typical students. Each feature then pushes the prediction up or down from there. For our flagged student:

    How it works

    1. Pick a background set, usually a few hundred typical rows. Its average prediction is the starting point.
    2. Reveal features in some order, one at a time, and record how the prediction moves each time.
    3. Repeat over every possible order. A feature's effect depends on which others are already known, so a single order would be unfair.
    4. Average each feature's moves. That average is its SHAP value.

    The idea comes from game theory: splitting a team's winnings fairly by contribution. Trying every order is far too slow with 30 features, so tools use shortcuts. TreeSHAP is exact and fast for boosting and forests, and is the usual choice for tables of data. KernelSHAP works on any model but is slower and noisier.

    How to read it

    • "+25" on homework undone means: compared with a typical student, this student's undone homework raises her risk by 25 points.
    • A negative bar pulled the risk down, like the revision hours above.
    • The bars always add up from the starting point to the prediction. If they don't, something is wrong.
    • A zero means the model never used that feature for her.
    • Change the background and every number changes, because "typical" changed. Say which background you used.
    Part 4

    Which to use, and what SHAP does not say

    ToolQuestion it answersScopeWatch out for
    Linear coefficientsHow much does each input add?GlobalOnly for linear models
    Permutation importanceWhat does the model rely on?GlobalCorrelated columns hide each other
    Mean absolute SHAPWhich features move predictions most?GlobalNo direction, depends on background
    SHAP valuesWhy this answer for this person?LocalNot causes, not advice

    SHAP is strong at:

    • Giving one consistent method for local reasons and global rankings.
    • Exact, fast results on tree models, which dominate tabular work.
    • Adding up cleanly, so each reason is checkable.

    It is weak at:

    • Causation. The model learned correlations. "Phone time pushed risk up by 6" does not mean cutting phone time lowers risk by 6.
    • Splitting credit among correlated features. The model may use either revision hours or practice papers, and the choice can flip when you retrain, at the same accuracy.
    • Checking the model is right. It describes what the model does, not whether it is correct or fair. A protected attribute can be absent from the chart yet enter through a correlated field such as postcode.

    In practice: use permutation importance for the global picture, TreeSHAP for individual cases, and a simple model when a plain rule is enough.

    Show the formula

    Prediction = starting point + sum of every feature's SHAP value. Each SHAP value is that feature's average added effect over all orders in which features could be revealed.

    In banking, SHAP charts are common in model validation, and they support, but do not replace, the formal reasons given to a declined customer.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Answering a local question with a global chart

      "The model mostly uses homework" does not explain why this student was flagged. Her own reasons can differ.

    • Reading SHAP as advice

      A push is arithmetic on this input, not a lever. Use a counterfactual when someone asks what to change.

    • Ranking correlated features

      Credit splits between near-copies and can shift on retraining. Group them, or say they are linked.

    • Forgetting the background

      Every SHAP value is relative to a baseline. Quote it, or readers will assume a different "typical".

    1. What is the difference between a global and a local explanation?

    Global shows what the model relies on across all cases. Local shows why one case got its answer.

    2. How does permutation importance work?

    Shuffle one column on held-out data and measure how much the score drops. A bigger drop means more reliance.

    3. A student's SHAP chart starts at 20% and ends at 50%. What must the bars add up to?

    Plus 30 points (on the scale used). The pushes always sum to the prediction minus the starting point.

    4. Two features are near-copies. Why is permutation importance misleading?

    When one is shuffled, the other covers for it, so both look unimportant even though together they matter.

    5. Does a big positive SHAP value for phone time mean cutting phone time would cut her risk?

    No. It shows how the model uses the input, not what would happen to her. That needs a causal argument or a counterfactual check.
