    Explaining a Model: Global Versus Local, and What SHAP Does and Does Not Say

    The same model gets two different questions, "what drives it?" and "why this customer?". How to read a SHAP chart, and the handful of things it is routinely and wrongly taken to prove.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA global explanation tells you how the model behaves across the population, a local one tells you why it gave this answer for this person, and SHAP is a fair way of splitting one prediction among the inputs given a chosen baseline, which is a statement about the model and not about cause, fairness or what to change.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Two people ask a bank about the same model in one week. A declined customer asks: "Why me?" The head of model risk asks: "What is this model relying on?"

    The customer needs a reason true for her: a debt-to-income ratio of 62% and two late payments pushed her risk up. The risk head needs a picture of the whole model. Think of a football season: one match report (local) does not describe how the team plays all year (global), and the season table does not explain one result. Most confusion starts when one question is answered with the other.

    • Global and local are two views of one model. Averaging local explanations over many customers gives the global one. Going the other way is where mistakes happen.
    • Every reason is relative to a baseline. "Utilisation of 85% pushed her risk up" means up compared with a typical applicant, like "tall" only means something against a reference group.
    Hold onto thisBefore trusting an explanation chart, ask: is it global or local, and explained relative to what baseline? Most bad conclusions skip one of these.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F8.1 · Global versus local, and who needs which

    Interpretable means simple enough to read directly: a scorecard, a short tree, a logistic regression. Explainable means a second method tells a story about a complicated model, which is only an approximation, like a tour guide's summary of a city. For high-stakes credit decisions, an accurate model that needs no explanation is a real option.

    QuestionScopeTypical toolsWho asks
    What does the model rely on overall?GlobalLinear coefficients; permutation importance; mean absolute SHAPModel risk, validators
    How does the output change as one input moves?Global shapePartial dependence and ICE plots (F8.3)Validators, business owners
    Why this answer for this customer?LocalSHAP; LIME; scorecard reason codesCase reviewers, the customer
    What would have changed the answer?LocalCounterfactuals: the smallest input change that flips the decisionCustomer, relationship manager

    SHAP is not a counterfactual. "Which inputs contributed" differs from "which changes would flip the decision". A customer told "short tenure counted against you" may wait, when lower utilisation this month would have flipped it. For advice, use a counterfactual method and check the change is possible.

    Permutation importance is the most trustworthy simple global tool. Shuffle one column on held-out data and see how far the metric falls, like removing one player and seeing how much the team suffers. Weakness: with two near-copies, the other covers for the shuffled one, so both look unimportant.

    Mean absolute SHAP is the average size of local explanations. It says which features move predictions most, not in which direction, nor whether the model is right on unfamiliar customers.

    F8.2 · Where Shapley values come from

    Shapley values come from game theory: how to split a team's winnings fairly by contribution. SHAP treats a prediction as the game. The players are features, and the payout is this customer's prediction minus a baseline prediction.

    A feature's credit depends on who else is playing: late payments may matter a lot when debt-to-income is high and little otherwise. So the Shapley value averages a feature's added effect over every order in which the features could be revealed. Two properties matter in practice: the credits add up exactly to the prediction minus the baseline, and a feature the model never uses gets zero.

    Doing this exactly is expensive (thirty features means over a billion combinations), so practical tools approximate or exploit structure. First, the arithmetic by hand.

    • You choose the baseline. It is the average over a background set. Change the background (all customers, approved only, last year) and every value changes, because "typical" changed.
    • Values are in log-odds, where effects add. Probabilities do not add: the same +0.5 moves a customer near 50% far more than one near 1%. Quote probabilities for the prediction, log-odds for the pushes.

    The versions you will meet: Kernel, Tree, and which "missing"

    MethodWhat it doesCatch
    TreeSHAPExact values for XGBoost, LightGBM, random forests.Fast; the bank default for tabular models.
    KernelSHAPModel-agnostic: samples combinations of known features.Slow, noisy, needs a background.
    Linear, Deep variantsExact for linear models; approximate for neural networks.Check approximations against a slow method on a few cases.

    The deeper choice is how to "remove" a feature from a model that expects all of them. The two answers are not interchangeable.

    • Interventional ("true to the model"). Fill missing features from random background rows. This shows what the model does with each input, but may test impossible combinations, such as a very high salary with a tiny balance.
    • Conditional ("true to the data"). Fill with realistic values given the known features. Credit then flows to features merely correlated with the ones the model uses (Simulation 3).

    In code, the choices are arguments:

    import shap, xgboost as xgb

model = xgb.XGBClassifier().fit(X_train, y_train)
background = X_train.sample(200, random_state=0)   # defines "typical"
explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(
    model, data=background,
    feature_perturbation="interventional",   # alternative: "tree_path_dependent"
    model_output="raw")                      # log-odds, where pushes add up
sv = explainer(X_test.iloc[:500])

shap.plots.waterfall(sv[0])   # local: why this customer
shap.plots.beeswarm(sv)       # global view built from many local ones

    What SHAP does not mean

    What it supports: "Relative to the reference population, this customer's output is higher mainly because of A and B, partly offset by C." That is the model's arithmetic on this input, nothing more. It does not support:

    • Causation or advice. The model learned correlations. A feature pushing risk up does not cause default, and "utilisation contributed +0.6" is not "cut utilisation and the score falls by 0.6". Inputs move together and interact (Simulation 2).
    • A ranking of correlated features. Salary credits and average balance move together. The model may use either, and the choice can flip on retraining at identical accuracy (Simulation 3).
    • Fairness. A protected attribute can be absent from the chart yet enter through postcode, employer or nationality-correlated fields (G2).
    • Validation or stable truth. It describes a model; it does not show the model is right or calibrated. It also depends on the background and the missing-feature choice, and it can be gamed by models that look innocuous under SHAP.

    The rule: with strongly correlated features, do not read the split between them. Group them ("income-related features" as one), drop one before training, or report their combined push.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Causes and recipes read from SHAP bars

      A top driver is called a cause, or a bar size is quoted as advice: "reduce this and your score improves by that much".

      Spot itWords like "drives" or "leads to". Recompute the prediction with the feature actually changed, and check the change is possible.
    • Ranking correlated features

      "Salary credits is number one; balance is fifth." After retraining, the order reverses at identical accuracy.

      Spot itCheck the correlation matrix first. Retrain with another seed, or group or drop one of the pair.
    • Unstated baseline or units

      A waterfall shows pushes from "the average", but of which population? Or log-odds bars are read as percentage points.

      Spot itAsk for the background set and its date. Check the axis label.
    • Global conclusions from a few local cases

      Three well-chosen waterfalls are taken to describe the model.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were chosen. Ask for the summary plot over a representative sample.
    • Importance that is really leakage

      One feature carries an implausible share, such as a status field filled in only after the outcome.

      Spot itAsk when each top feature becomes available relative to decision time (F3.1).
    • Absence from the chart read as absence of bias

      "Nationality is not a feature, so it isn't used." Proxies carry it.

      Spot itTest outcomes by group directly. Check whether features predict the protected attribute.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A customer's SHAP values are +3 for debt-to-income and −1 for tenure, with a baseline of −3 log-odds. What do they tell you, and what do they sum to?

    Relative to a typical applicant, debt-to-income pushed her risk up a lot and tenure pulled it down a little. Together they add up to +2, so nothing is left unexplained.

    2. A customer's SHAP values sum to +1.2 log-odds from a baseline of about 2% default probability. Why is "her risk is 1.2 points higher" wrong?

    The +1.2 is in log-odds, not percentage points. Converted to probability it takes her from about 2% to about 6%, and the same +1.2 from a different starting point would give a different change. Quote probabilities for the prediction and log-odds for the pushes.

    3. Why is exact Shapley computation impractical with many features, and what do banks do instead?

    The number of feature combinations doubles with every feature, passing a billion at thirty. Banks use TreeSHAP for tree models, which is exact and fast, or sampling approximations for other models.

    4. A model uses only salary credits, but balance is highly correlated with it. Why might one SHAP method still give balance a share of the credit, and is that wrong?

    The conditional method fills in unknown features realistically, so knowing balance tells you something about salary, and it gets credit for that information. The interventional method gives balance zero, since the model never uses it. Neither is wrong: one asks "what did the model use?", the other "what does this information imply?".

    5. A customer is told "reduce your utilisation and your risk drops by its SHAP value". Give two reasons this can be wrong.

    (a) Interactions: utilisation's effect may depend on late payments or debt-to-income, so the real change differs from the averaged SHAP value. (b) Correlated inputs: cutting utilisation also moves balances and spending, and the model responds to the whole movement.

    6. A colleague says nationality is not in the model, so SHAP shows it has no influence. What is wrong?

    A missing column is not a missing effect: postcode, employer or branch may proxy for it. SHAP attributes only among features present. Test outcomes by group instead (G2).

    7. Mean absolute SHAP or permutation importance on held-out data: which better tests whether the model depends on a feature for accuracy?

    Permutation importance: it measures how much accuracy falls when the feature is scrambled. Mean absolute SHAP measures how much the feature moves predictions, which can be large for a feature the model merely overfits. Both mislead with correlated pairs.

    8. In one sentence each: what does the baseline define, and what does the missing-feature choice define?

    The baseline defines "typical", the reference every push is measured against. The missing-feature choice defines how an unknown input is filled in, and so who gets credit when features are correlated.
