    Explaining a Model: Global Versus Local, and What SHAP Does and Does Not Say

    The same model gets two different questions, "what drives it?" and "why this customer?". Where Shapley values come from, how to read a SHAP chart, and the handful of things it is routinely and wrongly taken to prove.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA global explanation tells you how the model behaves across the population, a local one tells you why it gave this answer for this person, and SHAP is a fair way of splitting one prediction among the inputs given a chosen baseline, which is a statement about the model and not about cause, fairness or what to change.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A Gulf bank has a gradient-boosted model scoring personal-finance applications. In one week two people ask the bank about it.

    The first is a customer in Sharjah whose application was declined. She asks: "Why me?" The second is the head of model risk. He asks: "What is this model actually relying on?"

    These are different questions with different right answers. The customer needs a reason that is true for her: her debt-to-income ratio of 62% and two late payments pushed her score up. The risk head needs a picture of the whole model: across everyone, which inputs matter, and does the way they matter make sense? A reason that is true for one person tells you almost nothing about the model overall, and a ranking of overall importance can be quite wrong for any given person.

    The first is a local explanation. The second is global. Most confusion about explainability starts when someone answers one question with the other.

    Three things to take from it.

    • Global and local are two views of the same model. Averaging the local explanations over many customers gives the global one. Going the other way is where mistakes happen.
    • The "reason" depends on a comparison. "Utilisation of 85% pushed her risk up" means up relative to a typical applicant. An explanation always has a baseline, even when nobody says so.
    • SHAP is the most common recipe for local explanations in banks, and it is also the most over-read. Part 2 shows exactly what it computes so you can say what it supports and what it doesn't.
    Hold onto thisBefore anyone shows you an explanation chart, ask two things. Is it global or local: a statement about the model or about one decision? And explained relative to what baseline? Most bad conclusions drawn from SHAP skip one of these questions.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F8.1 · Global versus local, and who needs which

    Start with vocabulary. Two words get mixed up. Interpretable usually means the model is simple enough to read directly: a scorecard, a short decision tree, a logistic regression. Explainable means you apply a second method to a complicated model to produce a story about it. An explanation of a black box is an approximation of it, made by another procedure with its own assumptions. Rudin (2019) argues that for high-stakes decisions you should prefer a model that needs no explanation if it is accurate enough. In credit that is a real option, and worth asking about before reaching for an explanation tool.

    Now the global-versus-local split and the usual tools on each side.

    QuestionScopeTypical toolsWho asks
    What does the model rely on overall?GlobalCoefficients of a linear model; tree split counts (unreliable); permutation importance; mean absolute SHAP; a simple surrogate model fitted to the big model's outputsModel risk, validators, data owners
    How does the output change as one input moves?Global shapePartial dependence and ICE plots (next lesson, F8.3); SHAP dependence plotsValidators, business owners checking sense
    Why did the model say this for this customer?LocalSHAP values; LIME (a local simple fit); reason codes from a scorecardCustomer service, case reviewers, the customer
    What would have changed the answer?LocalCounterfactual explanations: the smallest change to the inputs that flips the decisionCustomer, relationship manager

    The last two rows are different questions and are frequently confused. "Which inputs contributed to the score" (SHAP) is not "which changes would flip the decision" (a counterfactual). A customer told "your short tenure counted against you" may take it to mean she should wait, when the real flip would have come from a lower utilisation she could achieve this month. If the aim is advice, use a method built for it, and check the suggested changes are things a person can actually do (you can't tell someone to be five years older).

    Permutation importance is the most trustworthy simple global tool: shuffle one column on a held-out set and see how much the chosen metric (say AUC) falls. If it falls a lot, the model leans on that column. It measures dependence on the feature for predictive accuracy, which is a cleaner idea than "contribution to predictions", but it has the same weakness with correlated columns: shuffle one of two near-copies and the model leans on the other, so each looks unimportant.

    Global from local. Mean absolute SHAP is just the average size of the local explanations across customers, which is why Simulation 1 puts both on one axis. It tells you which features move predictions most. It does not tell you the direction, the shape, or whether the model would still be right on customers unlike the ones it saw.

    F8.2 · Where Shapley values come from

    The idea is borrowed from cooperative game theory. Lloyd Shapley (1953) asked: a group of players earns a total payout together; how do you split it fairly according to each one's contribution? His answer is the unique rule satisfying a few fairness requirements. SHAP (Lundberg and Lee, 2017) casts a prediction as the game. The players are the input features. The payout is the gap between this customer's prediction and a baseline prediction.

    The difficulty: the credit for a feature depends on what the others are doing. Late payments may matter a lot when debt-to-income is high and little otherwise. So the Shapley value averages a feature's marginal contribution over every possible order in which the features could be revealed:

    φj=∑S⊆N∖{j}|S|!(n−|S|−1)!n![v(S∪{j})−v(S)]

    N is the set of n features, S a subset of the others, and v(S) the model's expected output when only the features in S are "known" and the rest are averaged out. The bracket is the extra effect of revealing feature j after S. The fraction weights each ordering equally. In words: average, over all orders, the change in prediction when feature j is added.

    That one definition gives the properties people quote:

    • Efficiency (additivity). The pushes add up exactly: baseline plus all the φ equals this customer's prediction. Nothing is left unexplained.
    • Symmetry. Two features that play identical roles get identical credit.
    • Missingness / dummy. A feature the model never uses gets zero.
    • Consistency. If a model changes so that a feature matters more everywhere, its value does not go down.

    The "average over all orders" is also the cost. With n features there are 2n subsets; thirty features means over a billion. Practical tools approximate or exploit structure, which Part 2's next section covers. First, the arithmetic done by hand, so the numbers are not magic.

    Two things the experiment makes concrete.

    • The baseline is the average over a background set, and you choose it. Change the background (all customers, only approved customers, only last year's) and every SHAP value changes, because "typical" changed. The numbers answer "why is this prediction different from that reference?".
    • SHAP values are in the units of the model output. For a classifier that is usually log-odds, where effects add. Converting a push to a probability is not additive: the same +0.5 in log-odds is a bigger shift in probability for a customer already near 50% than for one near 1%. Quote probabilities for the prediction, log-odds for the pushes, and be explicit about which.

    The versions you will meet: Kernel, Tree, and which "missing"

    Exact enumeration is only possible for a few features, so the shap library offers several routes.

    MethodWhat it doesCost and catch
    TreeSHAPExploits the tree structure to compute exact values for XGBoost, LightGBM, random forests, in polynomial time (Lundberg et al., 2020).Fast, the bank default for tabular models. Needs a choice of how to treat "missing" features (below).
    KernelSHAPModel-agnostic. Samples coalitions and fits a weighted linear regression to recover the values.Slow, noisy, needs a background sample, and the sampling treats features as independent.
    LinearSHAPFor linear models, closed form: weight times (value minus average) under independence.What Simulation 1 uses. Exact and instant.
    DeepSHAP, gradient variantsApproximations for neural networks.Approximate; check they agree with a slow method on a few cases.

    The deeper choice is what v(S) means, that is, how you "remove" a feature from a model that expects all of them. There are two main answers, and they are not interchangeable (Sundararajan and Najmi, 2020; Chen et al., 2020).

    • Interventional ("true to the model"). Replace the missing features with values from random background rows, breaking their link to the known ones. Measures what the model does with each input. It evaluates the model on combinations that may never occur, such as a very high salary with a tiny account balance.
    • Conditional ("true to the data"). Fill in missing features with values that are realistic given the known ones. Credit then flows to features that are merely correlated with the ones the model uses. Simulation 3 shows the effect.

    In code, the whole thing is a few lines, and the choices are arguments:

    import shap, xgboost as xgb

model = xgb.XGBClassifier().fit(X_train, y_train)

# the background defines "typical"; 100-500 representative rows is usual
background = X_train.sample(200, random_state=0)
explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(
    model, data=background,
    feature_perturbation="interventional",   # alternative: "tree_path_dependent"
    model_output="raw")                      # log-odds, where pushes add up
sv = explainer(X_test.iloc[:500])

shap.plots.waterfall(sv[0])   # local: why this customer
shap.plots.beeswarm(sv)       # global view built from many local ones

    What SHAP does not mean

    A SHAP chart looks authoritative: bars, signs, sizes that add up. It is easy to read more into it than it holds. The reliable reading is narrow.

    What it supports. "Relative to the reference population, the model's output for this customer is higher mainly because of A and B, partly offset by C." That is a statement about the model's arithmetic on this input.

    What it does not support.

    • Not causation. The model learned correlations. A feature pushing risk up does not mean it causes default, or that changing it would lower risk (F9.2 has the causal side). Tenure may proxy for something else entirely.
    • Not a recipe for action. "Utilisation contributed +0.6" is not "reduce utilisation and the score falls by 0.6". Inputs move together, models contain interactions, and Simulation 2's orange bars differ from the blue ones as soon as an interaction exists.
    • Not a ranking when features are correlated. Salary credits and average balance move together. The model can use one, the other or a mix, and which it picks may flip on retraining with identical accuracy. SHAP's split of credit follows that arbitrary choice. Simulation 3 puts a number on it.
    • Not fairness. A protected attribute can be absent from the chart while its effect enters through postcode, employer or nationality-correlated fields. A clean-looking SHAP plot proves nothing about proxy discrimination (G2).
    • Not the truth about the world, and not stable. It depends on the background, the model version and the missing-feature choice. And explanation methods can themselves be manipulated: Slack et al. (2020) built models that behave discriminatorily on real data but show innocuous explanations from LIME and SHAP, because the methods query the model on unrealistic, off-distribution points.
    • Not a replacement for validation. It describes a model. It does not tell you the model is right, calibrated or stable.

    The practical rule: when features are strongly correlated, do not read the split between them. Group them (explain "income-related features" as one), drop one before training, or report their combined push. For a ranked list of drivers to be defensible, the drivers should not be near-duplicates.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Explanation sits between the model and the people who have to trust, challenge or act on it. These are the neighbouring ideas.

    F8.3 – F8.5 · Partial dependence, ICE, and the rest of the moduleShape of the effect, not just its size

    Global importance says a feature matters. Partial dependence and ICE show how: monotone, thresholded, or different for different customers. Read them next to a SHAP summary.

    F4 · Trees and ensemblesWhy TreeSHAP exists

    Boosted trees are the usual bank tabular model, and their structure makes exact Shapley values cheap. Their built-in "split count" importance is biased towards high-cardinality fields, so prefer permutation or SHAP.

    F2.5 · Regression and GLMsThe model that explains itself

    In a logistic regression with independent inputs, SHAP is just coefficient times distance from average. The scorecard tradition is built on this, which is why regulators are comfortable with it.

    F9.2 · Causal inferenceWhat explanations cannot say

    "Because of X" in an explanation is a statement about the model. Whether changing X changes the outcome is a causal question, with its own methods and assumptions.

    F2.6 · ConfoundingWhy a feature can look protective

    Fitted models inherit confounding. A feature reducing predicted risk may be a marker of some selection process, not a protective factor.

    F7 · Calibration and monitoringExplaining a wrong number is still wrong

    SHAP explains the model output as it is. If the probabilities are miscalibrated, the explanation inherits that. Track explanation drift with drift monitoring: a feature's average push changing is an early warning.

    G2 · FairnessExplanations are not a fairness test

    Use group-level outcome tests for fairness, and use explanations to investigate why a gap exists once you have found one.

    G3 · Validation and model governanceWhat validators will ask for

    Independent validation typically wants global importance, dependence shapes, and local explanations for sample cases. See also what counts as a model and writing for validators.

    R5 · Mechanistic interpretabilityExplaining LLMs from the inside

    Feature attribution works on tabular inputs. For language models the frontier is looking at internal representations, a different toolkit with different promises.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Credit decisions and reason codes. An applicant declined for personal finance, a credit card limit, or a Sharia-compliant murabaha facility needs a stated reason. The reasons must be true of the decision. SHAP-based top-driver lists are common, but the mapping from "top pushes" to a customer-facing reason needs a human-reviewed dictionary and a clear policy on correlated and protected-proxy fields. (B4 covers the PD side.)
    • Fraud and AML alerts. An analyst has 90 seconds per alert. A three-line "why flagged" (unusual amount for this customer, new beneficiary, device change) saves time and exposes bad alerts. Here local explanations are a workflow feature, not a compliance exercise.
    • Model risk and validation. Global plots go in the model development document and the validation report, so a reviewer can see whether monotonic relationships make sense and whether a suspicious field is dominating.
    • Debugging. If one feature carries a surprising share of the model, suspect leakage first (F3.1). An "account closed flag" at the top of a churn model is a classic.
    • Complaints. "Why was I declined?" arrives in Arabic and English, sometimes with an escalation to the regulator. Having a stable, accurate explanation is part of the answer.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    SHAP is a settled working tool and an unsettled idea. The live arguments are about the missing-feature choice, correlated inputs, whether explanations can be trusted when someone has an incentive to game them, and whether post-hoc explanation is the right approach at all for high-stakes credit.

    Checked on 6 November 2026 with one light search, plus background knowledge. Older items are well-known and I am confident of them; the 2025–26 items are preprints reached through the search and I have not read them in full.

    1. 1953
      Shapley value

      The fair-division rule from cooperative game theory that SHAP later borrowed.

      Shapley, 1953
    2. 2014–17
      From games to predictions

      Štrumbelj and Kononenko applied Shapley values to explaining model outputs. Lundberg and Lee unified several earlier explanation methods (including LIME) under the SHAP framework and gave KernelSHAP.

      Štrumbelj & Kononenko, 2014; Lundberg & Lee, NeurIPS 2017
    3. 2016
      LIME

      Explain one prediction by fitting a simple model around it. Intuitive, but unstable between runs and sensitive to how the neighbourhood is sampled.

      Ribeiro et al., KDD 2016
    4. 2018–19
      Explanations that do not depend on the model, and the call to stop explaining black boxes

      Adebayo et al. showed some popular saliency methods give nearly the same output on a randomised model, failing a basic sanity check. Rudin argued that for high-stakes decisions, interpretable models should replace post-hoc explanations.

      Adebayo et al., NeurIPS 2018; Rudin, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019
    5. 2020
      Exact trees, and the first serious critiques

      TreeSHAP made exact computation practical for boosted trees. In the same year: Slack et al. showed SHAP and LIME can be fooled; Kumar et al. catalogued conceptual problems with using Shapley values as feature importance; Sundararajan and Najmi and Janzing et al. laid out the "which Shapley value" ambiguity and its causal reading; Chen et al. framed the choice as "true to the model" versus "true to the data".

      Lundberg et al., Nature MI, 2020; Slack et al., AIES 2020; Kumar et al., ICML 2020; Sundararajan & Najmi, ICML 2020; Janzing et al., AISTATS 2020; Chen et al., 2020
    6. 2022
      Central-bank work on accuracy of explanations for credit

      The Banco de España published a working paper testing how accurate common explanation methods are for credit decisions, a signal that supervisors treat explanations as something to be measured, not assumed.

      Banco de España, Documento de Trabajo 2222, 2022
    7. 2025–26
      Still the same cautions, now in credit-specific papers

      Recent preprints on credit scoring restate that SHAP can split credit between correlated variables unpredictably, and that it is an attribution of the model and not a causal analysis. One 2026 preprint on mortgage denials argues for causal mediation analysis alongside SHAP when discrimination is the question. These are preprints; read them before quoting.

      arXiv 2509.11389 (2025); arXiv 2603.27510 (2026), both preprints

    On regulation. I am not going to state specific CBUAE or SAMA explainability requirements, because I have not verified their current wording here, and they differ by use case. Treat the bank's model risk policy and the regulator's published guidance as the source of truth. For comparison, some jurisdictions (the US under ECOA and Regulation B, for example) require specific reasons for adverse credit decisions, which is why "reason code" practice exists.     The current consensus, in one lineUse SHAP as a useful, fast description of what a model computes, alongside permutation importance and dependence plots; state the baseline and variant; do not read causes, advice or fairness into it; and where a regulated decision must be justified, ask first whether a simpler model would do.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in bank analyses, vendor decks and AI-written reports. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • "Feature X causes default"

      A top SHAP driver is described as a cause. The model learned an association, possibly through a proxy or confounder.

      Spot itWords like "drives", "leads to", "causes". Ask whether the claim would survive an experiment or a causal argument, or whether it is "the model scores this higher".
    • "Reduce this and your score improves by that much"

      Advice to a customer taken straight from the size of a SHAP bar.

      Spot itRecompute the prediction with the feature actually changed. If the change differs from the bar, there are interactions or correlated inputs. Also check the change is possible.
    • Ranking correlated features

      "Salary credits is the number-one driver; balance is fifth." After retraining, the order reverses with identical accuracy.

      Spot itRetrain with another seed or drop one of the pair and see whether the ranking holds. Check the correlation matrix before reading any ranking.
    • Unstated baseline

      A waterfall plot shows pushes from "the average", but the average of which population: all applicants, approved ones, last year's?

      Spot itAsk for the background set and its date. A different one gives different numbers.
    • Mixing log-odds and probabilities

      Bars in log-odds read as percentage-point changes. A "+0.5" becomes "+50%".

      Spot itCheck the axis label. If the model output is log-odds, say so; convert only the final prediction to a probability.
    • Global conclusions from a few local cases

      Three well-chosen waterfalls in a deck are taken to describe the model.

      Spot itAsk how the cases were chosen, and ask for the summary plot over a representative sample.
    • Importance that is really leakage

      One feature carries an implausible share, for instance a status field only filled after the outcome.

      Spot itAsk when each top feature becomes available relative to the decision time. If it isn't known at decision time, it is leakage (F3.1).
    • Absence from the chart read as absence of bias

      "Nationality is not a feature, so it isn't used." Proxies carry it.

      Spot itTest outcomes by group directly, and check whether features predict the protected attribute.
    • Explaining a model that has not been validated

      Polished explanation plots for an under-tested or miscalibrated model lend false confidence.

      Spot itAsk for performance on a time-ordered holdout and calibration before discussing explanations.
    • Off-distribution queries

      KernelSHAP or interventional methods evaluate the model on feature combinations that never occur in real data, where its behaviour is arbitrary.

      Spot itLook for impossible combinations in the background mix (very high income, zero balance). Cross-check with a different method.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “There are two kinds of explanation. One tells us how the model behaves across all customers, which is what risk and audit need. The other tells us why it reached a decision for one customer, which is what a case officer or a customer needs. We can produce both, but they are descriptions of what the model computes, not proof of what causes default. For customer-facing reasons we map them to approved wording and review them for fairness, rather than printing the raw model output.”
    To a validator or model-risk reviewer · 45 seconds
    “For global behaviour I'll show permutation importance on the time-ordered holdout and SHAP summaries, with partial-dependence plots for the top features. SHAP is interventional with a 200-row background drawn from the same period; I can show how the top-five ranking changes with another background. Several inputs are strongly correlated, so I group them when ranking. Local explanations are for sample cases and for analyst display, and the document says clearly that they describe the model, not causes.”
    To a data scientist peer · 20 seconds
    “Which Shapley value is it: path-dependent, interventional or conditional? And what is the background? Those two choices change the plot more than the model does when features are correlated.”

    Questions you are likely to get

    “Can we tell the customer the top three SHAP features as the reason for decline?”

    Only through a reviewed mapping. The raw top pushes are relative to an average, can include correlated or proxy fields, and may not be actionable or even true in plain language ("tenure"). I'd map groups of features to approved reason wording, check the reasons are stable under small input changes, and agree the policy with compliance and legal. Which wording the CBUAE or SAMA expects is for compliance to confirm.

    “Why not just use the model's feature importance from XGBoost?”

    The built-in importance counts splits or gain, which is biased towards continuous and high-cardinality fields and tells you nothing about direction or about individual customers. Permutation importance on held-out data measures dependence for accuracy, and SHAP gives signed, per-customer contributions. I'd use permutation for the global ranking and SHAP for the local story.

    “SHAP says income is the top driver. Does that mean the bank should look at income more closely?”

    It means the model's output moves most with income, on this background. It does not say income is the truest predictor or that other information is redundant. If income is correlated with salary credits, the credit may be shared arbitrarily. I'd check the dependence plot, check correlated fields, and check that the signal is available at decision time.

    “The explanation changed after we retrained. Is the model broken?”

    Not necessarily. If correlated inputs are present, a retrain with the same accuracy can redistribute credit between them. Compare predictions on the same customers first. If they match, the explanation shift reflects the ambiguity, and grouping features gives a more stable story. If predictions also moved, then look at data and drift.

    “Is SHAP enough for regulators?”

    I wouldn't claim so without asking. It is a commonly accepted ingredient, but supervisors care about the model's development, validation and monitoring as a whole, and about fair and understandable outcomes for customers. I'd treat SHAP as one piece of evidence, backed by independent checks and a clear statement of what it can't show. I'd confirm local expectations with compliance.

    “Should we use a simpler model instead?”

    Worth testing, honestly. Fit a constrained scorecard or a monotonic GBM and compare out-of-time performance with intervals. If the gap is small, the simpler model is easier to defend and explain, and the explanation is exact rather than approximate. If the gap is large, the complex model earns its place and gets the full validation and explanation package.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A model's risk is f = 2·x₁ + 1·x₂ (log-odds), and the average customer has x₁ = 0 and x₂ = 0. A customer has x₁ = 1.5 and x₂ = −1. What are the SHAP values, assuming independent features?

    For a linear model with independent features, φ = weight × (value − average). So φ₁ = 2 × 1.5 = +3 and φ₂ = 1 × (−1) = −1. They sum to +2, which is exactly the customer's prediction minus the baseline.

    2. The baseline log-odds is −3.89 (about a 2% default probability) and the SHAP values for one customer sum to +1.2. What is her predicted probability, and what would be wrong with saying "her risk is 1.2 points higher"?

    Log-odds = −3.89 + 1.2 = −2.69. Probability = 1 ÷ (1 + e2.69) ≈ 1 ÷ 15.8 ≈ 6.3%. The push is +1.2 in log-odds, not percentage points; in probability terms it is a rise from 2% to 6.3%, about 4.3 points, and the same +1.2 would mean a different probability change from a different starting point.

    3. How many coalitions must exact Shapley values consider for 3 features? For 30?

    2n: 8 for three features, and 2³⁰ ≈ 1.07 billion for thirty. That is why TreeSHAP (which exploits tree structure) and sampling methods exist.

    4. A model uses only salary credits (weight 1, standardised). Balance has correlation 0.8 with salary credits. A customer sits at +2 SD on both. Under the conditional version, what does each feature get?

    Total push = 1 × 2 = 2. The conditional split gives balance φ = ½ρ·w·x₂ = 0.5 × 0.8 × 1 × 2 = 0.8, and salary credits 2 − 0.8 = 1.2. The interventional version gives salary 2 and balance 0, since the model never uses balance. The question differs: "what did the model use?" versus "what does this information tell us?".

    5. A customer is told "reduce your utilisation and your risk drops by the SHAP value of utilisation". Give two reasons this can be wrong.

    (a) Interactions: the effect of utilisation may depend on late payments or debt-to-income, so the "what if" change differs from the averaged-over-orders SHAP value. (b) Correlated inputs: reducing utilisation also changes other inputs (balances, spending), and the model responds to the whole movement. Also: the SHAP value is relative to the baseline population, not to her own current state, and it describes the model, not what the bank's future policy will do.

    6. Your colleague says nationality is not in the model, so SHAP shows it has no influence. What is wrong?

    Absence of a column is not absence of its effect: postcode, employer, branch or product choice may proxy for it. SHAP only attributes among the features present. Test outcomes and error rates by group directly, and check whether the included features predict the protected attribute (G2).

    7. Which is a better test for "does the model genuinely depend on this feature for accuracy": mean absolute SHAP or permutation importance on held-out data? Why?

    Permutation importance on held-out data is closer: it measures how much accuracy falls if the feature is scrambled. Mean absolute SHAP measures how much the feature moves predictions, which can be large for a feature that adds noise the model overfits. Use both, and be aware that both mislead with correlated pairs.

    8. In one sentence each: what does the baseline define, and what does the missing-feature choice (interventional or conditional) define?

    The baseline defines "typical": the reference every push is measured against. The missing-feature choice defines how an unknown input is filled in, either by ignoring its relationships (what the model does with it) or by respecting them (what its information implies), and decides who gets credit when features are correlated.
