    Explaining a Model: Global Versus Local, and What SHAP Does and Does Not Say

    One model, two questions: "what drives it?" and "why this customer?". How to read SHAP, and what it does not prove.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA global explanation says how the model behaves overall, a local one says why it gave this answer for this person, and SHAP splits one prediction among the inputs relative to a baseline, which says nothing about cause, fairness or what to change.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A declined customer asks: "Why me?" The head of model risk asks: "What is this model relying on?" The first needs a reason true for her (local). The second needs a picture of the whole model (global).

    Think of football: one match report does not describe the season, and the season table does not explain one result. Most confusion comes from answering one question with the other.

    • Global and local are two views of one model. Averaging local explanations gives the global one, not the other way round.
    • Every reason is relative to a baseline. "Utilisation pushed her risk up" means compared with a typical applicant, like "tall" needs a reference group.
    Hold onto thisAsk of any explanation chart: global or local, and relative to what baseline?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    F8.1 · Global versus local, and who needs which

    Interpretable means simple enough to read directly, like a scorecard or short tree. Explainable means a second method tells an approximate story about a complicated model. For high-stakes credit, an accurate model that needs no explanation is a real option.

    Global tools, such as permutation importance (shuffle a column on held-out data and see how far accuracy falls), serve model risk and validators. Local tools, such as SHAP and scorecard reason codes, serve case reviewers and customers.

    SHAP is not a counterfactual. "Which inputs contributed" differs from "which change would flip the decision". For advice, use a counterfactual method and check the change is possible.

    F8.2 · Where Shapley values come from

    Shapley values come from game theory: splitting a team's winnings fairly. In SHAP the players are features and the payout is this customer's prediction minus a baseline prediction.

    A feature's credit depends on who else is playing, so SHAP averages its added effect over every order in which features could be revealed. In practice the credits add up exactly to prediction minus baseline, and an unused feature gets zero.

    • You choose the baseline. Change the background set (all customers, approved only, last year) and every value changes, because "typical" changed.
    • Values are in log-odds, where effects add. Probabilities do not: the same push moves a customer near 50% far more than one near 1%.

    The versions you will meet: Kernel, Tree, and which "missing"

    TreeSHAP is exact and fast for XGBoost-style models and is the usual bank default. KernelSHAP works on any model but is slow and noisy.

    The other choice is how to "remove" a feature. Interventional fills it from random background rows: true to the model, but may test impossible combinations. Conditional fills it realistically given the known features: true to the data, so credit flows to features merely correlated with those the model uses (Simulation 3).

    What SHAP does not mean

    SHAP supports one claim: relative to the reference population, this output is higher mainly because of A and B, offset by C. It does not support:

    • Causation or advice. The model learned correlations. "Utilisation contributed +0.6" is not "cut utilisation and the score falls by 0.6".
    • A ranking of correlated features. The model may use either, and the choice can flip on retraining at identical accuracy.
    • Fairness. A protected attribute can be absent from the chart yet enter through proxies such as postcode.
    • Validation. It describes a model; it does not show the model is right.

    With strongly correlated features, do not read the split between them. Group them, drop one, or report their combined push.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Causes and recipes read from SHAP bars

      A top driver is called a cause, or a bar is quoted as advice.

      Spot itWords like "drives" or "leads to". Recompute with the feature actually changed.
    • Ranking correlated features

      "Salary credits is first, balance fifth", then the order reverses after retraining.

      Spot itCheck correlations; group or drop one of the pair.
    • Unstated baseline or units

      Pushes are from "the average" of an unnamed population, or log-odds are read as percentage points.

      Spot itAsk for the background set and the axis label.
    • Global conclusions from a few local cases

      Three well-chosen waterfalls are taken to describe the model.

      Spot itAsk how cases were chosen; ask for a summary plot over a representative sample.
    • Absence from the chart read as absence of bias

      "Nationality is not a feature, so it isn't used." Proxies carry it.

      Spot itTest outcomes by group directly (G2).
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. SHAP values are +3 for debt-to-income and −1 for tenure, baseline −3 log-odds. What do they say?

    Relative to a typical applicant, debt-to-income pushed risk up a lot and tenure pulled it down a little. They sum to +2, so the prediction is −1 log-odds.

    2. Why is "her risk is 1.2 points higher" wrong for a SHAP sum of +1.2?

    The +1.2 is in log-odds, not percentage points. The same push gives a different probability change from a different starting point.

    3. A model uses only salary credits, yet one SHAP method credits balance. Is that wrong?

    No. The conditional method credits information balance carries about salary, while the interventional method gives it zero. They answer different questions.

    4. A customer is told "reduce utilisation and your risk drops by its SHAP value". Why might that fail?

    Interactions mean the real effect depends on other inputs, and correlated inputs move together when utilisation changes.

    5. Which better shows the model depends on a feature for accuracy: permutation importance or mean absolute SHAP?

    Permutation importance, because it measures the accuracy lost. Mean absolute SHAP only shows how much predictions move.
