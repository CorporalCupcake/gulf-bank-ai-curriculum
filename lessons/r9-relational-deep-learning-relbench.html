    Learning on Bank Databases: Relational Deep Learning and Tabular Foundation Models

    Why the one-row-per-customer table throws away most of what a bank knows, how a database becomes a graph a neural network can read, and what it means for a pretrained model to "learn" a small table in a single forward pass.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRelational deep learning trains a network directly on linked tables so it finds the signals that hand-built joins and aggregates miss, while tabular foundation models bring a learned prior to small flat tables; both are strong research results that you should treat as challengers to a tuned gradient-boosting baseline, not replacements, until they win on your data with honest time-ordered tests.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Bank data is not one table. It is customers, accounts, transactions, merchants, devices and complaints, joined by keys.

    The usual practice flattens it with SQL into one row per customer: "transactions in 30 days", "average amount", "distinct merchants" (feature engineering, F3.4). It works, but it is lossy compression: a human guessed in advance what matters. It is like summarising a football match as the final score; the passing patterns are gone.

    Take mule accounts. A new account looks ordinary alone. What gives it away is that its senders were flagged last month and its money leaves to accounts sharing one device. Every clue is one or two joins away. A flat row holds "number of counterparties", not "how risky are my counterparties' counterparties", unless someone builds that column.

    Two ideas, one themeRelational deep learning (R9.1): do not flatten. Treat the database as a graph and let a neural network learn what to aggregate. Tabular foundation models (R9.2): for a flat table, do not train from scratch. Start from a model pretrained on millions of synthetic tables. Both swap hand-made design for learned design. Both are early, with honest limits.
    • Relational deep learning targets the biggest project cost: weeks of feature engineering across joined tables, repeated per task. Promising results, young tooling, little regulated-bank track record.
    • Tabular foundation models target small, valuable datasets: a few hundred confirmed AML cases, a new product with 2,000 customers. Boosting has little to learn from there and a pretrained prior adds value. You can try this within 90 days.

    First, the scale. The simulation builds a toy bank and asks: from one customer, how many rows can influence the prediction at one, two, three hops?

    Part 2

    How it really works

    From database to graph (R9.1)

    The core move of relational deep learning (Fey et al., 2023) is simple: a relational database already is a graph.

    Customerone node per row. Features: age band, segment, tenure, nationality group. Linked to its accounts through customer_id.
    Accountone node per row. Features: type, open date, limit. Links to its owner and every transaction sent or received.
    Transactionone node per row, with a timestamp. Features: amount, channel, currency. Links to its account and a merchant or payee account.
    Merchantone node per row. Features: category code, country. Links to all its transactions.

    Each row is a node, each primary-key to foreign-key link an edge, and columns become input features encoded by type. The graph is heterogeneous (several node and edge types, each with its own encoder) and temporal (a transaction exists only from its timestamp).

    A task is a query on the graph: "for customer C at time T, will they churn in 90 days?" or "will this account be confirmed a mule within 30 days?". The model sees a subgraph around the target built from rows no later than T. This is the point-in-time rule from F3.1: reaching a row that did not exist yet means learning from the future.

    Size is the problem. A customer has two accounts, each with 4,000 transactions, each touching a merchant with 90,000 transactions from others. By hop three you have touched much of the database. So the sampler keeps at most K neighbours per node per hop, usually the most recent. Try it.

    Message passing: what the network does with the subgraph

    A graph neural network (GNN) runs rounds of message passing, like rumours spreading through an office. Each node starts with a vector built from its own columns. Each round, it collects its neighbours' vectors, combines them (sum, mean, attention), mixes in its own, and passes the result through a small network. After L rounds, a node's vector summarises its L-hop neighbourhood.

    hv(l+1)=f(hv(l),AGG{hu(l):u∈N(v,t)})

    hv is node v's vector at round l. N(v, t) is its neighbours existing at time t. AGG is order-independent, because a database has no natural row order. f is a learned network. The point: the loss teaches the aggregation, where a data scientist would hand-write "sum of amounts in 30 days".

    The target node's final vector feeds a classifier or regressor, trained end to end. RelBench setups usually use a heterogeneous GraphSAGE-type GNN with temporal encoding so it knows each neighbour's age. Newer work uses graph transformers that attend across the sampled neighbourhood (F5).

    Mental model: a one-hop aggregation is a SQL GROUP BY on a foreign key (F3.3), but the network picks the aggregations and stacks them. Two rounds can express "average risk of my counterparties' counterparties", a feature almost nobody hand-builds because it needs a self-join of a self-join.

    When links carry the signal

    Following links helps exactly when the label depends on information in other rows. Analogy: judging a student from their own mock score versus also seeing their study group's scores. The simulation gives each customer a hidden risk r and shows a noisy reading x = r + noise. Fraud chance blends the customer's own risk, their counterparties' average risk and those counterparties' counterparties' risk; you set the blend. Three scorers compete: own row only, one-hop pooling, two-hop pooling.

    Three takeaways. Averaging noisy neighbours cleans the signal: one counterparty is weak, the mean of six is clear. The gain exists only when the label depends on the network; otherwise flattening loses nothing and a relational model only adds cost. Depth pays only if the signal really sits two hops out. So expect gains in fraud rings and AML networks, and little for churn driven by one customer's own behaviour. How to read the AUCs: a gap above about 0.02 between scorers is real, below that is noise, and "best possible" is the ceiling given noisy observations.

    RelBench: how this is measured

    RelBench (Robinson et al., 2024) is the standard benchmark: real relational databases (e-commerce, forums, sports, clinical trials and others) with defined tasks, fixed forward-in-time splits and standard metrics. From memory: seven databases, about 30 tasks and over 100 million rows at launch; RelBench v2 (2026) adds more. Tasks are queries at a point in time and splits run forward, the two habits this lesson keeps returning to.

    Headline result: a GNN-based relational model matched or beat gradient boosting on expert hand-built features on most classification tasks. The authors report about 12 hours and several hundred lines of code per task for the data scientist, against minutes of configuration (from memory; check the paper before quoting). Two caveats belong to the result:

    • Baselines are only as good as their tuning. A benchmark cannot show how hard a bank's engineers would push the flat model, and a 2026 look at RelBench protocols questions hyperparameter selection in some comparisons. That is a reproducibility concern, not a refutation. It is why you test on your own data.
    • The databases are not banks. They share the shape (many tables, timestamps, keys) but not the regulation, label delay or card-table scale.

    Tabular foundation models: learning in a forward pass (R9.2)

    Normally you fit a model on your rows: gradients, epochs, tuning. A tabular foundation model such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; Nature, 2025) skips that. You pass it your training rows and the test rows together, and one forward pass returns the test predictions. Nothing is fitted on your data. This is in-context learning, the same mechanism as few-shot prompting of a language model (see R6).

    Analogy: an experienced doctor who has seen thousands of patients can make a decent call from five of yours, without going back to medical school. The model was pretrained once on millions of synthetic tables, each sampled from a random data-generating process (random causal graphs, smooth functions, tree-like splits, noise, missing values). A transformer saw some labelled rows and had to predict held-out ones. Its weights now hold a general procedure for "given a few labelled rows from an unknown process, predict new ones". This is a prior-data fitted network (Müller et al., 2022): approximate Bayesian inference by training on the prior.

    p(y∣x,D)=∫p(y∣x,φ)p(φ∣D)dφ

    D is your labelled table, x a new row, φ a hypothesis about how the world works. The prediction averages every hypothesis, weighted by how well it explains your data (F1.4; Bayesian methods in F9). The integral is intractable, so the network is trained to output the answer directly. Its quality depends on whether the pretraining prior over φ resembles your real tables.

    That last sentence is the whole story. With scarce data, a good prior is worth many rows. With abundant data, the data overrules the prior and a tuned boosted model catches up or wins. The simulation shows this with a simple stand-in.

    A real tabular foundation model tries to be the green line in both worlds by making the pretraining prior very broad: smooth functions, interactions, tree-like thresholds, varied noise, missing values, useless columns. Versions to know:

    • TabPFN v1 (2022–23): about 1,000 rows, 100 numeric features, 10 classes. Beat tuned baselines on tiny data; too small for real use.
    • TabPFN v2 (Nature, January 2025): classification and regression, missing values, categoricals, about 10,000 rows and 500 features. Reported to beat heavily tuned boosted trees on small to medium data, in seconds instead of hours of tuning.
    • 2025–26 successors: TabPFN-2.5 (about 50,000 rows), open models such as TabICL and TabICLv2, further TabPFN releases. Each reports state of the art on its own chosen benchmarks, so read the claims with care (see Part 3).

    CARTE: when the columns have meaning

    TabPFN treats column 3 as just column 3. Bank columns have names (merchant_category, emirate, a free-text purpose), and tables from different systems rarely share a schema. CARTE (Kim, Grinsztajn and Varoquaux, ICML 2024) turns each row into a small star-shaped graph: column names label the edges, cell values are the nodes, both embedded with a language model, then a graph-attention network. Pretrained on the YAGO knowledge base, it can know that "Dubai" and "Abu Dhabi" are similar, or that two differently named columns mean the same. Use it for string-heavy columns, mismatched schemas and joining a small internal table to outside knowledge.

    Why boosted trees are so hard to dislodge (Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux, 2022): tables are irregular, many features are uninformative, and the target is rarely a smooth function, which clashes with the smooth bias of standard neural nets. Tabular foundation models bake that irregularity into the pretraining prior instead of fighting it on your small dataset.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Time leakage through the join

      The sampler reaches a transaction, complaint or label dated after the prediction time. AUC looks superb, live results do not.

      Spot itAsk how neighbours are time-filtered at every hop, and for results on a strictly forward test period. A suspicious jump over the flat baseline? Look here first.
    • Random splits on a benchmark

      "Beats XGBoost" on randomly split data. Real use is forward in time, where gaps shrink or reverse.

      Spot itCheck the split in the paper or notebook. Re-run on your data with a temporal split.
    • A weak baseline

      The rival is untuned boosting, default parameters or features nobody iterated on.

      Spot itAsk who tuned the baseline and for how long. A fair test gives it equal effort plus the obvious graph features.
    • Using the model outside its envelope

      A tabular foundation model run above its row or column limits, or on high-cardinality categoricals it handles poorly, with the degradation unnoticed.

      Spot itCheck documented limits against your table. Compare on a held-out slice against a tuned tree model.
    • Believing the prior covers your world

      Simulation 3 in miniature: a mismatched prior gives confident, wrong predictions that improve more slowly with rows than a trained model's.

      Spot itPlot a learning curve (accuracy against training size). If the foundation model's edge vanishes by a few thousand rows, you are in boosted-tree territory.
    • Paying for complexity the data does not need

      A relational pipeline built when the label depends only on the customer's own row. Simulation 2: the scorers tie.

      Spot itAdd a few cheap graph features to the flat model first. If they add nothing, the relational model will not either.
    • Opaque decisions and adverse-action reasons

      A flagged account or declined customer cannot be explained beyond "the subgraph looked risky". Customer-facing decisions and validators both ask for reasons.

      Spot itAsk what the explanation looks like for one decision and who validated it. A vendor assurance alone is unproven.
    • Data leaving the perimeter

      A hosted relational model needs linked customer data in someone else's cloud. Residency rules for UAE and Saudi banks are not a footnote. I have not verified current rules for a specific regulator; ask compliance.

      Spot itAsk where data and model run, and whether an on-premises or open-weight option exists. Check licence terms: some pretrained tabular models restrict commercial use.
    • Quoting vendor benchmarks as fact

      "Beats supervised models by 5%" from the company selling the model, on selected tasks.

      Spot itLook for independent replication or test on your own data before repeating the number.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. How does a relational database become a graph, and what does a "prediction task" look like?

    Each row is a node, each foreign-key link an edge, columns are node features. A task is a query at a point in time, such as "will customer C churn in the 90 days after T". The model sees C's neighbourhood built from rows dated no later than T.

    2. Why must neighbours be sampled, and why the most recent ones?

    Fan-out explodes: accounts have thousands of transactions, merchants more, and by three hops you cover much of the database. Capping neighbours keeps subgraphs small. Recent ones are usually most relevant, and anything dated after the prediction time must be excluded anyway.

    3. In Simulation 2 the own-row, one-hop and two-hop scorers all tie. What must be true of the fraud signal?

    It sits entirely in the customer's own row (both network sliders at zero). No join adds information, so a relational model is wasted effort. Run this check before building one.

    4. Why does pooling neighbours' signals help even though each neighbour is noisy?

    Averaging k independent noisy readings cuts noise variance by about k, so six counterparties give a much cleaner estimate than one. It helps only if the label really depends on that average.

    5. What does "learning in context" mean for TabPFN, and what was it trained on?

    You pass labelled and unlabelled rows as one input and one forward pass returns predictions, with no weight updates on your data. It was pretrained on millions of synthetic tables from a broad prior, so it approximates the Bayesian predictive distribution under that prior.

    6. In Simulation 3's interaction world, why does the right-prior learner plateau while the neighbours learner overtakes it?

    The prior covers only linear relationships, so a two-column interaction is outside what it can represent, however many rows it sees. The neighbours learner has no such limit and improves with data. A prior helps only if it covers your world, which is why real foundation models use very broad priors.

    7. A vendor shows their model beating XGBoost on a public benchmark. List three questions.

    How were the splits made (random or forward in time)? Who tuned the baseline, with what budget? Has anyone independent reproduced it, and does it hold on tables like ours in size, label delay and data type? Also ask for size limits and the licence.

    8. A table has 800 confirmed AML cases and 40 features. Would you try a tabular foundation model, and how would you test it?

    Yes, as a challenger: small and imbalanced is its home ground, and 40 features is within limits. Hold out the most recent period, give a boosted model a real tuning budget, compare precision at review capacity with bootstrap intervals (see imbalanced data), and plot accuracy against training size. With a few hundred positives, expect wide intervals and say so.
