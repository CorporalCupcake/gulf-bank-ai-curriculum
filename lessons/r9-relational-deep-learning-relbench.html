    Learning on Bank Databases: Relational Deep Learning and Tabular Foundation Models

    Why the one-row-per-customer table throws away most of what a bank knows, how a database becomes a graph a neural network can read, and what it means for a pretrained model to "learn" a small table in a single forward pass.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceRelational deep learning trains a network directly on linked tables so it finds the signals that hand-built joins and aggregates miss, while tabular foundation models bring a learned prior to small flat tables; both are strong research results that you should treat as challengers to a tuned gradient-boosting baseline, not replacements, until they win on your data with honest time-ordered tests.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Open the data behind any bank model and you do not find one table. You find customers, accounts, transactions, merchants, devices, products and complaints, joined by keys. A customer has accounts, an account has thousands of transactions, each transaction touches a merchant or another account.

    Machine learning on this data has a ritual. Someone writes SQL to flatten it into one row per prediction target: one row per customer, with columns like "number of transactions in 30 days", "average amount", "distinct merchants". That is feature engineering (F3.4), and it works. It is also a lossy compression, done by a human, in advance, guessing what will matter. Whatever the human did not think to aggregate is gone: the second-order patterns, the neighbourhood of a transfer, the timing between events.

    Consider mule-account detection. A new account looks ordinary on its own: small deposits, a few cash-outs. What gives it away is that the senders are accounts that were themselves flagged last month, and the money leaves to a small set of accounts that share a device. Every clue is one or two joins away. A flat row can hold "number of counterparties" but not "how risky are my counterparties' counterparties", unless somebody builds that column.

    Two ideas, one themeRelational deep learning (R9.1) says: do not flatten. Treat the database as a graph and let a neural network learn what to aggregate. Tabular foundation models (R9.2) say: for a flat table, do not train from scratch. Start from a model pretrained on millions of synthetic tables, so it already knows what tables tend to look like. Both replace hand-made design with learned design. Both are early, and both have honest limits.

    Where each sits in your working life as a data scientist at a bank:

    • Relational deep learning speaks to the biggest cost in most projects: weeks of feature engineering across joined tables, repeated for every new prediction task. If one learned model could skip that, it changes the economics of using bank data. It is the "edge" idea: promising results, a young tool chain, little regulated-bank track record.
    • Tabular foundation models speak to a different pain: the small, valuable dataset. A few hundred confirmed AML cases, a new product with 2,000 customers, a pilot in a new market. Here gradient boosting has little to learn from and a pretrained prior can add real value. This one you can try within 90 days.

    First, the scale of the problem. The simulation below builds a toy bank database and asks: starting from one customer, how many rows of the database can influence the prediction when we follow links one, two, three hops out?

    Part 2

    How it really works

    From database to graph (R9.1)

    The central move of relational deep learning (Fey et al., 2023) is almost embarrassingly simple. A relational database already is a graph:

    Customerone node per row. Features: age band, segment, tenure, nationality group. Linked to its accounts through customer_id.
    Accountone node per row. Features: type, open date, limit. Links to its owner and to every transaction it sent or received.
    Transactionone node per row, with a timestamp. Features: amount, channel, currency. Links to its account and to a merchant or a payee account.
    Merchantone node per row. Features: category code, country. Links to all its transactions.

    Each row becomes a node. Each primary-key to foreign-key link becomes an edge. Columns become that node's input features, encoded by type (numbers, categories, timestamps, text embeddings). The graph is heterogeneous: several node types and edge types, each with its own encoder. And it is temporal: a transaction node exists only from its timestamp onwards.

    A prediction task is then written as a query against this graph: "for customer C at time T, will they churn in the next 90 days?" or "will this account be confirmed as a mule within 30 days?". The model sees a subgraph around the target node, built from rows no later than T. That time rule is the same point-in-time discipline from F3.1: the sampler must never reach a row that did not exist yet, or the model learns from the future.

    The next problem is size. Following links fans out fast. A customer has two accounts; each account has 4,000 transactions; each transaction touches a merchant; each merchant has 90,000 transactions from other customers. By hop three you have touched much of the database. So the sampler takes at most K neighbours per node per hop, usually the most recent ones. Try it.

    Message passing: what the network does with the subgraph

    Given the sampled subgraph, a graph neural network (GNN) runs a few rounds of message passing. Every node starts with a vector built from its own columns. In each round, each node collects the vectors of its neighbours, combines them (sum, mean, attention), mixes the result with its own vector, and passes it through a small network. After L rounds a node's vector summarises its L-hop neighbourhood.

    hv(l+1)=f(hv(l),AGG{hu(l):u∈N(v,t)})

    hv is node v's vector at round l. N(v, t) is the set of v's neighbours that exist at time t (the time rule above). AGG is a permutation-invariant combiner: it cannot depend on the order of neighbours, because a database has no natural order. f is a learned network. The key point: the aggregation is learned from the loss, where a data scientist would have written "sum of amounts in 30 days" by hand.

    The final vector of the target node feeds a prediction head (a classifier, a regressor), trained end to end on the labels. In RelBench-style setups the GNN is usually a heterogeneous GraphSAGE-type network, with a temporal encoding so the model knows how old each neighbour is. Newer work replaces the GNN with a graph transformer that lets nodes attend across a sampled neighbourhood (see the attention lesson, F5, for the mechanism).

    Two connections to know. First, this is the neural version of what SQL does with GROUP BY and JOIN (F3.3): a one-hop aggregation is a group-by on a foreign key. The difference is that the network chooses the aggregations and can stack them. Second, a GNN with two rounds can express "average risk of my counterparties' counterparties", a feature almost nobody hand-builds because it needs a self-join of a self-join.

    When links carry the signal

    When does following links help? Exactly when the label depends on information that sits in other rows. The next simulation builds the cleanest version of that. Each customer has a hidden risk r. We observe a noisy version of it, x = r + noise. Each customer sends money to k random counterparties. The chance of fraud depends on a blend of the customer's own risk, the average risk of the counterparties, and the average risk of their counterparties. You choose that blend. Three scorers compete: one that sees only the customer's own row, one that also pools the counterparties' observed signals, and one that pools two hops.

    Three lessons from the picture. The gain comes from averaging noisy neighbours: one counterparty's signal is weak, the mean of six is much cleaner. The gain exists only when the label depends on the network; when it does not, flattening loses nothing and a relational model buys you nothing but cost. And the gain compounds with depth only if the signal really sits two hops out. That is why relational models do well in fraud rings and AML networks, and add little for a churn model driven by one customer's own behaviour.

    RelBench: how this is measured

    To compare methods fairly, Robinson et al. (2024) released RelBench: a set of real relational databases (e-commerce, forums, sports, clinical trials, an Amazon-style product graph and others), with defined prediction tasks, fixed temporal train/validation/test splits, and standard metrics. From memory: seven databases, about 30 tasks and over 100 million rows in the first release; RelBench v2 (2026) extends it to more databases and tasks. The key design choices are the ones this lesson keeps returning to: tasks are written as queries at a point in time, and splits are forward in time.

    The headline result of the first paper: a GNN-based relational model matched or beat a strong baseline, a gradient-boosted model on features hand-engineered by an experienced data scientist, on most classification tasks, and did so with a fraction of the human effort. The authors report the data scientist spent on the order of 12 hours and several hundred lines of code per task, against minutes of configuration for the relational model (figures from memory; check the paper before quoting them). Treat two caveats as part of the result:

    • Baselines are only as good as their tuning. Public benchmarks cannot capture how hard a bank's engineers would push the flat model. A second look at RelBench protocols in 2026 has raised questions about hyperparameter selection in some published comparisons. That is a reproducibility concern, not a refutation, but it is why you test on your own data.
    • The benchmark databases are not banks. They share the shape (many tables, timestamps, foreign keys) but not the regulation, the label delay, or the scale of a card-transaction table.

    Tabular foundation models: learning in a forward pass (R9.2)

    Now the flat table, and a different idea. Normally you fit a model on your training rows: gradients, epochs, tuning. A tabular foundation model such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; Nature, 2025) works differently. You hand it your training rows and the test rows together, as its input. One forward pass returns the predictions for the test rows. There is no fitting on your data at all. The "learning" is in-context, the same mechanism as few-shot prompting of a language model (see R6 for why in-context learning can behave like learning).

    How can that work? The model was pretrained, once, on a huge number of synthetic tables, millions of them. Each synthetic table is generated by sampling a random data-generating process: random causal graphs, random mixtures of smooth functions and tree-like splits, random noise and missing values. For each table, a transformer is shown some rows with labels and must predict the labels of held-out rows. After millions of such tasks it has learned, in its weights, a general-purpose procedure for "given a few labelled rows from an unknown process, predict new ones". This is the idea of a prior-data fitted network (Müller et al., 2022): approximate Bayesian inference by training on the prior.

    p(y∣x,D)=∫p(y∣x,φ)p(φ∣D)dφ

    D is your labelled training table, x a new row, φ a possible "way the world works" (a hypothesis about the data-generating process). The right-hand side is the Bayesian predictive distribution: average the predictions of every hypothesis, weighting each by how well it explains your data (F1.4 and the Bayesian-methods topic in F9). This integral is intractable for rich hypotheses. The pretrained network is trained to output its answer directly. Its quality depends on whether the pretraining prior over φ resembles your real tables.

    That last sentence is the whole story, and it also explains the strengths and limits. When data is scarce, a good prior is worth a lot of rows. When data is abundant, the data overrules the prior and a tuned boosted model catches up or wins. The next simulation shows this with a deliberately simple stand-in.

    A real tabular foundation model tries to be the green line in both worlds, by making the pretraining prior very broad: smooth functions, interactions, tree-like thresholds, different noise levels, missing values, uninformative columns. The progression to know:

    • TabPFN v1 (2022–23): small only, about 1,000 training rows, 100 numeric features, 10 classes. Beat tuned baselines on small data, but a ceiling for real use.
    • TabPFN v2 (Nature, January 2025): classification and regression, missing values and categorical columns handled, scaled to roughly 10,000 rows and 500 features, and reported to beat extensively tuned boosted-tree baselines on small to medium datasets, in seconds rather than hours of tuning.
    • 2025–26 successors: TabPFN-2.5 (reported scale of about 50,000 rows), open models such as TabICL and TabICLv2 that use a different architecture for larger tables, and further TabPFN releases. Each paper reports state-of-the-art on its chosen benchmarks (Part 4 explains why to read those with care).

    CARTE: when the columns have meaning

    TabPFN treats a table's columns as anonymous numbers: column 3 is just column 3. But a bank's tables have names: merchant_category, nationality, emirate, a free-text purpose field. And two tables from different systems rarely share a schema. CARTE (Kim, Grinsztajn and Varoquaux, ICML 2024) tackles that. It turns each row into a small star-shaped graph whose edges are labelled with the column name and whose nodes are the cell values, embeds both names and values with a language model, and applies a graph-attention network. It is pretrained on a large knowledge base (YAGO) so that it brings world knowledge: it can know that "Dubai" and "Abu Dhabi" are similar kinds of thing, or that two differently named columns mean the same. It is aimed at the awkward cases: string-heavy columns, mismatched schemas, joining a small internal table to outside knowledge.

    ApproachWhat it needsStrong whenWeak when

    Read the reason boosted trees have been so hard to dislodge in Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022): tables are irregular, features are often uninformative, and the target is rarely a smooth function of the inputs, a mismatch with the smooth bias of standard neural nets. Tabular foundation models are interesting precisely because they bake that kind of tabular irregularity into the pretraining prior instead of fighting it in your small dataset.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These two ideas sit where data engineering, representation learning and model risk meet.

    F3.3 · F3.4 · SQL and feature engineeringWhat RDL tries to automate

    The flattening you do by hand, joins, windows and aggregates, is exactly the step a relational model learns. Knowing the manual version tells you what the learned version must beat. See also SQL thinking.

    F3.1 · Leakage and point-in-timeThe time rule is not optional

    Relational samplers reach across many tables. If one reaches a row dated after the prediction time, the model sees the future. The neighbourhood function must filter by time at every hop.

    F4.2 · Trees and boostingThe incumbent to beat

    Both new approaches are judged against tuned boosted trees. Know why trees are strong on tables: they handle irregular, mixed-type features without scaling.

    F4.3 · Cross-validation and temporal splitsForward-in-time evaluation

    The only honest way to compare these models. Foundation-model benchmarks often use random splits, which flatter every method.

    F9 · Graph analytics and graph MLThe foundations of GNNs

    Graph ML basics, node embeddings and message passing, come first. RDL is graph ML applied to a database schema.

    F5.4 · Self-supervised and contrastive learningPretraining without labels

    Foundation models generalise by pretraining on a broad task. RDL work is also exploring self-supervised pretraining across databases.

    R9.3 · R9.4 · Time-series and graph foundation modelsThe same pretrain-once idea

    Chronos and TimesFM apply the foundation-model recipe to series; graph foundation models apply it to networks. Same logic, different data shape.

    B1 · Fraud detectionWhere networks carry signal

    Mule rings, shared devices and merchant compromise are relational patterns. See also transaction monitoring and entity resolution, which decides whether two rows are the same person and so defines the edges.

    P1.3 · Feature storesServing is the hard part

    A sampled-subgraph model needs neighbours at scoring time. Real-time scoring of a graph query within milliseconds is an engineering project, not a library call.

    F8 · Explanations · G3 · ValidationThe explainability gap

    Why was this account flagged? A boosted model gives SHAP values per column. A GNN gives a score from a subgraph. Validators will ask, see independent validation.

    R8.1 · Conformal predictionUncertainty on new models

    Pretrained models can be confidently wrong on unusual tables. Conformal wrappers give coverage guarantees without trusting the model's own confidence.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Mule and fraud-ring detection. The textbook relational problem. Accounts, devices, beneficiaries and IP addresses form a graph; shared attributes link bad actors.
    • AML. Typologies like layering and structuring are patterns over many accounts and time. Confirmed cases are scarce (see imbalanced data), which is also where a tabular foundation model could help a flat component.
    • Cross-sell and churn across products. Customer, accounts, cards, loans, complaints and branch visits. Many prediction tasks, one database: the case for a reusable relational model is strongest here.
    • Small-sample credit. A new SME product or a new market with a few thousand applications and a short history. A pretrained prior is attractive, but the validation and documentation burden is the same.
    • Arabic and mixed-language fields. Names, purposes of transfer and addresses in Arabic and English. CARTE-style language embeddings can help, but test per language: pretraining knowledge is thinner for Arabic than English.

    Where the field is now

    Both areas moved fast between 2023 and 2026. The honest summary: the research results are strong and still mostly from the groups that built the tools, and independent production reports from regulated banks are thin.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These show up in vendor decks and in AI-written analyses. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Time leakage through the join

      The neighbour sampler reaches a transaction, complaint or label dated after the prediction time. The AUC is excellent and the live result is not.

      Spot itAsk how the neighbourhood is filtered by time at every hop, and for the result on a strictly forward test period. Suspicious jump over the flat baseline? Look here first.
    • Random splits on a benchmark

      A claim that a foundation model "beats XGBoost" on datasets split at random. Real use is forward in time, where gaps shrink or reverse.

      Spot itCheck the split in the paper or the vendor notebook. Re-run on your data with a temporal split.
    • A weak baseline

      The new model is compared with untuned boosting, default parameters, or features nobody iterated on.

      Spot itAsk who tuned the baseline and for how long. A fair test gives the baseline the same effort, and adds the obvious graph features.
    • Using the model outside its envelope

      A tabular foundation model run on a table far above its supported rows or columns, or with high-cardinality categorical columns it handles poorly, with the degradation unnoticed.

      Spot itCheck the documented limits against your table. Compare on a held-out slice against a tuned tree model.
    • Believing the prior covers your world

      Simulation 3 in miniature: a pretrained prior that is a poor match for your data gives confident, wrong predictions that do not improve as fast as a trained model's with more rows.

      Spot itPlot a learning curve: accuracy against training size. If the foundation model's advantage vanishes by a few thousand rows, you are in boosted-tree territory.
    • Paying for complexity the data does not need

      A relational pipeline built when the label depends only on the customer's own row. Simulation 2: the three scorers tie.

      Spot itFirst add a few cheap graph features to the flat model. If they add nothing, the relational model will not either.
    • Opaque decisions and adverse-action reasons

      A flagged account or declined customer cannot be explained beyond "the subgraph looked risky". Customer-facing decisions and model validation both ask for reasons.

      Spot itAsk what the explanation looks like for one decision, and who has validated it. If the answer is a vendor assurance, treat it as unproven.
    • Data leaving the perimeter

      A hosted relational model needs a copy of linked customer data in someone else's cloud. Sensitive data and residency rules for UAE and Saudi banks are not a footnote. I have not verified the current rules for a specific regulator; ask compliance.

      Spot itAsk where the data and the model run, and whether an on-premises or open-weight option exists. Check the model's licence terms as well: some pretrained tabular models restrict commercial use.
    • Quoting vendor benchmarks as fact

      "Beats supervised models by 5%" from the company that sells the model, on selected tasks.

      Spot itLook for an independent replication or a test on your own data before repeating the number.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Our models today read one flat row per customer, and our team spends weeks building that row from many tables. New research lets a model learn directly from the linked tables, which should find patterns like mule rings that a flat row misses, and cut that build time. The evidence is promising but comes mostly from benchmarks and vendors. I'd run a small test on a task where links matter, against our current model, on future data, before any commitment.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd treat both as challengers to a tuned gradient-boosted baseline. For relational data, I'd first add graph features to the flat model, then try a temporal heterogeneous GNN with time-filtered neighbour sampling, and evaluate forward in time with the same tuning effort on both. For small tables, a TabPFN-class model is a cheap strong baseline and a good check on a tuned model, within its row and feature limits, with a learning curve to show where the advantage ends. Adoption depends on explanations, licence and data location.”
    “Can a model learn from the raw tables and skip feature engineering?”

    In research benchmarks, increasingly yes: relational deep learning matched or beat expert-engineered features on most tasks at far lower human effort. For us, two caveats. The benchmarks are not bank data, and our current features carry domain knowledge such as regulatory flags and label-delay handling. I'd test on one task where links matter and keep the feature-based model as the control.

    “What is a tabular foundation model, in plain words?”

    A model pretrained on millions of artificial tables so it already knows what typical tables look like. You give it your labelled rows and the rows to predict, and it answers in one pass, with no training on our data. It is strongest on small tables, where a good prior substitutes for missing data.

    “Will it beat XGBoost?”

    On small to medium tables, often yes or tie in published comparisons, with no tuning. On large tables, or with tight latency, boosted trees usually win on cost and maturity. It also depends on the split: I'd compare on forward-in-time data and give XGBoost a proper tuning budget. The cheap, useful move is to run both and see.

    “Can we use KumoRFM or a similar service on customer data?”

    That is a data governance decision before it is a modelling one. We would need to know where the data sits, whether an on-premises deployment is available, what our regulator and internal policy require for sharing linked customer records, and how the model would be validated. The vendor's reported accuracy gains are promising but unreplicated by independent parties, as far as I know.

    “How do you explain a GNN decision to a regulator?”

    With difficulty, which is a reason to start in non-customer-facing use. For alert prioritisation, an investigator can be shown the subgraph and the neighbours that drove the score. For adverse customer decisions, I'd keep an interpretable model in the loop or use the relational model to generate features, such as network risk scores, feeding a model we can explain.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. How does a relational database become a graph, and what does a "prediction task" look like?

    Each row is a node, each foreign-key link an edge, columns are node features. A task is a query at a point in time, for example "will customer C churn in the 90 days after T", and the model sees the neighbourhood of C built from rows dated no later than T.

    2. Why must neighbours be sampled, and why the most recent ones?

    Fan-out explodes: an account has thousands of transactions, a merchant has many more, and by three hops a customer's neighbourhood can cover much of the database. Capping neighbours per node keeps subgraphs small enough for a network. The recent ones are usually most relevant and respect the time rule, and any neighbour dated after the prediction time must be excluded.

    3. In Simulation 2 the own-row scorer, one-hop scorer and two-hop scorer all tie. What must be true of the fraud signal?

    It must sit entirely in the customer's own row (both network sliders at zero). Then no join adds information, and a relational model is wasted effort. This is the check to run before building one.

    4. Why does pooling neighbours' signals help even though each neighbour is noisy?

    Averaging k independent noisy measurements cuts the noise variance by about k. Six counterparties give a much cleaner estimate of their average risk than any one does. That is only useful if the label really depends on that average.

    5. What does "learning in context" mean for TabPFN, and what was it trained on?

    You supply labelled training rows and unlabelled test rows as one input, and a single forward pass returns predictions, with no weight updates on your data. It was pretrained on millions of synthetic tables drawn from a broad prior over data-generating processes, so it approximates the Bayesian predictive distribution under that prior.

    6. In Simulation 3's interaction world, why does the right-prior learner plateau while the neighbours learner overtakes it?

    The prior covers only linear relationships, so the interaction between two columns is outside what it can represent, however many rows it sees. The neighbours learner has no such restriction and improves with data. A prior is only helpful if it covers your world, which is why real foundation models use very broad priors.

    7. A vendor shows their model beating XGBoost on a public benchmark. List three questions.

    How were the splits made (random or forward in time)? Who tuned the baseline and with what budget? Has anyone independent reproduced it, and does it hold on tables like ours in size, label delay and data type? Also ask for the supported size limits and the licence.

    8. A table has 800 confirmed AML cases and 40 features. Would you try a tabular foundation model, and how would you test it?

    Yes, as a challenger: small and imbalanced is its home ground, and 40 features is well within limits. I'd hold out the most recent period, give a boosted model a real tuning budget, compare precision at review capacity with bootstrap intervals (see imbalanced data), and plot accuracy against training size. With a few hundred positive test cases, expect wide intervals and say so.
