    Reading and Steering a Model From Inside: Directions, Probes and Induction Heads

    How concepts sit along lines in a model's activations, why a good probe score proves less than it seems, how adding a vector steers behaviour, and the circuit that lets a model copy from its prompt.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceMany concepts are stored as directions you can read with a probe and nudge with a vector, but decodable is not used, steered is not controlled, and induction heads explain copying better than they explain all in-context learning.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank runs an open-weight language model to triage Arabic and English customer messages. At every layer the model holds an activation vector, a few thousand numbers, as its working memory. Can we read a concept from it and write to it without retraining?

    Often yes, because many concepts lie along a roughly straight line, like towns along a road. Reading means measuring position on the line (a linear probe); writing means moving along it (activation steering).

    Hold onto thisIs the concept really linear? Does the model use the direction, or can we merely decode it? Does nudging it change only what you wanted?
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R5.3 · Linear representations

    The linear representation hypothesis says concepts such as sentiment or truthfulness correspond to directions in activation space. Word vectors show it (king − man + woman lands near queen), and layers read activations through linear maps, so a direction is easy to extract.

    It is an approximation. Some features are circles, not lines (days of the week), and a direction mixes many neurons (superposition, R5.1).

    The concept direction is not the direction of greatest variation. The biggest axes are often mundane (position, language). The simplest estimate uses labels: the difference of class means. A trained probe tilts the line to avoid noisy axes and does slightly better.

    R5.3 · Probing: reading a concept

    A probe is a small classifier trained on frozen activations to predict a property of the input. Read an accuracy of 0.9 as "the information is present", nothing more. Three questions separate a finding from a mirage.

    1. Could it find this in noise? A probe with more dimensions than examples can fit random labels. The gap against that control, called selectivity, is what counts.
    2. Decodable, or used? Present is not relied on. Remove the direction and check whether behaviour changes.
    3. The concept, or a correlate? A fraud probe may be reading one word. Test in another language or phrasing.

    When you hear "we probed the model and found X", ask what the control was, how many examples against how many dimensions, and whether anyone intervened.

    R5.3 · Steering: writing a concept

    If a direction represents a concept, adding it to the activations pushes the model towards that concept. This is activation steering, part of representation engineering. A typical recipe averages the activation difference over many contrast pairs (behaviour present versus absent), then adds that vector, scaled by a strength you dial, while the model generates.

    Refusal in open chat models is mediated by a single direction; remove it and the model stops refusing. For a bank that cuts both ways: behaviour can be inspected, and anyone with the weights can edit safety behaviour out.

    Think of a radio dial: too little does nothing, too much gives distortion. Few contrast pairs give a noisy vector that drags side effects with it.

    R5.4 · Induction heads

    Given "Pay Al Noor Trading AED 500. Pay Al Ameen Exchange AED 500. Pay Al", a model picks a name from earlier in the prompt. The mechanism is the induction head: if the current token appeared before, predict what followed it last time.

    It takes two attention heads. A previous-token head writes "my predecessor was Al" into the representation at "Noor". A later induction head matches the current "Al" against that record and copies "Noor". They copy rather than understand: at position 8 below the rule gives the wrong answer.

    R5.4 · In-context learning

    In-context learning is a model improving at a task from examples in the prompt, with no weight updates. Induction heads explain token-level copying: repeated names, identifiers, boilerplate. Few-shot task learning relies on more. A few heads produce function vectors that encode "the task", and adding one to a zero-shot prompt makes the model do it. That is steering with the vector taken from the prompt.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Probe accuracy offered as proof the model "knows" something

      Decodable is not used.

      Spot itWas the direction removed and the behaviour re-measured?
    • No control task, too many dimensions

      4,096 inputs and 500 examples will fit nearly anything.

      Spot itAsk for shuffled-label results and test accuracy only.
    • Probe finds a surface cue, not the concept

      The "fraud" direction may fire on the word "unauthorised" alone.

      Spot itTest on paraphrases, other languages and neutral phrasing.
    • Steering shown on a few prompts, side effects ignored

      Tone can improve while accuracy or safety quietly drops.

      Spot itAsk for held-out prompts and your normal evaluation suite, on and off.
    • Treating a steering vector as a guardrail

      A vector is statistical, and anyone with the weights can remove it.

      Spot itAsk what checks sit outside the model.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is the direction of largest variance usually a poor concept direction?

    Variance measures spread, not class separation. The biggest axes are often mundane (position, language), so use labels or contrast pairs instead.

    2. A linear probe on 4,096 dimensions gets 99% training and 51% test accuracy on 400 examples. What happened?

    Overfitting: with more dimensions than examples a probe can fit almost any labelling. Use held-out accuracy and a shuffled-label control.

    3. A probe predicts "customer is a student" from a credit-model embedding at 90%. Does the model use student status?

    Not shown. It is decodable, perhaps via age or income. Remove the direction and see whether scores change.

    4. You steer with a formality vector at strength 6 and replies turn into repetitive nonsense. Why?

    The push has moved activations far outside what the model saw in training. There is a usable window of strength.

    5. What does the induction head wrongly predicting at position 8 of Simulation 4 tell you?

    Induction is a rule: "what followed this last time?". Copying is not checking.
