    Reading and Steering a Model From Inside: Directions, Probes and Induction Heads

    Why many concepts sit along straight lines in a model's activations, how a probe reads them and why a good probe score proves less than it seems, how adding a vector can steer behaviour (and break it), and the attention circuit that lets a model learn from its own prompt.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceInside a transformer many concepts are stored as directions, so you can read them with a simple linear probe and nudge them by adding a vector, but "decodable" is not "used", "steered" is not "controlled", and the best-understood piece of in-context learning, the induction head, explains copying far better than it explains everything the prompt can do.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank runs an open-weight language model on its own servers to triage Arabic and English customer messages. At every layer, for every token, the model holds an activation vector, perhaps four thousand numbers long: its working memory. Nobody designed what the numbers mean.

    Two questions follow. Can we read from it ("this is an angry complaint")? Can we write to it (make the model calmer, more formal) without retraining?

    Both rest on an empirical fact: many concepts lie along a roughly straight line. Picture one point per message in a space with thousands of dimensions. Angry messages sit a little further along some direction than calm ones, like towns further along a road. Reading means measuring position on the line (a linear probe); writing means moving along it (activation steering). Both are vector arithmetic.

    The second half covers one small circuit, the induction head: the mechanism by which a model sees "Al Noor Trading" once in the prompt and, on the next "Al", suggests "Noor".

    Hold onto thisAsk three things of every claim. Is the concept really linear? Does the model use the direction, or can we merely decode it? Does nudging it change the behaviour you wanted without damaging anything else? Each is a separate experiment.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R5.3 · Linear representations: the idea and why it is plausible

    The linear representation hypothesis says high-level concepts (sentiment, truthfulness, "is a place", a noun's gender) correspond to directions in activation space. Two things make it credible.

    • Word-vector arithmetic. In word embeddings, king − man + woman lands near queen (Mikolov et al., 2013): differences carry meaning that repeats across pairs (F5.6).
    • It suits transformers. The residual stream (F5.2) is a running sum that each layer reads through a linear map. A feature is easiest to read if it is a direction, because a dot product with a fixed vector extracts it cleanly.

    The sharper claim: a concept c has a direction vc such that activations move along it when the concept changes and little else moves. Park, Choe and Veitch (2023) made this precise using input pairs that differ only in the concept, and showed "orthogonal" needs a particular inner product, not the plain dot product. Three cautions:

    • Not every feature is a line. Engels et al. (2024) found multi-dimensional features, such as days of the week on a circle. One direction cannot represent "Tuesday".
    • Directions are not neurons. A direction mixes many coordinates, because models pack more features than dimensions (superposition, R5.1). Sparse autoencoders try to recover cleaner ones.
    • Linear is an approximation. A direction may work within a narrow set of inputs and drift across topics, languages or formats.

    A concept direction is not the direction of greatest variation. The biggest axes are often mundane (sentence position, a few huge "massive activation" dimensions, language), and the concept can live in a quiet one. Finding it needs labels or contrasts: pairs differing only in the concept. The simplest estimate is the difference of class means, the line between the two cloud centres. It is cheap, needs no training, and underlies many steering methods. Logistic regression tilts the line to ignore noisy directions and usually does a little better.

    R5.3 · Probing: reading a concept, and what a good score does and does not prove

    A probe is a small classifier trained on frozen activations to predict a property of the input (Alain and Bengio, 2016), like putting a thermometer into the model. A linear probe is a logistic regression:

    P(concept∣h)=σ(w·h+b)

    h is the activation vector at some layer and token position, w a learned direction (the probe), b a bias, σ the sigmoid. Train on labelled examples (angry or calm), test on held-out ones, report accuracy or AUC. If a probe at layer 20 works and one at layer 2 does not, the information became linearly available in between.

    Probing has found real structure: syntax, board state in a model trained only on Othello moves (Nanda et al., 2023), space and time coordinates in Llama-2 (Gurnee and Tegmark, 2023), and a true-versus-false direction (Marks and Tegmark, 2023). It is also easy to misuse. Read an accuracy of 0.9 as "the information is linearly present", nothing more; 0.5 on balanced classes is chance. Three questions separate a finding from a mirage.

    1. Could a probe find this in noise? Like a student who memorises answers, a probe with more dimensions than examples can fit random labels perfectly. Hewitt and Liang (2019) proposed control tasks: same inputs, random labels. The gap between real and control accuracy, selectivity, is what counts (Simulation 2).
    2. Decodable, or used? A probe shows information is present, not that the model relies on it. Amnesic probing (Elazar et al., 2021) removes the direction and checks whether behaviour changes. Steering and ablation turn a correlation into evidence of mechanism.
    3. The concept, or a correlate? A "fraud complaint" probe may be reading the word "unauthorised". Test where the cue and the concept come apart: another language, phrasing or domain.

    Keep the probe simple. The more expressive it is (a deep network, not a line), the more the result tells you about the probe, not the model.

    When you hear "we probed the model and found X", ask: what was the control, how many examples against how many dimensions, and was there an intervention? Belinkov (2022) is the standard review.

    R5.3 · Representation engineering and activation steering: writing a concept

    If a direction v represents a concept, adding it to the activations should push the model towards that concept, like nudging a thermostat setpoint. This is activation steering; the wider programme of reading and controlling internal representations is representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023). Two early methods set the pattern:

    • ActAdd (Turner et al., 2023): run two prompts such as "Love" and "Hate", subtract the activations at one layer, and add the scaled difference while generating.
    • Contrastive activation addition (Rimsky et al., 2023): average activation differences over hundreds of A/B pairs where one answer shows a behaviour (sycophancy, refusal) and the other does not, then add the average at every token position.
    h′=h+α·v^,v^=1k∑i=1k(hi+−hi−)

    h is the activation at the chosen layer, α the strength you dial, v̂ the average difference over k contrast pairs (h+ shows the behaviour, h− does not). Reading and writing use the same object: a probe asks "how far along v̂ is this?", a steer says "move it further along". Raising α from 2 to 4 doubles the push, not the behaviour.

    The strongest demonstration is refusal. Arditi et al. (2024) found that in 13 open chat models, refusal is mediated by a single direction: remove it and the model stops refusing; add it and the model refuses harmless requests. For a bank this cuts both ways. Internal behaviour can be located and inspected, and the safety behaviour of an open-weight model can be edited out by anyone with the weights.

    Steering fails in three ways, all visible in the simulation. Think of a radio dial: too little and you hear nothing, too much and you get distortion.

    • Noisy direction. With few contrast pairs, v̂ points partly the wrong way and moves other things too.
    • Strength window. Too small and nothing happens; too big and activations leave the region seen in training, so text turns incoherent or repetitive. The window differs by concept, layer and prompt.
    • Side effects. A direction that only approximates the concept drags correlated behaviours with it.

    Sparse autoencoders (R5.1) also produce feature directions that can be added or removed. Whether they steer better than plain difference-of-means vectors is contested; the evidence is weak that they do.

    R5.4 · Induction heads: how a transformer copies from its own prompt

    Take the prompt "Pay Al Noor Trading AED 500. Pay Al Ameen Exchange AED 500. Pay Al". Training data alone gives no reason to expect "Noor" or "Ameen", yet models pick one, because the names appeared earlier. Think of finding your place in a book by searching for the last phrase you read and looking at the word after it. The mechanism is the induction head (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022).

    The pattern is [A] [B] … [A] → predict [B]: if the current token was seen before, predict what followed it last time. It takes two attention heads (attention itself is in F5.5):

    1. A previous-token head in an earlier layer copies information about token j−1 into the representation of token j. The vector at "Noor" now records "my predecessor was Al".
    2. An induction head in a later layer forms a query from the current token ("I am Al"), compares it with the keys at earlier positions, matches the key at "Noor", attends there and copies "Noor" into the output.

    Because the second head reads what the first wrote, induction needs at least two attention layers.

    Two findings make induction heads more than a curiosity.

    • They appear suddenly in training. Olsson et al. (2022) saw a short "phase change" early on, a bump in the loss curve, exactly when the model starts using earlier context. Before it the model sees only local statistics. R6.1 shows other sudden shifts like this.
    • They go beyond exact copying. Fuzzy induction heads match approximate meaning ("ID card" earlier can serve "identity document" later), and translation-like copying has been reported.

    R5.4 · In-context learning: how much do induction heads explain?

    In-context learning (ICL) is a model improving at a task from examples in the prompt, with no weight updates. Show it three labelled complaints and it labels the fourth. Olsson et al. proposed induction heads as a major mechanism, with evidence strongest in small models and indirect in large ones. The current picture has three layers:

    • Induction explains token-level copying. Repeated names, identifiers, boilerplate, the repeated-random-token test above.
    • Few-shot task learning is something else on top. Todd et al. (2023) found function vectors: a few attention heads whose summed output encodes "the task" (say, "give the antonym"). Adding it to a zero-shot prompt makes the model do the task. Hendel et al. (2023) found a similar "task vector". Yin and Steinhardt (2025) compared them across twelve models: function-vector heads mattered much more than induction heads for few-shot ICL. Many start as induction heads and change role during training.
    • Another view treats ICL as a learning algorithm. Transformers can implement something like gradient descent or ridge regression on the in-context examples (R6.4).

    A function vector is a direction added to activations to trigger a behaviour: steering where the vector comes from the prompt. The two halves of this lesson are one idea seen from two ends.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Probe accuracy offered as proof the model "knows" something

      "A probe predicts nationality from the embedding with 92% accuracy, so the model uses nationality." Decodable is not used.

      Spot itWas the direction removed and the behaviour re-measured?
    • No control task, too many dimensions

      A probe with 4,096 inputs fitted to 500 examples will fit nearly anything.

      Spot itAsk for the same probe on shuffled labels, test accuracy only, and examples against dimensions.
    • Probe finds a surface cue, not the concept

      The "angry" direction fires on exclamation marks; the "fraud" direction on the word "unauthorised".

      Spot itTest on paraphrases, other languages and neutral phrasing.
    • One direction for a multi-dimensional concept

      Forcing a circle or hierarchy into a line loses structure and makes steering erratic.

      Spot itCheck the variance one direction explains; plot the top two or three components.
    • Steering shown on a few cherry-picked prompts

      A vector that works on demo prompts may weaken or reverse on others.

      Spot itAsk for hundreds of held-out prompts, a range of strengths, and a fluency measure.
    • Ignoring side effects

      Tone changes as hoped while accuracy or safety behaviour quietly drops.

      Spot itRun your normal evaluation suite, safety included, with the vector on and off.
    • Treating a steering vector as a guardrail

      An access-control rule is deterministic; a vector is statistical, and anyone with the weights can remove it.

      Spot itAsk what happens when the vector fails and what checks sit outside the model.
    • Claiming induction heads "are" in-context learning

      They handle copying; other heads matter more for few-shot task learning.

      Spot itAsk what ablating them does to few-shot accuracy, not only repeated-token loss.
    • Mistaking copying for reasoning

      A model completing "Al … Noor" may be matching a pattern, not checking the right counterparty (Simulation 4, position 8).

      Spot itBuild a test where the repeated pattern is wrong and see which the model follows.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is the direction of largest variance usually a poor concept direction?

    Variance measures spread, not class separation. The biggest axes are often mundane (position, language). The concept can sit on a quiet axis, so use labels or contrast pairs: difference of means or a probe (Simulation 1).

    2. A linear probe on 4,096 dimensions gets 99% training and 51% test accuracy on 400 examples. What happened, and what would you do?

    Overfitting: more dimensions than examples lets a linear probe fit nearly any labelling. Report held-out accuracy, add a shuffled-label control task, regularise or reduce dimensions, and collect more examples.

    3. A probe predicts "customer is a student" from a credit-model embedding at 90% accuracy. Does the model use student status?

    Not shown. It is decodable, perhaps because it correlates with age and income. To test use, remove or steer the direction and see whether scores change, and compare with a control.

    4. You steer an assistant with a formality vector at strength 6 and the replies become repetitive nonsense. Why?

    The push has moved activations far outside what the model saw in training, so later layers handle them badly. There is a usable window of strength. Sweep it and measure fluency alongside the behaviour.

    5. You estimate a steering vector from 5 contrast pairs and another from 500. Which works better, and why?

    The 500-pair vector. Averaging cuts noise roughly as one over the square root of the pair count, so the direction sits closer to the true one and drags fewer unrelated features (Simulation 3).

    6. Describe how the previous-token head and the induction head cooperate on "Al Noor … Al".

    The previous-token head writes "my predecessor was Al" into the representation at "Noor". At the second "Al" the induction head queries "I am Al", the key at "Noor" matches, and the head copies "Noor" to the output. It needs two layers because the second head reads what the first wrote.

    7. Position 8 of Simulation 4 shows an induction head predicting the wrong token. What does that tell you?

    Induction is a rule: "what followed this last time?". Where the context breaks the pattern (Al Ameen after Al Noor) it copies wrongly unless other heads override it. Copying is not checking.

    8. What did Yin and Steinhardt (2025) change about the story of induction heads and in-context learning?

    Across twelve models, function-vector heads were the main drivers of few-shot ICL; ablating induction heads mattered much less. Many function-vector heads start as induction heads and change role in training. Induction explains copying; few-shot task learning uses a different mechanism.
