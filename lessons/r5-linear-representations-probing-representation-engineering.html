    Reading and Steering a Model From Inside: Directions, Probes and Induction Heads

    Why many concepts sit along straight lines in a model's activations, how a probe reads them and why a good probe score proves less than it seems, how adding a vector can steer behaviour (and break it), and the attention circuit that lets a model learn from its own prompt.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceInside a transformer many concepts are stored as directions, so you can read them with a simple linear probe and nudge them by adding a vector, but "decodable" is not "used", "steered" is not "controlled", and the best-understood piece of in-context learning, the induction head, explains copying far better than it explains everything the prompt can do.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A bank runs an open-weight language model on its own servers to triage customer messages in Arabic and English. At every layer, for every token, the model holds a long list of numbers, an activation vector, perhaps four thousand numbers long. That list is the model's working memory. Nobody designed what the numbers mean.

    Two questions then come up quickly. Can we read something from that list, such as "this message is an angry complaint" or "the model believes the statement it just read is false"? And can we write to it, adding a nudge so that the model is calmer, more formal, or more cautious, without retraining?

    Both turn on a surprising empirical fact. Many concepts turn out to lie along a roughly straight line in that space. Picture a cloud of points, one per message, in a space with thousands of dimensions. Angry messages sit a little further along some direction than calm ones. If that is true, then reading the concept means measuring position along the line (a linear probe), and writing it means moving along the line (activation steering). Both are just vector arithmetic.

    The same craft also reaches into the model's machinery. The second half of the lesson looks at one small circuit, the induction head. It is the mechanism by which a model sees "Al Noor Trading" once in the prompt and, the next time it sees "Al", suggests "Noor". It is the cleanest example we have of a trained network doing something that looks like learning from the prompt.

    Hold onto thisThree questions apply to every claim in this area. Is the concept really linear, or only approximately so? Does the model use the direction, or can we merely decode it? And does nudging it change the behaviour you wanted without damaging anything else? Each is a separate experiment, and the sections below take them in turn.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    R5.3 · Linear representations: the idea and why it is plausible

    The linear representation hypothesis says that high-level concepts, such as sentiment, truthfulness, "is a place", or the gender of a noun, correspond to directions in the model's activation space. Two pieces of evidence made this credible.

    • Word-vector arithmetic. In 2013 Mikolov and colleagues found that in word embeddings, king − man + woman lands near queen. Differences between vectors carried meaning that repeated across pairs. This is the F5.6 story: an embedding space can be organised so that meaning is geometry.
    • Why it is natural for a transformer. The residual stream (F5.2) is a running sum: each layer adds its output to a shared vector, and the next layer reads it through a linear map (the query, key, value or MLP input weights). A feature that downstream layers need to read is easiest to use if it is a direction, because a dot product with a fixed vector extracts it cleanly.

    The sharper version of the claim is that a concept c has a direction vc such that the activation moves along vc when the concept changes and little else moves. Park, Choe and Veitch (2023) tried to make this precise, defining it through pairs of inputs that differ only in the concept, and showing that the notion of "orthogonal" needs a particular inner product rather than the plain dot product.

    Three cautions come with it, and the research since 2023 has mostly been about them.

    • Not every feature is a line. Engels and colleagues (2024) found multi-dimensional features, such as days of the week arranged in a circle. A single direction cannot represent "Tuesday".
    • Directions are not neurons. A direction is a combination of many coordinates. R5.1 explained why: a model packs more features than it has dimensions by letting them overlap (superposition), which is why sparse autoencoders try to recover cleaner directions.
    • Linear is an approximation. Within a narrow set of inputs a direction may work well. Across topics, languages or formats it may drift.

    The lesson of the picture is that a concept direction is not the direction of greatest variation. In real models the biggest axes of variation are often mundane (position in the sentence, a few very large "massive activation" dimensions, language). The concept you want can live in a quiet direction. Finding it needs labels or contrasts: pairs of inputs that differ in the concept and not otherwise. The simplest estimate is the difference of class means, which is the direction between the two cloud centres. It is cheap, needs no training, and is what many steering methods use. Logistic regression and related probes tilt the line to ignore noisy directions and usually do a little better, as the "best direction" button shows.

    R5.3 · Probing: reading a concept, and what a good score does and does not prove

    A probe is a small classifier trained on a model's frozen activations to predict some property of the input (Alain and Bengio, 2016). A linear probe is a logistic regression:

    P(concept∣h)=σ(w·h+b)

    h is the activation vector at some layer and token position, w is a learned direction, b a bias and σ the sigmoid. The direction w is the probe. Train it on a labelled set (angry or calm), test it on held-out examples, and report accuracy or AUC. If a linear probe at layer 20 predicts the property well and a probe at layer 2 does not, the information has become linearly available somewhere in between.

    Probing is the workhorse of interpretability and has a long record of finding real structure: syntax in language models, board state in a model trained only on Othello moves (Nanda et al., 2023), space and time coordinates in Llama-2 (Gurnee and Tegmark, 2023), and a direction separating true from false statements (Marks and Tegmark, 2023). It is also easy to misuse. Three questions separate a finding from a mirage.

    1. Could a probe find this in noise? A powerful probe can memorise. If you have more activation dimensions than examples, a linear probe can fit random labels perfectly. Hewitt and Liang (2019) proposed control tasks: give the same inputs random labels and see how well the probe does. The gap between real and control accuracy, selectivity, is what counts. Simulation 2 shows this.
    2. Is it decodable, or does the model use it? A probe shows information is present. It does not show the model relies on it. Amnesic probing (Elazar et al., 2021) removes the direction and checks whether behaviour changes. Steering and ablation, in the next section, are the interventions that turn a correlation into evidence of a mechanism.
    3. Is it the concept, or a correlate? A probe for "fraud complaint" may be reading the word "unauthorised". Test on inputs where the surface cue and the concept come apart, in another language, another phrasing, another domain.

    A fourth, practical one: the probe should be simple. The more expressive the probe (a deep network instead of a line), the more the result tells you about the probe and the less about the model.

    Many published probing results survive this scrutiny and many older ones do not. Belinkov (2022) is the standard review of what probes can and cannot support. When you hear "we probed the model and found X", your questions are: what was the control, how many examples against how many dimensions, and was there an intervention?

    R5.3 · Representation engineering and activation steering: writing a concept

    If a direction v represents a concept, then adding it to the activations should push the model towards that concept. This is activation steering, and the broader programme of reading and controlling internal representations is called representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023). Two early methods defined the pattern:

    • ActAdd (Turner et al., 2023): run the model on two prompts such as "Love" and "Hate", subtract the activations at one layer, and add the difference, scaled, to the activations while generating.
    • Contrastive activation addition (Rimsky et al., 2023): average the activation differences over hundreds of A/B pairs where one answer shows a behaviour (sycophancy, refusal, a corrigible stance) and the other does not. Add that average to every token position.
    h′=h+α·v^,v^=1k∑i=1k(hi+−hi−)

    h is the activation at the chosen layer, α the strength you dial, and v̂ the average difference over k contrast pairs (h+ shows the behaviour, h− does not). Reading and writing use the same object: the probe asks "how far along v̂ is this activation?", the steer says "move it further along".

    The strongest demonstration so far is refusal. Arditi and colleagues (2024) found that in 13 open chat models, refusing harmful requests is mediated by a single direction: removing it from the activations stops the model refusing, and adding it makes the model refuse harmless requests. That result cuts both ways for a bank. It shows that internal behaviour can be located and inspected. It also shows that the safety behaviour of an open-weight model can be edited out by anyone with the weights.

    Steering is not free. Three things go wrong, and the simulation shows all three.

    • Noisy direction. With few contrast pairs, your estimate v̂ points partly in the wrong direction, and the push moves other things as well.
    • Too small and nothing happens; too big and the model falls apart. Push far enough and activations leave the region the model saw in training. Text turns incoherent, repetitive, or off-topic. There is a window of useful strength, and it differs by concept, layer and prompt.
    • Side effects. A direction that is only approximately the concept drags correlated behaviours with it.

    One further point for anyone building on this. Steering lives in the same family as the direction-finding methods of R5.1: sparse autoencoders produce feature directions that can also be added or removed. Whether SAE features steer better than plain difference-of-means vectors is contested. Section 4 summarises the evidence.

    R5.4 · Induction heads: how a transformer copies from its own prompt

    A language model that has only seen the prompt "Pay Al Noor Trading AED 500. Pay Al Ameen Exchange AED 500. Pay Al" has no reason, from training data alone, to expect "Noor" or "Ameen". Yet models regularly pick one of them, because those names appeared earlier in the prompt. The mechanism for the simplest version of this is the induction head, found by Anthropic's interpretability team (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022).

    The pattern is: [A] [B] … [A] → predict [B]. If the current token was seen before, find what followed it last time and predict that. It takes two attention heads working together (F5.5 covers attention itself):

    1. A previous-token head in an earlier layer. At each position j it copies information about token j−1 into the representation of token j. After this, the vector at "Noor" also records "my predecessor was Al".
    2. An induction head in a later layer. At the current position it forms a query from the current token ("I am Al"). It compares that to the keys at every earlier position, and the key at "Noor" now says "my predecessor was Al", so it matches. The head attends there and copies the token it finds ("Noor") into the output, raising its probability for the next word.

    The two heads combine across layers, so a model with a single attention layer cannot do it. That is why you see induction behaviour only in models with at least two layers of attention. The result is a lookup using nothing but attention: find where I've been before, and do what came next.

    Two findings make induction heads more than a curiosity.

    • They appear suddenly during training. Olsson et al. (2022) saw a short "phase change" early in training, visible as a bump in the loss curve, at the same point where the model's ability to use earlier context jumps. Before it the model sees only local statistics; afterwards it is exploiting its prompt. R6.1 on grokking and double descent shows other sudden shifts of this kind.
    • They generalise beyond copying exact tokens. Fuzzy induction heads match approximate meaning, so "ID card" earlier can serve "identity document" later, and translation-like copying across languages has been reported. The line between "copy pattern" and "do the task from examples" is blurry, and that is the interesting part.

    R5.4 · In-context learning: how much do induction heads explain?

    In-context learning (ICL) is a model's ability to improve at a task from examples placed in the prompt, with no weight updates. Show it three labelled complaints and it labels the fourth. Induction heads were proposed by Olsson et al. as a major mechanism of ICL, supported by evidence that was, in the authors' own words, strongest in small models and indirect in large ones.

    The current picture is more layered:

    • Induction explains token-level copying well. Repeated names, identifiers and boilerplate, the repeated-random-token test above.
    • Few-shot task learning looks like something else on top. Todd and colleagues (2023) found function vectors: a small set of attention heads whose summed output encodes "the task" in the examples (for instance "give the antonym"). Adding that vector to a zero-shot prompt makes the model perform the task. Hendel and colleagues (2023) reached a similar compressed "task vector". Yin and Steinhardt (2025) compared the two across twelve models: function-vector heads were much more important than induction heads for few-shot ICL, and ablating induction heads mattered far less. Many function-vector heads appear to start life as induction heads and change role during training.
    • A second view treats ICL as a learning algorithm. Work from 2022 onwards shows transformers can implement something like gradient descent or ridge regression on the in-context examples. R6.4 covers it.

    Notice the link back to Part 2's first half. A function vector is a direction that can be added to activations to trigger a behaviour. It is steering where the vector comes from the prompt itself. The two halves of this lesson are one idea seen from two ends.

    How it connects

    This lesson is the "do something with the features" half of module R5. It leans on the geometry of F1 and F5, and its limits connect to everything in the explainability and safety tracks.

    R5.1 · R5.2 · Superposition, SAEs, attribution graphsWhere cleaner directions come from

    A probe or difference-of-means vector is a direction found with supervision. Sparse autoencoders search for many directions without labels. Both read the same space.

    F5.5 · F5.6 · Attention and embeddingsQueries, keys, values

    Induction is attention doing a lookup. If queries, keys and values are new to you, read this first. The embeddings half covers why meaning behaves like geometry.

    F1.1 · Vectors, matrices, geometryDot products and directions

    A probe is a dot product; a steering vector is addition; "orthogonal" and "angle between directions" are used throughout.

    F4 · Bias, variance and generalisationWhy probes overfit

    A probe with more dimensions than examples is a textbook high-variance model. Simulation 2 is that lesson applied to interpretability.

    F8.5 · The limits of post-hoc explanationA cousin problem

    Feature attributions on tabular models face the same question: does the explanation reflect what the model does, or merely correlate with it? Interventions answer it for both.

    R4.1 · R4.2 · R4.3 · Sycophancy and alignmentBehaviours you may want to detect or dial

    Sycophancy, deception and refusal were all studied as directions. See also chain-of-thought faithfulness: probes on activations are one way to check what the written reasoning leaves out.

    R6.1 · R6.4 · Grokking, ICL as gradient descentThe other half of in-context learning

    Induction heads are the circuit story; "ICL as implicit gradient descent" is the algorithmic story. Grokking is another sudden-change phenomenon like the induction phase change.

    R3.1 · R3.6 · Post-training and reward hackingWhat training put there

    Refusal and helpfulness are installed by post-training. Directions found by probes show where and how strongly it left a mark.

    R10.3 · What contrastive learning really learnsGeometry of learned embeddings

    Another case where meaning is arranged geometrically, and where "which directions matter" has to be argued rather than assumed.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • On-premises LLMs. Probing and steering need the weights. If your institution runs open-weight models inside its own infrastructure (for data-residency reasons, for example), you can do this work; with a hosted API you cannot. That is a real argument for keeping some models in-house, and for knowing which question needs which kind of access. See internal copilots for staff.
    • Monitoring. A cheap linear probe on hidden states can flag "the model may be hallucinating" or "this prompt looks like an injection attempt" at almost no inference cost. It is a signal for a monitoring dashboard, not a guarantee, and it needs the same drift checks as any model.
    • Fairness audits. Probing an embedding that feeds a credit or churn model for nationality, gender or age shows whether that information is present, even though the field was never an input. It is a screening tool: presence in the representation is not proof of discriminatory use, and absence under a weak probe is not proof of fairness.
    • Behaviour tuning. Steering a drafting assistant towards a more formal register, or a shorter style, may work. Compliance behaviours such as "never reveal account data" are not something to rest on a steering vector, because of the reliability results below. They belong in access controls and output filters.
    • Model risk and validation. I am not aware of CBUAE or SAMA guidance that treats probing or steering results as validation evidence for a model, and I would not assume it does. Treat them as supporting analysis that helps your validators ask better questions, and check current local guidance before putting any such result in a submission.

    Where the field is now

    The linear toolkit is mature enough to be used and well enough tested that its limits are now a research topic in their own right.     Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in papers, vendor decks and AI-written summaries. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Probe accuracy offered as proof the model "knows" something

      "A probe predicts the customer's nationality from the embedding with 92% accuracy, so the model uses nationality." Decodable is not used.

      Spot itAsk whether anything was intervened on: was the direction removed and the behaviour re-measured?
    • No control task, too many dimensions

      A probe with 4,096 inputs fitted to 500 examples will fit nearly anything.

      Spot itAsk for the same probe on shuffled labels, and for test accuracy only. Compare examples to dimensions.
    • Probe finds a surface cue, not the concept

      The "angry" direction fires on exclamation marks, or the "fraud" direction fires on the word "unauthorised".

      Spot itTest on paraphrases, other languages and neutral phrasing of the same concept.
    • One direction for a multi-dimensional concept

      Forcing a circle or a hierarchy into a line loses structure and makes steering erratic.

      Spot itLook at the variance explained by one direction. Plot the examples in the plane of the top two or three components.
    • Steering demonstrated on a handful of cherry-picked prompts

      A vector that "works" on the demonstration prompts may weaken or reverse on others.

      Spot itAsk for results over hundreds of held-out prompts, a range of strengths, and a fluency or quality measure alongside the behaviour measure.
    • Ignoring side effects

      The tone changes as hoped and factual accuracy or safety behaviour quietly drops.

      Spot itRun your normal evaluation suite, including safety and accuracy checks, with the vector on and off.
    • Treating a steering vector as a guardrail

      An access-control rule is deterministic; a vector is statistical. Anyone with the weights can also remove one.

      Spot itAsk what happens when the vector fails, and what checks sit outside the model.
    • Overclaiming that induction heads "are" in-context learning

      Induction heads handle copying, and recent comparisons find other heads matter more for few-shot task learning.

      Spot itAsk what ablating them does to few-shot task accuracy, not only to repeated-token loss.
    • Mistaking copying for reasoning

      A model that completes "Al … Noor" from the prompt may be matching a pattern rather than checking which counterparty is correct (see Simulation 4, position 8).

      Spot itBuild a test where the repeated pattern is wrong, and see whether the model follows the pattern or the facts.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Inside a language model, many ideas, such as tone or whether it thinks a statement is true, sit along measurable lines. That lets us build cheap monitors that watch a model's internal state, and in some cases nudge it. It is promising for monitoring and for diagnosing problems in the models we host ourselves. It is not yet something I'd rely on as a safety control, because recent studies show the nudges are unreliable and can have side effects. Our controls stay outside the model.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Linear probes establish that information is linearly decodable at a layer. To claim the model uses it I'd want a control task for selectivity, held-out data from a different distribution, and an intervention, ablating or steering the direction and measuring behaviour. For steering I'd estimate the vector by difference of means over several hundred contrast pairs, sweep the strength, and report behaviour change alongside fluency and unrelated capability. On in-context learning, induction heads explain exact-copy behaviour; for few-shot task learning, function-vector heads appear more important.”
    “Can we use activation steering to stop our assistant giving advice it shouldn't?”

    I wouldn't rely on it. Recent work reports steering vectors are unreliable across inputs and can induce side effects including misalignment, and anyone with the weights can remove one. It may help with tone. For restrictions, use access controls, retrieval limits and output checks, which are deterministic, and test the model's behaviour directly.

    “The vendor says they can see inside the model with a probe. What does that prove?”

    That some property can be predicted from the activations. Ask for the control-task accuracy, the number of examples against dimensions, results on held-out distributions, and whether removing the direction changes the model's behaviour. Without those it shows presence of information, not use.

    “What is an induction head, in two sentences?”

    It is a pair of attention heads that finds the last time the current token appeared and predicts whatever came next. It is how a model copies names or patterns from earlier in the prompt, and it appears abruptly during training.

    “Is in-context learning just induction heads?”

    No. Induction heads account for copying. A 2025 comparison across twelve models found a different set, function-vector heads that encode the task from examples, matter much more for few-shot learning, though many of those heads begin life as induction heads. There is also a separate line treating ICL as implicit gradient descent.

    “Why does this need our own hosted model?”

    Probing, ablation and steering all read or edit hidden activations, which a hosted API does not expose. With an open-weight model inside our infrastructure we can run these checks, monitor internals cheaply and keep data on-premises. The cost is that we own the engineering and the safety evaluation.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why is the direction of largest variance in a set of activations usually a poor choice for a concept direction?

    Variance measures how much the data spread, not how well the classes separate. The largest axes often reflect mundane factors such as position or language. The concept can sit along a quiet axis. Use labels or contrast pairs, as the difference of means or a probe does (Simulation 1).

    2. A linear probe on 4,096 dimensions gets 99% training and 51% test accuracy on 400 examples. What happened, and what would you do?

    Overfitting: more dimensions than examples lets a linear probe separate nearly any labelling. Report held-out accuracy, add a control task with shuffled labels, regularise or reduce dimensions, and collect more examples.

    3. A probe predicts "customer is a student" from a credit-model embedding at 90% accuracy. Does the model use student status?

    Not shown. It is decodable, perhaps because it correlates with age and income inputs. To test use, remove or steer the direction and see whether the scores change, and compare with the control.

    4. You steer an assistant with a formality vector at strength 6 and the replies become repetitive nonsense. Why?

    The push has moved activations far outside the region the model saw in training, so downstream layers receive input they handle badly. There is a usable window of strength. Sweep strength, and measure fluency along with the behaviour.

    5. You estimate a steering vector from 5 contrast pairs and another from 500. Which would you expect to work better, and why?

    The 500-pair vector. Averaging reduces noise roughly as one over the square root of the pair count, so its direction sits closer to the true one and drags fewer unrelated features along (Simulation 3).

    6. Describe how the previous-token head and the induction head cooperate on "Al Noor … Al".

    The previous-token head writes "my predecessor was Al" into the representation at "Noor". At the second "Al" the induction head queries with "I am Al"; the key at "Noor" matches; the head attends there and copies "Noor" to the output, raising its probability as the next token. It needs two layers because the second head reads what the first wrote.

    7. Position 8 of Simulation 4 shows an induction head predicting the wrong token. What does that tell you?

    Induction is a pattern-copying rule: "what followed this last time?". Where the new context breaks the pattern (Al Ameen after Al Noor) it copies wrongly unless other heads or later layers override it. Copying is not checking.

    8. What did Yin and Steinhardt (2025) change about the story of induction heads and in-context learning?

    They compared induction heads with function-vector heads across twelve models and found function-vector heads were the main drivers of few-shot ICL; ablating induction heads mattered much less. Many function-vector heads begin as induction heads and change role during training. So induction explains copying; few-shot task learning relies on a different mechanism.
