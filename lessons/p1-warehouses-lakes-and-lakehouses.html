    Where Bank Data Lives, and When It Moves: Warehouses, Lakes, Lakehouses, Batch and Streaming

    What a warehouse, a lake and a lakehouse each give a modeller, why the way tables keep history decides whether your training data is honest, and how much a fraud decision loses for every minute it waits.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank's data lives in a few kinds of store, each a trade between cheap storage, trustworthy tables and flexible formats; what a modeller needs from them is history that can be replayed as of any date, and a delivery speed (nightly, minutes, milliseconds) chosen by what a late decision costs, not by what sounds modern.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a city's water. The treated, piped network is clean, metered and checked. The reservoir is huge and cheap and takes whatever rains. Treating water where it sits lets the reservoir serve the taps.

    • Data warehouse (the pipes): structured tables, fixed schema, SQL, finance-grade numbers.
    • Data lake (the reservoir): files of any shape (card messages, call recordings, scanned letters of credit) in cheap object storage.
    • Lakehouse (treating in place): a table layer over the lake, giving versioned, transactional tables on open files, so you stop paying for two copies.

    The second question is time. A nightly churn scoring job and a model approving a card payment at the till read the same transactions. They differ in how long the bank will wait between event and decision: batch versus streaming.

    A modeller cares for two reasons. The store decides whether "what did this customer look like on 1 March?" gets a true answer, the heart of leakage-free training (F3.1). The delivery speed decides what a model can do: churn needs yesterday's data; card fraud needs the last thirty seconds.

    Hold onto thisWarehouse: trusted, governed numbers. Lake: cheap, raw, any format. Lakehouse: tables with history and transactions on lake storage. Separately: how fresh must the answer be? Storage and freshness are independent choices; mixing them up is the usual mistake.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Warehouses, lakes and lakehouses

    Four axes place any product (Snowflake, BigQuery, Synapse, Redshift, Databricks, Hive, Trino) in the right box.

    WarehouseLakeLakehouse
    What is storedStructured tables in the engine's own managed formatFiles of any kind (Parquet, JSON, CSV, audio, PDF, images) in object storageOpen column files (usually Parquet) plus a table-format log that tracks which files form the table
    SchemaSchema-on-write: data must fit the table before it goes inSchema-on-read: anything is accepted; you impose structure when you readSchema enforced at the table level, and it can evolve with recorded versions
    TransactionsYes (ACID). A report never sees half a load.No. A job that fails halfway leaves partial files that readers can see.Yes, through the table-format log

    Weak spotCost at scale; awkward for images, audio and free text; Python and ML access is by exportReliability and governance: "data swamp" with no owner, no quality gateNewer and more moving parts; performance tuning and catalogues need care

    Why the lakehouse exists. Banks ran both: a lake for raw and ML data, a warehouse for trusted reporting. That meant two copies, copy jobs, and arguments over which number was right. The Lakehouse idea (Armbrust et al., CIDR 2021): the lake lacked not storage but a transaction log. Record every table change as an atomic commit in a log beside the files, and the lake gets reliable tables, schema enforcement and reads as of an earlier commit. The open formats doing this are Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg and Apache Hudi.

    Bronze, silver, gold (medallion). Bronze: raw data landed unchanged, so you can replay it. Silver: cleaned, de-duplicated, conformed tables (one definition of "customer", one currency convention). Gold: business-ready tables, such as a monthly risk mart or a model's feature table. Like a kitchen: raw ingredients, prepped, plated.

    Columnar storage makes analytics cheap. Parquet and warehouses store each column together. "Average balance" reads one column of billions of rows and skips the other 200. Per-block min/max values let a filter on txn_date = '2026-03-01' skip most files; partitioning (folders by date) does it coarsely. That is why a year of card data answers in seconds and the same data as CSV would not.

    Where bank data comes from. Core banking (balances), card switch (authorisations, ISO 8583 or JSON), payments hubs (ISO 20022 payment data), CRM, call centre, app logs, and external feeds (credit bureaus such as AECB in the UAE or SIMAH in Saudi Arabia, sanctions lists). Operational systems are OLTP: many tiny fast writes. Analytics is OLAP: few big reads over history. Heavy model queries on the core database would slow customer payments, so data is copied out. How it is copied is the second half of this lesson.

    History is a feature of the table, not a courtesy

    Many operational tables hold only the current state: one row per account, overwritten on change. The limit today is 4,000; that it was 12,000 last spring is gone. Like judging last year's exam from this year's marked-up script, joining labels to that row gives every feature information from after the moment you are predicting from. That is leakage (see point-in-time correctness), and the code looks innocent.

    Three ways to keep history, weakest to strongest:

    • Periodic snapshots. Copy the table nightly or monthly. Robust but storage-heavy; you can only ask about snapshot dates.
    • Change records with valid dates (slowly changing dimension, type 2). Each change adds a row with valid_from and valid_to. Any date works, if everyone writes the joins correctly.
    • Table-format time travel. Delta and Iceberg keep old file versions; read the table as of a timestamp or version. Good for audit and rebuilding a past training set, until retention clean-up. It records when the platform changed, not when the event happened (bitemporal: event time versus recorded time), which matters when records are corrected late.

    Near the year's end, the charged-off status in the "now" column predicts the label perfectly because it is the label. The model scores brilliantly offline and is useless live. The cure is not clever modelling. It is keeping history and building training tables with an as-of join: for each (customer, date), take the latest record valid on or before that date. SQL thinking shows the window-function pattern; feature stores package it.

    Warehouses are catching up: they read open formats such as Iceberg and offer time travel, and lakehouses run warehouse SQL. Ask "what guarantees do I need?", not "which category is this?".

    Batch versus streaming

    Batch processes chunks on a schedule, like a post van collecting at 5pm. It is simple (input is fixed at start), easy to re-run after a bug, and cheap. Streaming handles each event as it arrives, holding state between events (for example "this card's last ten transactions"), like a phone call. Typical stack: an event log such as Apache Kafka stores events; a processor such as Apache Flink or Spark Structured Streaming computes on them. Micro-batch sits between: small batches every few seconds or minutes, which is how many "streaming" jobs really run.

    Choose by delay cost: how much damage per minute of waiting, and how many minutes does each design add? A scheduled job's expected wait is:

    E[delay]≈T2+P

    T is the gap between runs, P the runtime. An event lands at a random moment, so it waits half a gap on average, then P. A nightly job (T = 24 h) adds about 12 hours plus runtime on average, nearly a day at worst. It ignores the time before a pattern becomes visible; the simulation includes that. Going from T = 6 h to 3 h saves 1.5 hours on average.

    • Card fraud at authorisation: decision in tens of milliseconds while the customer waits. Streaming plus an online feature store. See designing a real-time fraud system.
    • AML transaction monitoring: people review alerts over days, so daily batch is usually fine; regulators care about coverage and explainability. Sanctions screening before a payment is released is the exception: it blocks the payment, so it is real-time.
    • Churn, propensity, credit refresh: daily or weekly batch. Scoring churn every second helps nobody. See batch propensity design.
    • Intraday liquidity: near-real-time dashboards, fed every few minutes.

    Ask "what does an hour of delay cost?", not "can we stream?" The simulation puts numbers on one case.

    Three readings. First, the curve flattens: day to hour saves a lot, hour to minute less, and below the attacker's 3-minute rhythm speed buys only the last charge or two. Second, runtime matters as much as frequency: a job that runs every five minutes but takes forty is a forty-minute system. Third, the right spot depends on attack volume and size. Few attacks: the cheap design wins. Thousands: streaming pays for itself. That break-even is the number to bring to a meeting.

    What makes streaming harder than it looks

    Streaming is not batch made quicker. Three things change.

    • Event time versus processing time. Like a parcel scanned at the depot late, a tap at 14:00:02 may reach you at 14:04 if the phone was in a lift. Group by arrival time and the same behaviour gives different features each day. Group by event time and accept that some events arrive late.
    • Watermarks. The engine must decide when a window ("transactions in the last 5 minutes") is complete. A watermark is its guess: "no event older than t minus W will still arrive." Short W: fast but misses late events. Long W: complete but slow. Same accuracy-versus-delay trade as batch (Akidau et al., 2015).
    • State, duplicates and failure. The job remembers things between events (counts, last location) and that state must survive a crash. Delivery is "at-least-once" (an event may arrive twice) or "exactly-once" within the platform, at a cost. A duplicate that doubles a velocity count is a bug no batch re-run would show.

    The slow 15% is the point. A tiny wait captures most of the count; the last few percent need disproportionately long waits, and in fraud the stragglers can be the interesting ones (offline merchant terminals uploading in bulk). Common answer: a hybrid. Decide at the till on what you have, reconcile later in batch with complete data. This is the lambda architecture (fast path plus complete path). Its alternative, kappa, uses one replayable log as the single source, so reprocessing is just replaying it (Kreps, 2014). Two paths mean two codebases that must agree. One path means the stream engine must handle everything.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Training from the current-state table

      Features come from tables that overwrite history. Offline results shine, live results do not.

      Spot itFor a customer in last March's sample, which table gave the credit limit, and would it have said the same in March? Any "different" feature is a leak.
    • The data swamp

      A lake with no owners, schemas or catalogue. Fifty copies of "customers", none agreeing.

      Spot itAsk who owns the table and when it was last validated. No answer means not a source for a regulated model.
    • Time travel treated as an audit trail

      Routine clean-up removes old versions after the retention window, so last year's training set cannot be rebuilt.

      Spot itCheck retention against your validation and record-keeping period. Record the table version, or export a snapshot, with each run.
    • Event time confused with load time

      Using the landing date as the transaction date. Corrections and late files shift history.

      Spot itCount rows whose load date is far after their event date. Keep both columns and say which a feature uses.
    • Streaming where batch would do

      Months of platform work for a churn score read once a week.

      Spot itAsk who consumes the output and how often. Read daily means hourly compute is waste.
    • Batch where an attack needs minutes

      A nightly fraud score on a product attackers drain in an hour.

      Spot itEstimate damage per hour of delay (Simulation 3) against platform cost. Ask how fast attackers cash out.
    • Training-serving skew

      Features built in SQL for training and in different code for the live stream. Same name, different numbers.

      Spot itLog live features, recompute the same rows offline, and compare values row by row.
    • Short watermark, silent loss

      Late events are dropped unannounced, so velocity features undercount for customers on weak connections.

      Spot itMonitor the late and dropped event rate, by channel.
    • Duplicates and double counting

      At-least-once delivery plus a counter: a retried message counts twice.

      Spot itCheck events carry a unique id and the job de-duplicates on it. Replay the same hour twice.
    • Copies drifting apart

      The warehouse says AED 1.84bn, the lake says AED 1.79bn, and each team trusts its own.

      Spot itName the system of record for each number and reconcile copies on a schedule.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A job runs every 6 hours and takes 45 minutes. What are the average and longest delays?

    Average ≈ T/2 + P = 3 h + 45 min = 3 h 45 min. Longest: an event that just misses a run waits 6 h + 45 min = 6 h 45 min.

    2. Why does a lake need a transaction log before it behaves like a warehouse table?

    Without one, a job that writes 200 files and fails after 120 leaves 120 files readers can see. A log records a commit only once all files are written, so readers see the old table or the new one, never a mixture. The same log lets you read an earlier commit.

    3. You build a default-risk training table by joining labels to today's customers table. What goes wrong, and how do you fix it?

    Features reflect the state after the prediction moment (a status that changed because of the default), so they leak the answer. Fix: snapshots per as-of date, type-2 history with an as-of join, or a table-format version as of that timestamp.

    4. Name three bank use cases and say batch, micro-batch or streaming, with one reason each.

    Card authorisation: streaming, the customer waits and attackers cash out in minutes. AML monitoring: daily batch, people work alerts over days. Intraday liquidity: micro-batch every few minutes, a current picture but no per-event decisions. (Sanctions screening before release is real-time because it gates the payment.)

    5. In Simulation 4, 85% of events arrive within seconds and 15% are slow. Why does more waiting give diminishing returns?

    A short wait counts the fast majority. After that, each extra second only collects a few stragglers with long, spread-out delays. The count creeps up while decision time grows linearly.

    6. What is the difference between event time and load time, and why can it break history?

    Event time is when it happened; load time is when the platform recorded it. A correction landing three weeks late can fall in or out of a query "as of last month" depending on which date you filter. Training features should use what was known at the as-of date (load time); analysis uses what happened (event time).

    7. A colleague says "move everything to streaming, it's modern." What do you ask?

    Which decision improves if made sooner, and by how much? What does an hour of delay cost? Who reads the result, and how often? If it is read daily, streaming adds cost and failure modes for no value.
