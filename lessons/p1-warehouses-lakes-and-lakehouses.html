    Where Bank Data Lives, and When It Moves: Warehouses, Lakes, Lakehouses, Batch and Streaming

    What a warehouse, a lake and a lakehouse each give a modeller, why the way tables keep history decides whether your training data is honest, and how much a fraud decision loses for every minute it waits.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA bank's data lives in a few kinds of store, each a trade between cheap storage, trustworthy tables and flexible formats; what a modeller needs from them is history that can be replayed as of any date, and a delivery speed (nightly, minutes, milliseconds) chosen by what a late decision costs, not by what sounds modern.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Picture the bank's data as a city's water supply. There is the treated, piped network: clean, metered, safe to drink, and everything that goes into it is checked first. There is the reservoir: huge, cheap, takes anything that rains, and nobody should drink from it directly. And there is the newer idea of treating water where it sits, so the reservoir itself can serve the taps.

    Those are the three storage ideas. A data warehouse is the piped network: structured tables, a fixed schema, SQL, finance-grade numbers. A data lake is the reservoir: files of any shape (card messages, call recordings, scanned letters of credit) in cheap object storage. A lakehouse keeps the reservoir and adds a table layer on top, so you get versioned, transactional tables over open files. It is an attempt to stop paying for two copies of the data.

    The second question is about time. A nightly job that scores every customer for churn, and a model that approves or declines a card payment while the customer is standing at the till, both read the same transactions. They differ in how long the bank is willing to wait between something happening and something being decided. That is batch versus streaming.

    Neither choice is about fashion. A modeller cares for two specific reasons. First, the store decides whether you can ask "what did this customer look like on 1 March?" and get a true answer, which is the heart of leakage-free training (F3.1). Second, the delivery speed decides what a model can be used for at all: a churn model needs yesterday's data; a card-fraud model needs the last thirty seconds.

    Hold onto thisThree words, three jobs. Warehouse: trusted, governed numbers for reporting. Lake: cheap, raw, any format. Lakehouse: tables with history and transactions on lake storage. Then a second, separate question: how fresh must the answer be? Storage choice and freshness choice are independent, and mixing them up is the usual mistake.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Warehouses, lakes and lakehouses

    The three designs differ along four axes. Know these and you can place any product name (Snowflake, BigQuery, Synapse, Redshift, Databricks, Hive, Trino) in the right box.

    WarehouseLakeLakehouse
    What is storedStructured tables in the engine's own managed formatFiles of any kind (Parquet, JSON, CSV, audio, PDF, images) in object storageOpen column files (usually Parquet) plus a table-format log that tracks which files form the table
    SchemaSchema-on-write: data must fit the table before it goes inSchema-on-read: anything is accepted; you impose structure when you readSchema enforced at the table level, and it can evolve with recorded versions
    TransactionsYes (ACID). A report never sees half a load.No. A job that fails halfway leaves partial files that readers can see.Yes, through the table-format log
    Typical usersFinance, risk reporting, BI analysts, regulatory returnsData engineers, ML engineers, anything unstructuredData scientists and engineers who want SQL, Python and Spark over the same tables
    Weak spotCost at scale; awkward for images, audio and free text; Python and ML access is by exportReliability and governance: "data swamp" with no owner, no quality gateNewer and more moving parts; performance tuning and catalogues need care

    Why the lakehouse exists. For years banks ran both: a lake for raw and ML data, and a warehouse for trusted reporting. That meant two copies, nightly jobs to copy between them, and arguments about which number was right. The lakehouse idea (Armbrust et al., CIDR 2021) is that the missing ingredient in the lake was not storage but a transaction log. If every change to a table is recorded as an atomic commit in a log next to the files, the lake gets reliable tables, schema enforcement, and the ability to read the table as it was at an earlier commit. The open table formats that do this are Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg and Apache Hudi.

    A common layout: bronze, silver, gold. Raw data lands unchanged in a bronze layer (so you can always replay it). Silver holds cleaned, de-duplicated, conformed tables: one definition of "customer", one currency convention. Gold holds business-ready tables for a purpose, such as a monthly risk mart or a feature table for a model. This is sometimes called the medallion pattern. The names are vendor language, but the idea is plain: keep the raw record, clean in steps, publish curated views.

    Columns and why they make analytics cheap. Parquet and warehouse storage keep each column together, not each row. A query for "average balance" reads one column from billions of rows and skips the other 200. Files carry min and max values per block, so a filter on txn_date = '2026-03-01' skips most files unread. Partitioning (folders by date, say) does this coarsely. It is why an analytics query over a year of card data can finish in seconds and why scanning the same data as CSV would not.

    Where a bank's data actually comes from. Core banking (balances, accounts), card switch (authorisations, as ISO 8583 or JSON messages), payments hubs (see ISO 20022 payment data), CRM, call-centre systems, channel logs from the mobile app, and external feeds (credit bureau such as AECB in the UAE or SIMAH in Saudi Arabia, sanctions lists). The operational systems are OLTP: built for many tiny, fast writes. Analytics wants OLAP: few big reads over history. Running heavy model queries on the core banking database would slow customer payments, which is why data is copied out to a separate analytical store. How it is copied is the subject of the second half of this lesson.

    History is a feature of the table, not a courtesy

    Here is the part that matters most to a modeller. Many operational tables hold only the current state: one row per account, overwritten when something changes. The credit limit today is 4,000; the fact that it was 12,000 last spring has gone. If you build a training table by joining labels to that current row, every feature carries information from after the moment you are pretending to predict from. That is leakage (see point-in-time correctness), and it is invisible in the code.

    Three ways a store can keep history, from weakest to strongest:

    • Periodic snapshots. Copy the whole table every night or month-end. Simple and robust, storage-heavy, and you can only ask about snapshot dates.
    • Change records with valid dates (slowly changing dimensions, type 2). Each change adds a row with valid_from and valid_to. You can ask about any date, but only if everyone writes the joins correctly.
    • Table-format time travel. Delta and Iceberg keep old versions of files; you can read the table as of a timestamp or version number. Handy for audit and for reproducing a past training set, with a retention window after which old versions are cleaned up. Time travel records when the data platform changed. It is not the same as when the event happened, a difference called bitemporal (event time versus recorded time) that causes real trouble when records are corrected late.

    Notice what happens near the end of the year. The charged-off status in the "now" column is a near-perfect predictor of the label, because it is the label. A model trained that way scores brilliantly offline and is useless live. The cure is not clever modelling. It is choosing stores and table designs that keep history, and a rule that training tables are built with an as-of join: for each (customer, date) pair, take the latest record whose valid time is on or before that date. SQL thinking shows the window-function pattern; feature stores package this so you do not rewrite it per project.

    Also worth knowing: warehouses are catching up. Modern cloud warehouses read open table formats such as Iceberg and offer their own time travel; lakehouse engines run warehouse-style SQL. The sharp distinction in the table above is blurring, which is why the better question is "what guarantees do I need?" than "which product category is this?".

    Batch versus streaming

    Batch processes data in chunks on a schedule: a nightly job, an hourly job. It is simple to reason about (the input is fixed when the job starts), easy to re-run after a bug, and cheap, because compute is used in bursts. Streaming processes each event as it arrives, continuously, with state held between events (for example, "this card's last ten transactions"). The usual stack is an event log such as Apache Kafka that stores the events, and a stream processor such as Apache Flink or Spark Structured Streaming that computes on them. In between sits micro-batch: small batches every few seconds or minutes, which is how many "streaming" jobs actually run.

    Choosing is a calculation about delay. For a decision whose value decays with time, ask: how much damage happens per minute of waiting, and how many minutes does each design add? The expected wait of a scheduled job has a simple form:

    E[delay]≈T2+P

    T is the gap between runs and P is how long a run takes to produce its answer. An event happens at a random moment between runs, so on average it waits half a gap for the next run to start, then P for the run to finish. A nightly job (T = 24 hours) adds about 12 hours plus its runtime to the average event, and nearly a full day to the unluckiest. This ignores the first moments after an event during which a pattern is not yet visible; the simulation below includes that.

    The right answer depends on the use case, and many are not close calls:

    • Card fraud at authorisation: the decision must come back in tens of milliseconds, while the customer waits. Streaming plus an online store of precomputed features. See designing a real-time fraud system.
    • Transaction monitoring for AML: alerts are reviewed by people over days. Batch (daily) is usually fine, and regulators care more about coverage and explainability than speed. Sanctions screening of a payment before it is released is the exception: that one blocks the payment, so it is real-time.
    • Churn, propensity, credit refresh: batch, daily or weekly. Nothing improves by scoring a customer's churn risk every second. See batch propensity design.
    • Intraday liquidity: somewhere between, near-real-time dashboards fed every few minutes.

    The first sensible question is rarely "can we stream?" but "what does an hour of delay cost?" The simulation puts numbers on one case.

    Three things to read from it. First, the curve flattens: going from a day to an hour saves a great deal, from an hour to a minute saves less, and below the 3-minute rhythm of the attacker, extra speed buys only the last charge or two. Second, runtime matters as much as frequency. A job that runs every five minutes but takes forty to finish is a forty-minute system. Third, the right place on the curve depends on how often attacks happen and how big they are. With a small monthly count, the cheap design wins; with thousands of attacks, streaming pays for itself. That break-even is exactly the number to bring to a meeting.

    What makes streaming harder than it looks

    Streaming is not batch made quicker. It changes three things.

    • Event time versus processing time. A tap on a phone at 14:00:02 may reach your system at 14:00:40, or at 14:04 if the phone was in a lift. If you group events by arrival time, the same customer behaviour gives different features on different days. Group by event time instead, and accept that some events arrive late.
    • Watermarks. The engine must decide when a window ("transactions in the last 5 minutes") is complete. A watermark is its running guess: "I assume no event older than t minus W will still arrive." A short W gives fast answers but misses late events; a long W is complete but slow. It is the same accuracy-versus-delay trade as batch, one level down (Akidau et al., 2015).
    • State, duplicates and failure. A streaming job remembers things between events (counts, last location), and that state must survive a crash. Systems provide "at-least-once" delivery (an event may come twice) or "exactly-once" processing within the platform, at a cost. A duplicated payment event that doubles a velocity count is a bug no batch re-run would show you.

    The slow 15% is the point. Most events are on time, so a tiny wait gets you most of the count. The last few percent need disproportionately long waits, and in fraud those late stragglers are sometimes the interesting ones (offline merchant terminals that upload in bulk). The common design answer is a hybrid: decide at the till on what you have, and let a batch job reconcile later with complete data. This reaches the idea sometimes called the lambda architecture (a fast path and a complete path) and its streaming-only alternative, kappa, where one replayable log is the single source and reprocessing is just replaying it (Kreps, 2014). Neither is free: two paths mean two codebases that must agree, and one path means the stream engine must be good enough for everything.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Storage and freshness sit under almost every model the bank runs. These are the places they matter.

    F3.1 · Point-in-time correctnessHistory in the table is what makes it possible

    Simulation 2 is leakage in miniature. Time travel and valid-from/valid-to rows are the machinery that lets you rebuild any past day.

    F3.3 · SQL thinkingQuery the store properly

    Warehouses and lakehouses both speak SQL. The as-of join, window functions and partition filters are what make your queries correct and cheap.

    P1.3 · Feature storesThe bridge between this lesson and serving

    An offline store (lakehouse tables) for training and an online store (key-value) for serving, kept consistent. The batch versus stream choice decides how features are filled.

    P1.4 · Lineage and metadataKnowing where a number came from

    A lake with no catalogue is a swamp. Lineage tells a validator which silver tables fed a model, and which gold table breaks if a source changes.

    P2 · Deployment patternsBatch scoring versus real-time serving

    The same trade seen from the model side: a batch job writing scores to a table, or a service answering one request at a time.

    P5 · Reference designsPutting streaming to work

    The real-time fraud design uses everything in Simulation 3 and 4: event log, stream processor, online features and a hybrid reconciler.

    P4 · Cloud and hybridWhere the stores physically run

    Object storage, compute that scales apart from storage, and the question of in-country regions. See also hybrid and on-premises.

    B1 · Fraud and feedbackThe use case that drives streaming

    Labels arrive late (delayed labels), so a store must hold both the event and the later outcome keyed to it, with their dates.

    F7 · Drift monitoringLate data is a source of false drift

    A monitoring job that reads partly arrived data will see "drift" that is just incompleteness. Read from a mature partition.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Regulatory and finance reporting. Warehouse-style gold tables that reconcile to the ledger. Models that feed provisioning (PD, LGD, EAD) must be traceable to those same numbers.
    • Data residency. Where the store physically sits matters. The CBUAE and SAMA expect banks to keep customer data and critical systems under defined controls, with specific rules on outsourcing and cloud. I have not quoted the rules here: check the current text with your compliance team, because they differ by data type and have changed recently. See sovereign AI for the in-country cloud context.
    • Unstructured sources. Call recordings, scanned trade documents and Arabic complaint text sit in the lake. Models over them (parsing PDFs and scans) start with a file path, not a table.
    • Reproducibility for validators. "Which exact data trained this model?" has an answer only if the table version or snapshot is recorded with the run (model lifecycle).
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The big ideas are settled. The live questions are about open table formats converging, how real streaming is becoming a default tool rather than a specialist one, and how all of this is placed under sovereignty rules.

    Open questionWhere things stand
    One table format or several?Delta, Iceberg and Hudi coexist. Catalog interfaces and metadata translation are making them substitutable for reads. Banks usually pick the one their primary engine supports best and avoid betting on full interoperability.
    Streaming and batch with one engine and one code pathFlink and Spark both claim unified APIs. In practice many teams still have different code for training features and serving features, and that gap is the root of training-serving skew (see feature stores).

    The current consensus, in one lineKeep the raw record, publish curated tables with history, choose open formats where you can, and match freshness to the cost of delay: most bank models are happy with batch, and a few (card authorisation, payment screening) need streaming.
    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    These turn up in real projects and in AI-written pipelines. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Training from the current-state table

      Features are joined from tables that overwrite history. Offline results are excellent, live results are not.

      Spot itAsk: for a customer in last March's sample, what table did the credit limit come from, and would it have given the same value in March? Any feature that "would have been different" is a leak.
    • The data swamp

      A lake with no owners, no schemas and no catalogue. Fifty copies of "customers", none agreeing.

      Spot itAsk who owns the table and when it was last validated. If nobody can say, it is not a source to train a regulated model on.
    • Time travel treated as an audit trail

      Old versions are removed by routine clean-up after a retention window, so the training set you used cannot be rebuilt a year later.

      Spot itCheck the retention setting against your model-validation and record-keeping period. Record the table version or export a snapshot with each model run.
    • Event time confused with load time

      Using the date a row landed as the transaction date. Corrections and late files then shift history.

      Spot itCount rows whose load date is much later than their event date. Always keep both columns and say which one a feature uses.
    • Streaming where batch would do

      Months of platform work for a churn score that is read once a week.

      Spot itAsk who or what consumes the output and how often. If it is read daily, hourly compute is waste.
    • Batch where an attack needs minutes

      A nightly fraud score on a product that attackers drain in an hour.

      Spot itEstimate the damage per hour of delay as in Simulation 3, and compare it to the platform cost. Ask how long attackers typically take to cash out.
    • Training-serving skew

      Features computed in SQL for training and in different code for the live stream. Same name, different numbers.

      Spot itLog live features and recompute the same rows offline. Compare distributions and row-by-row values. Differences show the skew.
    • Short watermark, silent loss

      Late events are dropped without anyone being told, so velocity features undercount for exactly the customers on weak connections.

      Spot itAsk for the rate of late and dropped events as a monitored metric, by channel.
    • Duplicates and double counting

      At-least-once delivery plus a counter. A retried message counts twice.

      Spot itCheck that events carry a unique id and that the job de-duplicates on it, then test by replaying the same hour twice.
    • Copies drifting apart

      The warehouse says AED 1.84bn, the lake says AED 1.79bn, and each team trusts its own.

      Spot itName the system of record for each number, and reconcile the copies on a schedule.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “We don't need to rebuild everything to get models into production. The question for each use case is how fresh the answer must be. Churn and campaign scoring can run overnight at low cost. Card fraud can't: attackers drain a card in minutes, so that one needs decisions at the till. For the data itself, we should keep the full history of every table, because without it we can't train honestly or show a validator what a model saw. I'd start with those two decisions and let the technology follow.”
    To a data engineer or architect
    “For training I need point-in-time-correct tables: either type-2 history or a table format with time travel, with the table version recorded per run. I'd like event time and load time both on the rows. For serving, I'd want the same feature definitions computed once and used in both paths, with late-event and duplicate rates monitored. Where do we stand on in-country regions for the lakehouse, and who owns the silver customer table?”
    “What's the difference between a data lake and a lakehouse?”

    A lake is files in cheap storage with no guarantees. A lakehouse adds a transaction log over those files, which gives you real tables: atomic updates, enforced schemas and the ability to read the table as of an earlier version. So the data stays in open formats on cheap storage, and you get most of what a warehouse offers.

    “Do we still need a warehouse if we have a lakehouse?”

    Often yes, for a while. The warehouse holds the finance-reconciled numbers that regulatory reporting relies on and is operated by a team that owns them. The lakehouse is where data science and unstructured data live. Over time the line blurs, since warehouses read open tables and lakehouses run warehouse SQL, but I wouldn't migrate trusted reporting only for tidiness.

    “Why can't we just query the core banking database?”

    It's built for fast small transactions, and a heavy model query can slow payments for customers. It also usually holds only current state, so you cannot rebuild what a customer looked like in the past, and several systems each hold part of the picture. So we copy to an analytical store that keeps history.

    “Should the fraud model be real-time?”

    For card payments at authorisation, yes: the decision happens while the customer waits, and a nightly job would let an attack run all day. I'd put numbers on it: loss per hour of delay times attack volume, against the extra platform cost. For a monitoring queue reviewed by analysts the next morning, batch is the right answer.

    “What is Kafka, and what is Flink?”

    Kafka is a durable log of events: producers append, consumers read at their own pace, and old events can be replayed. Flink is an engine that computes on those events as they arrive, holding state such as "this card's transactions in the last ten minutes". One stores and delivers events, the other computes on them.

    “Is streaming always more accurate because it's fresher?”

    No. Streaming sees data before late events have arrived, so some features are less complete than in a batch run. You trade completeness for speed. That's why strong designs decide fast on what they have and reconcile later with complete data.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. A scheduled job runs every 6 hours and takes 45 minutes to finish. What is the average delay between an event and the result, and the longest?

    Average ≈ T/2 + P = 3 hours + 45 minutes = 3 hours 45 minutes. The longest is an event that just misses a run: a full 6 hours of waiting plus 45 minutes of runtime, so 6 hours 45 minutes.

    2. Why does a lake need a transaction log before it behaves like a warehouse table?

    Without one, a job that writes 200 files and fails after 120 leaves 120 files that readers can see. A log records a commit only once all files are written, so readers see either the old table or the new one, never a mixture. The same log lets you read the table as of an earlier commit.

    3. You build a default-risk training table by joining labels to the customers table as it stands today. What goes wrong, and what are two ways to fix it?

    Features reflect the state after the moment you are predicting from (a status that changed because of the default, for example), so they leak the answer. Fix by building snapshots at each as-of date (monthly copies, or type-2 history with valid dates and an as-of join), or by reading a table-format version as of that timestamp. Check the result against the live data to confirm.

    4. Name three use cases at a bank and say whether each should be batch, micro-batch or streaming, with one reason each.

    Card authorisation: streaming, the customer waits and attackers cash out in minutes. AML transaction monitoring: daily batch, alerts are worked by people over days and coverage matters more than speed. Intraday liquidity view: micro-batch every few minutes, treasury needs a current picture but not per-event decisions. (Sanctions screening of a payment before release is real-time, because it gates the payment.)

    5. In Simulation 4, 85% of events arrive within seconds and 15% are slow. Why does adding more waiting give diminishing returns?

    The fast majority is counted by a short wait. After that, each extra second only collects the few slow stragglers, whose delays are long and spread out. The count rises quickly, then creeps, while the decision time keeps growing linearly with the wait.

    6. What is the difference between event time and load time, and why can it break history?

    Event time is when the thing happened; load time is when the platform recorded it. If a correction lands three weeks later and is stored with its load date, a query as of last month could include or exclude it depending on which date you filter on. For training, features should use what was known at the as-of date (load time), while analysis uses what happened (event time). Know which one you use.

    7. A colleague says "we should move everything to streaming, it's more modern." What do you ask?

    What decision gets better if it is made sooner, and by how much? What does an hour of delay cost here? Who reads the result, and how often? If the output is read once a day, streaming adds cost and failure modes without adding value. Reserve it for the decisions where the value of the answer decays in minutes.
