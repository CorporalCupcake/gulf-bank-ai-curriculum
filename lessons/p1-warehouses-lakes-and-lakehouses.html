    Warehouses, Lakes, Batch and Streaming

    Part 1

    Where the data lives

    In one sentenceA warehouse stores tidy tables you can trust, a lake stores any kind of file cheaply, and a lakehouse puts reliable tables on top of the lake. Separately, batch moves data in scheduled chunks and streaming moves it event by event.

    Think of a city's water. The piped network is clean, metered and checked. The reservoir is huge, cheap and takes whatever rain falls. Banks need both, and a lot of the work is deciding which data goes where.

    How they differ

    WarehouseLakeLakehouse
    HoldsStructured tablesFiles of any kindOpen column files plus a log
    StructureMust fit before loadingAdded when you readEnforced at table level
    Half-finished loadNever visiblePartial files visibleNever visible
    Main usersFinance, risk reportsData and ML engineersData scientists
    Weak spotCostly at scaleCan become a "swamp"Newer, more to tune
    • Why lakehouses exist. Banks ran a lake for raw and model data and a warehouse for trusted reports. That meant two copies and arguments over which number was right.
    • The missing piece was a log. A lakehouse records every table change as one all-or-nothing step (a transaction log) next to the files. Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg and Apache Hudi do this.
    • A data swamp is a lake with no owner, no schema and fifty disagreeing copies of "customers".
    Part 2

    How data is tidied up

    Most platforms clean data in layers, like a kitchen: raw ingredients, prepped, plated.

    1. Land it (bronze). Copy raw data in unchanged from core banking, the card switch, payments, CRM, app logs and credit bureaus, so you can replay it.
    2. Clean it (silver). Remove duplicates and give "customer" one definition.
    3. Make it useful (gold). Build business-ready tables: a risk mart, or the feature table for a model.
    • Source systems do many tiny fast writes (called OLTP, online transaction processing). Analytics does few big reads over history (OLAP, online analytical processing). Heavy model queries would slow customer payments, so data is copied out.
    • Columnar storage makes analytics cheap. "Average balance" reads one column of billions of rows and skips the other 200.

    Keep the history, or models cheat

    Many source tables hold only the current state: one row per account, overwritten on change. If a limit was 12,000 last spring and is 4,000 today, spring's value is gone. Join old labels to that row and the features carry information from after the moment you are predicting from. That is leakage.

    Three ways to keep history, from weakest to strongest:

    • Snapshots. Copy the table nightly or monthly. Simple, but you can only ask about snapshot dates.
    • Dated change rows. Each change adds a row with a start date and an end date, so any date works.
    • Time travel. Delta and Iceberg keep old table versions, so you can read the table as of a timestamp. Clean-up eventually deletes them.
    Part 3

    Batch or streaming

    In one sentenceBatch collects events and processes them on a schedule, like a post van at 5pm. Streaming processes each event as it arrives, like a phone call.

    Micro-batch sits between: small batches every few seconds or minutes. Kafka is a common tool for storing the event stream, and Flink or Spark Structured Streaming compute on it.

    How to read the delay

    An event lands at a random moment between runs, so it waits half a gap on average, plus the job's runtime.

    Nightly job
    About 12 hours of waiting on average, nearly a day at worst.
    Every 6 hours, takes 45 minutes
    About 3 hours plus 45 minutes, so roughly 3 h 45 min on average. Runtime counts as much as frequency.
    Runs every 5 minutes but takes 40
    It is a 40-minute system, whatever the schedule says.

    Choose by what an hour of delay costs, not by whether you can stream.

    • Card authorisation: a decision in tens of milliseconds while the customer waits. Streaming.
    • Sanctions screening before a payment is released: real-time, because it blocks the payment.
    • Anti-money-laundering alerts: people review them over days, so daily batch is usually fine.
    • Churn or credit refresh scores: daily or weekly batch.

    Why streaming is harder than it looks

    • Late events. A tap at 14:00:02 may reach you at 14:04 if the phone was in a lift. Group by when it happened (event time), not when it arrived.
    • Watermarks. The engine must guess how long to wait before a window such as "the last 5 minutes" is complete. A short wait is fast but misses stragglers. A long wait is complete but slow.
    • State and duplicates. The job remembers counts between events. A retried message can be counted twice.

    A common answer is a hybrid: decide at the till on what you have, then reconcile later in batch with complete data.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    ChoicePick it whenCost
    WarehouseRegulatory returns, finance, trusted reportingPricey at scale
    LakeRaw landing, text, images, call audioNeeds owners and a catalogue
    LakehouseModels and reports on one copy with historyMore moving parts
    BatchResults read daily or weeklyCheap, easy to re-run
    StreamingEach minute of delay loses moneyComplex, more failure modes
    • Strong at (streaming): decisions in seconds.
    • Weak at (streaming): late events, duplicates and crash recovery all need design.

    In banking, the store decides whether "what did this customer look like on 1 March?" gets a true answer, and the delivery speed decides whether a model can stop card fraud in seconds or only report it tomorrow.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Training from the current-state table

      Offline results shine and live results do not. Check whether last March's table would have said the same thing in March.

    • Letting the lake become a swamp

      Copies drift apart: the warehouse says AED 1.84bn and the lake says AED 1.79bn. Ask who owns the table and when it was last validated.

    • Streaming everything

      Months of work for a churn score read weekly. Estimate what an hour of delay costs first.

    • Silent loss in streams

      A short watermark drops late events unannounced, and retries can double count. Monitor the late and dropped rate, and give events unique ids.

    1. A job runs every 6 hours and takes 45 minutes. How stale are its answers?

    About 3 h 45 min on average (half the gap plus runtime), and up to 6 h 45 min.

    2. Why does a lake need a transaction log to behave like a warehouse table?

    Without one, a failed job leaves partial files visible. The log records a commit only when everything is written, so readers see the old table or the new one, never a mixture.

    3. You join default labels to today's customer table. What goes wrong?

    The features reflect the state after the prediction moment, so they leak the answer. Use snapshots, dated history, or time travel to rebuild each customer as they were.

    4. Batch or streaming for AML alerts and for card authorisation?

    AML is daily batch, because people work alerts over days. Card authorisation is streaming, because the customer is waiting and attackers cash out in minutes.

    5. A colleague says "move everything to streaming". What do you ask?

    Which decision improves if made sooner, and what an hour of delay costs. If the result is read daily, streaming adds cost for no gain.
