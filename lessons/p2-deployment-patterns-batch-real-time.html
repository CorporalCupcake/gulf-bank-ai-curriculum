    Deploying Models: Batch, Real-Time, Shadow and Canary

    Part 1

    What deploying means

    In one sentenceDeploying a model means putting it where real decisions use it, and every deployment answers two questions: when does it score (batch or real-time), and how do we switch to a new version safely (shadow, then canary).

    Running example: a bank's fraud model. A card payment must be scored in the 100 milliseconds (ms) between tap and reply. A product offer can be scored overnight. Scoring faster than a decision needs costs money. Scoring slower makes the answer stale before anyone acts.

    How it differs from training

    • Training happens once, on stored data, with no time limit.
    • Deploying means the model runs again and again on live data, under a time limit, and customers feel its mistakes.
    • A good test score is only about a third of the job. The rest is timing, safe release and watching it afterwards.
    Part 2

    When does it score?

    Think of a printed timetable (batch), asking the driver right now (real-time), and a live departures board (streaming).

    • Batch: a scheduled job scores everyone and stores the scores. Used for churn, next-best-product and IFRS 9 (loan-loss accounting) runs. Scores are as old as the last run.
    • Real-time: a service scores one request in tens to hundreds of ms. Used for card authorisation and instant-payment screening. It needs always-on servers.
    • Streaming: events update features or scores within seconds to minutes, such as "transactions in the last hour". It is the hardest to build: late and out-of-order events cause errors.

    How to choose

    Ask how fast the decision's value fades. If most of it is gone in minutes, score online. If it fades over days, a daily batch captures almost all of it at a fraction of the cost.

    • Hybrids are common: batch builds a customer profile overnight, and a light real-time model adds the live payment.
    • Real-time limits the model: a model needing 300 features from five systems blows a 100 ms budget. Teams train a smaller model or precompute features.
    • Batch fails quietly: a dead real-time service pages someone. A dead nightly job leaves yesterday's scores in use and everything looks normal. Add a freshness check that alarms if scores are older than expected.
    Part 3

    How do we switch to a new model safely?

    A fraud model that is 2 points worse crashes nothing. Dashboards stay green and the losses show up weeks later. So a new model (the challenger) is introduced like a trainee driver, while the current model (the champion) keeps working.

    • Shadow: the challenger scores a copy of live traffic. Its output is logged, never acted on. You learn latency, errors, missing features and score spread, with zero customer risk. You cannot learn whether its decisions would have been better.
    • Canary: a small share of traffic (1%, then 5%, 25%, 100%) is decided by the challenger. You get real outcomes with a live control group. Assign by customer, not per transaction, so each customer always meets one model.
    • Blue-green: traffic flips from the old environment to the new at once, with an instant switch back. It tests the plumbing, not model quality.

    Skip shadow and infrastructure bugs reach customers. Skip canary and quality bugs do.

    Guardrails and rollback

    • Guardrails are limits agreed before release on latency, error rate, share of missing features, approval rate and, once outcomes arrive, precision and recall. Breach one and the canary rolls back automatically, without a meeting. Write the limits down before you see the numbers.
    • A release is a sealed bundle: model, feature definitions, code version, training data, threshold, evaluation report and approval. Rolling back means moving a pointer to the previous bundle, not retraining under pressure.
    • Rollback has limits: decisions already acted on, such as blocked accounts, are not undone. Keep a list of what the bad version decided.
    Part 4

    How to read a canary

    • Labels are slow. Whether a payment was fraud may only be known after a customer disputes it, around 21 days later. A canary can have enough traffic in two days and still say nothing about fraud. So watch fast proxies too: approval rate, alert volume and how often analysts override the model.
    • Rare events make small canaries slow. What counts is the number of fraud cases in each group, not transactions. A canary at 1% of traffic takes far longer to prove a problem than one at 10%, but a bigger canary harms more customers if the model is bad.
    • Shadow disagreement is not accuracy. If the challenger would have blocked something the champion approved, you do not know it was fraud. Have people review a sample of disagreements.
    • With very rare cases (say 40 money-laundering cases a month), a canary will never be statistically clear. Rely on shadow comparison, expert review and a pre-agreed rollback plan.
    Part 5

    Which to use

    PatternCustomers affectedUse it when
    BatchVia stored scoresThe decision can wait hours or days
    Real-timeEach requestThe value fades in seconds, such as card payments
    ShadowNoneFirst step for any new model: checks it runs and behaves
    CanaryA small shareYou need real outcomes before full release
    Blue-greenAll, at onceYou are changing infrastructure and need a fast way back
    • Strong at: shadow and canary together catch both plumbing and quality bugs before everyone is exposed.
    • Weak at: none of them proves a model is good. Only real outcomes do, and they arrive late.

    In banking, a model that influences a regulated decision, such as credit, needs independent validation before the canary, not after.

    Part 6

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Real-time by default

      Always-on serving is costly and fragile. If a nightly batch captures nearly all the value, use it.

    • Silent batch failure

      A job that dies leaves old scores in place. Check how old the scores are every day.

    • Setting limits after seeing results

      Limits chosen after the numbers arrive get bent to fit. Agree them first.

    • Treating shadow as proof of quality

      Shadow shows the model runs. It cannot show its decisions are better.

    1. What is the difference between a champion and a challenger?

    The champion is the model currently making decisions. The challenger is the new version being tested against it.

    2. A decision loses most of its value within a minute. Batch or real-time?

    Real-time (or streaming). A nightly batch would score long after the moment has passed.

    3. What can shadow mode tell you that a canary cannot, and the reverse?

    Shadow tests the plumbing with zero customer risk, but cannot show better decisions. A canary shows real outcomes, but exposes some customers.

    4. Why does a canary on fraud take weeks even with lots of traffic?

    Fraud labels arrive late, around 21 days, and fraud cases are rare. Traffic is not the limit; waiting for outcomes is.

    5. What does rolling back actually involve?

    Moving a pointer to the previous sealed bundle. It does not undo decisions already acted on.
