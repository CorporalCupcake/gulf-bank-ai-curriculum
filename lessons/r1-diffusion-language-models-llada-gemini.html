    Writing Without Left-to-Right: Diffusion Language Models, Bytes and Multi-Token Prediction

    Three attempts to loosen the one-token-at-a-time rule: refine a whole draft in parallel, read raw bytes instead of a fixed vocabulary, or guess several tokens per forward pass. What each buys, what each costs, and what is real as of late 2026.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA standard LLM writes one token per pass, left to right, from a fixed vocabulary; diffusion models, byte-level models and multi-token prediction all try to get more output per pass or drop the tokenizer, and each pays for it in coherence, compute per pass or engineering complexity, so today they matter mainly as speed tricks and as a signal of where inference cost is heading.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Think of a typist who must type each word once, in order, and can never go back. That is how every chat model works. It reads the text so far, produces one probability table over its vocabulary, picks one token (a word piece), appends it and repeats. A 50-token answer needs 50 passes through the network. This is called autoregressive generation, and it has two costs.

    • Speed. Passes are sequential, and the GPU spends much of each one waiting on memory, not computing.
    • Rigidity. The model commits as it goes. It cannot fix word 3 after writing word 10. Its fixed tokenizer (M1.1) also treats Arabic, numbers and typos unevenly.

    Three ideas relax those rules:

    IdeaWhat changesThe promiseThe price
    Diffusion language modelStart with a fully masked answer and fill in many positions per pass, over a few refinement rounds.Fewer passes for the same length. Can edit anywhere.Tokens decided together can contradict each other. Each pass is heavier.
    Tokenizer-free (byte-level) modelRead raw bytes, grouped into patches by the model itself, not by a fixed vocabulary.No tokenizer bias, robust to typos and scripts, one model for every language.Sequences become several times longer unless the architecture compensates.
    Multi-token predictionTrain extra output heads that guess tokens two, three, four steps ahead.Richer training signal, and a built-in draft for faster decoding.Extra heads, and the later guesses are less reliable.
    Hold onto thisSpeed is counted in sequential passes, not arithmetic. Anything that settles several tokens per pass, or checks a draft instead of recomputing it, cuts latency. All three ideas hit the same wall: tokens are not independent, so deciding them together risks incoherence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Diffusion language models: filling in a masked draft

    Think of a crossword: you fill the clues you are sure of first, and those answers help with the rest. Masked diffusion works like that. Image diffusion removes Gaussian noise, but you cannot add a little noise to the word "transfer". So text uses masking instead. The forward process hides each token independently with probability t, from 0 (nothing hidden) to 1 (all [MASK]). The model learns to undo it: given a partly masked sentence, predict the hidden tokens.

    LLaDA, the first large open model of this kind, samples t at random per training sentence and scores the model only on the masked tokens. The weighted cross-entropy is a valid bound on the text likelihood, so this is a principled language model, not a trick (Nie et al., 2025; Sahoo et al., 2024).

    ℒ=−Et,x,xt[1t∑i:xt[i]=MASKlogpθ(x[i]∣xt)]

    Hide a share t of the tokens of text x to get xt, then recover them. The 1/t weight balances light and heavy masking. Unlike the ordinary loss of F1.6, the model sees unmasked tokens on both sides.

    Generation runs backwards. Start with, say, 256 [MASK] tokens after the prompt. In each of T steps the model predicts every masked position at once; you keep the most confident predictions and leave the rest masked. Some systems also re-mask committed tokens that later look inconsistent. If T is far below the length, you need far fewer sequential passes. Three consequences:

    • Bidirectional context. Each position sees both sides. The LLaDA authors reported it beat GPT-4o on a reversal task (completing a poem line from the next line), the "reversal curse" of left-to-right models (Nie et al., 2025).
    • No simple KV cache. Autoregressive models reuse stored keys and values of past tokens (M1.3). Here every pass can change the whole sequence, so each pass costs more. Block diffusion (blocks written left to right, each filled in parallel) and approximate caching narrow the gap (Arriola et al., 2025).
    • A quality-speed dial. More steps give better text, fewer give faster text. Read any speed headline as: at how many steps, and what quality?

    The wall: tokens decided together are not independent

    Two people each name half a city without hearing each other: one says "New" or "Los", the other "York" or "Angeles". Independent choices give "New Angeles" half the time. A diffusion model fixing two positions in one pass does exactly this: it samples each from its own probability table without conditioning on the other. An autoregressive model never clashes, because the second word is chosen after the first.

    In general, with m equally likely phrasings and k positions fixed in one pass, only m of the mk combinations are consistent, so the chance of a coherent phrase is m1−k. With 3 phrasings and 4 tokens that is 1/27.

    So practical decoders fix tokens adaptively: commit a position only when the model is very sure, keep the rest masked, sometimes re-mask. Boilerplate goes in big chunks. A reference number or amount, where one wrong digit is a different fact, is settled slowly. That is why diffusion looks best on code and templated text, and why speeds vary so much by task.

    Who is building them

    • LLaDA (Renmin University and Ant Group): 8B parameters, trained from scratch, open weights. It proved scale: competitive with Llama 3 8B on many benchmarks in the authors' tests. Later versions include a 100B variant converted from an autoregressive model, now a common pattern.
    • Mercury (Inception Labs): the first commercial diffusion LLMs, aimed at coding and low latency, via API. The company reports around a thousand tokens per second.
    • Gemini Diffusion (Google DeepMind): experimental, shown May 2025, about 1,500 tokens per second in demos at roughly a small Flash-class quality. Not a general product at the time of writing.

    All figures are vendor-reported. Read them as "several times faster than a similar-quality autoregressive model on easy-to-parallelise tasks", not a universal result.

    Tokenizer-free models: reading bytes

    A tokenizer (M1.1) is a frozen lookup table that cuts text into frequent chunks, like a dictionary of stock phrases. Anything outside it shatters: rare words, typos, IBANs and Arabic phrases split into many pieces, numbers split inconsistently, and non-English scripts cost more tokens, so more money and context.

    The tokenizer-free fix is to feed bytes, the 256 values of UTF-8. No vocabulary means no out-of-vocabulary word and no built-in language bias. The catch is length: English letters take 1 byte, Arabic letters 2, and attention cost grows with the square of length (F5.3). A full transformer over raw bytes is expensive.

    The modern answer is hierarchical, patch-based models: a small network groups bytes into patches, a large network works on patches, a small network turns them back into bytes. Meta's Byte Latent Transformer (Pagnoni et al., 2024) sizes patches dynamically by the entropy (F1.7) of a small byte model. Easy stretches, like the rest of a common word, get long patches; surprising ones, like a new word or number, get short ones. Compute goes where the text is surprising. The authors reported matching tokenised Llama 3 models up to 8B parameters with up to about half the inference compute, and better robustness to noisy input. Related work: MegaByte, MambaByte and H-Net (Hwang et al., 2025), which learns chunking end to end.

    For a Gulf bank the pitch is one model over Arabic, English and mixed text, robust to OCR errors, transliterated names and typos. That is the pitch, not today's reality: no bank-grade supported byte-level product has been confirmed.

    Multi-token prediction: guess ahead, then check

    Think of a chess player who plans two moves ahead, then checks the plan. Standard training asks one question per position: what is the next token? Multi-token prediction (MTP) adds output heads that also predict tokens two, three or four steps ahead from the same internal state. Gloeckle et al. (2024) found this helped code and reasoning at larger sizes. DeepSeek-V3 used a variant with one extra module for a denser training signal (DeepSeek-AI, 2024).

    At inference the extra heads become drafts for speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023): draft a few tokens cheaply, run the full model once to check them all in parallel, keep drafts up to the first disagreement and take the model's own token there. The output is identical to the full model alone, but several tokens may arrive per full pass. With MTP the model drafts for itself.

    If each draft is accepted with probability a, and each needs the earlier ones accepted, then with k drafts the expected tokens per full pass is:

    E=1+a+a2+…+ak=1−ak+11−a

    The leading 1 is the token you always get; draft j adds aj. With a = 0.87 and k = 1 this is 1.87 tokens per pass, close to the roughly 1.8 times speed-up DeepSeek reported at 85 to 90% acceptance. Reading it: going from 0.87 to 0.60 acceptance cuts this to 1.60, and later drafts add little.

    All three trade more arithmetic per pass for fewer sequential passes, which suits GPUs limited by memory traffic. They differ on guarantees: speculative decoding with verification is lossless; diffusion with fewer steps is a different, possibly worse, sampler.

    Where this lands at the bank. Status: speculative decoding and MTP are live in serving stacks, usually invisibly; commercial diffusion is a pilot-stage option needing your own evaluation; open-weight diffusion and byte-level models are research. None has displaced autoregressive transformers as the frontier default, and I know of no CBUAE or SAMA guidance on architecture, so treat them as any other model under model-risk rules. Practical uses:

    • Chat and contact-centre latency. Faster decoding is a customer-experience feature; ask vendors about speculative decoding.
    • Code assistants. Diffusion's early strength is code and structured output (A15).
    • Arabic and mixed-script documents. Names, IBANs and OCR noise fragment tokenizers. Today, test your chosen model on your own Arabic text.
    • Structured extraction. Parallel filling suits fixed schemas but exposes the one-wrong-digit risk. Validate fields independently.
    • Procurement. For "10x faster", ask: at what quality, which task, how many steps, which hardware, and is the output guaranteed identical?
    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Quoting tokens per second without quality or steps

      "1,000 tokens per second" at few steps says nothing about answer quality.

      Spot itAsk for the benchmark score at that speed, the steps, hardware, batch size and prompt length.
    • Incoherence at fixed positions

      Parallel decoding gives mismatched pairs: a name from one candidate and a surname from another, a currency from one clause and an amount from another.

      Spot itTest structured outputs with dependent fields. Check consistency automatically.
    • Digit and identifier errors

      One wrong digit in an IBAN or amount is a different fact. Parallel filling plus weak numeric reasoning makes this likelier than headline accuracy suggests.

      Spot itRound-trip tests (extract, re-render, compare), IBAN checksums, reconciled totals.
    • Comparing against a weak autoregressive baseline

      A diffusion model beats "a similar-size model" that is older or untuned.

      Spot itCheck the baseline's release date, size and training tokens. Prefer a head-to-head on your tasks.
    • Assuming bytes mean no language bias

      Bytes remove tokenizer bias, not data bias. A model trained on 95% English still does worse in Arabic.

      Spot itEvaluate per language and dialect, including Gulf Arabic, on your content.
    • Ignoring the longer sequences

      A byte model looks free of tokenizer cost until the latency and memory bill for much longer sequences arrives.

      Spot itCompare compute per character or byte of text, not per token.
    • MTP acceptance that collapses off-distribution

      Acceptance measured on chat falls on rare legal Arabic or numeric tables, and the speed-up shrinks.

      Spot itMeasure tokens per pass on your own prompts.
    • Assuming lossless when it is not

      Verified speculative decoding keeps the same distribution; fewer diffusion steps or looser acceptance thresholds do not.

      Spot itAsk: is the output guaranteed identical to the base model, or only usually similar?
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. A model writes a 256-token answer. How many sequential passes does an autoregressive model need, and a diffusion model run for 32 steps? What is the ideal speed-up, and why might the real one be smaller?

    256 against 32: an ideal 8 times. Real gains are smaller because each diffusion pass handles the whole sequence without a simple KV cache, and quality may force more steps.

    2. Four tokens in a chunk are fixed in one pass and there are 3 equally likely coupled phrasings. What is the chance the chunk is coherent?

    m1−k = 3−3 = 1/27, about 3.7%: 3 consistent combinations out of 34 = 81. Hence adaptive, confidence-based unmasking.

    3. Why does masked diffusion see "both sides" of a position, and what task does this help?

    It predicts masked tokens from all unmasked tokens, left and right, with no causal mask. This helps infilling and reversal-type questions, such as completing a line given the line after it.

    4. An Arabic sentence has 19 characters including 3 spaces. How many UTF-8 bytes does it have, and what does that say about byte-level models?

    16 letters at 2 bytes plus 3 spaces at 1 byte is 35 bytes, about 1.8 per character against 1 for English. A naive byte model spends more compute on Arabic; patching contains that.

    5. With patches of 4 bytes instead of raw bytes, roughly how much does attention work over the central model fall?

    Length falls 4 times, attention work about 42 = 16 times, to about 6% of the byte-by-byte cost. This ignores the small byte-level encoder and decoder.

    6. MTP with one drafted token and 88% acceptance. How many tokens per full pass, and what if the drafting costs 5% of a pass?

    E = 1 + 0.88 = 1.88 tokens per pass. Net speed-up = 1.88 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 1.79 times, consistent with DeepSeek's roughly 1.8 times.

    7. What is the difference between speculative decoding and running a diffusion model with fewer steps, in terms of output quality guarantees?

    Speculative decoding verifies drafts with the full model, so the output distribution is unchanged. Fewer diffusion steps is a different sampler whose quality falls as steps drop. One is lossless; the other is a dial.
