    Writing Without Left-to-Right: Diffusion Language Models, Bytes and Multi-Token Prediction

    Writing Without Left-to-Right: Diffusion Language Models, Bytes and Multi-Token Prediction

    Three ways to loosen the one-token-at-a-time rule, what each buys, and what is real as of late 2026.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceDiffusion, byte-level and multi-token models all try to get more output per pass or drop the tokenizer, and each pays in coherence or complexity, so today they matter mainly as speed tricks.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A standard chat model is a typist who can never go back. It reads the text so far, picks one token (a word piece), appends it and repeats, so a 50-token answer needs 50 passes. This is autoregressive generation. It is slow, because passes are sequential, and rigid, because the model cannot fix word 3 after writing word 10.

    Three ideas relax this: diffusion fills in many positions per pass, byte-level models drop the fixed vocabulary, and multi-token prediction guesses several tokens ahead.

    Hold onto thisSpeed is counted in sequential passes. All three ideas hit the same wall: tokens are not independent, so deciding them together risks incoherence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Diffusion language models: filling in a masked draft

    Think of a crossword: fill the answers you are sure of first, and they help with the rest. Text diffusion starts with an answer made entirely of [MASK] and, over a few rounds, reveals the most confident positions. It is trained by hiding random tokens and predicting them from both sides.

    Two consequences matter. Every position sees context on both sides, which helps infilling. And there is no simple cache, so each pass is heavier. More steps give better text, fewer give faster text, so read any speed headline as "at how many steps?"

    The wall: tokens decided together are not independent

    Two people each name half a city without hearing each other: one says "New" or "Los", the other "York" or "Angeles". Half the time you get "New Angeles". A diffusion model fixing two positions in one pass does the same, because neither choice sees the other. An autoregressive model never clashes, since the second word comes after the first.

    So practical decoders commit a position only when the model is very sure and leave the rest masked. Boilerplate goes fast. An amount or reference number, where one wrong digit is a different fact, goes slowly. That is why diffusion looks best on code and templated text.

    Who is building them

    LLaDA is the open-weight research model that showed the idea scales. Mercury (Inception Labs) is a commercial diffusion model aimed at coding and low latency. Gemini Diffusion (Google DeepMind) is an experiment shown in 2025. All speed figures are vendor-reported: read them as "several times faster on easy-to-parallelise tasks", not a universal result.

    Tokenizer-free models: reading bytes

    A tokenizer is a frozen dictionary of frequent chunks. Anything outside it shatters: rare words, typos, IBANs and Arabic phrases split into many pieces, which costs more money and context. The fix is to read raw bytes instead, so nothing is out of vocabulary.

    The catch is length: Arabic letters take two bytes, and attention gets expensive on long sequences. The answer is patches: a small network groups bytes, a large network works on the groups. Meta's Byte Latent Transformer makes patches long for predictable text and short for surprising text. For a Gulf bank the pitch is one model across Arabic, English and noisy OCR text, but no bank-grade byte-level product has been confirmed.

    Multi-token prediction: guess ahead, then check

    Think of a chess player who plans two moves ahead, then checks the plan. Multi-token prediction adds output heads that also guess the tokens after the next one. That enriches training, and at inference the guesses become drafts for speculative decoding: the full model checks all drafts in one pass and keeps those up to the first disagreement.

    The output is identical to the full model alone, but several tokens may arrive per pass. The gain depends on how often drafts are accepted, and each extra draft needs all earlier ones right, so the curve flattens quickly.

    All three trade more arithmetic per pass for fewer sequential passes. Speculative decoding is lossless; diffusion with fewer steps is a different, possibly worse, sampler.

    Where this lands at the bank. Speculative decoding is already live in serving stacks, usually invisibly. Commercial diffusion is a pilot-stage option needing your own evaluation. Byte-level models are research. Treat all as any other model under model-risk rules. When a vendor says "10x faster", ask: at what quality, which task, how many steps, and is the output identical to the base model?

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Speed quoted without quality or steps

      "1,000 tokens per second" at few steps says nothing about answer quality.

      Spot itAsk for the benchmark score at that speed, plus steps and hardware.
    • Incoherence and digit errors

      Parallel decoding gives mismatched pairs, or one wrong digit in an IBAN or amount.

      Spot itTest dependent fields; use round-trip checks and IBAN checksums.
    • Weak baseline

      A diffusion model beats "a similar-size model" that is older or untuned.

      Spot itPrefer a head-to-head on your own tasks.
    • Assuming bytes mean no language bias

      Bytes remove tokenizer bias, not data bias, and sequences get much longer.

      Spot itEvaluate per language, including Gulf Arabic, and compare compute per character.
    • Assuming lossless when it is not

      Verified speculative decoding keeps the same output; fewer diffusion steps do not. Draft acceptance also falls on rare legal or numeric text.

      Spot itAsk if output is guaranteed identical, and measure tokens per pass on your prompts.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is the real diffusion speed-up usually smaller than passes saved?

    Each diffusion pass handles the whole sequence without a simple cache, and quality may force more steps.

    2. Why do tokens fixed in the same pass risk clashing?

    Each is chosen without seeing the others. Hence adaptive decoding: commit only confident positions.

    3. Why does masked diffusion see both sides of a position, and what does it help?

    It predicts masked tokens from all unmasked ones, left and right. This helps infilling and reversal-type tasks.

    4. Why do patches make byte-level models practical?

    They shorten the sequence the large model reads, cutting attention work sharply. Arabic's extra bytes per character would otherwise make it costlier than English.

    5. How do speculative decoding and fewer diffusion steps differ in quality guarantees?

    Speculative decoding verifies drafts with the full model, so output is unchanged. Fewer diffusion steps is a quality dial.
