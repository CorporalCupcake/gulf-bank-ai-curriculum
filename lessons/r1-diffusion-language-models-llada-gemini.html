    Writing Without Left-to-Right: Diffusion Language Models, Bytes and Multi-Token Prediction

    Contents
    Part 1

    The intuition

    A standard chat model is a typist who can never go back. It reads the text so far, picks one token (a word piece), appends it and repeats, so a 50-token answer needs 50 passes. This is autoregressive generation. It is slow, because passes are sequential, and rigid, because the model cannot fix word 3 after writing word 10.

    Three ideas relax this: diffusion fills in many positions per pass, byte-level models drop the fixed vocabulary, and multi-token prediction guesses several tokens ahead.

    Hold onto thisSpeed is counted in sequential passes. All three ideas hit the same wall: tokens are not independent, so deciding them together risks incoherence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Diffusion language models: filling in a masked draft

    Think of a crossword: fill the answers you are sure of first, and they help with the rest. Text diffusion starts with an answer made entirely of [MASK] and, over a few rounds, reveals the most confident positions. Image diffusion removes Gaussian noise, but you cannot add a little noise to the word "transfer", so text uses masking instead.

    Training. For each training sentence, pick a random masking level between none and all, hide that share of tokens, and score the model only on the hidden ones. Because the level varies, the model learns every task from "fill one gap" to "write the lot". This is a principled language model, not a trick: the loss bounds the text likelihood (LLaDA, Nie et al., 2025).

    Generation. Start with, say, 256 masked slots after the prompt. In each of T steps the model predicts every masked slot at once. You keep the most confident predictions and leave the rest masked. Some decoders also re-mask a committed token that later looks inconsistent. If T is well below the length, you save sequential passes.

    Three consequences follow.

    • Bidirectional context. Every position sees both sides, which helps infilling and "reversal" tasks such as completing a line from the line after it.
    • No simple cache. Autoregressive models reuse stored attention keys and values (the KV cache) for past tokens. Here any pass can change the whole sequence, so each pass costs more. Block diffusion (blocks written left to right, each filled in parallel) and approximate caching narrow the gap.
    • A quality-speed dial. More steps give better text, fewer give faster text. Read any speed headline as "at how many steps?"

    The wall: tokens decided together are not independent

    Two people each name half a city without hearing each other: one says "New" or "Los", the other "York" or "Angeles". Half the time you get "New Angeles". A diffusion model fixing two positions in one pass does the same, because neither choice sees the other. An autoregressive model never clashes, since the second word comes after the first. The more tokens you fix together and the tighter their coupling, the worse the odds.

    So practical decoders commit a position only when the model is very sure and leave the rest masked. Boilerplate goes fast. An amount or reference number, where one wrong digit is a different fact, goes slowly. That is why diffusion looks best on code and templated text. The confidence threshold is the key knob: lower it for speed, raise it for safety.

    Who is building them

    LLaDA (Renmin University and Ant Group) is the open-weight research model, 8B parameters trained from scratch, that showed the idea scales to roughly Llama 3 8B-level benchmarks in the authors' tests. Larger versions converted from an autoregressive model followed, now a common shortcut. Mercury (Inception Labs) is a commercial diffusion model aimed at coding and low latency, sold through an API. Gemini Diffusion (Google DeepMind) is an experiment shown in 2025, not a general product. All speed figures are vendor-reported: read them as "several times faster on easy-to-parallelise tasks", not a universal result.

    Tokenizer-free models: reading bytes

    A tokenizer is a frozen dictionary of frequent chunks. Anything outside it shatters: rare words, typos, IBANs and Arabic phrases split into many pieces, which costs more money and context. The fix is to read raw bytes instead (the 256 values of UTF-8), so nothing is out of vocabulary and there is no built-in language bias.

    The catch is length: Arabic letters take two bytes, and attention gets expensive on long sequences. The answer is patches: a small network groups bytes, a large network works on the groups. Meta's Byte Latent Transformer sizes patches by entropy (how surprising the next byte is, judged by a small byte model): the rest of a common word gets a long patch, a new number or name gets a short one. Compute goes where the text is hard. The authors reported matching tokenised Llama 3 models at up to 8B parameters with less inference compute and better robustness to noise. Related work includes MegaByte and H-Net. For a Gulf bank the pitch is one model across Arabic, English and noisy OCR text, but no bank-grade byte-level product has been confirmed.

    Multi-token prediction: guess ahead, then check

    Think of a chess player who plans two moves ahead, then checks the plan. Multi-token prediction adds output heads that also guess the tokens after the next one. That gives a denser training signal (reported to help code and reasoning at larger sizes, and used in a form by DeepSeek-V3), and at inference the guesses become drafts for speculative decoding: the full model checks all drafts in one pass and keeps those up to the first disagreement.

    The output is identical to the full model alone, but several tokens may arrive per pass. The gain depends on how often drafts are accepted, and each extra draft needs all earlier ones right, so the curve flattens quickly. Knobs: how many drafts, and how much extra compute each costs.

    All three trade more arithmetic per pass for fewer sequential passes, which suits GPUs limited by memory traffic. Speculative decoding is lossless; diffusion with fewer steps is a different, possibly worse, sampler.

    Strengths, limits and when to use it

    Strong at

    • Latency, via speculative decoding and MTP. Decoding is limited by memory traffic, not arithmetic, so checking several drafts in one pass is nearly free. The output is unchanged.
    • Easy-to-parallelise text with diffusion. Code, boilerplate and templated output have many confident positions that can be committed together.
    • Infilling and editing with diffusion. Seeing both sides of a gap suits "rewrite the middle" and structured fill-in.
    • Messy, multilingual text with byte models. With no frozen vocabulary, typos, OCR noise, IBANs and Arabic do not shatter into odd pieces.

    Weak at, or don't use when

    • Tightly coupled output. Names, amounts and identifiers decided in the same pass can clash or carry one wrong digit.
    • Anything that needs a guaranteed-identical output. Fewer diffusion steps change the sampler. Only verified speculative decoding is lossless.
    • Ecosystem maturity. Diffusion and byte models have little tooling, few fine-tuned variants, and no long track record in regulated production.
    • Large batches. When servers are already compute-bound, extra drafts or heavier diffusion passes have no spare capacity to hide in.
    • Byte models on long documents. Sequences get much longer, so memory and latency can grow even when tokenisation is better.

    In practice. First try speculative decoding in your serving stack, and measure tokens per pass on your own prompts. Then compare tokenizers on your Arabic text before reaching for byte models. Treat diffusion as a pilot: run a head-to-head on your tasks against a current, tuned autoregressive baseline, and report quality at each step count. Diffusion at LLaDA's scale needs the same GPU class as an 8B model; byte models need pre-training-scale data and compute, so for a bank they mean buying, not building.

    Production status: speculative decoding is live in serving stacks, usually invisibly. Commercial diffusion is pilot-stage. Byte-level models are research, and I know of no CBUAE or SAMA guidance on architecture, so treat all as any other model under model-risk rules. What would change this: independent benchmarks showing diffusion matches a frontier autoregressive model at lower cost, and a supported byte-level model with strong Gulf Arabic results.

    Where it shows up: chat and contact-centre latency (faster decoding feels better), code assistants (diffusion's early strength), structured extraction (fast, but validate each field independently), and Arabic or mixed-script documents (test your chosen model on your own text). When a vendor says "10x faster", ask: at what quality, which task, how many steps, and is the output identical to the base model?

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    • Speed quoted without quality or steps

      "1,000 tokens per second" at few steps says nothing about answer quality.

      Spot itAsk for the benchmark score at that speed, plus steps and hardware.
    • Incoherence and digit errors

      Parallel decoding gives mismatched pairs, or one wrong digit in an IBAN or amount.

      Spot itTest dependent fields; use round-trip checks and IBAN checksums.
    • Weak baseline

      A diffusion model beats "a similar-size model" that is older or untuned.

      Spot itPrefer a head-to-head on your own tasks.
    • Assuming bytes mean no language bias

      Bytes remove tokenizer bias, not data bias, and sequences get much longer.

      Spot itEvaluate per language, including Gulf Arabic, and compare compute per character.
    • Assuming lossless when it is not

      Verified speculative decoding keeps the same output; fewer diffusion steps do not. Draft acceptance also falls on rare legal or numeric text.

      Spot itAsk if output is guaranteed identical, and measure tokens per pass on your prompts.
    • Ignoring the longer sequences

      A byte model looks free of tokenizer cost until the latency and memory bill for much longer sequences arrives.

      Spot itCompare compute per character of text, not per token.
    • Lost cache savings

      Diffusion passes cannot reuse the simple KV cache, so a lab demo at batch size 1 can look far better than your busy production server.

      Spot itBenchmark at your real batch size and prompt length.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why is the real diffusion speed-up usually smaller than passes saved?

    Each diffusion pass handles the whole sequence without a simple cache, and quality may force more steps.

    2. Why do tokens fixed in the same pass risk clashing?

    Each is chosen without seeing the others. Hence adaptive decoding: commit only confident positions.

    3. Why does masked diffusion see both sides of a position, and what does it help?

    It predicts masked tokens from all unmasked ones, left and right. This helps infilling and reversal-type tasks.

    4. Why do patches make byte-level models practical?

    They shorten the sequence the large model reads, cutting attention work sharply. Arabic's extra bytes per character would otherwise make it costlier than English.

    5. How do speculative decoding and fewer diffusion steps differ in quality guarantees?

    Speculative decoding verifies drafts with the full model, so output is unchanged. Fewer diffusion steps is a quality dial.

    6. Your chatbot is too slow and you have a week. Which would you try first: speculative decoding, a diffusion model or a byte-level model, and why?

    Speculative decoding. It is already supported in serving stacks and leaves the output identical, so there is no new model-risk question. Diffusion needs a full evaluation and a new model. Byte-level models are still research.

    7. You must extract IBANs, amounts and names from documents. Would you choose a diffusion model for speed? What would you check?

    Not by default. These fields are tightly coupled and a single wrong digit is a different fact, which is where parallel decoding is weakest. If you pilot it, validate each field independently (IBAN checksums, round-trip checks, reconciled totals) and compare against a tuned autoregressive baseline at the same quality.
