    Writing Without Left-to-Right: Diffusion Language Models, Bytes and Multi-Token Prediction

    Three attempts to loosen the one-token-at-a-time rule: refine a whole draft in parallel, read raw bytes instead of a fixed vocabulary, or guess several tokens per forward pass. What each buys, what each costs, and what is real as of late 2026.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceA standard LLM writes one token per pass, left to right, from a fixed vocabulary; diffusion models, byte-level models and multi-token prediction all try to get more output per pass or drop the tokenizer, and each pays for it in coherence, compute per pass or engineering complexity, so today they matter mainly as speed tricks and as a signal of where inference cost is heading.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    Every chat model you have used works the same way. It reads the text so far, produces one probability table over its vocabulary, picks one token, appends it, and repeats. Fifty tokens of answer means fifty passes through the network, strictly in order. The model cannot go back and rewrite the third word once it has written the tenth, and it cannot write the end before the beginning.

    That design is called autoregressive, and it has two costs. Speed: the passes are sequential, so a long answer takes a long time even on a fast chip, and a GPU spends much of that time waiting on memory rather than computing. Rigidity: the model commits as it goes. Two further oddities come from the input side: text is first chopped into tokens by a fixed tokenizer (M1.1), which treats Arabic, numbers and typos unevenly.

    This lesson covers three ways of relaxing those rules.

    IdeaWhat changesThe promiseThe price
    Diffusion language modelStart with a fully masked answer and fill in many positions per pass, over a few refinement rounds.Fewer passes for the same length. Can edit anywhere.Tokens decided together can contradict each other. Each pass is heavier.
    Tokenizer-free (byte-level) modelRead raw bytes, grouped into patches by the model itself, not by a fixed vocabulary.No tokenizer bias, robust to typos and scripts, one model for every language.Sequences become several times longer unless the architecture compensates.
    Multi-token predictionTrain extra output heads that guess tokens two, three, four steps ahead.Richer training signal, and a built-in draft for faster decoding.Extra heads, and the later guesses are less reliable.
    Hold onto thisSpeed in an LLM is mostly counted in sequential passes, not in arithmetic. Anything that lets one pass settle several tokens at once, or lets a pass be checked rather than recomputed, cuts latency. The three ideas here are three different routes to the same goal, and each hits the same wall: tokens are not independent, so deciding them together risks incoherence.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    Diffusion language models: filling in a masked draft

    Image diffusion models start from pure noise and remove it step by step. Text is made of discrete tokens, and you cannot add a little Gaussian noise to the word "transfer". The workable text version replaces noise with masking. The forward (noising) process takes a real sentence and hides each token independently with probability t, where t runs from 0 (nothing hidden) to 1 (everything is a [MASK]). The model is trained to undo it: given a partly masked sentence, predict the hidden tokens.

    This is the family called masked diffusion (discrete diffusion). LLaDA, the first large open model of this kind, trains it like this: sample t at random for each training sentence, mask that fraction of tokens, and score the model only on predicting the masked ones. The loss is a weighted cross-entropy, and it is a valid bound on the likelihood of the text, which is why people consider it a principled language model and not a trick (Nie et al., 2025; Sahoo et al., 2024).

    ℒ=−Et,x,xt[1t∑i:xt[i]=MASKlogpθ(x[i]∣xt)]

    Take a real text x, hide a random share t of its tokens to get xt, and ask the model to recover the hidden ones. The 1/t weight keeps lightly masked and heavily masked examples balanced. Compare with the ordinary language-model loss of F1.6: there the model sees only the left context; here it sees all unmasked tokens on both sides.

    Generation runs the process backwards. Begin with a block of, say, 256 [MASK] tokens after your prompt. At each of T steps, the model predicts a token for every masked position at once. You keep only some of those predictions, usually the most confident, leave the rest masked, and go again. Some systems also re-mask tokens they had committed to if later steps look inconsistent. After T steps, nothing is masked. If T is much smaller than the length, you needed far fewer sequential passes than an autoregressive model.

    Three consequences follow from the design, and they explain almost every claim you will read.

    • Bidirectional context. A masked position sees tokens to its left and right. This is why LLaDA handles tasks where order is awkward: the authors reported it beat GPT-4o on a reversal task (completing a poem line given the next line), the so-called reversal curse that trips up left-to-right models (Nie et al., 2025).
    • No simple KV cache. An autoregressive model reuses the stored keys and values of past tokens (M1.3). In a diffusion model every pass can change the whole sequence, so the cache is not straightforward. Each pass costs more than one autoregressive step. Research on approximate caching and on block diffusion, which writes blocks left to right but fills each block in parallel, narrows the gap (Arriola et al., 2025).
    • Quality-speed dial. More steps give better text, fewer steps give faster text. That is a knob autoregressive models do not have. It is also how you should read any speed headline: at what number of steps, and what quality?

    The wall: tokens decided together are not independent

    Here is the central difficulty. When the model fills two masked positions in the same pass, it computes a probability for each position separately, then samples them separately. It does not condition the second on what the first turned out to be. If the two tokens depend on each other, they can clash.

    Take a question where the answer is either "New York" or "Los Angeles", equally likely. Position 1 is "New" half the time and "Los" half the time. Position 2 is "York" half the time and "Angeles" half the time. Sample both independently and you get "New Angeles" or "Los York" half the time. An autoregressive model never has this problem, because the second word is chosen after the first is fixed.

    In general, if m equally likely phrasings exist and you fix k positions in one pass, independent sampling produces a coherent phrase with probability m1−k. Only one of the mk combinations per phrasing is consistent: m good ones out of mk.

    Practical decoders therefore choose how many tokens to fix adaptively: commit a position when the model is very sure of it (a confidence threshold), keep uncertain ones masked for later rounds, and sometimes re-mask. Easy stretches, like boilerplate text, go in huge chunks. A bank reference number or an amount, where one wrong digit is a different fact, gets settled slowly. This is also why diffusion models look best on code and templated text, and why reported speeds depend so much on the task.

    Who is building them

    Three names recur, and they are different kinds of thing.

    • LLaDA (Renmin University and Ant Group researchers): an 8-billion-parameter masked diffusion model trained from scratch and released with open weights. Its significance was proof of scale: it was competitive with Llama 3 8B on many benchmarks in the authors' tests, so diffusion was not only a small-model curiosity. Later versions scaled up, with a 100-billion-parameter variant built by converting an existing autoregressive model, a pattern now common.
    • Mercury (Inception Labs): the first commercial diffusion LLMs, aimed at coding and low-latency use, served through an API, with throughput of the order of a thousand tokens per second reported by the company on modern GPUs.
    • Gemini Diffusion (Google DeepMind): an experimental model shown in May 2025, reported at roughly 1,500 tokens per second in Google's demonstrations, with quality about that of a small Gemini Flash-class model. Not a general product at the time of writing; treat any figure as vendor-reported.

    Figures like these are best read as "several times faster than an autoregressive model of similar quality on easy-to-parallelise tasks", not as a universal result.

    Tokenizer-free models: reading bytes

    A tokenizer (M1.1) is a lookup table learned once, before training, that breaks text into frequent chunks. It is cheap and compresses well, but it is a separate, frozen component with side effects you have seen: a rare word, a typo, an IBAN or an Arabic phrase can shatter into many small pieces; numbers are split inconsistently, which hurts arithmetic; and a model trained mostly on English tokens spends more tokens, and so more cost and context, on other scripts.

    The tokenizer-free idea is to feed the model bytes: the 256 possible values of UTF-8 encoding. There is no vocabulary to be unfair to anyone and no out-of-vocabulary word. The catch is length. In UTF-8, English letters take 1 byte but Arabic letters take 2, so a byte sequence is much longer than a token sequence, and attention cost grows with the square of length (F5.3). Reading everything byte by byte with a full transformer is expensive.

    The modern answer is hierarchical, patch-based models. A small, cheap network reads the raw bytes and groups them into patches; a large network works on the patches; a small network turns patches back into bytes. The Byte Latent Transformer from Meta (Pagnoni et al., 2024) makes the patch boundary dynamic, using the entropy of a small byte model (F1.7): where the next byte is easy to predict, such as the rest of a common word, it makes a long patch; where it is uncertain, such as the start of a new word or a number, it makes a short one. Compute is then spent where the text is surprising. The authors reported matching tokenizer-based Llama 3 models at up to 8 billion parameters while using up to about half the inference compute, and showed better robustness to noisy input. Other lines of work include MegaByte, MambaByte (state-space models handle long byte sequences cheaply) and H-Net (Hwang et al., 2025), which learns the chunking end to end.

    For a Gulf bank, the appeal is concrete: one model over Arabic, English and mixed text without a tokenizer that favours English, and robustness to the noise real data contains (OCR errors, transliterated names, typos in free-text fields). That is the pitch, not the present reality. See Part 4.

    Multi-token prediction: guess ahead, then check

    Standard training asks the model one question per position: what is the next token? Multi-token prediction (MTP) adds extra output heads that also predict the token two, three or four steps ahead, all from the same internal state. Gloeckle et al. (2024) found that this improved code and reasoning-style tasks at larger model sizes, and gave a model whose extra heads can be used at inference. DeepSeek-V3 adopted a variant: one extra module predicting one more token, used during training to give a denser signal (DeepSeek-AI, 2024).

    The inference trick is speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023). Cheaply draft a few next tokens, then run the full model once over the draft to check all of them in parallel. Keep the drafted tokens up to the first one the full model disagrees with, and take the model's own token there. The output is the same as if the full model had written it alone, but several tokens may arrive for one full pass. With MTP the model drafts for itself, with no separate small model.

    If each drafted token is accepted with probability a, and a token can only be accepted if the ones before it were, then with k drafted tokens the expected number of tokens per full pass is:

    E=1+a+a2+…+ak=1−ak+11−a

    The leading 1 is the token you always get. The first draft adds a on average, the second adds a2 (it needs the first to be right too), and so on. With a = 0.87 and one extra token (k = 1) this is 1.87 tokens per pass, close to the 1.8 times speed-up DeepSeek reported with acceptance rates of 85 to 90% on its second token.

    Notice what is shared. Diffusion, speculative decoding and MTP all trade more arithmetic per pass for fewer sequential passes, which suits modern GPUs, where memory traffic rather than arithmetic is the bottleneck. They differ in whether the output is guaranteed identical: speculative decoding with verification is lossless; diffusion with fewer steps is a different (and possibly worse) sampler.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    These ideas sit on the language-model pipeline at three different points: the input (tokenizer), the training objective, and the decoding loop.

    M1.1 – M1.2 · Tokens and the transformerWhat the tokenizer does and where it hurts

    The baseline this lesson departs from. Token fertility on Arabic and numbers is the practical motivation for byte-level models.

    M1.3 · Context windows and the KV cacheWhy diffusion lacks a simple cache

    The KV cache is why autoregressive decoding is cheap per token. Understanding it explains why diffusion passes are heavier and why block diffusion exists.

    M1.4 · Pretraining then post-trainingWhere a new objective plugs in

    LLaDA-style models are pretrained with a masked objective and then instruction-tuned. Converting an existing autoregressive model is a pretraining shortcut.

    F5.3 · AttentionWhy length is expensive

    Attention scales with the square of sequence length, so byte sequences are costly and patching is not cosmetic. Diffusion uses attention without a causal mask.

    F1.6 – F1.7 · Likelihood and entropyCross-entropy and surprise

    The masked loss is a likelihood bound. Entropy-based patching sends compute to high-surprise bytes.

    R1.1 – R1.2 · Mixture-of-experts and challengersOther routes to cheaper inference

    MoE saves arithmetic per token; this lesson saves passes. They combine, and the next lesson asks whether reasoning needs tokens at all.

    R1.5 · Reasoning in latent spaceAnother departure from token-by-token

    Coconut-style models think in continuous vectors instead of written tokens: a different way of loosening the same rule.

    A5 · Inference economicsWhy inference got cheap

    Speculative decoding and MTP are part of the serving toolbox. See also reasoning models and test-time compute, where long outputs make latency painful.

    M2.3 · Model choice, cost and latencyWhere this shows up in a buying decision

    Latency per token is a vendor feature you can ask about. Diffusion APIs sell on it.

    A14 · Arabic LLMsArabic and tokenization fairness

    Byte-level and tokenizer-light designs speak directly to the Arabic cost and quality gap.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Contact-centre and chat assistants. Latency is felt directly. A faster decoder, whether diffusion or an MTP-enabled model, is a customer-experience feature, and speculative decoding is already a vendor-side setting to ask about.
    • Code assistants. Diffusion's early strength is code and structured output, the exact use in analytics and engineering teams (A15).
    • Arabic and mixed-script documents. Names, IBANs, amounts and OCR noise are where tokenizers fragment. Byte-level robustness is the research answer; today the practical answer is to test your chosen model on your own Arabic text.
    • Structured extraction. Tasks that fill a fixed schema from a document suit parallel filling, and also expose the "one wrong digit" risk. Validate extracted fields independently.
    • Procurement. When a vendor claims "10x faster", you now know to ask: at what quality, which task, how many steps, which hardware, and is the output guaranteed identical?
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The honest summary: all three are real and shipped in some form, but none has displaced the autoregressive transformer as the default for frontier general-purpose models. Multi-token prediction is the most deployed, diffusion the most hyped, byte-level models the least mature commercially.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Mostly these are failures of interpretation: of speed claims, of benchmark comparisons and of what "tokenizer-free" buys.

    • Quoting tokens per second without quality or steps

      "1,000 tokens per second" for a diffusion model at few steps says nothing about whether it answers as well as a slower model.

      Spot itAsk for the benchmark score at that speed, the number of denoising steps, the hardware, the batch size and the prompt length.
    • Incoherence at fixed positions

      Parallel decoding yields mismatched pairs: a name from one candidate and a surname from another, a currency from one clause and an amount from another.

      Spot itTest on structured outputs with dependent fields. Check consistency between related fields automatically.
    • Digit and identifier errors

      One wrong digit in an IBAN or amount is a different fact, and parallel filling plus weak numeric reasoning makes this likelier than the headline accuracy suggests.

      Spot itRound-trip tests: extract, re-render, compare with the source; checksum IBANs; reconcile totals.
    • Comparing against a weak autoregressive baseline

      A diffusion model beats "a similar-size model" that is older or untuned.

      Spot itCheck the baseline's release date, size and training tokens. Prefer a head-to-head on your own tasks.
    • Assuming bytes mean no language bias

      Byte-level models remove tokenizer bias but not data bias. A model trained on 95% English data still does worse in Arabic.

      Spot itEvaluate per language and per dialect, including Gulf Arabic, on your own content.
    • Ignoring the longer sequences

      The byte-level model looks free of tokenizer cost, until the latency and memory bill for sequences several times longer arrives.

      Spot itAsk for compute per character or per byte of text, not per token, when comparing.
    • MTP acceptance that collapses off-distribution

      Draft acceptance measured on chat falls on rare legal Arabic or numeric tables, and the promised speed-up shrinks.

      Spot itMeasure tokens per pass on your own prompts, not the vendor's.
    • Assuming lossless when it is not

      Speculative decoding with verification gives the same distribution; reducing diffusion steps or loosening acceptance thresholds does not.

      Spot itAsk: is the output guaranteed identical to the base model, or only usually similar?
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To an executive · 30 seconds
    “Today's chatbots write one word at a time, so long answers are slow. Newer designs write many words at once, like refining a draft, and vendors report several times the speed. That helps live customer chat and coding tools. But writing words together can make them inconsistent, and in banking one wrong digit matters. So we would pilot them on low-risk, latency-sensitive tasks and test accuracy on our own documents before trusting any speed claim.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “Masked diffusion language models train a bidirectional denoiser with a weighted masked cross-entropy, then decode by iteratively unmasking high-confidence positions. They cut sequential passes but lose the KV cache and decide tokens in parallel, so there is a coherence cost for coupled tokens; block diffusion and adaptive thresholds mitigate it. Byte-level models such as BLT use entropy-based patching to avoid the quadratic cost of long byte sequences. MTP adds heads for future tokens and doubles as a self-draft for lossless speculative decoding. I'd evaluate each on task accuracy at matched latency, not on tokens per second alone.”
    “Will diffusion models replace GPT-style models?”

    Not on current evidence. They have matched autoregressive models at small and mid sizes and are very fast on easy-to-parallelise tasks, and the 2026 trend is to convert existing models. But the ecosystem, tooling, long-context behaviour and frontier reasoning are all autoregressive-first. The likelier future is both: diffusion or hybrid decoders for latency-critical and editing tasks, autoregressive for the rest.

    “Why can't a diffusion model just write everything in one step?”

    Because tokens depend on each other. Fixing every position at once from independent probabilities produces clashes, like "New Angeles". The more steps, the more each token can condition on already-fixed neighbours. Fewer steps means faster and less coherent. Good decoders fix only tokens they are very sure of.

    “Is a tokenizer-free model better for Arabic?”

    In principle it removes a source of unfairness, since a fixed vocabulary built mostly from English splits Arabic into more pieces. In practice quality still depends on training data, and byte sequences for Arabic are about twice as long as the characters, so you need patching to keep cost sane. I would test on our own Arabic and mixed-script documents rather than assume it.

    “Does speculative decoding change the answers?”

    Not when done correctly with verification: the accepted output has the same distribution as the base model. What changes is how many tokens you get per full pass. It is therefore one of the few speed-ups with no quality trade-off, which is why it is widely used in serving.

    “A vendor says their diffusion model is ten times faster. What do you ask?”

    Faster than what, at what quality, on which task. Number of steps, hardware, batch size, prompt and output length. Benchmark results at that setting, ideally our own test set, including Arabic and numeric extraction. And whether the output is guaranteed equal to a reference model or only similar.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer. The numeric ones need only the formulas in Part 2.

    1. A model writes a 256-token answer. How many sequential passes does an autoregressive model need, and a diffusion model run for 32 steps? What is the ideal speed-up, and why might the real one be smaller?

    256 against 32: an ideal 8 times. Real speed-up is smaller because each diffusion pass processes the whole sequence without a simple KV cache, so it costs more than one autoregressive step, and because quality may force more steps.

    2. Four tokens in a chunk are fixed in one pass and there are 3 equally likely coupled phrasings. What is the chance the chunk is coherent?

    m1−k = 3−3 = 1/27, about 3.7%. Only the 3 consistent combinations out of 34 = 81 count. This is why adaptive, confidence-based unmasking matters.

    3. Why does masked diffusion see "both sides" of a position, and what task does this help?

    The model predicts masked tokens given all unmasked tokens, left and right, without a causal mask. It helps tasks like infilling and reversal-type questions that left-to-right models handle poorly, such as completing a line given the line after it.

    4. An Arabic sentence has 19 characters including 3 spaces. How many UTF-8 bytes does it have, and what does that say about byte-level models?

    16 letters at 2 bytes plus 3 spaces at 1 byte is 35 bytes, so about 1.8 bytes per character against 1 for English. Byte sequences for Arabic are longer, so a naive byte model spends more compute on Arabic; patching is how modern byte-level models contain that.

    5. With patches of 4 bytes instead of raw bytes, roughly how much does attention work over the central model fall?

    Sequence length falls by 4, attention work by about 42 = 16 times, to about 6% of the byte-by-byte cost. This ignores the small byte-level encoder and decoder that still run.

    6. MTP with one drafted token and 88% acceptance. How many tokens per full pass, and what if the drafting costs 5% of a pass?

    E = 1 + 0.88 = 1.88 tokens per pass. Net speed-up = 1.88 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 1.79 times, consistent with the roughly 1.8 times DeepSeek reported.

    7. What is the difference between speculative decoding and running a diffusion model with fewer steps, in terms of output quality guarantees?

    Speculative decoding verifies drafts with the full model and keeps only what it would have produced, so the output distribution is unchanged. Fewer diffusion steps is a different sampler whose quality falls as steps drop. One is lossless; the other is a dial.
