    Diffusion Language Models and Multi-Token Prediction

    Part 1

    What these ideas are

    In one sentenceToday's chat models write one token at a time, left to right. Diffusion models and multi-token prediction try to produce several tokens per pass so answers arrive faster.

    Running example: the sentence "Please send the money today". A token is a word piece. A standard model (called autoregressive) is a typist who can never go back, so five tokens need five passes, and it cannot fix word 2 after writing word 4.

    The three ideas

    • Diffusion: start with blanks and fill many positions per pass.
    • Multi-token prediction (MTP): guess a few tokens ahead, then check the guesses.
    • Byte-level models: drop the fixed vocabulary and read raw bytes. This one is about reading text, not speed.

    All the speed ideas hit the same wall: tokens are not independent, so choosing them together risks text that does not fit together.

    Part 2

    Diffusion language models

    Think of a crossword: fill in the answers you are sure of first, and they help with the rest. Image diffusion removes noise, but you cannot add "a little noise" to the word "transfer", so text diffusion uses masking instead.

    1. Train by hiding. For each sentence, pick a random share of tokens (none to all), hide them and score the model only on the hidden ones.
    2. Start fully masked. At generation, put, say, 256 blank slots after the prompt.
    3. Predict every blank at once. The model guesses all masked positions in one pass.
    4. Keep the confident ones. The rest stay masked for the next round. Some decoders can also re-mask a token that later looks wrong.
    5. Stop after T rounds. If T is well below the length, you saved passes.

    How to read it

    • Steps are a dial. More steps give better text, fewer give faster text. Read any speed headline as "at how many steps?"
    • Passes saved is not time saved. Autoregressive models reuse a stored cache of past work. In diffusion any pass can change everything, so each pass costs more.
    • Both sides visible. Every position sees text on its left and right, which helps infilling ("rewrite the middle").

    The wall: tokens decided together

    Two people each name half a city without hearing each other: one says "New" or "Los", the other "York" or "Angeles". Half the time you get "New Angeles". A model fixing two positions in one pass can do the same. So real decoders commit a position only when the model is very sure. Boilerplate goes fast. An amount or account number, where one wrong digit is a different fact, goes slowly.

    Who has it: LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025) is the open research model, 8B parameters trained from scratch, which the authors showed reaching roughly Llama 3 8B-level benchmarks. Mercury (Inception Labs) is a commercial diffusion model for coding and low latency. Gemini Diffusion (Google DeepMind) was an experiment shown in 2025, not a general product. Speed figures are vendor-reported.

    Part 3

    Multi-token prediction and byte-level models

    Multi-token prediction adds extra output heads that also guess the tokens after the next one. In training this gives a denser learning signal. At serving time the guesses become drafts, a method called speculative decoding.

    • Lossless: the output is identical to the full model alone, because every draft is verified.
    • Gain depends on acceptance. If 87% of drafts are accepted, one draft gives about 1.8 times faster decoding (DeepSeek-V3 reported a similar figure). Each extra draft needs all earlier ones right, so the curve flattens fast. On unfamiliar text acceptance falls and extra drafts stop paying.

    Byte-level models address a different problem. A tokenizer is a frozen dictionary of frequent chunks, and anything outside it shatters: typos, IBANs and Arabic phrases split into many pieces, costing money and context. Reading raw bytes means nothing is out of vocabulary. The catch is length: Arabic letters take two bytes each. Meta's Byte Latent Transformer groups bytes into patches, long for predictable text and short for surprising text, and its authors reported matching tokenised Llama 3 models up to 8B parameters with less inference compute.

    Part 4

    Which to use

    OptionStrong atWeak at
    Standard autoregressiveBest quality, tooling and support todayLatency, when untuned
    Speculative decoding / MTPFaster serving, identical outputLarge batches or unusual text (low acceptance)
    Diffusion LLMCode, templates, infilling, low latencyExact amounts and IDs; little tooling
    Byte-level modelNoisy, multilingual inputLong sequences; not a supported product
    Better tokenizer or smaller modelArabic costing too many tokens; cost controlNeeding a new architecture's properties

    Usable today? Speculative decoding is live in serving stacks, usually invisibly. Commercial diffusion is pilot-stage. Byte-level models are research. Try speculative decoding first, and measure tokens per pass on your own prompts. For diffusion, run a head-to-head against a tuned autoregressive baseline and report quality at each step count.

    In banking, latency matters in chat and contact centres, but fields like IBANs and amounts are exactly where parallel decoding is weakest, so validate each field with checksums and round-trip checks.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Speed quoted without quality or steps

      "1,000 tokens per second" at few steps says nothing about answer quality. Ask for the benchmark score at that speed.

    • Assuming "lossless" when it is not

      Verified speculative decoding keeps the output the same. Fewer diffusion steps do not.

    • A weak baseline

      A diffusion model that beats an older or untuned model of similar size proves little. Compare on your own tasks.

    • Believing bytes mean no bias

      Bytes remove tokenizer bias, not data bias, and sequences get much longer. Evaluate per language, including Gulf Arabic.

    1. Why is the real diffusion speed-up usually smaller than the passes saved?

    Each pass handles the whole sequence without a simple cache, and quality may force more steps.

    2. Why do tokens fixed in the same pass risk clashing?

    Each is chosen without seeing the others. Hence adaptive decoding: commit only the confident positions.

    3. How do speculative decoding and fewer diffusion steps differ in quality guarantees?

    Speculative decoding verifies drafts with the full model, so the output is unchanged. Fewer diffusion steps is a quality dial.

    4. Why do patches make byte-level models practical?

    They shorten the sequence the large model reads, cutting attention work sharply. Otherwise Arabic's extra bytes per letter would make it costlier than English.

    5. Your chatbot is too slow and you have a week. What do you try first?

    Speculative decoding. It is already supported in serving stacks and leaves the output identical, so there is no new model-risk question.
