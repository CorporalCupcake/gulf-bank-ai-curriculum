    Sanctions Screening, Name Matching and the Road from Alert to Report

    Why one Arabic name has a dozen Latin spellings, how matching trades missed hits against a flood of false alarms, and how an alert becomes (or does not become) a suspicious activity report.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceScreening is a search for a few genuine matches hidden among millions of near-misses, so the work is in making names comparable (normalisation, transliteration, secondary identifiers), choosing a threshold you can defend, and then spending scarce analyst time where a report is most likely to be needed, with every step explainable to a regulator.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A sanctions list is a list of names. A bank must not do business with the people and entities on it, and must act without delay if it finds one. So every customer at onboarding, every payment as it moves, and every list update overnight is checked against those names. That sounds like a lookup. It is not, because the same person has no single spelling.

    Take the common Arabic name محمد. In Latin letters you will meet Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed, Mohammad, Mohamad, Mahomet, Mhd, Mouhamed and more. Arabic is written without the short vowels, so a clerk must guess them. Gulf, Levantine, North African and French-influenced spelling habits differ. A passport may say one thing, a payment message another, the list a third. A system that looks for exact matches misses real hits. A system that is loose enough to catch them also flags thousands of innocent people called Mohammed Ali.

    Real sanctions hits are very rare. Real false alarms are common. That is the base-rate problem from imbalanced data, in its purest form: you hunt for a handful of needles, and every loosening of the net brings in more hay. Everything in this lesson is a way of making the net smarter rather than just looser.

    Two ideas to take from it.

    • Matching is a design choice, not a measurement. Each method defines "similar" differently. None is right. Each is a bet about what kind of variation your data contains.
    • The threshold is a regulatory decision wearing a number. Set too high, you miss a listed person and you may have breached a legal duty. Set too low, a team of analysts drowns and real hits get lost in the pile. Part 2 shows the shape of that trade.

    The second half of the lesson looks at what happens after an alert fires, from sanctions or from transaction monitoring (B2.1 – B2.2). Someone has to decide whether it is real, and if there are grounds for suspicion, a report goes to the financial intelligence unit. Most alerts die along the way. The question for an AI team is which ones, and who decides.

    Hold onto thisA screening system has three jobs: make names comparable, score the comparison, and decide what a human sees. Most gains come from the first and third. Fancy scoring on badly prepared names helps little, and a perfect score still needs a person to say what it means.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What is screened, against what, and when

    Start with the plumbing, because most real problems live there.

    • The lists. UN Security Council consolidated list, national lists (in the UAE, the Local Terrorist List alongside the UN list, under the Executive Office for Control and Non-Proliferation, EOCN), plus whatever a bank chooses to honour from OFAC, the EU and the UK for correspondent banking reasons. Which lists are legally binding and which are risk appetite differs, and the guidance changes. Check the current text; do not rely on a lesson.
    • What gets screened. Customer names at onboarding and on every list update (the "delta" run). Counterparty names and free-text fields in payments. Beneficial owners and directors of companies. Vessels, banks and countries in trade finance documents. For entities there is also ownership: OFAC's 50 percent rule treats an entity as blocked if listed parties own half or more of it, and the list itself will not tell you.
    • Real-time versus batch. A payment must be held or released within seconds, so payment screening is low-latency with a hold-and-review queue. Customer base screening is a nightly batch against list changes.
    • What the list gives you. A primary name, aliases (some good, some poor), a date or year of birth, nationality, sometimes a passport or national ID, sometimes only a name. The more identifiers on both sides, the easier the decision.

    The core operation, repeated billions of times: for a name string on our side, find list entries whose names are close enough to deserve a human look.

    Making names comparable: normalisation and transliteration

    Before any scoring, clean both sides into a common form. For Arabic and Arabic-origin names this is where most of the value is.

    Within Arabic script. Strip diacritics (tashkeel) and the stretching letter tatweel. Fold the alef forms أ إ آ into ا. Fold the final ى into ي and the ة into ه, which writers use inconsistently. Normalise Arabic-Indic digits. These steps are cheap and safe.

    Name structure. A Gulf name is usually a chain: given name, father's name, grandfather's name, then a family or tribal name, with particles like bin or bint (son or daughter of), Abu, Al (the, often part of the family name) and compounds such as Abd al-Rahman that are one name written as two or three words. Western systems expect "first, middle, last". Order varies between the passport, the list and the payment message. A good matcher compares sets of name parts and tolerates reordering and omission (a list entry may have four parts and the customer record only two).

    Across scripts. The hard part. Arabic to Latin is not a function, it is a relation: the same Arabic string has many acceptable Latin renderings, and the same Latin string can come from several Arabic ones. The reasons are mechanical.

    • Missing vowels. محمد is written m-h-m-d. Muhammad, Mohammed, Mohamed and Mohamad are all the same four letters. Mahmoud (محمود) is a different name that adds one long-vowel letter; a Latin consonant skeleton throws that letter away, so the two collide.
    • One letter, several sounds. خ may be kh, h or ch. ج is j in formal Arabic, g in Egyptian and some Gulf speech, and dj in French-influenced writing. ق is q, k, g or a glottal stop depending on dialect and habit. ش is sh, ch (French) or s (a lazy skeleton).
    • One sound, several letters. ح and ه both map to h, ع and أ both give a vowel-like or apostrophe sound.
    • Competing conventions. ISO 233, BGN/PCGN, DIN 31635, the UN's own romanisation, and the passport office's habit, which is often just whatever the officer typed.

    There are two broad responses. Many-to-many: generate plausible Latin variants of the Arabic name and plausible Arabic originals of the Latin name, and compare across the set. Common space: reduce both to a canonical form, such as the consonant skeleton in Simulation 1, so spelling variance disappears before scoring. The second is cheaper and stronger at recall, and, as the simulation showed, it also creates collisions. Mahmoud and Muhammad share a skeleton.

    Scoring: edit distance, phonetics, tokens and learned models

    Once names are cleaned, you need a number for "how close". The standard tools, from oldest to newest:

    Production systems combine them. A typical pipeline: normalise, block, score, decide. Blocking means using a cheap test (shared phonetic code, shared rare n-gram, shared token) to pick a few hundred candidates out of a list of hundreds of thousands, so the expensive scorer runs on only those. It is the same retrieve-then-rerank pattern as in hybrid search. Your recall can never exceed the recall of your blocking step, and nobody notices when blocking quietly drops a hit.

    Statistically, this is the oldest problem in the data-matching literature. Fellegi and Sunter (1969) framed record linkage as a decision between "match", "non-match" and "possible match", with each field contributing a weight based on how often it agrees among true matches against how often it agrees by chance. Agreement on a rare surname is strong evidence; agreement on "Mohammed" is almost none. That idea of weighting by rarity is still the heart of every good matcher, and it is Bayes' rule (F1.4) applied to name parts. The "possible match" band is the human review queue.

    The threshold: why alerts and misses trade off

    Each screening produces a best score against the list. Innocent names produce a spread of scores, centred somewhere in the middle, with a long upper tail of coincidentally similar names. Genuine matches sit higher, but not cleanly: those with unusual transliteration or missing parts score lower and sit inside the innocent tail. Where you draw the line decides both numbers at once.

    A comfortable intuition from precision, recall and thresholds applies, with one harsh difference. In fraud, a missed case costs a known amount of money and you can weigh it against review cost. In sanctions, a miss is a possible legal breach, with penalties and reputational damage that do not fit a simple per-case figure. So the usual cost-minimising logic gives way to a risk-appetite decision, documented, tested and approved by compliance. The data science job is to show the consequences clearly.

    Three lessons sit in that picture.

    • Hard hits are exactly where innocent names are densest. You cannot fix this by moving the line. You fix it by moving the curves apart, which means better normalisation and more evidence than the name alone.
    • You cannot measure your miss rate from your alerts. The true hits you never alerted on are invisible. Testing means inserting known variants of listed names and seeing whether they fire (sometimes called above-the-line and below-the-line testing), plus periodic sampling of near-threshold scores. Regulators and auditors expect this documented, and it is the closest thing to a ground truth you will get.
    • The cost of one more percentage point of recall can be thousands of alerts. Make the exchange rate visible: "to catch two more hard variants a month we review 11,000 more alerts" is a sentence leaders can decide on.

    Adding evidence: secondary identifiers and risk-ranked queues

    The name is weak evidence. Most false alerts die as soon as you compare something else: date of birth, nationality, a national ID or passport number, a country of residence, a company's registration number. A person born in 1984 cannot be a listed person born in 1961. Three levers follow, each with a cost.

    • Better name matching (transliteration-aware). Lifts recall on hard variants, but pulls more innocent names in as well.
    • Secondary identifiers. Automatically closes alerts where both sides have a date of birth and they disagree. It only works where the list entry actually has one, and a mis-keyed customer date of birth could wrongly clear a true match. Good practice is to allow tolerance, such as year-only or day-month swaps, and to log every automatic close.
    • A model that ranks alerts using past analyst decisions. Used as a fast lane (a quicker look at low-risk alerts) it saves time with no recall loss. Used as an auto-close it removes humans from a legal obligation and needs strong validation and sign-off. Many regulators are cautious here; ask your compliance team what is permitted.

    Notice what the toggles do not do. None of them removes the need for the threshold, and each one carries a failure that is silent on the alert queue: the identifier rule can clear a true hit and nobody sees the alert it never raised. That is why every automated closure needs an audit trail and a periodic sample re-reviewed by a person.

    From alert to report: triage and the suspicious activity report workflow

    The same pattern runs after any alert, whether from name screening, transaction monitoring rules or an ML model (B2.1 – B2.2), or from a staff referral. Sanctions matches follow their own legal route (freeze, then report to the authority); suspicion-based alerts follow the suspicious transaction or activity report route (STR or SAR, the terms are used interchangeably, and the regimes are described below). Click through the stages.

    Four properties make this workflow different from most ML problems.

    • The unit of evidence is a case, not a row. An investigator looks at the customer, the account history, the counterparties, the stated business purpose, adverse media. A "good" model feature set is one that mimics that file, which is why entity-level history and network features beat transaction-level scores.
    • Reasons are part of the product. A filing must explain why the activity is suspicious, in prose, to a reader at the financial intelligence unit who has never seen the account. A score with no reasons cannot be filed. This is where explanations (local explanations) and, carefully, language models for drafting narratives matter.
    • You file on suspicion, not proof. The legal test is reasonable grounds to suspect. A weak SAR is not a good outcome and a missed one can be an offence, so the system errs toward over-reporting at the margin, but a high volume of poor-quality reports is also criticised. The measurable output is usefulness to law enforcement, which the bank mostly never sees.
    • You must not tip off. The customer must not learn that a report exists. That limits what a copilot or a customer-facing system may say, and what you may write in a shared log.

    In the UAE, reports go to the Financial Intelligence Unit through the goAML platform; Saudi Arabia has its own financial intelligence unit and channels. Timelines and report types are set by law and guidance and have been revised recently. State them to colleagues only after reading the current text from your compliance team.

    The gain curve is the practical meaning of "AI for alert triage": same team, same alerts, a different order. It is also the point where feedback loops bite. Your training labels are the outcomes of the alerts someone reviewed. If a model learns to rank, analysts follow its order, the lowest-ranked alerts never get reviewed, and the model never learns whether it was wrong about them. Keep a random sample of low-ranked alerts reviewed, on purpose, forever.

    Part 3

    How it connects

    Screening and triage sit where text matching, rare-event statistics, regulation and human process meet.

    B2.1 – B2.2 · Transaction monitoringWhere most alerts come from

    Monitoring generates the bulk of the alert queue that Simulation 4 triages. Screening and monitoring feed the same case-management and reporting process.

    B3 · Customer due diligenceIdentifiers come from KYC

    Dates of birth, nationality, ownership chains and risk ratings are collected at onboarding. Poor KYC data caps how much Simulation 3's identifier lever can save.

    F7 · Precision, recall, thresholdsThe same trade, different stakes

    Screening is precision and recall at a threshold. The difference is that recall here has a legal floor.

    F3.5 · Imbalanced dataA dozen needles in a million

    Base rates, wide intervals on tiny positive counts, and the habit of testing with known positives all carry over directly.

    F1 · F5 · Embeddings and contrastive learningLearning name similarity

    Name embeddings trained so that variants of one name sit close together are contrastive learning on pairs; vector geometry explains why nearest-neighbour search can then replace slow pairwise scoring.

    M3 · RetrievalBlocking is retrieval

    Candidate selection followed by a precise re-scorer is the same architecture as search. Hybrid methods combine exact tokens, n-grams and embeddings.

    M1 · TokenisationArabic text in models

    Subword tokenisers split Arabic names unpredictably, which is one reason general language models are shaky on name matching.

    A14 · Arabic NLPArabic language models

    Arabic-first models understand the script and dialect better; see also Arabic OCR, since names often reach screening through scanned documents with extraction errors.

    A13 · Payment dataStructured names in payments

    Richer, structured payment messages give the screener separate fields for names, addresses and identifiers, which makes matching easier than parsing free text.

    B1 · Labels and feedbackAlert outcomes as training data

    Models trained on analyst decisions inherit selective labels. Reports filed are a thin and delayed signal of what was truly criminal.

    G3 · ValidationModels must be validated

    A screening matcher or alert-ranking model is a model for governance purposes; see also ongoing monitoring and approval gates for human sign-off design.

    A9 · Fraud and scamsIdentities that are not real

    Synthetic and manipulated identities defeat identifier checks, which is why screening cannot be the only control at onboarding.

    Where it shows up at the bank:

    • Onboarding and ongoing customer screening. Individuals and companies against the lists, with directors and owners.
    • Payments. Cross-border and domestic, including trade finance documents where vessels, ports and goods are also screened. Low-latency hold queues.
    • Remittance and exchange corridors. High volumes of Arabic, South Asian and Filipino names, where transliteration noise is extreme.
    • Islamic and correspondent banking. Counterparty banks and structured products add layers of ownership to screen through.
    • Case management and reporting. One queue from monitoring, screening and staff referrals, one reporting line to the financial intelligence unit.
    Part 4

    Where the field is now

    The mathematics of matching is old and settled. What is moving is the machinery around it: learned matchers, language models for evidence summaries and narratives, and regulators' attitude to automation. Most public material on AI in screening is written by vendors, so treat figures such as "95 to 99% of alerts are false positives" as plausible and unverified. I could not trace them to a named study.

    Part 5

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most of these are quiet: nothing crashes, a hit just never fires. Each comes with the quickest check.

    • Judging the matcher by its alert volume

      The queue is manageable, so the system seems fine. But the hits that never alerted are invisible.

      Spot itAsk for the last below-the-line test: known variants of listed names, and what share fired.
    • English-first normalisation applied to Arabic names

      No alef folding, no handling of Abd-compounds, "Al" treated as a surname, middle names ignored. Arabic-script customer records may not be screened against Arabic list entries at all.

      Spot itTest with the same person in Arabic script, with and without "Al", with word order swapped, and with a compound written as one or two words.
    • Over-aggressive skeletons and phonetic codes

      Collapsing everything to consonants matches Mahmoud to Muhammad. Alert volume jumps after a "recall improvement".

      Spot itLook at alert volume by name origin before and after a change. A sudden rise concentrated in common Arabic given names is the signature.
    • Blocking that hides hits

      A pre-filter by first letter or phonetic code means a spelling that changes the first letter (Qaddafi, Kadhafi, Gaddafi) never reaches the scorer.

      Spot itTest recall at the candidate stage separately from the final scoring.
    • Identifier rules that clear true matches

      A mis-keyed or default date of birth (1 January) closes a real alert automatically.

      Spot itCount automatic closures by rule, sample them monthly, and treat placeholder dates as missing.
    • Stale or truncated lists

      A list update fails to load, or aliases are dropped by an ingestion step. No model can fix that.

      Spot itReconcile loaded entry counts to the source list after every update; alert if a delta run did not complete.
    • Training the ranker on its own decisions

      Low-ranked alerts never reviewed, so the model never learns it was wrong about them.

      Spot itAsk for the random-review sample of low-ranked alerts and its hit rate over time.
    • Copying the alert disposition as the label

      "Closed as false positive by first line" treated as ground truth. Analysts under time pressure, or a quick policy change, change the label without changing the world.

      Spot itRe-review a random sample with a senior investigator and measure agreement (see annotator agreement).
    • Language-model narratives with invented detail

      A drafted SAR narrative states a transaction or relationship that is not in the case file, and nobody checks.

      Spot itRequire every sentence to link to a source record; sample drafts against the file. See groundedness checks.
    • Leaking that a report exists

      Tools, chat summaries or customer-facing agents expose that a case or SAR is open.

      Spot itReview where case data flows, who can read it, and what an assistant is allowed to say.
    Part 6

    What to say in the room

    To a head of compliance · 30 seconds
    “Our alert volume tells us how noisy the screen is, not how many hits we are missing. I'd measure the misses directly: take a sample of listed names, create realistic Arabic and English variants, and see what fires. Then we can say, for example, that we catch 97% of easy variants and 74% of hard ones at today's threshold, and show what each extra point of recall costs in alerts. You choose the risk appetite; I make the trade visible.”
    To a technical interviewer or validator
    “I'd normalise Arabic script, handle name structure as sets of parts, and compare across scripts with a skeleton or learned matcher, always behind a blocking step whose own recall I'd test. The model's output is a score that feeds a threshold and secondary identifiers; any ranking model sits after that as a fast lane, with a random review sample of low-ranked alerts to avoid a feedback loop. Evaluation uses planted variants and below-the-line sampling, not alert counts.”
    “Why not just use an LLM to decide whether two names are the same?”

    Language models can be good at cross-script judgement, but they are slow and expensive at screening volumes, their output is hard to reproduce and explain, and their errors are silent. I'd use them as an extra scorer or reviewer on the borderline band, with deterministic matching as the backbone, and I'd test them on planted variants like anything else.

    “We get 20,000 alerts a month and 99% are false. Can AI cut that?”

    Partly. Better normalisation and secondary identifiers reduce alerts; a ranking model reduces review time and lets us put people on the likeliest cases. What it should not do silently is close alerts. First measure what a false alert costs, what share the identifier rules could already close, and how weak our list and KYC data are, since those cap the gain.

    “Is it OK to auto-close low-risk alerts with a model?”

    That's a policy and validation question before it's a modelling one. Supervisors in the region may not accept it, or may accept it with conditions. Technically I'd want a documented rationale, a validated model, a conservative threshold, a random sample of closed alerts re-reviewed by people, and a rollback. I'd ask compliance what CBUAE or SAMA currently expect.

    “How do you know the model isn't missing real hits?”

    I can't know from production alerts alone. I'd insert known listed names in realistic variants, spelling, order, script and missing parts, and measure the catch rate at every stage including blocking; sample near-threshold cases; and re-run after every list or logic change. The result is a measured miss rate with an interval, not a claim.

    “What makes a good SAR model, given we have so few reports?”

    The labels are thin and biased: only reviewed alerts get outcomes, and a filed report is not proof of crime. I'd rank for investigator usefulness rather than predict crime, use entity-level features, evaluate on gain at team capacity, keep a random sample reviewed, and watch for the model amplifying existing rules.

    Part 7

    Self-check

    Try each one before opening the answer.

    1. Why can't a bank screen names with an exact match?

    Arabic names have many acceptable Latin spellings (the script omits short vowels, letters map to several sounds, conventions differ), word order and compounds vary, and list entries and customer records carry different parts. An exact match misses real hits; the cure has to be normalisation plus similarity scoring.

    2. A skeleton method scores Mahmoud against Muhammad at 1.00. Is it broken?

    No, it did what it was designed to: remove vowel variation. The price is collisions between different names that share consonants. Identifiers, rarity weighting of name parts and a human review decide which are the same person.

    3. At a threshold of 0.85, about 7,600 alerts a month come with about 9 of 12 genuine hits. At 0.95 you get about 60 alerts and fewer than 3 hits. What is the right threshold?

    There is no purely statistical answer. It's a documented risk-appetite decision for compliance, informed by the trade-off, the legal duty, the capacity to review and the effect of other controls. The data scientist's job is to show the curve and the cost of each point, and to push the curves apart with better evidence.

    4. Why is blocking dangerous?

    It sets a ceiling on recall. Anything dropped at the candidate stage never reaches the scorer, and the alert queue shows no sign. Measure candidate-stage recall separately.

    5. Why is a ranking model safer as a fast lane than as an auto-close?

    A fast lane still has a human look at every alert, so recall is unchanged and it only saves time. Auto-close removes the human, so a model error becomes a missed obligation with no trace. It needs validation, sampling and regulatory comfort.

    6. In Simulation 4, 357 reports come from 12,000 alerts. Check the arithmetic.

    12,000 × 0.10 = 1,200 escalated; × 0.35 = 420 recommended; × 0.85 = 357 filed, which is 2.98% of alerts. Hours: 12,000 × 20/60 = 4,000 first-line, 1,200 × 6 = 7,200 investigation, 420 × 0.5 = 210 sign-off, so 11,410 in all, or about 32 hours per report.

    7. What is the feedback-loop risk in training an alert-ranking model?

    Only alerts that people review get outcomes. A model that pushes some alerts down the queue means they are never reviewed, so its mistakes on them are never seen. Keep a random review sample across all ranks to measure missed value and retrain on unbiased outcomes.

    8. What must a SAR narrative contain that a score cannot supply?

    The reasons: who, what, when, how much, why it is unusual for this customer, and what the grounds for suspicion are, written for a reader who has never seen the account. Every statement should be traceable to a record in the case file.
