    Sanctions Screening, Name Matching and the Road from Alert to Report

    Why one Arabic name has a dozen Latin spellings, how matching trades missed hits against a flood of false alarms, and how an alert becomes (or does not become) a suspicious activity report.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceScreening hunts a few genuine matches among millions of near-misses, so the work is making names comparable, choosing a defensible threshold, and spending scarce analyst time where a report is most likely, with every step explainable to a regulator.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A sanctions list is a list of names, and a bank must not deal with anyone on it. So every customer, payment and list update is checked. That sounds like a lookup, but the same person has no single spelling.

    Take محمد: Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed, Mohammad, Mhd. Arabic omits short vowels, so a clerk guesses them. Exact matching misses real hits. Loose matching flags thousands of innocent Mohammed Alis.

    Real hits are very rare and false alarms common: the base-rate problem from imbalanced data. Each loosening of the net brings in more hay. This lesson is about making the net smarter, not looser.

    • Matching is a design choice, not a measurement. Each method defines "similar" differently and is a bet about what variation your data contains.
    • The threshold is a regulatory decision wearing a number. Too high and you miss a listed person. Too low and analysts drown, and real hits get lost in the pile.

    After an alert fires, someone decides whether it is real. If there are grounds for suspicion, a report goes to the financial intelligence unit (see B2.1 – B2.2 for transaction alerts). Most alerts die on the way. The AI question is which ones, and who decides.

    Hold onto thisScreening has three jobs: make names comparable, score the comparison, decide what a human sees. Most gains come from the first and third.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What is screened, against what, and when

    Most real problems live in the plumbing.

    • The lists. The UN consolidated list, national lists (in the UAE, the Local Terrorist List), plus whatever the bank honours from OFAC, the EU and the UK. Which are legally binding changes, so check the current text.
    • What gets screened. Customers at onboarding and on every list update; payment counterparties; owners and directors. OFAC's 50 percent rule treats an entity as blocked if listed parties own half or more, and the list will not tell you.

    The core operation: for a name on our side, find list entries close enough to deserve a human look. More identifiers on both sides (date of birth, ID) make that decision easier.

    Making names comparable: normalisation and transliteration

    Clean both sides into a common form before scoring. For Arabic names this is where most of the value lies.

    Within Arabic script. Strip diacritics, fold the alef forms أ إ آ into ا, and unify letters writers mix up (ى/ي, ة/ه). Cheap and safe.

    Name structure. Gulf names are chains: given name, father, grandfather, family name, with particles like bin (son of) and Al (the). Abd al-Rahman is one name in two or three words. Order varies between documents, so a good matcher compares sets of name parts and tolerates reordering and omission.

    Across scripts. One Arabic string has many valid Latin forms, like "Chaikovsky" or "Tchaikovsky". Nobody is wrong. The main causes:

    • Missing vowels. محمد is m-h-m-d, so Muhammad, Mohammed and Mohamad are the same four letters. Mahmoud (محمود) is a different name that a consonant skeleton collides with them.
    • One letter, several sounds. خ is kh or h. ج is j, g or dj. ق is q, k or g.

    Two responses. Many-to-many: generate plausible variants of each name and compare the sets. Common space: reduce both to one canonical form, such as the consonant skeleton in Simulation 1. It is cheaper and better for recall, but creates collisions: Mahmoud and Muhammad share a skeleton.

    Scoring: edit distance, phonetics, tokens and learned models

    After cleaning, you need a number for "how close". The standard tools, oldest to newest:

    MethodIdeaGood atWeak at
    Edit distanceFewest single-character changes to turn one string into the other.Typos, a dropped or doubled letter.Reordering, vowel drift, different scripts (scores about 0).
    Phonetic codesMap names to a sound-alike code so similar-sounding spellings collide.Cheap blocking: only compare names with the same code.Built for English; coarse codes create the collisions in Simulation 1.
    Token and n-gram overlapSplit into words or character pieces and measure the overlap, as in keyword search.Reordering, missing middle names, long company names.Near-equal words; common words dominating without weighting.

    Production systems combine them: normalise, block, score, decide. Blocking is a cheap test (shared phonetic code or rare token) that picks a few hundred candidates from hundreds of thousands, so the expensive scorer runs only on those, as in hybrid search. Recall can never exceed the blocking step's recall, and nobody notices when blocking drops a hit.

    The classic framing weighs agreement by rarity: matching on a rare surname is strong evidence, matching on "Mohammed" is almost none. A middle "possible match" band becomes the human review queue.

    The threshold: why alerts and misses trade off

    Think of a smoke alarm: set it twitchy and it goes off for toast, set it sluggish and it misses the fire. Innocent names have a long upper tail of coincidentally similar scores. Genuine matches sit higher, but those with odd transliteration or missing parts score lower and sit inside that tail. One line sets both error counts at once.

    The logic matches precision, recall and thresholds, with one harsh difference. A missed sanctions hit is a possible legal breach that does not fit a per-case figure. So the choice is a documented risk-appetite decision approved by compliance.

    • Hard hits sit where innocent names are densest. Moving the line cannot fix that. Move the curves apart instead, with better normalisation and more evidence than the name.
    • You cannot measure your miss rate from your alerts. Hits you never raised are invisible. Insert known variants of listed names and check they fire. Auditors expect this.
    • The last point of recall can cost thousands of alerts. State the exchange rate: "to catch two more hard variants a month we review 11,000 more alerts" is a sentence leaders can decide on.

    Adding evidence: secondary identifiers and risk-ranked queues

    The name is weak evidence. A second fact kills most false alerts: one Ahmed Khan born in 1984 cannot be the listed one born in 1961. Three levers, each with a cost:

    • Transliteration-aware matching. Lifts recall on hard variants but pulls in more innocent names.
    • Secondary identifiers. Auto-close alerts where both dates of birth exist and disagree. It only works where the list entry has one, and a mis-keyed date could clear a true match.
    • A ranking model trained on past analyst decisions. As a fast lane (quicker look at low-risk alerts) it saves time with no recall loss. As an auto-close it removes humans from a legal obligation and needs strong validation.

    Each lever fails silently: the identifier rule can clear a true hit and nobody sees the alert it never raised. Every automated closure needs an audit trail and a periodic human re-review of a sample.

    From alert to report: triage and the suspicious activity report workflow

    Every alert follows the same path. Sanctions matches take their own legal route (freeze, then report to the authority). Suspicion-based alerts go to a suspicious transaction or activity report (STR or SAR). Click through the stages.

    Three properties set this workflow apart from most ML problems.

    • The unit of evidence is a case, not a row. Investigators read the customer, account history, counterparties and adverse media. Features that mimic that file beat transaction-level scores.
    • Reasons are part of the product. A filing must explain the suspicion in prose to a reader who has never seen the account. A score with no reasons cannot be filed (local explanations).
    • You file on suspicion, not proof, and you must not tip off. The margin errs towards reporting, and the customer must never learn a report exists. That limits what a copilot or shared log may say.

    In the UAE, reports go to the Financial Intelligence Unit via goAML. Timelines and report types change, so confirm with compliance before quoting them.

    "AI for alert triage" means same team, same alerts, different order, like an A&E triage nurse: the sickest are seen first. It also triggers a feedback loop. Labels come only from reviewed alerts, so low-ranked alerts go unreviewed and the model never learns it was wrong about them. Keep a random sample of them reviewed, permanently.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are quiet: nothing crashes, a hit just never fires. Each has a quick check.

    • Silent misses behind a healthy queue

      A manageable alert volume looks fine, but hits that never alerted are invisible. A failed list update causes the same thing.

      Spot itAsk for the last below-the-line test (known variants, share that fired) and reconcile loaded list counts to the source.
    • English-first normalisation on Arabic names

      No alef folding, no Abd-compound handling, "Al" treated as a surname.

      Spot itTest one person in Arabic script, with and without "Al", with word order swapped.
    • Skeletons and blocking that distort

      Collapsing to consonants matches Mahmoud to Muhammad, so alerts jump. A first-letter pre-filter means Qaddafi and Gaddafi may never reach the scorer.

      Spot itCompare alert volume by name origin before and after a change; test candidate-stage recall separately.
    • Identifier rules that clear true matches

      A mis-keyed or default date of birth (1 January) auto-closes a real alert.

      Spot itCount auto-closures by rule, sample them monthly, treat placeholder dates as missing.
    • Training the ranker on its own decisions

      Low-ranked alerts are never reviewed, so the model never learns it was wrong. "Closed as false positive" is also not ground truth.

      Spot itAsk for the random-review sample of low-ranked alerts, and a senior re-review of closures.
    • Invented or leaked report detail

      A language-model draft states a transaction not in the case file, or tools reveal a report is open.

      Spot itRequire each sentence to link to a source record (groundedness); review who can read case data.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can't a bank screen names with an exact match?

    Arabic names have many valid Latin spellings, and word order and name parts vary. Exact match misses real hits, so you need normalisation plus similarity scoring.

    2. A skeleton method scores Mahmoud against Muhammad at 1.00. Is it broken?

    No. It removes vowel variation by design, and the price is collisions between different names. Identifiers and a human decide who is the same person.

    3. At 0.85 you get about 7,600 alerts a month and about 9 of 12 genuine hits. At 0.95 you get about 60 alerts and fewer than 3 hits. What is the right threshold?

    There is no purely statistical answer. It is a documented risk-appetite decision for compliance. Your job is to show the curve, price each point of recall, and push the curves apart with better evidence.

    4. Why is blocking dangerous?

    It caps recall. A hit dropped at the candidate stage never reaches the scorer, and the queue shows no sign. Measure candidate-stage recall separately.

    5. Why is a ranking model safer as a fast lane than as an auto-close?

    A fast lane still has a human look at every alert, so recall is unchanged. Auto-close removes the human, so a model error becomes a silent missed obligation.

    6. Only about 3% of alerts in Simulation 4 end in a report. Where does AI help?

    Most cost is in looking at alerts that go nowhere. A ranking model lets the same team see the likely ones first, and a fast lane makes the unlikely ones cheaper to clear.

    7. What is the feedback-loop risk in training an alert-ranking model?

    Only reviewed alerts get outcomes, so the model's mistakes on low-ranked alerts stay invisible. Keep a random review sample across all ranks.

    8. What must a SAR narrative contain that a score cannot supply?

    The reasons: who, what, when, how much, why it is unusual for this customer, written for a reader who has never seen the account. Each statement should trace to a record.
