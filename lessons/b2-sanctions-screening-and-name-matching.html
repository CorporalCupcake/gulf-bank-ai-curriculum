    Sanctions Screening, Name Matching and the Road from Alert to Report

    Why one Arabic name has a dozen Latin spellings, how matching trades missed hits against a flood of false alarms, and how an alert becomes (or does not become) a suspicious activity report.

    Contents

    The whole lesson in one sentenceScreening hunts a few genuine matches among millions of near-misses, so the work is making names comparable, choosing a defensible threshold, and spending scarce analyst time where a report is most likely, with every step explainable to a regulator.

    Part 1

    The intuition

    A sanctions list is a list of names. A bank must not deal with those on it and must act without delay on finding one. So every customer at onboarding, every payment in flight, and every overnight list update is checked against it. That sounds like a lookup, but the same person has no single spelling.

    Take محمد. In Latin letters you meet Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed, Mohammad, Mohamad, Mahomet, Mhd, Mouhamed. Arabic omits short vowels, so a clerk guesses them, and Gulf, Levantine, North African and French-influenced habits differ. Passport, payment message and list may each spell it differently. Exact matching misses real hits. Matching loose enough to catch them also flags thousands of innocent Mohammed Alis.

    Real hits are very rare and false alarms common. This is the base-rate problem from imbalanced data: you hunt a handful of needles, and each loosening of the net brings in more hay. This lesson is about making the net smarter, not looser.

    • Matching is a design choice, not a measurement. Each method defines "similar" differently and is a bet about what variation your data contains.
    • The threshold is a regulatory decision wearing a number. Too high and you miss a listed person, a possible legal breach. Too low and analysts drown, and real hits get lost in the pile.

    After an alert fires, from sanctions or from transaction monitoring (B2.1 – B2.2), someone decides whether it is real, and if there are grounds for suspicion a report goes to the financial intelligence unit. Most alerts die on the way. The AI question is which ones, and who decides.

    Hold onto thisScreening has three jobs: make names comparable, score the comparison, decide what a human sees. Most gains come from the first and third. Fancy scoring on badly prepared names helps little, and a perfect score still needs a person to say what it means.
    Part 2

    How it really works

    What is screened, against what, and when

    Most real problems live in the plumbing.

    • The lists. The UN Security Council consolidated list; national lists (in the UAE, the Local Terrorist List alongside the UN list, under the Executive Office for Control and Non-Proliferation, EOCN); plus whatever a bank honours from OFAC, the EU and the UK for correspondent banking. Which are legally binding and which are risk appetite changes, so check the current text.
    • What gets screened. Customers at onboarding and on every list update (the "delta" run); payment counterparties and free-text fields; beneficial owners and directors; vessels, banks and countries in trade finance. For entities, OFAC's 50 percent rule treats an entity as blocked if listed parties own half or more, and the list will not tell you.
    • Real-time versus batch. A payment is held or released in seconds (low latency, hold-and-review queue). The customer base is screened in a nightly batch against list changes.
    • What the list gives you. A primary name, aliases (some poor), date or year of birth, nationality, sometimes a passport or ID, sometimes only a name. More identifiers on both sides make decisions easier.

    The core operation, repeated billions of times: for a name on our side, find list entries close enough to deserve a human look.

    Making names comparable: normalisation and transliteration

    Clean both sides into a common form before scoring. For Arabic names this is where most of the value lies.

    Within Arabic script. Strip diacritics (tashkeel) and the stretching letter tatweel. Fold the alef forms أ إ آ into ا, final ى into ي, and ة into ه, which writers mix up. Normalise Arabic-Indic digits. All cheap and safe.

    Name structure. Gulf names are chains: given name, father, grandfather, family or tribal name, with particles like bin/bint (son/daughter of), Abu and Al (the). Compounds like Abd al-Rahman are one name in two or three words. Order differs between passport, list and payment message. A good matcher compares sets of name parts and tolerates reordering and omission (a list entry with four parts, the customer record with two).

    Across scripts. Arabic to Latin is a relation, not a function: one Arabic string has many valid Latin forms, and one Latin string can come from several Arabic ones. Like spelling "Chaikovsky" or "Tchaikovsky", nobody is wrong. The causes:

    • Missing vowels. محمد is m-h-m-d, so Muhammad, Mohammed, Mohamed and Mohamad are the same four letters. Mahmoud (محمود) is a different name with one extra long-vowel letter that a consonant skeleton discards, so the two collide.
    • One letter, several sounds. خ is kh, h or ch. ج is j, g (Egyptian, some Gulf) or dj (French-influenced). ق is q, k, g or a glottal stop. ش is sh, ch or s.
    • One sound, several letters. ح and ه both give h; ع and أ both give a vowel-like sound or apostrophe.
    • Competing conventions. ISO 233, BGN/PCGN, DIN 31635, the UN's romanisation, and whatever the passport officer typed.

    Two responses. Many-to-many: generate plausible Latin variants of the Arabic name and Arabic originals of the Latin name, and compare across the sets. Common space: reduce both to one canonical form, such as the consonant skeleton in Simulation 1, so spelling variance vanishes. Common space is cheaper and stronger on recall, but creates collisions: Mahmoud and Muhammad share a skeleton.

    Scoring: edit distance, phonetics, tokens and learned models

    After cleaning, you need a number for "how close". The standard tools, oldest to newest:

    Production systems combine them: normalise, block, score, decide. Blocking is a cheap test (shared phonetic code, rare n-gram or token) that picks a few hundred candidates from hundreds of thousands, so the expensive scorer runs only on those. It is the retrieve-then-rerank pattern of hybrid search. Recall can never exceed the blocking step's recall, and nobody notices when blocking drops a hit.

    The oldest framing is Fellegi and Sunter (1969): decide "match", "non-match" or "possible match", with each field weighted by how often it agrees among true matches versus by chance. Agreeing on a rare surname is strong evidence; agreeing on "Mohammed" is almost none. Rarity weighting is Bayes' rule (F1.4) on name parts, and the "possible match" band is the human review queue.

    The threshold: why alerts and misses trade off

    Think of a smoke alarm: set it twitchy and it goes off for toast, set it sluggish and it misses the fire. Every screening gives a best score against the list. Innocent names spread around a middle value with a long upper tail of coincidentally similar names. Genuine matches sit higher, but those with odd transliteration or missing parts score lower and sit inside the innocent tail. One line sets both error counts at once.

    The logic matches precision, recall and thresholds, with one harsh difference. A missed fraud costs a known sum you can weigh against review cost. A missed sanctions hit is a possible legal breach with penalties that do not fit a per-case figure. So the choice is a documented risk-appetite decision approved by compliance, and you show the consequences.

    • Hard hits sit where innocent names are densest. Moving the line cannot fix that. Move the curves apart instead, with better normalisation and more evidence than the name.
    • You cannot measure your miss rate from your alerts. Hits you never raised are invisible. Test by inserting known variants of listed names and checking they fire (above-the-line and below-the-line testing), plus sampling near-threshold scores. Auditors expect this documented; it is the closest thing to ground truth.
    • The last point of recall can cost thousands of alerts. State the exchange rate: "to catch two more hard variants a month we review 11,000 more alerts" is a sentence leaders can decide on.

    Adding evidence: secondary identifiers and risk-ranked queues

    The name is weak evidence. A second fact kills most false alerts, as with two people called Ahmed Khan: one born in 1984 cannot be the listed one born in 1961. Useful extras are date of birth, nationality, ID or passport number, residence country and company registration number. Three levers, each with a cost:

    • Transliteration-aware matching. Lifts recall on hard variants but pulls in more innocent names.
    • Secondary identifiers. Auto-close alerts where both sides have a date of birth and they disagree. This only works where the list entry has one, and a mis-keyed customer date could clear a true match. Allow tolerance (year-only, day-month swaps) and log every closure.
    • A ranking model trained on past analyst decisions. As a fast lane (quicker look at low-risk alerts) it saves time with no recall loss. As an auto-close it removes humans from a legal obligation and needs strong validation and sign-off. Ask compliance what is permitted.

    None of the levers removes the threshold, and each fails silently: the identifier rule can clear a true hit and nobody sees the alert it never raised. Every automated closure needs an audit trail and a periodic human re-review of a sample.

    From alert to report: triage and the suspicious activity report workflow

    Every alert follows the same path, whether it comes from name screening, transaction monitoring (B2.1 – B2.2) or a staff referral. Sanctions matches take their own legal route (freeze, then report to the authority). Suspicion-based alerts go to a suspicious transaction or activity report (STR or SAR, used interchangeably). Click through the stages.

    Four properties set this workflow apart from most ML problems.

    • The unit of evidence is a case, not a row. Investigators read the customer, account history, counterparties, stated purpose and adverse media. Features that mimic that file (entity history, network links) beat transaction-level scores.
    • Reasons are part of the product. A filing must explain the suspicion in prose to a reader who has never seen the account. A score with no reasons cannot be filed. This is where local explanations and, carefully, language-model drafting matter.
    • You file on suspicion, not proof. The test is reasonable grounds to suspect. A missed report can be an offence, so the margin errs towards reporting, but piles of poor reports are criticised too. The true output measure is usefulness to law enforcement, which the bank mostly never sees.
    • You must not tip off. The customer must never learn a report exists. That limits what a copilot or customer-facing system may say, and what goes in a shared log.

    In the UAE, reports go to the Financial Intelligence Unit via goAML; Saudi Arabia has its own unit and channels. Timelines and report types change, so confirm the current text with compliance before quoting them.

    The gain curve is what "AI for alert triage" means: same team, same alerts, different order. Think of an A&E triage nurse: nobody is turned away faster, the sickest are just seen first. It also triggers a feedback loop. Labels come only from reviewed alerts, so if analysts follow the model's order, low-ranked alerts go unreviewed and the model never learns it was wrong about them. Keep a random sample of low-ranked alerts reviewed, permanently.

    Part 3

    How it fails, and how to spot it

    Most failures are quiet: nothing crashes, a hit just never fires. Each has a quick check.

    • Judging the matcher by its alert volume

      A manageable queue looks healthy, but hits that never alerted are invisible.

      Spot itAsk for the last below-the-line test: known variants of listed names, and what share fired.
    • English-first normalisation applied to Arabic names

      No alef folding, no Abd-compound handling, "Al" treated as a surname, middle names ignored. Arabic-script records may not be screened against Arabic list entries at all.

      Spot itTest one person in Arabic script, with and without "Al", with word order swapped, and with a compound as one or two words.
    • Over-aggressive skeletons and phonetic codes

      Collapsing to consonants matches Mahmoud to Muhammad, so alert volume jumps after a "recall improvement".

      Spot itCompare alert volume by name origin before and after a change. A rise concentrated in common Arabic given names is the signature.
    • Blocking that hides hits

      A pre-filter on first letter or phonetic code means Qaddafi, Kadhafi and Gaddafi may never reach the scorer.

      Spot itTest recall at the candidate stage separately from final scoring.
    • Identifier rules that clear true matches

      A mis-keyed or default date of birth (1 January) auto-closes a real alert.

      Spot itCount auto-closures by rule, sample them monthly, treat placeholder dates as missing.
    • Stale or truncated lists

      A list update fails to load, or ingestion drops aliases. No model fixes that.

      Spot itReconcile loaded entry counts to the source after every update; alert if a delta run did not complete.
    • Training the ranker on its own decisions

      Low-ranked alerts are never reviewed, so the model never learns it was wrong about them.

      Spot itAsk for the random-review sample of low-ranked alerts and its hit rate over time.
    • Copying the alert disposition as the label

      "Closed as false positive" is not ground truth: time pressure or a policy change alters the label without changing the world.

      Spot itHave a senior investigator re-review a random sample and measure agreement (see annotator agreement).
    • Language-model narratives with invented detail

      A drafted SAR states a transaction or relationship that is not in the case file, and nobody checks.

      Spot itRequire each sentence to link to a source record; sample drafts against the file. See groundedness checks.
    • Leaking that a report exists

      Tools, chat summaries or customer-facing agents reveal that a case or SAR is open.

      Spot itReview where case data flows, who can read it, and what an assistant may say.
    Part 4

    Self-check

    1. Why can't a bank screen names with an exact match?

    Arabic names have many valid Latin spellings (no short vowels, letters with several sounds, rival conventions), and word order and name parts vary. Exact match misses real hits, so you need normalisation plus similarity scoring.

    2. A skeleton method scores Mahmoud against Muhammad at 1.00. Is it broken?

    No. It removes vowel variation by design, and the price is collisions between different names with the same consonants. Identifiers, rarity weighting and a human decide which is the same person.

    3. At a threshold of 0.85, about 7,600 alerts a month come with about 9 of 12 genuine hits. At 0.95 you get about 60 alerts and fewer than 3 hits. What is the right threshold?

    No purely statistical answer exists. It is a documented risk-appetite decision for compliance, weighing the legal duty, review capacity and other controls. Your job is to show the curve, price each point of recall, and push the curves apart with better evidence.

    4. Why is blocking dangerous?

    It caps recall. A hit dropped at the candidate stage never reaches the scorer, and the queue shows no sign. Measure candidate-stage recall separately.

    5. Why is a ranking model safer as a fast lane than as an auto-close?

    A fast lane still has a human look at every alert, so recall is unchanged. Auto-close removes the human, so a model error becomes a silent missed obligation and needs validation, sampling and regulatory comfort.

    6. In Simulation 4, 357 reports come from 12,000 alerts. Check the arithmetic.

    12,000 × 0.10 = 1,200 escalated; × 0.35 = 420 recommended; × 0.85 = 357 filed (2.98%). Hours: 12,000 × 20/60 = 4,000 first-line, 1,200 × 6 = 7,200 investigation, 420 × 0.5 = 210 sign-off. Total 11,410, about 32 hours per report.

    7. What is the feedback-loop risk in training an alert-ranking model?

    Only reviewed alerts get outcomes. Alerts the model ranks low are never reviewed, so its mistakes on them stay invisible. Keep a random review sample across all ranks and retrain on unbiased outcomes.

    8. What must a SAR narrative contain that a score cannot supply?

    The reasons: who, what, when, how much, why it is unusual for this customer, and the grounds for suspicion, written for a reader who has never seen the account. Each statement should trace to a record in the case file.
