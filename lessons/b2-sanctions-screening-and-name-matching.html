    Sanctions Screening and Name Matching

    Part 1

    What sanctions screening is

    In one sentenceSanctions screening is checking every customer and payment name against official lists of banned people and companies, and sending anything that looks close to a human to review.

    A sanctions list is just a list of names, such as the UN consolidated list or a national list. A bank must not deal with anyone on it. So the bank checks its customers when they join, every payment's sender and receiver, and everything again whenever a list changes.

    The hard part is that a name has no single spelling. Take محمد: it can be written Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed or Mohammad in Latin letters. Arabic leaves out short vowels, so whoever writes it down has to guess. We will follow one example: the list holds Muhammad Hashimi.

    How it differs from other checks

    • Not transaction monitoring. Monitoring looks at behaviour (odd amounts, odd patterns). Screening only asks who the parties are.
    • Not an exact lookup. Exact matching misses real hits with a different spelling. Very loose matching flags thousands of innocent Mohammed Alis.
    • Not a fraud model. Real hits are very rare, so almost every alert is a false alarm. The aim is a smarter net, not a wider one.
    Part 2

    How it works

    1. Clean both sides. Lower-case the letters, strip accents and marks, and for Arabic fold the letter variants أ إ آ into ا. This step is cheap and does much of the work.
    2. Split into name parts. Gulf names are chains: given name, father, grandfather, family name, with words like bin (son of) and Al (the). Compare the set of parts, so reordering or a missing middle name does not break the match.
    3. Pick candidates. Comparing every customer with every entry is slow. A cheap test (a shared sound code or a rare word) keeps a few hundred entries for the careful scorer. This step is called blocking. If it drops a real hit, nothing later can recover it.
    4. Score. Give each candidate a similarity from 0 to 100.
    5. Apply the threshold. At or above the line is an alert. Below is cleared. Extra details such as date of birth and ID number help the analyst decide.

    Ways to score a name

    MethodIdeaStrong atWeak at
    Edit distanceCount the single-letter changes to turn one name into the otherTypos, a dropped or doubled letterReordered words, vowel drift, different scripts
    Sound codesNames that sound alike get the same codeCheap candidate pickingBuilt for English. Too coarse: Muhammad and Mahmoud can collide
    Word overlapShare of name parts in commonMissing or reordered partsCommon words (Mohammed) dominate unless weighted
    Learned modelTrain on pairs labelled same or differentCross-script, noisy dataNeeds labelled pairs. Hard to explain to a regulator

    Real systems combine them: clean, pick candidates, score, decide. Agreement on a rare surname is strong evidence. Agreement on "Mohammed" is almost none.

    Show the formula

    Similarity from edit distance = 1 − (edits ÷ length of the longer name). "Hashimi" against "Hashemi" is one edit over 7 letters, so 1 − 1/7 ≈ 0.86, or 86 out of 100.

    Part 3

    How to read scores and thresholds

    Rule of thumbThe threshold is a smoke-alarm setting. Twitchy: it goes off for toast. Sluggish: it misses the fire.
    Score 100
    The names are identical after cleaning. Even so, it is only a name match. The analyst checks date of birth and ID to see if it is the same person.
    Threshold 85
    Fewer alerts, but a real hit with a heavily changed spelling (the lone green dot) slips below the line.
    Lower the threshold to 70
    The odd spellings get caught, but the alert count can multiply. Analysts drown, and real hits get lost in the pile.
    Who sets the line
    A missed sanctions hit can be a legal breach. So the threshold is a documented risk decision that compliance approves, not a number the data science team picks alone.

    After an alert

    An analyst compares the identifiers. If the match is real, the bank freezes or blocks the funds as the law requires and reports to the financial intelligence unit (the national body that receives these reports). Most alerts are closed as false. AI can rank alerts so analysts see likely hits first, but a person still makes the call.

    Part 4

    What to screen, and where it goes wrong

    • Strong at: catching spelling variants, reordered names and transliteration, which exact matching misses.
    • Weak at: hidden ownership. The US OFAC "50 percent rule" treats a company as blocked if listed parties own half or more of it, and the list will not tell you. You need ownership data.
    • Weak at: lookalike names. A sound-code approach can score Mahmoud (a different name) as a perfect match for Muhammad.
    • Check the law: which lists are legally binding changes. Read the current text, and do not rely on this lesson.

    Always measure on your own names: take known hits with variant spellings and see how many each method catches at your threshold.

    Part 5

    Common mistakes and quick check

    • Exact matching only

      It misses Mohamed when the list says Muhammad. Real hits slip through.

    • Setting the threshold to cut the alert count

      A lower alert count looks efficient until a listed person is missed. The line is a risk decision, not a workload one.

    • Ignoring the candidate-picking step

      Recall can never be higher than that step's recall, and nobody notices when it drops a hit. Test it on its own.

    • Treating a high score as proof

      It shows the names are alike, nothing more. Two different people can share a name.

    1. Why is exact name matching not enough?

    The same person has many valid spellings, especially across Arabic and Latin scripts, so an exact lookup misses real hits.

    2. You lower the threshold. What happens to alerts and missed hits?

    Alerts go up, often by a lot, and missed hits go down. You trade analyst workload for lower risk.

    3. Why do most alerts turn out to be false?

    Real hits are very rare and many innocent names are coincidentally similar, so false alarms far outnumber true ones.

    4. What is blocking, and why does it matter?

    It is a cheap test that picks a few candidates from a huge list. If it drops a real hit, the careful scorer never sees it.

    5. Who decides the threshold?

    Compliance approves it as a documented risk decision. A missed hit can be a legal breach.
